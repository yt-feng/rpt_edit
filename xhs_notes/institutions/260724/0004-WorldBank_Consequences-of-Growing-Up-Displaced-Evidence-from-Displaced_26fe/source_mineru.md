## Abstract

This paper examines how forced displacement shapes child development beyond socioeconomic disadvantage. The paper uses original data on roughly 1,600 Venezuelan refugees, Colombian internally displaced individuals, and nondisplaced children in Medellín, Colombia. The analysis adapts rank-rank regressions from the intergenerational mobility literature to a forced displacement setting, ranking each child's developmental outcomes against household wealth. The paper documents substantial gaps that persist after holding wealth and parental background fixed. It also shows that the cause of displacement shapes the nature of the gaps. Venezuelan refugees displaced by acute material deprivation fall 19 and 13 rank points behind in cognitive and physical development, respectively. Colombian children displaced by armed conflict fall 12 rank points behind in depression and exhibit elevated trauma. Access to regularization and social services is associated with narrower gaps.

## I INTRODUCTION

Forced displacement is one of the defining crises of our time. Driven by armed conflict, political instability, and humanitarian collapse, the global displaced population has more than quintupled in the last fifty years, reaching 123 million people in 2025 (UNHCR, 2024b). Children account for nearly 45 percent of this population, yet they remain largely invisible in both policy and empirical research (UNHCR, 2024a). Displaced children face the trauma of forced migration, family disruption, prolonged legal uncertainty, interrupted schooling, the loss of social networks, and discrimination in host communities, during the years when the returns to human capital investment are highest (Heckman, 2006). These stressors can permanently impair cognitive, physical, and socioemotional trajectories in ways that general poverty alone does not predict. Whether the developmental deficits these children exhibit reflect displacement-specific mechanisms or simply socioeconomic disadvantage carries direct implications for how social protection systems should respond.

We examine three questions. First, we ask whether displaced children exhibit developmental gaps relative to non-displaced peers, and whether the cause of displacement shapes the dimensions in which those gaps appear. Children displaced by armed conflict face trauma exposure and elevated mental health risks, while those fleeing a humanitarian and economic crisis face more acute material deprivation. Second, we ask whether these gaps can be accounted for by socioeconomic status or parental background. We approach this in two steps. We first condition on household wealth (and parental background) to ask whether gaps remain at the same average level of resources. We then adapt the rank-rank regression framework of Chetty et al. (2014) and Jensen and Manning (2025) to ask whether gaps fade across the wealth distribution. In the voluntary migration literature, developmental differences among children of immigrants largely disappear once parental socioeconomic status is held fixed (Abramitzky et al., 2021a; Jensen and Manning, 2025). If the same pattern holds for forcibly displaced children, standard poverty-targeting policies may suffice; if gaps persist at equivalent levels of household wealth, targeted integration and mental health interventions may be necessary. Third, for refugee children, we ask whether access to legal status, health care, education, and social services is associated with narrower gaps.

We examine these questions in Colombia, where forcibly displaced people, including IDPs and refugees, account for 21 percent of the population and 9.6 percent of the world's displaced. The country has experienced decades of protracted internal conflict and currently hosts 8.4 million Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) legally recognized by the state (Unidad para las Víctimas, 2024). It is also the primary destination for refugees fleeing the Venezuelan crisis. $^{1}$ Since 2015, around three million Venezuelans have crossed into Colombia, fleeing acute food shortages and the collapse of public services (UNHCR, 2024a). The Colombian government responded with one of the most inclusive regularization programs in the developing world, granting roughly two million refugees temporary legal status and access to the social protection system. Colombia is therefore one of the few middle-income countries where IDPs displaced by armed conflict and refugees displaced by humanitarian collapse coexist at scale within the same institutional environment.

The two displaced populations face distinct conditions, both before and after arrival. Venezuelan refugees flee acute material deprivation (Cabra-Ruiz, Rozo and Sviatschi, 2024) and arrive in Medellín without productive or social assets, but typically without direct exposure to armed conflict (Moya and Rozo, 2025). Colombian IDPs are entitled to the same social protection benefits as non-displaced Colombians in principle, but their effective access remains lower, and they carry the burden of conflict exposure, transmitted directly or across generations (Ibáñez and Moya, 2010; Moya and Carter, 2019). These contrasts shape the developmental dimensions in which gaps are likely to appear.

Our analysis draws on the Venezuelan Refugee Panel Survey for Kids (VenReps Kids), a representative survey of children in Medellín that we designed and fielded for this study. Medellín is one of the Colombian cities with the highest concentrations of both Venezuelan refugees and Colombian IDPs. The survey covers Venezuelan refugee, Colombian IDP, and non-displaced Colombian children aged 5 to 10, and includes harmonized measures of cognitive, physical, socioemotional, and mental health development. To our knowledge, it is the first dataset to collect comparable developmental information on these three populations within a single host setting in a developing country.

Our analysis proceeds in three steps, one for each question. First, we document gaps in each developmental domain between displaced and non-displaced children. Second, we ask how much of each gap is accounted for by household wealth and parental background. We begin with unconditional differences, then condition on average household wealth, and then adapt the rank-rank framework of Chetty et al. (2014) and Jensen and Manning (2025) to characterize the relationship between household wealth rank and child developmental rank separately for refugees, IDPs, and non-displaced children. This framework distinguishes two features of the wealth-development relationship: the rank gap between displaced and non-displaced children at equivalent points in the wealth distribution, and the wealth-development gradient within each group, which captures how strongly household resources map into children's outcomes. Third, we ask whether, among refugee children, access to legal residence through regularization and other dimensions of service access is associated with narrower gaps. The main analysis centers on the first survey wave, which is representative and unaffected by differential attrition.

We document three key results. First, displaced children exhibit substantial developmental gaps relative to non-displaced peers, and the developmental domains in which gaps appear depend on the cause of displacement. Venezuelan refugee children show the largest gaps in cognitive development and physical health, scoring 0.34 standard deviations below non-displaced peers on receptive vocabulary and 0.50 standard deviations below on age-standardized Body Mass Index. These patterns are consistent with the material deprivation and limited access to services that characterize deprivation-driven displacement. Colombian IDP children, by contrast, show gaps concentrated in mental health and socioemotional development, scoring 0.25 standard deviations below non-displaced peers on depression, 0.21 on socioemotional functioning, and 0.18 on trauma symptoms, consistent with conflict exposure (Sánchez-Ariza, Cuartas and Moya, 2023). The IDP pattern holds for children directly affected by displacement and for those affected indirectly through their parents, pointing to both direct exposure and intergenerational transmission as plausible channels. The coefficients differ between IDPs and refugees in five of the six outcomes we examine, indicating that the two types of displacement are associated with distinct developmental patterns rather than a single uniform displacement experience.

Second, the gaps cannot be accounted for by socioeconomic disadvantage. Conditioning on parental and grandparental education leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. Further conditioning on household wealth attenuates the refugee gaps in cognitive development and physical health but does not close them, and the IDP gaps in mental health remain largely unchanged. The rank-rank analysis delivers the same message with sharper detail. At equivalent points in the wealth distribution, refugee children rank 18 percentile points below non-displaced peers in cognitive development and 13 points below in physical health, and IDP children rank 10 and 12 points below in cognitive development and depression. Household wealth narrows only the refugee cognitive gap. For IDPs, gaps are flat across the wealth distribution in every domain we examine, and the refugee physical health gap is similarly flat. The developmental disadvantages associated with displacement therefore do not respond to material resources in the way that ordinary socioeconomic gaps do.

Third, access to Colombia's regularization program, which grants refugees the right to work and access to the social protection system, is associated with substantially narrower gaps among refugee children. Undocumented refugee children score 0.77 standard deviations below non-displaced peers in receptive vocabulary, compared with 0.24 standard deviations for regularized refugees, and we observe a similar pattern for physical health. The association extends to other dimensions of service access, including social registry enrollment, education, and health insurance. Regularized refugees nonetheless retain gaps in cognitive development and physical health, indicating that institutional access narrows but does not eliminate the developmental disadvantages associated with displacement.

Our paper contributes to three groups of literature. First, we contribute to the literature on forced migration (Card, 1990; Foged and Peri, 2016; Dustmann, Vasiljeva and Piil Damm, 2019; Steinmayr, 2021; Michalopoulos et al., 2025; Chiovelli et al., 2021; Rozo, 2025; Moya and Rozo, 2025) by providing the first representative, longitudinal dataset on the holistic development of forcibly displaced children in a low- and middle-income host country. Over three-quarters of the world's displaced populations are hosted in settings comparable to ours, yet the existing evidence on displaced youth relies almost entirely on clinical samples, cross-sectional surveys, or data from high-income resettlement countries where selection into arrival may differ systematically from the broader displaced population (Fazel et al., 2012; Draper et al., 2022). We assemble a representative sample of Venezuelan forced migrants and Colombian internally displaced persons aged 5 to 10 in Medellín, combining snowball sampling with satellite imagery of informal settlements and administrative records from NGOs to reach a population for which no census exists. Critically, we measure development across four domains simultaneously, spanning cognitive functioning, socioemotional development, physical health, and mental health outcomes, allowing us to document how displacement-related deficits compound across developmental domains and stages in ways the existing literature, which concentrates disproportionately on a single outcome in isolation, has not addressed (Choudhary et al.,

2023; Bernhardt et al., 2024).

Second, we extend the intergenerational mobility literature to the context of forced displacement (Borjas, 1993; Card, DiNardo and Estes, 2000; Abramitzky et al., 2021a; Jensen and Manning, 2025). A central finding of this literature is that worse outcomes among migrants are largely explained by parental socioeconomic disadvantage rather than by migration status itself. We extend this question to a South-to-South forced displacement outflows, where there is genuine scope for a different conclusion. Unlike voluntary economic migrants, forcibly displaced people are more likely to have suffered trauma, asset losses, chronic poverty, and ongoing rights uncertainty that economic vulnerability by itself does not capture. We adapt the rank-rank regression framework of Chetty et al. (2014) and Jensen and Manning (2025) to ask whether developmental gaps between displaced and non-displaced children persist after accounting for household wealth or whether they can be fully attributed to socioeconomic disadvantage, and we exploit variation in the cause of displacement to assess whether conflict-induced and crisis-induced displacement differ in this respect. By focusing on child development outcomes rather than adult wages, our analysis speaks directly to the timing of policy intervention, given the substantially higher returns to investment in early life (Heckman and Mosso, 2014).

Finally, we contribute to the literature linking forced displacement to mental health outcomes for children in two distinct ways (Pluess, Brown and Panter-Brick, 2025). First, we document evidence for internally displaced persons and displacement in Latin America, which has received far less attention than refugee displacement in developed countries, the Middle East, or sub-Saharan Africa (Chen et al., 2019; Salami et al., 2020; Hazer and Gredebäck, 2023; Popham et al., 2022; McEwen et al., 2023). Second, by comparing Venezuelan forced migrants and Colombian IDPs within the same institutional setting in a middle income country where full mobility is allowed and displaced populations have legal support, we contribute direct evidence on both gaps and document patterns consistent with the intergenerational transmission of conflict-related trauma and its interactions with service access, building on prior evidence of how political violence shapes mental health and economic outcomes in Colombia (Ibáñez and Moya, 2010; Moya and Carter, 2019; Marroquín Rivera et al., 2020; Sánchez-Ariza, Cuartas and Moya, 2023).

## II THE COLOMBIAN DISPLACEMENT CONTEXT

Colombia hosts 9.8 percent of the forcibly displaced populations in the world, encompassing Venezuelan refugees displaced by an extreme economic and humanitarian crisis and internally displaced Colombians uprooted by decades of armed conflict. The Colombian government's policy response toward both groups ranks among the most comprehensive globally (Ibáñez, Moya and Velásquez, 2022; Moya and Rozo, 2025). Below we briefly describe each crisis and refer readers to Appendix A for additional institutional and contextual detail, including the scale and geographic distribution of Venezuelan migration to Colombia, the policy response and resulting rights and service access available to Venezuelan refugees, key differences in access to services such as education between Colombians and Venezuelans, and a brief overview of the geography and historical trends of internal displacement within Colombia.

II.A Refugees in Colombia: humanitarian crisis-driven displacement from Venezuela

Over the past decade, more than 7.7 million Venezuelans have been forced to flee their country due to service collapse and severe material deprivation (Cabra-Ruiz, Rozo and Sviatschi, 2024). The majority sought refuge in Latin American countries, with Colombia becoming the primary destination. By 2023, Colombia hosted approximately 2.8 million Venezuelan refugees, representing 5.8 percent of the country's population relative to the 2018 census (Migración Colombia, 2023). Venezuelan refugees settled primarily in border areas and major cities, including Bogotá, Medellín, Cúcuta, and Barranquilla.

Colombia's policy response expanded considerably in response to the massive inflows of Venezuelan refugees. Regularization programs, initially limited to better-educated refugees, broadened significantly in 2018 and 2021, culminating in the Permiso por Protección Temporal (PPT), which granted legal status to over 1.8 million refugees by 2023 (including anyone who had arrived to Colombia by 2021). Research shows that these programs improved refugees' labor market outcomes, income, and consumption (Ibáñez et al., 2024). Regardless of legal status, Venezuelan refugee children and adolescents are entitled to basic education and emergency healthcare. However, access to graduation credentials, full subsidized health insurance, and eligibility for social programs through Sisbén remains restricted to regularized refugees. $^{2}$

## II.B IDPs in Colombia: conflict-driven displacement from rural areas

Colombia hosts the largest population of internally displaced persons (IDPs) in the world. By 2025, the country had registered more than 8.8 million internally displaced individuals, equivalent to 18 percent of the national population according to the 2018 census (UN-HCR, 2025). Internal displacement has been driven primarily by a protracted armed conflict involving the Colombian government, nonstate armed groups including the FARC and ELN, and organized criminal organizations (de Memoria Histórica et al., 2021). Displacement intensified sharply over the last three decades, reaching a peak in 2003.

Colombia has developed one of the world's most comprehensive legal frameworks for internally displaced persons, combining victim recognition, land restitution, psychosocial support, and financial compensation (Ibáñez, Moya and Velásquez, 2022). $^{3}$ Evidence suggests these reparations programs improved housing, education, and health outcomes for recipients, though effects on labor market integration have been more limited (Guarin, Vélez and Posso, 2023). Despite persistent challenges in implementation capacity and budget constraints, Colombia's policy framework has meaningfully advanced the rights and material conditions of millions displaced by the conflict.

## II.C Contrasting the Two Displaced Populations

The two displaced populations in Colombia differ along three dimensions relevant to children's development. The first is the cause of displacement. Venezuelan refugees were uprooted by economic collapse and the failure of public services, but most arrived without direct exposure to armed conflict (Moya and Rozo, 2025). Colombian IDPs were uprooted by armed conflict, and many carry direct or household exposure to violence into displacement (Sánchez-Ariza, Cuartas and Moya, 2023).

The second is demographic origin. Venezuelan refugees come from a range of socioeconomic backgrounds and predominantly urban settings, reflecting the breadth of Venezuela's collapse. Colombian IDPs originate predominantly from rural areas and have lower average levels of formal education, reflecting the geographic concentration of armed conflict in the Colombian countryside.

The third is institutional standing. Refugees face a clock and a status. Their access to schooling, full health insurance, and Sisbén-targeted programs depends on regularization, and discrimination, language and cultural barriers, and limited administrative capacity further restrict de facto access. IDPs are Colombian citizens with full legal standing and dedicated victims-registry rights, yet their effective access to social protection lags that of non-IDPs.

These contrasts motivate our empirical comparison. If displacement operated through a single channel, the two populations would show similar developmental gaps. If the cause of displacement and service access shapes which developmental domains are affected, the gaps should differ in nature, not only in magnitude.

## III DATA: THE VENREPS-KIDS STUDY

To conduct this study, we collected the Venezuelan Refugee Panel Study for Kids (VenRePs-Kids), a longitudinal household survey designed to measure the developmental trajectory ries of forcibly displaced children in Colombia. The study comprises a representative sample of Venezuelan refugee and Colombian households with children aged 5 to 10 in Medellín, Colombia. Appendix B provides additional detail on the survey timeline, data collection procedures, sampling frame construction, questionnaire design, the geographic distribution of surveyed households, survey modules, and instrument validation.

Location. We conducted the study in Medellín, Colombia's second-largest city and a major urban hub with a long history of both international and internal forced displacement. Medellín hosts the third-largest concentration of Venezuelan migrants in Colombia, representing roughly 6.4 percent of the city's total population (Medellín Cómo Vamos, 2024). It is also among the regions most affected by Colombia's protracted armed conflict, with IDPs comprising about 8 percent of its residents (Unidad para las Víctimas, 2024). This dual role as a host to both Venezuelan forced migrants and Colombian IDPs makes Medellín an exceptional setting for examining the developmental consequences of different types of forced displacement within a shared geographic and institutional environment.

Timeline. The baseline survey was conducted between October and December 2022. Six months later, we implemented a short follow-up to update contact information and ensure sample continuity. Approximately one year after baseline, between October and December 2023, we administered the full survey again to capture changes in children's well-being, integration, and household context.

Design and sampling. The baseline survey is representative of children (ages 5–10) residing in Medellín. The first consists of Colombian-born youth with Colombian parents, of whom 34.3 percent belong to internally displaced households. The second consists of Venezuelan-born youth with Venezuelan parents who migrated to Colombia between 2016 and 2020 during the height of the Venezuelan exodus. The sample was stratified by gender and socioeconomic level, based on Colombia's neighborhood income classification system (strata 1 through 6), and deliberately restricted to strata 1 through 4 to avoid overrepresentation of higher-income households less likely to include refugees in need of support.

Colombian households were sampled using the 2018 National Population Census. Residential blocks were stratified by socioeconomic level and a representative sample of blocks was randomly selected. Within each selected block, households with children aged 5–10 were identified and randomly sampled to ensure representativeness across gender, age, and socioeconomic level. Internal displacement status for Colombian households was subsequently verified by merging survey data with administrative records. The final sample included 1,370 households, from which we collected information on 1,579 children (47 percent Venezuelan).

The sampling frame for Venezuelan households was developed through an extensive listing exercise combining multiple sources, including the 2018 Population Census, NGOs, humanitarian agency databases such as iMMAP, and satellite imagery of informal migrant settlements, to identify areas with high concentrations of Venezuelan migrants. Field teams conducted on-site verification of these settlements and implemented a structured snowball sampling strategy to reach mobile and informal communities not captured in official registries. This multi-source approach was designed to reduce the selection toward more socially connected households that standard snowball methods can introduce. The listing process identified approximately 7,000 Venezuelan families with children in Medellín, from which households were randomly selected to ensure comparability across age, gender, and socioeconomic background.

Eligibility criteria. The study targeted children aged 5–10 residing in Medellín. Colombian participants were eligible if both the child and parents were Colombian nationals. Venezuelan participants were eligible if both the child and parents were Venezuelan nationals who migrated to Colombia between 2016 and 2020 and had not acquired Colombian citizenship. $^{4}$ In households with more than two eligible children, up to two were randomly selected to participate.

Tracking rates. At the follow-up survey conducted one year after baseline, we successfully recontacted 1,355 of the 1,579 baseline participants, representing an overall tracking rate of 85.8 percent. Tracking rates varied by displacement status: 68.8 percent for non-displaced Colombians, 84.5 percent for IDPs, and 60.8 percent for Venezuelan migrants. These differences are consistent with the higher residential mobility and socioeconomic vulnerability typically observed among forcibly displaced populations. Attrition was not random. Households with lower wealth, lower caregiver education, and weaker connections to public services were significantly more likely to exit the sample, and the dominant predictor of attrition among Venezuelan households was lack of regularization status (see Appendix B.8 for more details). Our main analysis, therefore, focuses on the first-wave data, which is representative and unaffected by differential attrition. In secondary analysis, we use the panel to assess whether the baseline gaps persist over time, but interpret those results cautiously given the non-random nature of attrition. Specifically, we report results for the observed sample without imputation and complement them with Lee bounds that account for differential attrition under monotonicity assumptions (Lee, 2005).

Data collection. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by a team of 35 trained enumerators, including 12 social workers and psychologists, all of whom were residents of Medellín with prior experience in household surveys, anthropometric measurement, and child development assessments. Each interview lasted approximately two hours and involved two enumerators: one interviewed the primary caregiver, typically the mother, while the other engaged directly with the child. The primary caregiver was defined as the person most familiar with the child's daily activities and decision-making. Caregivers received a grocery voucher valued at approximately 10 US dollars as compensation for their participation in each survey round.

Survey modules. The questionnaire comprised seven modules covering household characteristics, sociodemographics, education, health and nutrition including anthropometric measures, time use and child labor, prosocial preferences and migration outlook, and socioemotional and mental health. Prior to fieldwork, enumerators completed standardized training on questionnaire administration and on the use of validated instruments to measure mental health and developmental outcomes.

Refugee children face significant educational disadvantages relative to non-displaced peers with refugee children exhibiting substantially larger gaps than IDP children. As illustrated in Panel A, the three groups are balanced on gender and age. However, the groups diverge sharply on years of education. This result is consistent with school enrollment barriers related to legal status and institutional access rather than socioeconomic disadvantage alone.

Household characteristics suggest that the educational disadvantages of refugee children cannot be explained by parental human capital, and are instead consistent with barriers in accessing Colombia's social protection system. Panel B reports that caregiver education differs markedly across groups. IDP caregivers have the lowest average education at 9.2 years, significantly below non-displaced households (10.2 years). IDP households have been displaced for an average of 205 months (over 17 years), while refugee households average 48 months (about 4 years). Access to the social protection system, measured by whether the household is registered in Sisben, also reveals significant gaps for refugees. The share of households registered in Sisben is 0.84 among IDPs, slightly above the 0.81 observed for non-displaced households, while only 0.38 of refugee households are registered; all differences are statistically significant. Although 80 percent of refugee households in our sample hold some form of legal status through regularization programs, their low Sisben affiliation suggests that de jure access to services has not translated into de facto integration into Colombia's social protection system. Considering that Sisbén affiliation is the primary gateway to subsidized health insurance and social transfers in Colombia, this gap between formal regularization and actual enrollment in the social protection system may help explain why refugee children face persistent school enrollment disadvantages even when their households have a regularized status.

## IV EMPIRICAL STRATEGY

Our empirical analysis proceeds in several steps. First, we estimate developmental gaps between displaced and non-displaced children across cognitive, physical, socioemotional, and mental health outcomes. We begin with unconditional gaps and then add controls for child demographics, parental education, and household wealth to assess how much of the gap is explained by differences in socioeconomic position. Second, we adapt the rank-rank framework of Chetty et al. (2014) and Jensen and Manning (2025) to examine whether displaced children rank below non-displaced children at similar points in the wealth distribution, and whether the wealth–development gradient differs by displacement group. Third, we examine whether these gaps vary with access to legal regularization and other services. We then use the two-wave panel to assess whether gaps persist over time. The main analysis uses the first wave, which is representative and unaffected by differential attrition; Section IV.E presents the longitudinal evidence. $^{5}$

## IV.A Main outcomes

We focus our analysis on six primary outcomes that capture distinct dimensions of child development among children ages 5 to 10.

1. Cognitive development. We measure cognitive development using the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT), an untimed assessment of receptive vocabulary administered directly to children. The test comprises 125 items of increasing difficulty, each presenting four images from which the child identifies the one corresponding to a spoken word. We conducted a preliminary validation with Venezuelan mothers in our sample to verify that vocabulary items carried consistent meanings across Colombian and Venezuelan dialects. Our primary outcome is the percentile rank based on the published test norms.

2. Socioemotional development. We measure socioemotional development using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), a 25-item behavioral screening instrument completed by caregivers. The SDQ covers emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity and inattention, peer relationship difficulties, and prosocial behavior. Our primary outcome is the 20-item total difficulties score, which aggregates the

first four domains.

3. Anxiety, depression, and trauma symptoms. We measure mental health using the Trauma Symptom Checklist for Young Children (TSCYC), a standardized caregiver-report instrument designed to assess acute and chronic trauma-related symptoms in children. From this instrument, we use the 9-item Anxiety subscale, the Depression subscale, and the total trauma score as separate outcomes. The full TSCYC is a 90-item measure that captures a broad range of post-traumatic symptoms, including anxiety, depression, anger, dissociation, and trauma-specific responses.

4. Physical development. We assess physical development using body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and standardized using World Health Organization (WHO) reference norms by age and sex. Because the standardized BMI score does not distinguish between deviations above and below the reference mean, we also construct separate indicators for underweight (BMI more than one standard deviation below the WHO reference mean) and overweight (BMI more than one standard deviation above it) to distinguish the direction of nutritional deficits.

All instruments used in the analysis have been validated in Spanish-speaking populations. To facilitate comparison across outcomes measured on different scales, we standardize each measure using the Wave 1 mean and standard deviation in our sample. When relevant, we also construct indicator variables for whether a child exceeds established clinical thresholds. Appendix B.7. reports the psychometric properties of each instrument, including internal consistency and confirmatory factor analysis statistics, separately by displacement group.

## IV.B Developmental gaps among displaced children

We examine the developmental gaps between displaced and non-displaced children using the following specification:

$$
y _ {i} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \mathrm{IDP} _ {i} + \alpha_ {2} \text { Refugee } _ {i} + \Omega^ {\prime} X _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i},\tag{1}
$$

where $y_{i}$ denotes the standardized outcome of child i, IDP $_{i}$ and Refugee $_{i}$ are indicators for Colombian internally displaced and Venezuelan refugee children, respectively, and the omitted category is non-displaced Colombian children. We begin by estimating equation (1) without controls, so that $\alpha_{1}$ and $\alpha_{2}$ capture unconditional gaps relative to non-displaced peers. We then sequentially augment $X_{i}$ to examine how these gaps change when conditioning on observed characteristics. We first add child age, sex, and parental education, characteristics plausibly predetermined with respect to the child's current developmental outcomes; and then add a household wealth index to assess the extent to which the lower average wealth of displaced families accounts for the observed differences. Standard errors are clustered at the household level throughout to account for correlation between siblings in the same household.

A limitation of the wealth-control specification is that the household wealth index is measured at the time of the survey for all groups and therefore reflects contemporaneous living standards after displacement or migration has already occurred. For both Venezuelan refugee and IDP households, this raises a bad-control concern because current wealth may itself be an outcome of forced migration and displacement rather than a predetermined measure of socioeconomic status. In other words, conditioning on contemporaneous wealth may absorb part of the mechanism through which displacement affects children's development rather than isolate pre-existing differences in household economic position. To mitigate this concern, we complement the wealth-control specification with a measure of grandparents' education, which serves as a proxy for the household's socioeconomic background prior to the Venezuelan economic and humanitarian crisis and is less likely to be directly affected by the migration or displacement shock itself. This alternative control recovers pre-displacement living standards, providing a proxy for household background that is less directly affected by displacement.

## IV.C Developmental rank-rank gradients by displacement status

Conditioning on average wealth answers the question of whether displaced children exhibit worse outcomes than non-displaced children with the same average level of household resources. It does not reveal whether the displacement penalty varies across the wealth distribution—that is, whether wealthier displaced households are better able to buffer their children from the costs of displacement. To address this, we adapt the rank-rank framework of Chetty et al. (2014) and Jensen and Manning (2025) to our setting. We rank each child by their score on each developmental outcome (normalized to the unit interval, with higher values indicating better outcomes) and rank households by wealth index that summarizes material living conditions at the time of the baseline survey. We then estimate:

$$
Y _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {j \in \{\mathrm{IDP}, \mathrm{Ref} \}} \beta_ {1} ^ {j} d _ {i} ^ {j} + \beta_ {2} W _ {i} + \sum_ {j \in \{\mathrm{IDP}, \mathrm{Ref} \}} \beta_ {3} ^ {j} \left(d _ {i} ^ {j} \times W _ {i}\right) + \beta_ {4} ^ {\prime} X _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}\tag{2}
$$

where $Y_{i}$ is the percentile rank of child $i$ in the relevant outcome, $d_{i}^{j}$ are displacement group indicators, $W_{i}$ is the household's wealth rank, and $X_{i}$ includes child age, sex, and survey cohort fixed effects. The coefficient $\beta_{1}^{j}$ captures the displacement penalty at the bottom of the wealth distribution (the level shift in the wealth-outcome relationship for each displacement group). The coefficient $\beta_{2}$ measures how strongly wealth predicts outcomes for non-displaced children, and $\beta_{3}^{j}$ measures how much steeper or flatter that gradient is for displaced children.

This specification answers two questions that the conditional-mean comparison cannot. First, do displaced children rank below non-displaced peers even at the same position in the wealth distribution? A significant $\beta_{1}^{j}$ indicates that the gap persists beyond what economic position can explain. Second, does household wealth help displaced children close the remaining gap? If $\beta_{3}^{j} > 0$ , wealthier displaced families partially offset the displacement penalty; if $\beta_{3}^{j} = 0$ , the penalty is constant across the wealth distribution. If both $\beta_{1}^{j}$ and $\beta_{3}^{j}$ are jointly insignificant, the developmental gaps are fully explained by household wealth rather than displacement itself, which is the pattern documented in the voluntary migration literature for children of immigrants in Denmark (Jensen and Manning, 2025).

We implement equation (2) using the wealth index as a continuous variable and check robustness to alternative functional forms using wealth deciles and ventiles (Appendix E). We do not implement the full percentile-dummy approach of Jensen and Manning (2025) given our sample sizes of approximately 1,600 children, for which 100 dummies would be severely over-parameterized. A potential concern is that the post-migration wealth index may itself be a consequence of displacement. We address this concern by replacing it with retrospectively collected wealth reported for Venezuelan households, and find qualitatively unchanged results, suggesting that our estimates are not driven by post-displacement changes in household wealth. $^{6}$

## IV.D Service access and legal status

Venezuelan refugees in Colombia face a distinctive policy environment in which legal residence status governs access to formal labor markets, health insurance, the means-tested social registry (Sisbén), and formal education. We exploit variation in access to these margins to assess whether they are associated with narrower developmental gaps among refugee children. For this purpose, we replace the single refugee indicator in equation (1) with separate indicators for refugees with and without access to a given service dimension:

$$
y _ {i} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} \mathrm{IDP} _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} \text {Refugee} _ {i} \cdot S _ {i} + \gamma_ {3} \text {Refugee} _ {i} \cdot (1 - S _ {i}) + \Omega^ {\prime} X _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i},\tag{3}
$$

where $IDP_{i}$ is an indicator for Colombian internally displaced children, $Refugee_{i}$ is an indicator for Venezuelan refugee children, $S_{i} \in \{0,1\}$ indicates access to the service dimension under consideration, and the omitted category is non-displaced Colombian children. The coefficients $\gamma_{2}$ and $\gamma_{3}$ capture the developmental gap relative to non-displaced peers for refugee children with and without access to the relevant service, respectively. We estimate equation (3) separately for each service dimension: legal residence through regularization programs, enrollment in Sisben, $^{7}$ access to formal education, and health insurance coverage. $^{8}$

Regularization status and service access are not randomly assigned. Refugees who obtained legal residence may differ from those who did not on unobserved dimensions including time since arrival, social networks, and initiative in navigating bureaucratic processes. The estimates from equation (3) therefore capture conditional associations rather than the causal effect of service provision.

## IV.E Persistence over time

We extend equation (1) to the two-wave panel to test whether the baseline gaps narrow over time. We estimate two specifications: a pooled OLS that averages the gap across both waves, and a wave-interaction specification that interacts each displacement indicator with a wave-two indicator to capture changes in the gap between waves. Because attrition between waves is non-random and concentrated among more vulnerable households (Appendix B.8), we estimate each specification under three approaches to missing data: the unbalanced panel without imputation, simple mean imputation for wave-two attriters, and Lee (2005) bounds that bracket the estimates under monotone selection. Comparing results across these approaches allows us to assess the sensitivity of the dynamic estimates to alternative attrition assumptions.

The distinction carries direct policy implications: IDP children need tailored mental health support alongside cognitive interventions, while refugee children need nutritional and health services that household economic resources alone cannot provide.

## V.D Dynamic effects

The persistence analysis should be interpreted with caution because attrition between waves is substantial and differential. About one quarter of baseline children were not reinterviewed in wave two. Attrition rates were 32 percent among Venezuelan households, 25 percent among IDPs, and 17 percent among non-displaced Colombians. Appendix B.8 shows that attrition is correlated with baseline vulnerability, so the wave-two sample no longer preserves the baseline representative design. The estimates in this section are therefore suggestive.

We estimate the pooled OLS and wave-interaction specifications described in Section IV.E under three approaches to missing data: the observed unbalanced panel, simple mean imputation for wave-two attriters, and Lee (2005) bounds. Across the observed and mean-imputation specifications, the wave-interaction coefficients are small and statistically indistinguishable from zero for five of the six outcomes: cognitive development, physical health, socioemotional difficulties, anxiety, and trauma. The exception is IDP depression, where the interaction is positive in both the observed panel and the mean-imputation specification, indicating some narrowing of the depression gap between waves. The Lee bounds are wide for all outcomes and include both convergence and divergence, so they do not provide informative evidence on the direction of change. Full results are reported in Appendix F.

Overall, we find little evidence that developmental gaps close over a one-year horizon. This pattern is consistent with the baseline results. Refugee gaps in cognitive and physical development persist over time, suggesting that interrupted schooling, nutritional stress, and limited access to services do not resolve quickly after settlement. IDP mental health gaps also remain largely stable, consistent with the interpretation that conflict-related trauma is not explained by contemporaneous household resources alone.

## VI CONCLUSION

This paper studies how forced displacement shapes child development beyond what poverty alone would predict. Using original longitudinal data on Venezuelan refugee, Colombian internally displaced, and non-displaced children in Medellín, we find that displacement is associated with significant developmental penalties that persist after accounting for household wealth. These penalties are not uniform. Refugee children, who faced acute material deprivation but not armed conflict, bear the largest cognitive and physical health costs. Internally displaced children, displaced by armed conflict, exhibit elevated mental health burdens consistent with conflict-related trauma. Legal access to the social protection system through regularization is associated with a large reduction in the refugee gaps but does not fully eliminated them. These penalties show no evidence of convergence over our one-year panel.

From a social protection perspective, these findings suggest that displaced children require support that extends well beyond what standard anti-poverty programs can offer. Income transfers and poverty-targeting instruments, while essential, do not address the specific developmental needs that displacement creates. Refugee children need not only legal status but actual enrollment in health insurance, education, and nutritional support. For internally displaced families, the persistence of mental health difficulties despite full legal access and over a decade of settlement suggests that standard social protection frameworks were not designed to address the psychological legacy of armed conflict. Effective policy responses will require integrating targeted mental health services into existing programs for displaced households. More broadly, our results suggest that governments cannot treat displaced populations as a single category. Deprivation-driven and conflict-driven displacement produce distinct developmental profiles, and social protection systems that fail to distinguish between them risk addressing the wrong needs.

Our study has several limitations that future research should aim to solve. Our estimates document conditional associations rather than causal effects of displacement, as displacement is not randomly assigned. Families that were displaced may differ systematically from those that were not on dimensions that our controls cannot capture, including pre-displacement mental health, social capital, and exposure to community violence. The relationship between regularization and narrower gaps is similarly suggestive, as refugees who obtained legal status may differ on unobserved dimensions from those who did not, including time since arrival, social networks, and proactiveness in seeking services. Our panel covers only one year, which is too short to determine whether the gaps we document will diminish with sustained integration. Longer panels that can track developmental trajectories as displaced children age, complementary evidence from other host countries, and research designs that can more cleanly identify the causal mechanisms linking displacement to child development represent important directions for future work.

## B.3 Eligibility criteria

The study targeted children aged 5 to 10 residing in Medellín. Colombian participants were eligible if both the child and parents were Colombian nationals. Venezuelan participants were eligible if both the child and parents were Venezuelan nationals who arrived between 2016 and 2020 and had not naturalized. Children from binational households or recently naturalized households were excluded to focus on the integration experiences of fully displaced populations. Within each household, up to two children were randomly selected when multiple eligible children were present.

## B.4 Sampling frame and design

Colombian households were sampled using the 2018 National Population Census. Residential blocks were stratified by socioeconomic strata (levels 1 through 4), and a representative sample of blocks was randomly selected. Within selected blocks, households with children aged 5 to 10 were identified and randomly sampled to achieve representativeness across gender, age, and socioeconomic strata.

The sampling frame for Venezuelan households was constructed through an extensive listing exercise. We combined multiple sources, including the 2018 Population Census, databases from NGOs and humanitarian agencies (such as iMMAP), and satellite imagery of informal settlements, to identify locations with high concentrations of Venezuelan refugees. Field teams conducted on-site verification of settlements and implemented a structured snowball sampling strategy to reach mobile and informal communities not captured in official registries. This approach led to the construction of a comprehensive list of approximately 7,000 Venezuelan families with children in Medellín. From this listing, households were randomly selected to ensure comparability by age, gender, and socioeconomic background.

To maximize comparability, the study excluded binational households (with one Colombian and one Venezuelan parent) and focused only on households where all members shared the same nationality. Internal displacement status for Colombian households was verified by merging survey data with the Colombian official conflict victims registry (RUV), using household identifiers to match records.

The final sample included 1,370 households, from which we collected information on 1,579 children (47 percent Venezuelan).

The omitted reference category is non-displaced Colombian children. IDP refers to Colombian internally displaced persons displaced by armed conflict. Refugee refers to Venezuelan refugee children.

The variable Post is an indicator equal to one for the second survey wave. Each displacement indicator is interacted with Wave 2 to capture whether the developmental gap for that group narrows or widens between waves relative to the reference group. Columns (1) and (2) report results for the observed sample without attrition adjustment, estimated without and with controls, respectively. Columns (3) and (4) address attrition using simple mean imputation. Columns (5)-(8) report Lee (2009) bounds, presenting lower and upper bound estimates under alternative assumptions about selective attrition.

Control variables include child gender, child age, and maximum education level within the household. Standard errors clustered at the household level are reported in parentheses. All hypothesis tests are two-sided. $^{*}p < 0.10$ , $^{**}p < 0.05$ , $^{***}p < 0.01$ .

# 1. Executive summary

The central role of tax in financing for development, and the importance of the provision of high-quality technical assistance on tax was reaffirmed at the highest political level in 2025. The Sevilla Commitment of the Fourth International Conference on Financing for Development (Sevilla Commitment) stressed the role of taxation in financing for development (FfD). In addition, there was a commitment to double funding for domestic resource mobilisation (DRM), the only area of development co-operation to receive such a commitment. Moreover, the Platform for Collaboration on Tax (PCT) report to the G20\* highlighted both the progress and continued scope for technical assistance on tax.

\* PCT (2025), Progress in Strengthening Frameworks for Building Tax Capacity: A Report to G20 Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors, https://www.tax-platform.org/sites/default/files/publications/Progress-in-Strengthening-Frameworks-for-Building-Tax-Capacity.pdf.

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) support to developing countries on tax continues to evolve, reflecting changing demands, and contexts. With a growing number of developing countries at different stages of adopting an expanding set of international standards on tax, their technical assistance needs have become more diverse. At the same time technological developments have expanded the scope to provide assistance, while the potential for collaboration and co-ordination with partners continues to increase. These developments are taking place in a tight funding environment requiring greater efficiencies in the delivery of assistance. In this context the OECD has sought to focus its efforts where demand is clear and where it can provide identifiable added value.

Demand remains high for support across the range of Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) and Transfer Pricing Actions, which is being met by a combination of bilateral and multilateral support. The number of bilateral BEPS and Transfer Pricing programmes running in 2025 reached 28. While these programmes provide the most comprehensive support in these areas, they are no longer the only means through which support on BEPS and transfer pricing issues is provided. There is growing use of multilateral approaches, ranging from large-scale training on foundational concepts, to in-depth extended workshops with small groups on specific topics. In addition, reflecting more niche and complex demands, support is becoming more tailored to specific issues such as on the Global Minimum Tax (GMT) and tax incentives, or the extractive sector.

Supporting developing countries in response to developments on the GMT was an area of focus in 2025, and is likely to become an even greater priority in 2026. With numerous countries already implementing the GMT, many developing countries were keen to understand their options, both under the GMT, and with respect to their tax incentives, while also tracking progress under the Side-by-Side package $^{1}$ .

Updated economic impact assessments were shared with countries, while 20 countries received bilateral support on the GMT and tax incentives reform. This was complemented by a range of multilateral events and workshops, and the PCT's publication of the Tax Incentives Principles $^{2}$ . Following the agreement on the Side-by-Side package in January 2026, bringing increased stability and certainty, as well as some important simplifications, further work will be needed in 2026 and beyond, to support countries to understand their options and implement their policy responses to the GMT.

Tax Inspectors Without Borders (TIWB) celebrated its 10 $^{th}$ anniversary in 2025, and its unique approach was recognised by inclusion in the Sevilla Platform for Action. Fourteen TIWB programmes were launched in 2025, including the first pilot programme on the GMT. Revenues raised from TIWB programmes passed a cumulative total of USD 2.72 billion. However, securing the expertise for TIWB programmes is becoming increasingly challenging due to a combination of

financial pressures in partner administrations and more specialised requirements for expertise.

The Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes (Global Forum) supported 88 developing countries through its capacity-building and outreach activities in 2025, reflecting the continued growth in demand for support, both in volume and complexity. This support covered the full spectrum of transparency standards, including the signing and ratification of the Multilateral Convention on Mutual Administrative Assistance in Tax Matters (MAAC); implementation and effective use of the Exchange of Information on Request (EOIR) and Automatic Exchange of Information (AEOI) standards; implementing the new Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF); and strengthening Information Security Management (ISM). In this area, the ISM Maturity Assessment Initiative, launched in 2024, is playing a valuable role in helping countries develop a pathway towards AEOI implementation.

Developing countries are making increased use of transparency standards. In 2024 -the most recent year for which data on developing countries is available- an additional EUR 4 billion was raised through offshore tax investigations and voluntary disclosure programmes, bringing the total raised since 2009 to EUR 48 billion. In the same year, developing countries sent 4 600 requests for information to other jurisdictions, representing a $43\%$ increase compared to 2023. Developing countries participating in AEOI received information on 53 million financial accounts with a total value of EUR 2.7 trillion.

Developing countries continue to request OECD analysis on a range of tax policy issues, including health financing and social protection. New reports on social protection financing in Senegal and Thailand were delivered in 2025, with the work in Thailand already feeding into a recent pension reform and increase in social security ceiling. There has also been continued work on health financing, tobacco taxation, tax incentives and informality.

Demand for support on implementing the international standards for value added tax (VAT) on cross-border e-commerce remains high, with a big increase in requests from Francophone Africa in 2025. The international standards on VAT provide a tried and tested approach, which has proven to deliver significant revenues. As a result, a growing number of developing countries are implementing these standards, often with OECD support. Following successful OECD-supported reforms in Benin, several other Francophone countries have approached the OECD seeking similar assistance, which is now being provided.

Four additional countries joined the OECD Global Revenue Statistics initiative, which by the end of 2025 included 139 economies. It produces data increasingly used by governments, academia and the wider public. The Global Revenue Statistics database covers economies at all income levels and is an increasingly valuable tool for governments and researchers alike. The Academy also serves as an entry point for engagement with the Task Force on Tax Crimes and Other Financial Crimes (TFTC), the Ten Global Principles for Fighting Tax Crimes, and the TIWB-Criminal Tax Investigation (TIWB-CI) programme, in which 20 developing countries have now participated.

The PCT remains a key partnership for the OECD, where the four partners are speaking collectively on key issues on tax and development. In 2025, the PCT delivered several useful interventions, including a side-event at the Fourth International Conference on Financing for Development (FfD4), a set of principles on tax incentives, as well as a well-received report to the G20 on progress in tax capacity building. These interventions demonstrate a strong shared view across the PCT partners that can contribute to maintaining the global focus on tax and development, with an increasing emphasis on delivering tangible results through improved monitoring and evaluation.

A number of the recommendations from the external evaluation conducted in 2024 and published in 2025 $^{3}$ have been acted on in 2025. These include:

\- Increased focus on ex ante support to countries. This has been pursued across a range of the new standards including support for the GMT impact assessment, as well as extensive outreach and dialogue with

countries on the Subject to Tax Rule (STTR) and the simplified approach to transfer pricing (Amount B).

\- Further refinement and strengthening of relationships and co-ordination with regional partners. In Africa, the evolving relationship with partners has enabled OECD technical assistance to focus on more complex and specialised areas of support, while in the Caribbean strong collaboration between partners is supporting a regional programme with broad participation from both the countries in the regions and regional/global development partners.

Priorities for 2026 will include supporting developing countries in their policy responses to the GMT and the further development of the new platform for self-paced training, while continuing to respond to growing demand for assistance as efficiently as possible. Following the agreement on the side-by-side package in January, developing countries face a range of possible policy responses. The OECD will further articulate these options, and support countries in navigating them. In addition, the self-paced training offer under the Global Relations Programme has moved to a new platform, requiring a transition for its more than ten thousand users. More broadly, demand for assistance remain high across the full range of areas covered in this report, while Official Development Assistance (ODA) budgets are shrinking, requiring a strong focus on efficiency.

## 2. The OECD offer to support domestic resource mobilisation

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has developed a comprehensive range of support, with a proven track record, that developing countries can integrate into their revenue strategies to strengthen domestic tax collection. This support is designed to assist countries that wish to participate in tax-related work undertaken at the OECD, as well as benefit from the tools and instruments developed at the OECD, the data it collects and the expertise available across both the Secretariat and the membership of the OECD-hosted forums on tax.

![](images/5ec408b26c57cccc2593e25651f846f27de380ace76c2fa7e13719fac4f50727.jpg)

The importance of such support, and the central role of tax in financing for development (FfD), was highlighted in 2025. The Sevilla Commitment of the Fourth International Conference on Financing for Development (Sevilla Commitment) emphasis on both the role of taxation in financing for development, and the commitment to double support for domestic revenue mobilisation showed a strong shared recognition of the centrality of tax in development.

In parallel, spending needs in developing countries remain high, underscoring the importance of continued efforts to strengthen domestic resource mobilisation (DRM). Since the pandemic, the global financing gap for achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) has widened to around USD 4 trillion a year (UN, Interagency Task Force, 2024). Estimates suggest that achieving progress on a subset of SDGs would require significant additional financing, particularly in low-income countries. Half of this financing need could be met by increasing the tax-to-Gross Domestic Product (GDP) ratio by 3 percentage points by 2030 (a target considered as “ambitious but not unrealistic”). Ultimately, achieving the SDGs without unsustainably high levels of external financing or public debt will require substantial further improvements in DRM.

The Platform for Collaboration on Tax (PCT) report on progress in supporting tax capacity development further underlined the need for increased and improved technical assistance $^{1}$ . The report, produced in response to a request from the G20, provided a stocktake on the role of tax in development and trends in support since the Third Financing for Development Conference in Addis Ababa. It also reaffirmed the key enablers of effective support on tax identified in the 2016 PCT report on the same topic $^{2}$ , tracked progress in these areas, and identified a future agenda focussing on the fundamentals of domestic tax systems, building on recent progress in international taxation, encouraging the use of Medium Term Revenue Strategies, and deepening the focus on collaboration and sustainability.

It is in this context, that the OECD, together with a wide range of partners across the globe, works with developing countries to provide a range of support. This offer has expanded over the years $^{3}$ enabling countries to tailor support to their needs across policy analysis and design, capacity building, tax administration and international collaboration.

Policy analysis and design is a core component of the OECD's support to developing countries, combining evidence-based analysis with practical policy advice to inform DRM and broader tax-related matters. Analytical work includes the production and expansion of global public datasets such as the Global Revenue Statistics Database $^{4}$ , which now covers 139 economies; datasets on carbon pricing and energy taxation developed through Effective Carbon Rates $^{5}$ now including 37 developing countries; the Investment Tax Incentives Database; and Corporate Tax Statistics $^{6}$ . This analytical work is complemented by an extensive programme of publications covering a wide range of DRM and tax policy topics. Over the last five years this has included health financing $^{7}$ , social protection $^{8}$ , tobacco taxation $^{9}$ , tax incentives $^{10}$ , taxing the informal sector and the use of presumptive regimes $^{11}$ , and tax morale $^{12}$ , among others. Building on this evidence, the OECD supports countries in the design, adaptation and implementation of tax policy frameworks aligned with international standards and national circumstances. This includes work on value added tax (VAT), notably for digital trade and

low-value imports, international taxation (transfer pricing, treaties, Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) minimum standards and the Global Minimum Tax (GMT)), and targeted areas such as tax incentives, environmental taxation, social policy-related taxes, and the extractive industries.

\- Capacity building is at the heart of the OECD's engagement with developing countries on taxation. The OECD has developed a range of capacity-building modalities, enabling countries to tailor support to their needs, through a combination of bilateral and multilateral approaches.

\- Multilateral training is undertaken through various programmes and initiatives. The Global Relations Programme (GRP) is the largest one, which provides multilateral training on international tax topics through both live and self-paced formats. The OECD Academy for Tax and Financial Crime Investigation (the Academy) has expanded to centres in Africa, Asia-Pacific, Europe and Latin America. In addition, recent years have seen an increased focus on regional training by the OECD, bringing together peers from across a region to address common challenges, and create peer networks. Examples include the Caribbean, Central Asia and West Africa.

\- Bilateral programmes supplement multilateral training, providing more intensive support where needed. These programmes cover tax transparency,

Recent years have also seen support expand to cover tax incentives, especially in relation to the GMT. Complementing these efforts, the OECD–United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) Tax Inspectors Without Borders (TIWB) initiative marked its 10th anniversary in 2025 $^{13}$ . Demand for TIWB assistance remains high, reflecting the programme's expanded scope, which now includes support related to the GMT, digitalisation of tax administrations, criminal tax investigations, effective use of Automatic Exchange of Information (AEOI), and implementation and use of Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting.

\- Digitalisation of tax administration remains a high priority, explicitly recognised in the Sevilla Commitment, which calls for enhanced support "to modernise revenue administration, especially digitalisation” $^{14}$ , and reaffirmed in the PCT report to the G20 on capacity building $^{15}$ . Recent efforts have included developing a Digital Transformation Maturity Model (DTMM) self-assessment tool (28 developing countries have already shared their results with the OECD), and inviting developing countries to join the Inventory of Tax Technology Initiatives (ITTI), with 54 participating by the end of 2025. In addition, the OECD continues to be part of the International Survey on Revenue Administration $^{16}$ which compiles a range of data on 164 tax administrations around the world.

International collaboration shows strong synergies between the OECD's tax standard-setting work and its offer to support DRM. Over the last 15 years, key reforms – including the introduction of AEOI, the publication of the package of measures to address

BEPS (the BEPS Package), and more recently the agreement on a GMT – have significantly reduced, and aim to further curb, tax evasion and tax avoidance affecting developing countries. These standards are open to all countries, and developing countries have played an increasingly active role in shaping them. Since the establishment of the Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes (Global Forum) in 2009 and the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework on BEPS (Inclusive Framework) in 2016, developing country members have participated directly in the development, negotiation, refinement and peer reviews of these standards. To support developing country engagement, a range of measures has been introduced to ensure that developing countries' perspectives are effectively

reflected in international tax discussions. As shown in Figure 1, participation in OECD tax instruments and forums has grown steadily since 2009. The influence of developing country priorities is evident in several key outcomes of recent negotiations. Examples include the simplifications to transfer pricing through Amount B, the priority given to domestic top-up taxes under the GMT, and the development of the Subject to Tax Rule (STTR) Multilateral Instrument, which helps preserve source-country taxing rights.

The OECD continues to support developing countries' effective participation in international collaboration to ensure their needs and perspectives inform the design of international tax standards. The OECD offer is demand-driven, flexible and fully respectful of the sovereignty of developing countries. There is no model or standard engagement with the OECD, nor do countries need to join the Inclusive Framework or Global Forum to be able to receive OECD support. Countries wishing to work with the OECD are able to tailor their engagement to their needs. This can include the whole lifecycle of tax reforms, targeted assistance on specific issues, training on key issues, or peer-to-peer support on live cases through TIWB.

## The OECD offer is delivered through multiple

partnerships. Alongside the TIWB initiative partnership with UNDP, the OECD takes part in the PCT, together with the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the United Nations (UN) and the World Bank Group (WBG) and works closely with a number of Regional Tax Organisations and Regional Development Banks. A wide range of developed and developing countries provide experts to help deliver OECD training and TIWB programmes. In addition, the work with developing countries is supported financially by generous donations from 15 countries and the European Union (EU).

The diverse range of support from the OECD produces a wide range of results, all contributing to improving the effectiveness of DRM in developing countries. Much of the OECD support aims to increase understanding of tax, and especially international standards and good practices. To that end the OECD provides significant training, with over 160 000 officials trained in the last five years. In addition, over 30 toolkits and tax policy reports have been produced in the same period, while OECD data sets on tax have also been expanded to include more developing countries, with an extra 26 developing countries included in the Global Revenue Statistics database in the last five years. A recent external evaluation highlighted the impact of OECD support with 90% of stakeholders agreeing that OECD support has enhanced the knowledge and skills of beneficiaries, and 85% agreeing that OECD support has improved the way participants carry out their work and has strengthened relevant processes and procedures in their countries $^{18}$ . Through more practical technical assistance the OECD supports countries in applying new standards and approaches: in the last five years, 39 countries have received support on VAT and 88 TIWB programmes have been launched; around 30 countries a year are supported on BEPS and Transfer Pricing issues, and over 65 a year on transparency and exchange of information. This support has led to numerous new and updated

pieces of legislation – over 65 in the area of BEPS and transfer pricing in the last five years alone – as well as developing country integration into the tools and standards for international tax collaboration (as shown in Figure 1). Ultimately, these impacts are reflected in the increased influence of developing countries on international tax standards, as well as in the additional revenues raised – TIWB programmes have realised over USD 2.72 billion in additional revenues, while developing countries have identified nearly EUR 48 billion in additional revenues through Exchange of Information on Request (EOIR) and the implementation of AEOI, including through related voluntary disclosure. Tracking the full impact of the support is challenging however, as a significant share of the impact relates to changes in taxpayer behaviour, which are more difficult to track and quantify accurately. To help improve the understanding of the results, the OECD has prepared a number of country case studies providing a more holistic overview of the support provided $^{19}$ . The OECD is also working with the PCT partners to improve the monitoring and evaluation of support on tax.

## BEPS IMPLEMENTATION

## A decade of the BEPS initiative

A decade after the BEPS Package was released in 2015, the Inclusive Framework took stock of progress in a Report to G20 Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors $^{3}$ . The report showed that:

\- Profits and substance are better aligned – the ratio of profits to employees for multinational enterprises (MNEs) in investment hubs has dropped by more than a quarter in recent years.

\- The sensitivity of the location of profits to tax rates has declined by up to 40% relative to pre-2015 estimates, indicating that business fundamentals are increasingly driving the location of MNEs profits.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT) rates have stabilised after a prolonged downward trend.

\- Transparency has reached unprecedented levels: jurisdictions accounting for 98% of outbound Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) have introduced Country-by-Country (CbC) Reporting, while jurisdictions representing the majority of global Gross Domestic Product (GDP) are exchanging information rulings.

\- Dispute resolution has also been reinforced through wider and deeper use of Mutual Agreement Procedures (MAP).

For developing countries, the report highlighted that the BEPS Project has been directly relevant to domestic resource mobilisation (DRM), and further potential remains. Anti-avoidance measures can reduce tax avoidance and support CIT revenues, but effectiveness depends on capacity, data access and enforcement. Accordingly, the Inclusive Framework will continue to monitor implementation and impact, simplify and streamline processes where this can be achieved without weakening standards, and support practical delivery – particularly by ensuring access to CbC reports, strengthening risk assessment and audit use, and

sustaining capacity building with development partners (including through the Platform for Collaboration on Tax (PCT)).

With the maturity of the BEPS Project, revisions to the Inclusive Framework peer reviews have been established, which provide a simpler and more pragmatic approach, especially for developing countries:

\- For Action 5, the review process now adopts a more pragmatic approach, with a BEPS impact assessment preceding legislative review and less frequent monitoring.

\- For Action 6, peer reviews now take place every five years, with the next review in 2028, supported by technical assistance.

\- For Action 13, the annual peer review has been simplified and allows developing countries to choose their implementation timetable with Secretariat support.

\- For Action 14, a simplified peer review has been introduced for developing countries without significant MAP experience who were previously deferred from the peer review; Stage One of the simplified process was completed for 42 developing countries.

Thirteen low- and middle-income Inclusive Framework members have indicated that they aim to be in a position to send and receive CbC reports within two years from September 2025.

## BOX 4. Putting capacity building on transfer pricing into practice – Zambia Revenue Authority v. Nestlé Zambia Trading Limited

On 20 August 2025, the Supreme Court of Zambia ruled in favour of Zambia Revenue Authority (ZRA) in its transfer pricing dispute with Nestlé Zambia Trading Limited (NZL), ordering NZL to pay an additional

![](images/a0666bccec3c41f45f1845d2bcf7c9cdbdad1cc4015b74f6e5b8ece4ffa270b2.jpg)

USD 600 000 in tax. This landmark decision underscores Zambia's commitment to implementing and enforcing transfer pricing rules, supported by OECD capacity-building initiatives since 2014.

Zambia has made significant progress in recent years, notably following its success in the Mopani case (May 2020) and its accession to the Global Forum in 2024. The NZL ruling further advances Zambia's transfer pricing framework by clarifying and reinforcing key international principles from OECD Guidelines for both taxpayers and ZRA. Specifically, the Supreme Court judgement established:

Burden of proof: once ZRA issues an assessment, the taxpayer must demonstrate compliance with the arm's length principle.

\- Documentation requirements: the case highlighted the need for robust transfer pricing documentation and confirmed ZRA's right to request any documentation supporting the pricing approach adopted.

Functional analysis and characterisation: by upholding NZL's classification as a Low-Risk Distributor, the Court emphasised a thorough analysis of functions performed, and risks assumed by the entity.

Transaction aggregation in transfer pricing methods: the ruling endorsed aggregation for interrelated transactions, consistent with OECD guidance, rather than applying a transaction-by-transaction approach that fails to reflect the integrated nature of certain series of transactions.

The case with NZL originated in 2016, when ZRA conducted a transfer pricing audit of NZL's related-party transactions to assess compliance with the arm's length principle. NZL had reported continuous losses since its incorporation in 2010. Following the audit, ZRA adjusted NZL's taxable profit by K56.5 million, resulting in a tax assessment of K13.8 million (approximately USD 565 000). NZL appealed to the Commissioner General and subsequently to the Tax Appeals Tribunal, which initially overturned ZRA's assessment in 2019. ZRA then appealed to the Supreme Court on five grounds, prevailing on four, successfully defending its audit approach and transfer pricing methodology and challenging a major multinational's structure.

The case illustrates the complexity and international dimension of transfer pricing disputes, with ZRA relying on foreign jurisprudence and OECD guidance in its arguments. A key recommendation advanced by ZRA following the case is the need for introduction of Amount B under the OECD framework.

Had Amount B been in place, the NZL case could have been resolved more swiftly and with less contention on major grounds for appeal. It would have reduced reliance on subjective benchmarking in the arm's length test by introducing a standardised pricing matrix and minimised disputes over comparables through the use of a global dataset. The Amount B framework would not necessarily have prevented the dispute altogether, but it would have significantly reduced complexity for qualifying transactions.

4. The Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes

Since 2009, the Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes (Global Forum) has been instrumental in facilitating effective international co-operation to tackle cross-border tax evasion. The Global Forum promotes the implementation of the international tax transparency standards: the Standard on Transparency and Exchange of Information on Request (EOIR) and the Standards on Automatic Exchange of Information (AEOI), i.e. the Common Reporting Standard for Automatic Exchange of Financial Account Information (CRS), and the Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF).

![](images/cec9f82944d92492d98cac700964061544336d7db47c0094a15c7d2595bef692.jpg)

Since 2011, capacity building has been a core duty of the Global Forum, supporting jurisdictions around the world in fighting tax evasion and other illicit financial flows (IFFs), promoting tax compliance, and mobilising domestic resources. The Secretariat of the Global Forum (Secretariat) accompanies the efforts to tackle existing challenges with a multi-pronged approach, which aims at building and disseminating knowledge and skills. With more than half of its 173 members being developing countries and with more advanced transparency standards being introduced, the Global Forum's capacity-building programme has continued to evolve in scale and depth.

The Secretariat's capacity-building programme continues to contribute to the significant results achieved globally, regionally and locally through the implementation and use of the tax transparency standards. In 2025, a record of 111 jurisdictions, including 88 developing countries, benefitted from bilateral assistance, reflecting growing experience among developing countries in successfully applying tax transparency standards, leading to substantial benefits:

\- Close to EUR 48 billion of additional revenues were identified by developing countries in the period 2009-2024 through EOIR and the implementation of the CRS, including related voluntary disclosure programmes (VDPs). This corresponds to about $36\%$ of the EUR 135 billion identified worldwide by Global Forum members.

\- In 2024 alone, they accounted for approximately $70\%$ of the nearly EUR 4 billion in additional revenue identified through offshore tax investigations and VDPs.

During this period, African, Asian and Latin American jurisdictions identified EUR 4.2 billion, EUR 24 billion and EUR 28.4 billion in additional revenue respectively.

More developing countries are actively using the tax transparency standards.

\- In 2024, they sent more than 4 600 requests for information to other jurisdictions to support cross-border tax investigations (increase of $43\%$ compared to 2023).

\- In the same year, developing countries participating in CRS exchanges received information from their partners on 53 million financial accounts, with a total value of EUR 2.7 trillion, - almost 2.5 times the number of financial accounts developing countries provided, and over 6 times the total value of their own exchanges.

Developing the knowledge and skills of officials, in particular tax auditors and exchange of information officers, is critical to ensure the effective use of exchange of information.

In 2025, the training programme contributed to build the capacities of 20 062 officials (+91% compared to 2024) through training events led by the Secretariat (9 421 officials; +64% compared to 2024), e-learning courses (5 634 officials; +117% compared to 2024), and the training led by the certified trainers of the Train-the-Trainer series (7 007 officials). In line with the objective of promoting gender balance, 54% of the participants in Global Forum trainings were women.

\- The Global Forum offers now ten e-learning courses. Over 18 400 officials were self-trained since the e-learning modules were developed.

Members positively rated the technical assistance (4.7/5) and training programmes (4.5/5).

## 5. Policy and statistics

The importance of strengthening domestic tax policy was underscored in the Platform for Collaboration on Tax (PCT) report to the G20.\* Governments are increasingly turning to tax policy not only to mobilise revenues, but also to support social protection systems, strengthen health financing, encourage business formalisation, and address broader structural challenges. Achieving these objectives requires tax policy that is firmly grounded in robust evidence, rigorous economic analysis, and reliable tax and revenue statistics that enable informed decision-making and effective monitoring of outcomes. In 2025, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) continued to support developing countries in this area by combining analytical work, sustained country engagement, and the development of high-quality statistical data to strengthen evidence-based tax policy and revenue administration. Support on value added tax (VAT) reforms to address the challenges of cross-border e-commerce remained in high demand.

Over the past several years, the OECD has expanded its engagement with developing countries on tax policy, including through their inclusion into the annual Tax Policy Reforms $^{1}$ report. ## Health and tobacco

The OECD Health Tax Revenue Database continued to expand and now covers approximately 200 jurisdictions, consolidating comparable data from the Global Revenue Statistics Database $^{2}$ , the World Health Organisation (WHO), European Union National Tax Lists and national sources. The database provides information on revenues from excise taxes on tobacco, alcohol, sugar-sweetened beverages and certain food products from 2000 to 2024, and its unique scope and comparability make it a frequently requested resource for both countries and international organisations.

Work on tobacco taxation $^{3}$ has contributed to policy changes on tobacco taxation in Mexico and Brazil. The OECD has provided technical assessments of reform proposals and prepared policy briefs for high-level officials involved in Finance Law drafting. In Latin America and the Caribbean, this support contributed to policy changes, notably Mexico's introduction of a tobacco tax and a sugar-sweetened beverages tax reform in 2025 and the design of the new tobacco tax in Brazil that will be presented to the Brazilian Congress later in 2026. Progress was also made in South-East Europe, where the OECD held online and in-person meetings with Ministries of Finance, Customs and Tax Administrations, Ministries of Health, and Public Health Institutes, and participated in regional tobacco taxation policy roundtables, including a high-level meeting on tobacco tax reform in Albania, on illicit tobacco trade

in Montenegro, and on tobacco taxation in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Countries welcomed OECD support given high smoking prevalence and limited analytical capacity.

## Social protection

The OECD continued its work on social protection financing, publishing reports on Senegal $^{4}$ and Thailand. $^{5}$ In Senegal, the launch attracted participation from 50 representatives across 26 institutions, including Members of Parliament, technical advisors to the Presidency, Prime Minister and Ministry of Finance, and the head of the Social Protection and Solidarity Directorate General. In Thailand, the report contributed to the recently passed pension reform and the planned increase in the social security ceiling, to be implemented through a tiered approach from 2026.

Additional reports for Cameroon and Paraguay have advanced, with Cameroon's findings presented in Yaoundé and strong interest expressed for continued support on domestic resource mobilisation (DRM). In particular, the OECD will carry out an analysis of personal income tax return data to inform a personal income tax reform in Cameroon. Work for Paraguay was presented in October, with the final report to be shared with stakeholders in 2026.

## Tax policy capacity building and country reviews

The Tax Policy Reforms report continued to be well received, accompanied in 2025 by a dedicated brochure focusing on developing countries, $^{6}$ published as a separate Policy Brief. Developing country participation in the OECD's Working Party No. 2 on Tax Policy and Statistics also increased, with Azerbaijan, Brazil, Morocco, and Nigeria presenting their recent reforms.

Country-level capacity building continued in Tunisia to establish a Tax Policy Analysis Unit in partnership with the European Commission, with training delivered on behavioural responses to tax reforms and environmental taxation.

Country Tax Policy Reviews advanced in Costa Rica and Peru, with Peru's review set to inform the next government programme in 2026. OECD Tax Policy Reviews provide tailored tax policy recommendations based on an in-depth analysis of countries' tax systems using reliable comparative data, tax modelling tools, country benchmarking, and international good practices. They identify tax policy reform recommendations for the short and longer run that enable countries to raise revenues in ways that are supportive to economic growth, inclusive, and sustainable.

New analytical work began on labour and capital personal income tax microdata in Thailand, supported by the Swedish International Development Co-operation Agency (SIDA), to study effective tax rates and assess reform scenarios, including analysis of tax expenditures and revenue buoyancy. Work with Ukraine continued through the Swiss State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) and the OECD-Ukraine Country Programme, including revisions of tax benchmarks for personal income tax, corporate income tax (CIT), VAT, and excise taxes.

The OECD also advanced its analysis of tax incentives in the Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) region and continued its work with Egypt, benchmarking tax incentives and modelling generosity using forward-looking effective tax rates. A report that analysed the tax system through an informal economy lens was shared with Egypt as part of the OECD Egypt country program. A training session on using OECD data, focusing particularly on revenue buoyancy, was delivered in Egypt.

## Tax morale and taxpayer education

A new project on tax morale in Asia was launched in 2025. Funded by Japan, this project will follow a similar approach to the LAC project launched in 2024, commencing with an analysis of public perceptions on tax in the region, and following this up with an analysis of tax administration perceptions, policies, and practices.

Public Trust in Tax 2025: Asia and Beyond $^{7}$ provided the largest public perception survey of attitudes on tax in Asia, covering 19 countries in Asia. Produced in partnership with the International Federation of Accountants (IFAC), the Association of Chartered Certified Accountants (ACCA) and Chartered Accountants Australia and New Zealand (CAANZ), it looked at attitudes on the fiscal contract, as well as how different actors are consulted and trusted on tax issues. In addition to the 19 Asian countries, there were a further ten comparator countries included from Europe and Latin America, as well as the Anglophone Pacific $^{8}$ .

Key findings suggest a stronger fiscal contract between citizens and state in much of Asia, especially South-East Asia, than in Western Europe or Latin America. In almost all countries people are more likely to have a positive than negative experience of the tax administration, while politicians are, on average, the least trusted source of information on tax. In addition, taxpayers in Asia are much more likely than those in Western Europe and Latin America to say the tax system is equitable.

The LAC tax morale project (funded by Spain) continued in 2025, with further analysis of tax administration perceptions that have been shared with countries in the region. The results have been shared with all countries that participated in the survey and will be presented in a report that will issue for consultation with countries in the region in 2026.

## Supporting tax and development reporting and analysis

The OECD Official Development Assistance (ODA) database $^{9}$ continues to be the primary source of data on development co-operation on tax. The data is widely used both within and beyond the OECD. In 2025, the data continued to be used by the Addis Tax Initiative (ATI) in its monitoring and reporting, as well as by the United Nations (UN) in the Inter-Agency Task Force (IATF) monitoring work. It also featured in the PCT report to the G20. With the inclusion of a commitment to double development co-operation on tax in the Sevilla Commitment of the Fourth International Conference on Financing for Development (Sevilla Commitment), this dataset is likely to receive further attention in the coming years.

ODA to Domestic Revenue Mobilisation rose by 27% in 2024 to USD 1.1 billion, with over 65% provided by the World Bank Group (WBG). ODA to Domestic Revenue Mobilisation from Development Assistance Committee (DAC) countries increased from USD 233 million to USD 297 million between 2023 and 2024. This was primarily due to an increase in loans which increased from USD 23 million to USD 83 million. Funding from multilaterals increased from USD 630 million to USD 800 million. This was primarily driven by an increase in grants from USD 89 million in 2023 to USD 213 million in 2024. As in previous years, the WBG continues to be the largest source of ODA to Domestic Revenue Mobilisation, providing USD 739 million (67.4% of all funding).

The Sevilla Commitment contained a new voluntary commitment on ‘reducing tax exemptions on government-to-government aid on a voluntary basis.’ $^{10}$ This commitment follows the previous commitment in the Addis Action Agenda to ‘review’ such exemptions.

The OECD hub on the tax treatment of official development assistance $^{11}$ remains the most comprehensive source of data on the tax treatment of ODA. The hub was updated in early 2025 with two additional DAC members joining the hub (Austria and Lithuania), bringing the total to 24 members. There remains potential for further work on this topic, which will be taken forward if resourcing allows in 2026.

## SUPPORT ON DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION OF VALUE ADDED TAXES (VAT)

The VAT Digital Toolkits $^{12}$ continued to guide major reforms of VAT regimes across developing economies, complemented by targeted capacity building. While the toolkits are fulfilling one of their primary purposes, to empower jurisdictions to develop an effective strategy for VAT on digital trade independently, there are still demands for technical assistance to complement the toolkits. In 2025, demand grew particularly among French-speaking African jurisdictions. Benin's successful implementation of VAT on digital services prompted follow-on requests from Togo, where the OECD held preliminary meetings to support early design choices, and from Burkina Faso, where the OECD and ATAF are jointly assisting ahead of a technical review of draft regulations.

Nine countries are currently being supported through comprehensive bilateral support. Box 10 provides some case studies. In addition, support is being provided regionally to the Pacific through the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) led Pacific One Stop Shop (POSS) initiative. Many more countries are also supported through bespoke targeted assistance, focused on specific aspects of a VAT reform or on addressing specific issues encountered by jurisdictions.

Countries continued to explore VAT reforms for low-value imported goods as a second phase following digital services reforms. Peru requested support after implementing digital VAT in 2024. Azerbaijan also sought assistance on designing a point-of-sale VAT system for low-value imported goods, while the OECD continued to work closely with the Thailand Revenue Department on secondary regulations for its forthcoming regime.

A core function of OECD support is facilitating structured dialogue between tax administrations and the international business community. This work aims to ensure that new VAT regimes are workable for non-resident suppliers while maintaining revenue integrity.

There is also growing interest from developing countries in other areas of the OECD's work on VAT, especially in digital continuous transactional reporting (DCTR) and the VAT treatment of the crypto-asset economy. The OECD will explore avenues to provide more support to developing countries in these areas in 2026 and beyond.

## PARTNERSHIPS

The OECD works in close partnership across its full range of tax and development activities, helping to reduce duplication, reinforce complementarities and maximise impact. These partnerships have been central to the delivery of the OECD's work to date, allowing it to draw on a broad and diverse network of expertise from governments, international organisations, regional bodies and donors. Many of the activities detailed in this report would have been impossible without the commitment and dedication of the numerous partners, including those who provide the funding. As global attention on tax and development continues to increase, and as the number of actors involved expands, effective co-ordination, collaboration and partnership will become more critical in the years ahead.

At the same time, there is a growing emphasis on strengthening the monitoring and evaluation of support on taxation to developing countries. Measuring impact in this area is inherently complex, given time lags and the many factors influencing revenue outcomes. In this context, the OECD can support efforts to strengthen monitoring and evaluation by building on evidence generated through its technical assistance, making use of its comparative datasets, and facilitating dialogue and learning across the tax and development co-operation communities.

In 2025, the OECD continued to support the work of the Platform for Collaboration on Tax (PCT), which facilitates enhanced partnerships between the PCT partners (International Monetary Fund (IMF), OECD, UN, World Bank Group (WBG)) across various dimensions -both bilateral and multilateral- as well as through activities carried out explicitly under the PCT banner. The OECD moderated the Technical Working Group of the PCT in 2025, and played an active role in advancing the PCTs work plan.

In response to a request from the G20, the PCT produced a report on Strengthening Frameworks for Building Tax Capacity. $^{4}$ This report was developed by the PCT partners and informed by consultations with a range of regional tax organisations and multilateral development banks. The report provided a stocktake on the role of tax in development and trends in support since the Third Financing for Development Conference in Addis Ababa.

The OECD also participates as an observer to the UN Committee of Experts on Tax Matters and attends all sessions where observers are permitted. The OECD also partners with the UN in various other ways, including on capacity building for tax treaty negotiations, some of which is done in collaboration with the other PCT partners.

Much of the OECD's training and capacity building is also delivered through partnerships. Practicing experts from a range of governments around the world deliver a large share of the OECD tax training, including through the GRP and the OECD Academy on Tax and Financial Crime Investigation. The programme on extractives is built on a comprehensive partnership with the Intergovernmental Forum on Mining, Minerals, Metals and Sustainable Development (IGF) which in early 2026 was strengthened through a new Memorandum of Understanding (MoU). In addition, there are also regional partnerships with ATAF in Africa, CIAT in Latin America and the ADB in Asia-Pacific. Capacity building is also delivered in partnership with the WBG in several countries. ## 7. Next steps for the year to come

Building on the work completed in 2025, priorities for 2026 and beyond include:

\- Advance policy and analytic support on tax incentives and the Global Minimum Tax (GMT) in light of the side-by-side package.

\- Continue to refine the approach to bilateral capacity building to ensure programmes are responding to demand and further explore the potential for capacity building for judiciary.

\- Develop a strategy to engage developing countries in the new workstreams of the Inclusive Framework on global mobility and tax, inequality and growth.

\- Continue to evolve the Tax Inspectors Without Borders (TIWB) initiative, together with the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP).

\- Deepen the partnership with the Inter-Governmental Forum on Mining, Minerals, Metals and Sustainable Development (IGF), including through a new Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) to provide comprehensive support on BEPS in Mining signed in early 2026.

\- Continue supporting and scaling up the effective implementation of international tax transparency standards especially Exchange of Information on Request (EOIR) (with an emphasis on advancing beneficial ownership transparency), the common reporting standard (CRS) and the crypto-asset reporting framework (CARF).

\- Strengthen the evidence base on the impact of tax transparency to help demonstrate the added value of the standards, encourage sustained political commitment, and inform future strategic orientations.

\- Continue to expand engagement with developing countries in the Task Force on Tax Crime and Other Financial Crimes (TFTC), including through engagement in the preparations for the third edition of the guidance document on the Ten Global Principles.

\- Sustain broad coverage of high-quality, comparable data from developing countries and increase its coverage and use for analysis across the globe.

\- Continue to provide country reviews and notes on diverse topics (tax policy, tax administration) and continue to conduct targeted outreach on tax policy with special focus on boosting tax buoyancy and discussions on tax and informality and social protection financing at the country level.

\- Continue to ensure that Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) training on taxation is widely available, with a focus on establishing the OECD Academy taxation learning pathway as a replacement to the Knowledge Sharing Platform (KSP).

\- Continue to prioritise building and maintaining partnerships with other organisations to maximise outreach, provide expertise complementarity, identify efficiencies and avoid work duplication.

Supporting developing countries in their efforts to strengthen tax systems is a long-standing priority for the OECD.

When plastic waste is unmanaged or improperly discarded – as an estimated 130 million tonnes is every year – it can become a major source of pollution.

As awareness of this challenge has grown, action has expanded at all levels. Significant efforts have been made to tackle the mounting challenge of plastic waste, from negotiations towards a global plastics treaty to a growing range of international, regional and national initiatives aimed at addressing plastic pollution and advancing circular economy solutions.

Examining plastic pollution and biodiversity loss as a shared economic, environmental and social challenge also reveals important opportunities. # Executive summary

By tackling plastic pollution and biodiversity loss as an interconnected challenge, we can conserve the critical ecosystems that underpin the viability of human societies and economies.

Rates of extinction among animal and plant species are now tens to hundreds of times higher than the historical average. Plastic pollution is among the top five drivers of this biodiversity loss. Since 1950, annual plastic production has surged from 2 million to nearly 500 million tonnes, yet only 10% of all plastic ever made has been recycled. Every year, 130 million tonnes of plastic waste pollutes the planet's air, land and water. Without urgent action, that figure could more than double by 2040 – equivalent to dumping a lorry-load of plastic on nature every second.

Plastic waste harms biodiversity in three ways. Through physical harm – entanglement and ingestion – it affects over 4,000 species, from zooplankton to whales. It degrades habitats, suffocating mangrove roots, elevating disease risk in coral reefs and contaminating nesting sites for sea turtles. And through chemical pollution, it disrupts reproductive systems and compromises soil fertility and crop yields.

The economic stakes are huge. One peer-reviewed study concluded that the 75-150 million tonnes of plastic in the world's oceans have led to losses of between \$500 billion and \$2.5 trillion in marine ecosystem services every year. Plastic pollution is eroding fisheries, coastal tourism, agricultural productivity and the carbon sequestration capacity of ecosystems such as mangroves, which store four times more carbon per hectare than tropical forests. Plastic's greenhouse gas emissions of 2.7 billion tonnes a year are on course to exceed every country's in the world, bar China and the US, by 2040.

## Mapping the geography of impact broadens stakeholder cooperation

This synthesis report draws on field assessments conducted across nine geographies on three continents – Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Indonesia, Nigeria, Peru, the Philippines, Viet Nam and the Indian state of Maharashtra – commissioned by the World Economic Forum's Global Plastic Action Partnership (GPAP), with support from the Government of Canada.

One important contribution from this research is a map-based approach to pinpoint where plastic pollution and biodiversity loss converge most acutely. Instead of a waste management approach, researchers for GPAP's nine reports developed national risk maps that overlay plastic pollution pathways with biodiversity data – identifying the hotspots where interventions will deliver the greatest returns.

This geographic lens yields powerful insights. In Colombia, plastic generated in highland cities travels 1,000 kilometres down the Magdalena River to damage coral reefs and mangroves on the Caribbean coast – a connection invisible to the separate agencies responsible for urban waste and coastal biodiversity. In Costa Rica, more than 35% of Key Biodiversity Areas display ecological damage related to plastic pollution.

Mapping plastic's journey unites stakeholders who may not normally work together: inland cities and coastal settlements, farmers and fishers, corporates and traditional leaders such as the guardians of Nigeria's Osun-Oshogbo sacred groves. It creates a “source-to-sea” logic for governance and action that cuts across administrative boundaries and focuses effort where it can do most good.

## Businesses face mounting risks – but also opportunities for engagement

Businesses face rapidly escalating risks from their exposure to plastic pollution and its impact on biodiversity. The Forum's Global Risks Report 2026 ranks biodiversity loss and ecosystem collapse as the second most severe global risk over the next decade. Corporate liability cases linked to plastic pollution could reach \$100 billion per year by 2030; mandatory extended producer responsibility (EPR) schemes could add another \$100 billion to that bill. Reputational risks from association with high-polluting supply chains are already tangible. Yet only 12% of companies have set targets to halt biodiversity loss – compared with 78% that have climate targets – and fewer than 1% disclose their biodiversity impacts.

The opportunities, however, are equally significant. Some 91% of plastic waste is currently burned, buried or thrown away – representing \$80-120 billion a year in packaging value lost after a single use. Upstream, companies that spend \$570 billion manufacturing single-use plastic could nearly halve plastic pollution by 2040, through shifting towards reusable and compostable packaging, creating 8.6 million jobs in the process. Downstream, better product design could raise the share of economically recyclable plastic from 22% to 54%.

Companies across the plastics value chain – from producers and packagers to consumer brands that rely on them – are encouraged to embed biodiversity into their core strategies, disclose plastic-related impacts, and include EPR and biodiversity in their risk registers.

## Communities, cities and governments all have a role to play

Communities are on the frontlines of this crisis – and often its most effective responders. Informal networks – such as the women waste pickers of Ambujwadi near Mumbai – collect and sort nearly two-thirds of all recycled plastic globally, yet receive no formal recognition, stable wages or legal protection. Indigenous Peoples and local communities have long served as guardians of biodiversity: in Raja Ampat, Indonesia, they restrict fishing to allow ecosystems to regenerate; in Colombia’s Amazon, they turned waste sorting into an income once they recognised the threat to the rivers and forests on which their livelihoods depend. Incentive programmes, such as cash-for-plastic in the Philippines, have proved more effective at changing behaviour than regulation or penalties.

Cities are critical actors. Urban plastic waste degrades the biodiversity that underpins cities' drinking water, food supply chains and flood protection. Innovative municipal solutions range from motorbike-and-sidecar waste collection in narrow Philippine streets to AI-powered plastic-flow monitoring in Viet Nam.

National governments face a more structural challenge: plastic waste management and biodiversity conservation are governed by different ministries using different metrics. Priorities include breaking policy silos, connecting EPR targets to

ecological outcomes rather than tonnes collected, and ensuring sufficient resources reach local level to back up enforcement.

Global governance is evolving but incomplete. The Kunming-Montréal Global Biodiversity Framework sets ambitious targets for reducing pollution – yet most national biodiversity action plans fail to identify plastic as a primary threat. Meanwhile, negotiations on a global plastics treaty have stalled.

## Introduction

The nine studies were commissioned by the Global Plastic Action Partnership (GPAP), the World Economic Forum's multi-stakeholder platform tackling plastic pollution, and supported by the Government of Canada (see Box 1).

This research contributes new insights into an important but still underexamined aspect of plastic waste. Conducted in Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Indonesia, Nigeria, Peru, the Philippines, Viet Nam and the Indian state of Maharashtra, this body of work represents the first detailed multi-country baseline of evidence for the biodiversity losses caused by plastic pollution and the consequent damage to ecosystem services and the economic sectors that depend on them.

While the country studies share a common framework, their methodologies, data availability and analytical depth vary across contexts, which may affect comparability. As the research was conducted within the GPAP network, the findings reflect the scope and priorities of these partnerships' geographic specificities. The insights presented should therefore be understood as indicative of emerging patterns and promising practices rather than definitive or universally generalizable conclusions.

This synthesis report captures the insights and best practices from these country studies and presents them in the context of the latest global research on plastic pollution's impact on biodiversity.

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The transport sector is one of the world's largest contributors to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Carbon pricing, including mechanisms like carbon taxes and emissions trading systems (ETS), is emerging as a powerful tool to incentivize emissions reductions, drive technological innovation, and promote sustainable practices. This study aims to evaluate global and regional trends in carbon pricing in the transport sector and to identify carbon pricing opportunities in the Central Asia Regional Economic Cooperation (CAREC) Program. The report assesses global trends and policies in carbon pricing mechanisms and their application in the transport sector, analyzes the status of CAREC countries in carbon pricing mechanisms, and develops a potential pipeline of projects for the CAREC region. These projects can create revenue streams for governments, which can then be reinvested into low-carbon mobility and improve the financial viability of low-carbon transport measures.

The contribution of transport emissions, excluding land-use change and forestry, to total GHG emissions in CAREC countries ranges from 5% for Mongolia to 23% for Georgia, with a median of 9% for all CAREC countries. Transport emissions in all CAREC countries are growing significantly faster than overall GHG emissions, showing the importance of introducing effective measures to curb these emissions.

The main measures identified by CAREC countries to reduce GHG transport emissions relate to electric mobility, low-carbon fuels, expansion and improvement of public transport services, nonmotorized transport, efficiency improvements in the transport sector, and shifting road to rail and road to shipping for both freight and passenger transport. Carbon pricing instruments for transport have been included by the People's Republic of China (PRC) in their subnational ETS. CAREC countries show that the transport sector can play a role in carbon markets.

Carbon pricing is an instrument that captures the external costs of GHG emissions, giving economic actors a pricing signal. Carbon pricing instruments are primarily carbon taxes, ETS, and carbon crediting mechanisms.

Carbon taxes have been imposed in numerous countries worldwide for over 3 decades, with pricing levels ranging from \$0.10 to \$150 per ton of carbon dioxide (tCO $_{2}$ ). Research has shown that carbon taxes have a larger impact on fuels used in the transport and housing sectors than in other economic sectors as the production of goods or services in these sectors cannot be reallocated across borders. Some countries allow for offsetting of carbon taxes with carbon credits. Within CAREC countries, no country has a carbon tax, but Azerbaijan, Mongolia, and Tajikistan are considering establishing one.

Most CAREC countries have a tax on transport fuels, which can be expressed as an implicit carbon tax (i.e., a tax embedded in fuel prices through an existing value-added, excise, or other tax, rather than a direct tax, which is related to the carbon dioxide $[CO_{2}]$ emissions of the fuel). The value in 2024 ranged from 4–100 per $tCO_{2}$ for diesel and 4–145 per $tCO_{2}$ for gasoline. Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, and Turkmenistan sell transport fuels (gasoline and diesel) significantly below costs.

Transport fuel taxes are ineffective in significantly influencing demand in the short term because fuel demand at any given point in time is inelastic. Hence fuel price increases, e.g., via a carbon tax, are a poor short-term tool for achieving GHG emissions reductions.

Significant revenue streams for governments can arise from a $CO_{2}$ tax on fuels. Such revenue streams can form part of the general budget and be used to reduce other taxes, they can be returned to the population or companies, or they can be earmarked for specific purposes, such as road construction or GHG mitigation projects. If carbon tax revenues finance GHG mitigation projects the impact is twofold: a reduction of fuel consumption due to the carbon tax and related fuel price increase, plus a reduction in GHG emissions due to investments in GHG mitigation projects. Based on prevailing prices for high-quality carbon projects in the transport sector, the indirect impact from investments of a carbon tax revenue on GHG mitigation projects is estimated to be 50–100 times larger than the direct impact resulting from reduced fuel consumption. This means that a low-carbon tax can be established while still achieving a high GHG emissions reduction impact if revenues are recycled to investments in GHG mitigation projects.

A low-carbon tax of \$0.01 per liter of gasoline and diesel sold, for instance (a “climate cent fund”), could be used to create a steady revenue stream for a fund, which then purchases domestic carbon reductions from the transport sector. A small increase in the fuel price directly will not result in significant emissions reductions due to the price inelasticity of fuel. The estimated emissions reductions are a consequence of using proceeds from the increase to pay for transport mitigation activities, e.g., through a carbon market system. Such a fund could achieve, on average, 75% of the estimated domestic unconditional nationally determined contribution (NDC) target for transport sectors in CAREC countries listed in their climate action plans under the Paris Agreement. Surplus reductions could be sold, together with corresponding adjustments to other countries, allowing for further growth of the fund and additional transport mitigation measures.

An ETS under a cap-and-trade system is managed by a governing jurisdiction that sets a limit (cap) on total emissions for obligated participants covered by the ETS within a given period. An ETS under a baseline-and-credit system sets emissions targets (which are also caps) for regulated entities, which can then earn tradable credits for emissions below their baseline. Allowances can be traded among market participants. ETS can be implemented on a regional, national, or subnational level. Numerous countries or subnational entities have already implemented ETS, with the price rate varying from \$1 to \$70 per tCO $_{2}$ . An ETS in general only includes certain sectors (typically the energy and industry sectors) and large emitters. Most ETS allow for quantitatively limited offsets with carbon credits from non-ETS entities. ETS for the transport sector are considered to have the largest potential in relation to establishing fuel efficiency, CO $_{2}$ standards, or targets for new vehicles registered in a given country, combined with tradable permits.

Within CAREC countries, the PRC has established multiple subnational ETS and a national ETS, which was structured based on the experiences in subnational pilot schemes. Some subnational ETS included the transport sector. Kazakhstan implemented an ETS in 2013 with regulated entities from the industry, energy, and mining sectors. Offsets generated by domestic projects can be used to meet compliance obligations within the ETS; however, the current price of around \$1/tCO $_{2}$ is not attractive enough to develop a transport offset project. Azerbaijan, Pakistan, and Tajikistan are considering establishing ETS.

A transport sector can be integrated into an ETS by (i) including large transport companies as regulated entities, (ii) including transport fuels in an ETS, or (iii) establishing an allowance system for $CO_{2}$ emissions of vehicles.

California's cap-and-trade system includes fuel distributors. They can invest in low-carbon fuels, e.g., by blending biofuels with fossil fuels, to reduce the number of allowances they need to acquire.

Multiple countries, including the PRC, have established CO $_{2}$ emissions targets or standards for vehicles combined with tradable zero-emission vehicle credits or with pooling of manufacturers. Emission pooling or trading significantly impacts electric vehicle (EV) sales by creating financial incentives for car manufacturers to increase EV production and sales. Carbon markets are systems wherein carbon credits issued for activities that reduce or remove GHG emissions are traded. Basically, there are (i) national compliance markets created through laws and regulations by regional or national governments or international compliance markets; and (ii) voluntary carbon markets on a regional, national, and international scale. In voluntary markets, participants have no formal obligations and set their own goals to meet voluntary commitments while in compliance markets the goals are established by governments or international treaties.

## Exploring the Impact and Opportunities of Carbon Pricing in the Transport Sector in CAREC Countries

The transport sector is a major and rapidly growing contributor to global greenhouse gas emissions, including in the Central Asia Regional Economic Cooperation (CAREC) region. This report explores how carbon pricing—through carbon taxes, emissions trading systems, and carbon crediting mechanisms—can help reduce emissions and support low-carbon transport transitions. Drawing on global experience and regional analysis, it assesses current policies and emerging practices in CAREC countries. By linking carbon pricing with transport interventions, the report highlights how CAREC countries can mobilize new financing, improve project viability, and accelerate progress toward sustainable and low-carbon mobility systems.

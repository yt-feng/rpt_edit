# Executive summary

Papua New Guinea embarked on the Tariff Reduction Programme (TRP) as part of a broader economic recovery and reform agenda that aimed to improve economic efficiency and integrate with global markets. By 2015, Papua New Guinea's tariffs were at historic lows, around 3.2 per cent on average, making it one of the most open economies in the region. After further tariff reductions, the government suspended the final stage of tariff cuts in 2018. It progressively raised tariffs on some products, citing the need to protect infant industries and boost revenue. As Papua New Guinea implements new national development frameworks, including the Papua New Guinea National Trade Policy 2017–2032, the Medium-Term Development Plan IV (2023–2027), and the Medium-Term Revenue Strategy (2023–2027), as well as other sector-specific plans in fisheries, forestry, agriculture, and manufacturing, there is a need for a structured and evidence-based assessment of the TRP.

This report provides a review of Papua New Guinea's TRP from 2010 to the present, examining its chronology and phases, the policy rationale for both liberalisation and reversal, and the impacts. To evaluate the outcomes and impacts of the TRP, the study relies on two complementary methodologies: an ex-post econometric analysis of trade flows, which assesses the results of the implementation of the TRP on specific economic variables, and an ex-ante partial equilibrium simulation, which evaluates the expected changes to occur as a result of tariff changes on trade flows, tariff revenue, sectoral impacts, consumer welfare, and other broader business impacts, holding all other variables constant. The analysis focuses on two periods for comparisons: TRP phase 2 (2012-2017) and the TRP suspension (2018-2023). To supplement the quantitative analysis of the TRP impact assessment, stakeholder consultations, in the form of a survey, were conducted with national government agencies, business associations, and companies to identify the perceived benefits, challenges, and recommendations for future measures to support Papua New Guinea firms in building competitiveness.

On tariff outcomes, the review highlights a policy shift from liberalization to selective protectionism. During the TRP, tariffs declined across all product categories, with consumer goods falling from 6.4 to 5.2 per cent, raw materials from 11.4 to 10.8 per cent, intermediates from 2 to 1.5 per cent, and capital goods near zero. This period also saw reduced dispersion of rates, leading to a simpler tariff schedule. However, after the TRP's suspension in 2018, tariffs moderately increased, with consumer goods rising to 5.3 per cent and raw materials to 11.3 per cent (both in simple average terms), reflecting renewed protection for targeted industries. By 2023, tariffs signalled support for infant industries, but overall dispersion was more rationalized than in 2010. This policy volatility created uncertainty: 61 per cent of surveyed firms reported that tariff revisions between 2018-2020 disrupted investment decisions. While the overall trend of moving from broad liberalization to selective protectionism aimed to foster domestic industries and reduce import dependence, it may come at the cost of higher consumer prices, complex trade-offs for firms, and weaker competitiveness if uncertainty persists.

On economic outcomes and sectoral impacts, the TRP coincided with a period of reduced import demand. Therefore, Papua New Guinea did not experience an import surge, linked to the lowering of tariffs. Instead, it benefited from strong export earnings coming from mining and petroleum. When Papua New Guinea reversed course and raised tariffs in 2018, the tariff hikes had a limited additional dampening effect on import levels, which had already been low and stable. Overall, Papua New Guinea's economy overall exhibited remarkable gross domestic product (GDP) growth averaging 6.3 per cent annually during the active TRP period, compared to the post-suspension period growth of two per cent. GDP per capita at Purchasing

Power Parity (PPP) showed a similar trend in growth of 4.4 per cent for the 2010-2017 period and 3.8 per cent for the 2018-2024 period. It is, however, important to consider multiple factors that contributed to the differential trends, including global commodity cycles and major resource project developments (for the TRP), as well as the COVID-19 outbreak (for the TRP suspension phase). The gravity model's findings support the conventional wisdom that tariff increases yield a reduction in imports: model runs on data during 2012-2023 showed that a 1 per cent increase in tariffs (ad valorem) is associated with roughly a 2.1 per cent decrease in import value on average. The partial equilibrium findings showed single-digit millions of dollars change in imports for major partners (e.g. a \$6.8 million drop in imports from China due to post-2018 tariff hikes), reinforcing the notion that other factors overshadowed the tariff policy in determining Papua New Guinea's trade volumes.

On tariff revenue, tariff policy plays an important but diminishing role in Papua New Guinea's fiscal revenue. The tax-to-GDP ratio declined to 15.8 per cent in 2023, even as tax revenue grew in absolute terms. Import duties rose from \$71.5 million in 2010 to \$112 million in 2023, $^{1}$ yet their share in total revenue fell as the Global Sales Tax and other taxes became more prominent. Consumer goods remain the largest source of tariff revenue because of high import volumes, especially for fuel, vehicles, alcohol, and tobacco. In contrast, raw materials and intermediates generate little revenue despite some high nominal rates, due to low import volumes. Simulations indicate tariff cuts during the TRP (2012–2017) reduced revenue by only about \$7.8 million annually, or just one per cent of tariff revenue, a negligible effect. After 2018, tariff hikes were expected to boost revenue, but collections were about \$15.6 million lower than they would have been under 2018 baseline tariffs.

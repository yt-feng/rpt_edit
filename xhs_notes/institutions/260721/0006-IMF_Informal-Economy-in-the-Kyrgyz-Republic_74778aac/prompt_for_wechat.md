## INFORMAL ECONOMY IN THE KYRGYZ REPUBLIC $^{1}$

## A. Introduction

1. Informality is very common in developing countries. According to World Bank research, in developing countries, 70 percent of workers and 30 percent of production are concentrated in the informal economy (The World Bank 2018). The literature indicates that informality results from a combination of factors. Although informality exists everywhere, it is more prevalent in low-income and developing countries and is mainly driven by suboptimal education systems, gaps in tax and social protection system design, deficiencies in labor market regulations, limited access to finance, and weak institutions and governance (IMF 2021).

2. Informality has a negative effect on economic development through several channels. Firms in the informal sector tend to be small, as they have limited access to credit, advanced technologies, and international markets. High levels of informality narrow the tax base, limiting government resources for investment in human capital and public infrastructure—both important for long-term economic development. In addition, high informality creates distortions in taxation, as formal firms and workers are excessively taxed.

3. People working in informal sector are more prone to poverty rather than those working in the formal sector. Empirical research suggests a significant correlation between informal employment and poverty or vulnerability (ILO, 2014). Most people working in the informal economy have low literacy levels, limited skills, and inadequate training opportunities. They typically lack state-provided protection and security—such as minimum wages, social protection, and job stability—which makes them highly vulnerable to poverty and economic shocks.

4. At the same time, informality in low-income and developing economies provides employment opportunities and income for individuals in contexts where formal jobs are scarce. Many studies suggest that policy should not focus on eliminating informality immediately, but rather on gradually making formality more attractive by improving institutions, reducing regulatory burdens, and expanding access to social protection (World Bank 2018). Blunt efforts to eliminate informality may instead lead to higher unemployment, increased poverty, and rising crime.

## B. Labor Market and the Main Drivers of Informality in Kyrgyz Republic

5. Informality in Kyrgyzstan is a complex phenomenon driven by multiple, interrelated factors. As in many developing and transition economies, low educational attainment is strongly associated with informal employment. Global evidence shows that around 45 percent of informal workers have only primary education or less, compared with about 7 percent of formal workers (OECD, 2024), a pattern that is also relevant for Kyrgyzstan given the concentration of low-skilled labor in informal activities, particularly in agriculture, trade, and services. In addition, the role of

structural and institutional factors—such as tax policy, labor market rigidities, weak institutional capacity, and regulatory and financial market constraints—in sustaining high levels of informality. These factors are particularly salient in the Kyrgyz context, where small firms and self-employed workers often face significant barriers to formalization.

6. The economy of the Kyrgyz Republic is characterized by a high level of informality and informal employment. The informal economy employs about 65 percent of the workforce and accounts for an estimated 19 percent of GDP. Informal activity is mainly concentrated in trade, construction, and services (Text Figure 1). It is associated with limited social protection, low wages, minimal job security, and an uneven tax burden between formal and informal businesses.

8. The weak quality of education in Kyrgyz Republic limits human capital development and contributes to high informality in the labor market. Although the Kyrgyz Republic spends one of the highest shares of GDP on education in the region (around 6 percent, Text Figure 4), outcomes remain weak (World Bank, 2021). ## Text Box 1: Tax Fragmentation and Compliance Costs in the Kyrgyz Republic

The Kyrgyz tax system is highly fragmented. In addition to the main national taxes—personal income tax, corporate income tax, VAT, excises, and the sales tax—there are numerous special tax regimes, including the patent regime, single tax regime, free economic zones, high-technology park regime, crypto-mining regime, electronic commerce regime, gambling activity regime, and trading zones regime (IMF, 2024).

Businesses may operate under the patent, simplified, or general tax regime, but complexity goes well beyond this choice. Within each regime, tax obligations vary depending on several factors, including the type of activity, whether transactions are cash or non-cash, whether the final consumer is a registered taxpayer or an anonymous end-user, and whether the business operates in a special tax zone. This layered structure increases compliance complexity and raises administrative costs for firms.

The parallel operation of simplified and general corporate income tax regimes creates particularly strong distortions. While simplified regimes are intended to ease compliance and promote formalization among small businesses, they are widely used in sectors such as trade that already have high levels of informality. This encourages firms to remain small, split operations, or underreport turnover to stay below eligibility thresholds. As a result, incentives to move into the general tax regime remain weak, constraining productivity growth, distorting competition, and limiting the tax base. Over time, this fragmentation undermines full formalization and reinforces persistent informality, despite the overall competitiveness of the tax system.

## Text Box 1: Tax Fragmentation and Compliance Costs in the Kyrgyz Republic (concluded).

In addition to fragmentation, the tax system is subject to frequent changes. The adoption of the new Tax Code in 2022 introduced important reforms, including expanded electronic reporting, a higher VAT registration threshold (from KGS 8 million to KGS 30 million), and a stronger legal framework to broaden the tax base. However, the transition also increased administrative burdens, as taxpayers had to adapt quickly to new rules, reporting formats, and accounting procedures. According to the World Bank's tax compliance cost survey, the average time legal entities spent on tax accounting rose from 44.2 working days in 2021 to 76.3 working days in 2023 (Box Figure 3). The 2023 World Bank Enterprise Survey shows that firms in the Kyrgyz Republic spend more than 280 hours per year preparing and paying taxes—more than twice the average in the Caucasus and Central Asia and Emerging Europe (Text Figure 11). About 25.8 percent of firms identify tax administration as a major constraint (Text Figure 12). High compliance costs, especially for small and medium-sized enterprises, weaken the perceived benefits of formality and reinforce incentives to remain outside the tax system.

A major driver of the increase in compliance time in 2023 was the introduction of electronic consignment notes to improve the traceability of goods. While this measure was intended to strengthen oversight, it substantially increased the time required to meet tax reporting obligations. The government has since streamlined the requirement, which now applies only to selected goods such as alcohol, tobacco, and petroleum products. Together with ongoing efforts to digitalize tax administration, compliance time is likely to decline relative to 2023, even though updated survey data are not yet available.

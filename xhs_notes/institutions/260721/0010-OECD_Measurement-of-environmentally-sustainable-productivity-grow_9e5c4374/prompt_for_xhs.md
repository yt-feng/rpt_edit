This report reviews six indices of environmentally sustainable productivity growth (SPG) that integrate agricultural total factor productivity (TFP) growth and trends in four agri-environmental externalities at the national level.

The findings reveal only minor differences across indices when environmental dimensions are assigned low weights, but these differences widen as weights increase. This variation reflects index-specific conditions under which SPG exceeds TFP. Some indices require externalities to grow more slowly than outputs or input (relative decoupling) while others require externalities to decrease (absolute decoupling).

Three case studies (Mexico, The Netherlands and New Zealand), illustrate potential drivers of SPG. In Mexico, improved market access resulting from trade agreements, alongside shifts in agricultural support appeared to be key factors. In the Netherlands, regulatory measures targeting ammonia, fertilisers and manure management contribute to positive SPG outcomes. In New Zealand, performance is associated with export-driven output growth, herd dynamics, and the adoption of new technologies.

## Key Messages

\- This report compares six indices $^{1}$ of environmentally sustainable productivity growth (SPG $^{2}$ ) that integrate agricultural total factor productivity (TFP) growth and trends in four agri-environmental externalities (emissions of greenhouse gases (CH $_{4}$ , N $_{2}$ O, CO $_{2}$ ), ammonia (NH $_{3}$ ) emissions and nitrogen and phosphorus run-off and leaching) at the national level.

• The results show that:

In the case of three of the reviewed indices (EATFPO, EATFPI, ESPII), SPG exceed traditional TFP growth when the selected environmental externalities grow more slowly than agricultural outputs or inputs (relative decoupling).

For the other three indices (ESPIO, WEPI, WEPO), SPG exceed TFP growth only if environmental externalities decline faster than increases in outputs (ESPIO) or in TFP (WEPI/WEPO) (absolute decoupling).

All SPG metrics provide useful information regarding the relative growth rates of and outputs and inputs and environmental externalities. However, positive SPG does not necessarily imply good environmental performance or declining environmental externalities.

\- Across all metrics, differences between SPG and TFP growth increase with the number of environmental dimensions and with the magnitude of their unit prices, reflecting the reduced relative weight of traditional TFP.

\- Case studies for Mexico, the Netherlands and New Zealand illustrate how SPG, which incorporates environmental externalities, compares to traditional TFP growth estimates.

○ Estimated SPG generally exceeds TFP growth for three indices (EATFPO, EATFPI or ESPII) in the three countries, as they experienced relative decoupling of environmental impacts. However, for the other three indices (ESPIO, WEPI, WEPO), SPG is lower than TFP growth for Mexico and New Zealand. This can be explained by the simultaneous increases in environmental externalities and in agricultural outputs and inputs (i.e. no absolute decoupling).

\- Factors explaining trends in agri-environmental performance are country-specific:

In Mexico, major shifts in agricultural support policies during the 1980s/90s, coupled with trade liberalisation and technology adoption in selected value chains, were key drivers of output growth. While externalities increased with this expansion, they did so at a slower pace.

In the Netherlands, declines in ammonia and nutrient inputs and balances during 1990-2010 largely explain the gap between SPG and TFP growth. Regulatory measures on manure, ammonia and fertiliser were instrumental in driving improved on-farm management and technology adoption.

In New Zealand, SPG dynamics reflect structural factors, shocks and technology adoption. In the 1990s, the rapid expansion in herd sizes was followed by a rapid increase in ammonia and greenhouse gases ( $CH_{4}$ , $N_{2}O$ and $CO_{2}$ ). From the mid-2010s, as herd sizes stabilised and with the adoption of improved technologies and practices, the economic value of environmental externalities per unit of input and output began to decline.

# Executive Summary

Achieving sustained output growth in an environmentally sustainable way is key to ensuring food security, while simultaneously providing livelihoods for farmers and improving the environmental sustainability of the sector. This requires producing more with less; achieving sustainable productivity growth is therefore essential for increasing output while reducing environmental pressures. Traditional productivity metrics, however, typically focus only on conventional outputs and inputs, leaving aside environmental externalities.

Several indices have been proposed in the literature to measure increases in agricultural productivity in ways that account for environmental dimensions. This report reviews alternative measures using six indices of environmentally sustainable productivity growth (SPG), by comparing their sensitivity to methodological choices. The six indices considered are: environmentally adjusted TFP indicators with input (EATFP-I) and output (EATFP-O) adjustments, the environmentally sustainable productivity index with input (ESPII) and output (ESPIO) adjustments, and the weak environment-productivity growth indicator where the weights of the externality are given by their importance relative to the value of inputs (WEPI) and outputs (WEPO).

Calculating an environmentally sustainable productivity index relies on several assumptions and choices, including the intended use of the index, the environmental dimensions included, and the perspective of the environmental valuation (producer vs. societal valuation). Each of these choices involves trade-offs between simplicity of estimation for improved interpretation versus more realistic representation with complex estimations. While many choices are technical, the perspective used for the valuation also depends on the intended use of the index and whether the index intends to capture sector-specific or broader social welfare impacts.

The analysis shows that SPG metrics differ in important ways. Some treat the environment as an output, others as an input, and they aggregate traditional and environmental outputs and inputs in different ways. As a result, growth rates derived from different indices vary, as do the conditions under which SPG rates exceed those of TFP.

\- Most indices rely on the concept of an environmentally adjusted output (or traditional output) growing faster than traditional input (or environmentally adjusted input).

\- Some indices (EATFPI, EATFPO, ESPII) require only relative decoupling between the output or inputs and externalities for their growth rates to exceed those of traditional TFP.

\- Others (WEPI, WEPO, ESPIO) require absolute decoupling (i.e. a decrease in the overall level of externalities at a certain speed).

These differences stem from the way in which different methods aggregate environmental aspects and outputs or inputs.

Positive SPG rates do not necessarily imply a good environmental performance of the agricultural sector or that the production methods can be maintained over the long-term without degrading natural ecosystems. Instead, SPG metrics provide a valuable measure of economic performance that integrates the environment. While this report does not assess the environmental performance of agricultural production systems, it highlights the need for complementary analyses with indicators that also track the levels of environmental externalities.

As an illustration, SPG metrics and TFP growth rates were estimated under several different methodological assumptions for Mexico, the Netherlands and New Zealand. These estimations were conducted from 1990 onwards, using agri-environmental indicators representing four environmental externalities (direct emissions of greenhouse gases $CH_{4}$ , $N_{2}O$ and $CO_{2}$ , ammonia emissions, and estimates of nitrogen leaching and phosphorus run-off). Two insights emerge:

\- First, when weights assigned to the environment are low relative to those assigned to traditional outputs/inputs, differences across the indices are small. This is intuitive as traditional TFP drives these results in these cases.

\- Second, as the weight assigned to the environmental externalities increases, differences between indices become more visible. In countries where relative (but not absolute) decoupling of externalities from output/input has occurred (e.g. Mexico and New Zealand), SPG rates are generally higher than TFP growth for 3 out of 6 indices. For the Netherlands, where the reduction of externalities has been faster than output and input growth since the 1990s, SPG rates are consistently higher than traditional TFP.

Further country-level insights can be drawn by disaggregating trends in SPG metrics.

\- Components explaining SPG differ across countries: whereas environmental performance (and ammonia emissions in particular) contributes the most towards SPG in the Netherlands, the growth in traditional outputs and inputs remain the key components explaining SPG in the cases of Mexico and New Zealand, with adjustments being relatively small.

\- Drivers of these components are country-specific: in Mexico, improved market access as a result of trade agreements, alongside shifts in agricultural support and output expansion are considered key factors. In the Netherlands, regulatory factors (especially targeting ammonia, fertilisers and manure) are considered key drivers of positive adjustments to SPG. In New Zealand, export-driven output expansion, herd dynamics, and technology or practice adoption are seen as important explanations for the observed performance over time.

Using a log decomposition of EATFPI can be decomposed as:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {E A T F P I _ {t}}{E A T F P I _ {t - 1}}\right) = \ln (\frac {Y _ {t}}{Y _ {t - 1}}) - \ln \left(\frac {E A I _ {t}}{E A I _ {t - 1}}\right)
$$

Where EAI is the value of environmentally adjusted input (not the index). The contribution of output is given simply by the (log) growth rate of output. For the environmentally adjusted input, it needs to be separated further into the contribution of inputs and externalities. The contribution of the externalities can be calculated in the following way:

$$
C o n t E x t e r n a l i t i e s _ {t, t - 1} = \left(\frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {p _ {e} E _ {t}}{p _ {x} X _ {t} + p _ {e} E _ {t}}\right) + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}{p _ {x} X _ {t - 1} + p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}\right)\right) * \ln \left(\frac {p _ {e} E _ {t}}{p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}\right)
$$

Which is essentially the average weight of the externalities as a share of the total value of inputs plus externalities times the growth rate in the value of externalities. Note that for the contribution of a single externality, the calculation is essentially the same. The only difference is that the growth rate of that externality is multiplied by the ratio of the monetary value of that externality as a share of the value of Inputs plus externalities.

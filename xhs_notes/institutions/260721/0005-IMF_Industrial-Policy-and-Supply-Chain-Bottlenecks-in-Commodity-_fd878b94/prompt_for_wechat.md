# Industrial Policy and Supply Chain Bottlenecks in Commodity Markets: Evidence from Rare Earth Elements

## 1 Introduction

Interdependencies created by decades of globalization and the explosive growth of cross-border supply chains have become increasingly weaponized. Over the last decade, countries have progressively resorted to tariffs, sanctions, export controls, and other economic tools to pursue geopolitical objectives, most recently in the form of Western restrictions on semiconductors and Chinese restrictions on critical minerals (Dürr and Heilmann, 2025). As a result, global supply chains — long shaped exclusively by cost optimization — now face potential disruptions and geoeconomic fragmentation. For policymakers in import-dependent countries, this raises questions about how to de-risk critical supply chains and which industrial policies are best suited to do so.

These questions are important in global markets for rare earth elements (REEs), which saw the introduction of export licensing requirements and industrial policy announcements in 2025. A chokepoint is more likely to emerge when an input is difficult to substitute and three further conditions align: (i) extreme geographic concentration in a single country, (ii) a potential for disruption and, (iii) barriers to rapid diversification. Rare earths satisfy all three. On April 4, 2025, shortly after the U.S. imposed higher import tariffs on most of its trading partners, China — the world’s top producer of rare earth elements across all segments of the value chain including mining and refining — introduced export licensing requirements for seven REEs and permanent magnets, citing national security concerns and international non-proliferation obligations (Ministry of Commerce of the People’s Republic of China, 2025). These export licensing requirements caused dramatic slowdowns in magnet exports to the rest of the world between April and June, but proved short-lived, with monthly Chinese export volumes quickly returning to their positive trend and displaying double-digit year-on-year growth rates. $^{1}$ In response, several countries have launched industrial policies aimed at de-risking REE production.

These supply chain disruptions and new industrial policies to build alternative supply chains all highlight the urgent need to better understand the economics of rare earths. To this end, this paper asks: how achievable are meaningful levels of REE supply chain de-risking under alternative industrial policy scenarios over the next decade, and at what fiscal cost? We find that while substantial relative to the size of the market, the fiscal costs associated with the required industrial policies would likely be manageable, particularly under simultaneous actions among various importing countries. $^{2}$

To analyze alternative industrial policies, we develop a calibrated, dynamic trade model of the global rare earths market featuring two stages of production: mining (upstream) and refining (downstream). In the first stage, miners extract raw REE compounds and sell them to refiners on a global market for unprocessed REEs. Refiners then separate these compounds into REE oxides, which are sold on a global market to manufacturers such as permanent magnet producers. In each country/block, a representative miner and a representative refiner invest in productive capacity to maximize profits, taking prices as given.

The model is calibrated to the global market for rare earths by partitioning the world into six countries/regions: the US, China and Myanmar (which, following industry convention, is referred to as China+), Europe, Japan, other Anglophone countries (Canada, Australia), and rest-of-world (RoW). This partitioning reflects each country or region's role in the REE supply chain — as a major producer (China+), a significant consumer (Japan, Europe), a source of untapped mining potential (Anglophone countries), or an economy actively pursuing supply chain de-risking through industrial policy (the US) — with some countries spanning multiple roles.

The model calibration draws on detailed market, industry, and geological data, with particular attention to mining and refining costs across countries. We follow a two-step procedure. First, baseline cost parameters are derived from a breakeven analysis of rare earth projects worldwide. For producers outside China, we evaluate 21 feasibility studies, computing rare earth breakeven prices (in \$/kg) after harmonizing key parameters such as discount rates. For China, breakeven costs are inferred from the 2006–2010 period, when China held a near-monopoly in REE production and the domestic industry was highly competitive, making observed prices a reasonable proxy for marginal costs. $^{3}$ Because cost data by production stage in China are not directly observed, the mining–refining decomposition is implemented transparently as a calibration assumption disciplined by project-level evidence from other producing countries and by the model's price and production targets. Second, taking the baseline cost parameters as given, slope parameters of the cost functions are calibrated to match each country's observed mining and refining production shares. Together, these two steps anchor the cost calibration in both forward-looking project-level estimates and historically revealed cost advantages.

Next, we ask the model to quantify the impact of a major supply disruption, which we proxy by simulating a hypothetical fragmentation of global REE markets into two blocks. We use the model to assess the effects of two industrial policies designed to mitigate the potential macroeconomic effect of the supply disruption: investment subsidies and stylized downstream revenue-support policies (henceforth also referred to as price floors) applied to refining/oxide separation (the most crucial processing stage). We study two implementation scenarios: (1) unilateral action by the United States, in which the policy instruments apply only to U.S.-based producers, and (2) simultaneously among all various net importing countries (U.S., EU, Japan, Australia, and other economies), in which instruments are applied uniformly across participating countries.

To illustrate the trade offs involved with each policy, instruments are calibrated to increase the share of domestic rare earth consumption that is not dependent on the largest import source by 15 percentage points in the U.S., from 10 to 25 percent. This measure of diversification can be interpreted as a metric of exposure to the dominant supplier. This is 15 percentage points higher than our baseline at unchanged policies and in line with IEA projections that imply—under announced projects outside China and stated government policies—that diversification levels outside of China would increase by 16 percentage points, from 8 to 24 percent, over the next decade (International Energy Agency, 2025).

The quantitative results from the industrial policy simulations are as follows. First, sizable interventions are needed to attain the 25 percent de-risking targets. For example, in unilateral (simultaneous) scenarios the investment subsidy must cover 74.4 (74.0) percent of total investment costs to reach the 25 percent de-risking benchmark by 2035. This rises to 88.7 (87.9) percent to achieve 50 percent de-risking. As for revenue-support instrument (price floor), the required policy intervention is also sizable: 2.18x (2.0x) of the period market price for the unilateral (simultaneous) scenario. These large policy interventions reflect the relative efficiency of Chinese producers but also point to important price effects. Because there are already large capacities in both mining and refining, boosting production in a well-supplied market depresses prices and profits, which reduces private investment incentives and, all else equal, requires more generous government interventions to induce additional capacity.

Second, investment subsidies are typically more fiscally efficient than the price floors when evaluated in present-value terms. This reflects the fact that investment subsidies are targeted at new capacity, whereas the revenue-support instrument also generates windfall gains for incumbent producers by supporting existing production. As a result, for a given de-risking target, the net present value of subsidy payments is lower than that of revenue support through price floors. To achieve the 25 percent target under the unilateral (simultaneous) scenario, U.S. fiscal costs associated with the investment subsidy over the first decade amount to 134 (90) percent of the U.S. REE market size—equivalent to about \$1.13 billion (\$0.76 billion). $^{4}$ At the same time, investment subsidies are more costly in the short term than price floors as they front-load fiscal outlays, with costs declining in the long term once investment is largely limited to replacing depreciated capital.

Third, simultaneous action among various importers reduces the fiscal cost of achieving a given de-risking target. For example, when the participating net-REE importing economies simultaneously incentivize investment in refining, individual countries' de-risking is achieved through a less concentrated buildup of capacity, as part of the fiscal cost is outsourced to participating countries. Under simultaneous action, those economies can also leverage the U.S. relatively higher efficiency in REEs processing and see substantial gains in de-risking, at comparatively lower fiscal costs.

Fourth, fiscal costs increase disproportionately with the degree of industrial policy intervention. For example, with higher CAPEX subsidies the U.S. can attain higher levels of de-risking levels in the downstream market, but the fiscal cost increases faster than the attained de-risking rate. This result suggests that very high levels of de-risking can be extremely costly, whereas moderate levels are quickly achievable and at moderate costs.

Contribution to the literature. Our paper contributes to the literature on geoeconomics, which studies how countries leverage economic interdependencies to pursue geopolitical objectives (Clayton et al., 2026). Within this literature, Clayton et al. (2024) develop a theory of economic coercion in which large powers exploit trade and financial dependencies, highlighting the risks of highly concentrated supply chains. Kooi (2025) complements this perspective by formalizing the national security externality — the idea that markets may undervalue resilience in strategic sectors — and quantifies the value of reshoring semiconductor production capacity to the US. On the policy side, Baquie et al. (2025) argue that the effectiveness of industrial policies depends critically on careful design, with poorly targeted interventions yielding limited gains. While these contributions provide useful conceptual frameworks, the literature rarely quantifies the costs and effectiveness of specific policy instruments within particular supply chains, with Kooi (2025) being a notable exception for semiconductors.

We contribute by developing a calibrated dynamic model of the global rare earth supply chain that incorporates investment decisions in both mining and refining capacity and allows the evaluation of alternative policy instruments, including investment subsidies and price floors. The model captures the mining-to-refining bottleneck that characterizes rare earth supply chains, distinguishing between upstream extraction and the technologically more complex refining stage where global supply is most concentrated. Our results provide a cost-based rationale for the thesis in Clayton et al. (2024) that for chokepoint inputs, modest diversification can substantially improve resilience: we find that fiscal costs rise exponentially as de-risking targets increase, so that a small amount of supply chain diversification delivers the largest gains per dollar spent.

A second strand of the literature examines how trade restrictions reshape critical mineral supply chains. Chen (2025) examines mineral export restrictions as a form of industrial policy and finds that such policies reduce upstream exports while sometimes encouraging downstream processing, although empirical evidence of broader industrial upgrading remains limited. Kee and Xie (2025) study Indonesia's nickel export ban and show that while the policy succeeded in attracting foreign investment and raising the domestic value added ratio of exports, it also led to the entry of less efficient firms, highlighting the unintended allocative costs of such interventions. Turning to rare earths specifically, Alfaro et al. (2025) study how the 2010 REE supply shortage induced directed technological change in downstream industries abroad, increasing efficiency in the use of REEs and reshaping trade patterns in REE-intensive sectors. Shifting to the importer perspective, Shao et al. (2026) shows that a substantial share of global REE consumption occurs indirectly through manufactured goods exported by China, implying that dependence on Chinese REEs is significantly larger than raw mineral trade statistics suggest. Our paper contributes to this literature by developing a quantitative framework that jointly models upstream extraction and downstream processing across countries, allowing us to evaluate how specific industrial policy interventions such as subsidies and price floors reshape global REE production and trade patterns.

A third strand of the literature assesses the criticality of REEs and the macroeconomic consequences of supply disruptions. Nassar et al. (2015, 2020) develop frameworks for assessing mineral supply risk, identifying REEs as among the most strategically exposed inputs due to their concentrated production and importance for high-technology industries. $^{5}$ Relatedly, Bogmans et al. (2026) quantify how a REE supply shock propagates through input–output networks, amplifying GDP losses across advanced economies. Our paper builds on these findings by developing a dynamic model of the global REE supply chain that can assess how investment and production decisions respond to policy interventions over time. Rather than tracing the macroeconomic effects through input–output networks as in Bogmans et al. (2026), we study the impact of a market bifurcation on the REE supply chain itself, disentangling the effects on upstream and downstream markets.

Section 3 presents a dynamic trade model of global markets for raw and refined REEs, and Section 4 describes the calibration strategy. Section 5 reports the quantitative results, including the effects of different industrial policies in various net-REE importing countries, and the trade-off between fiscal costs and de-risking targets. Section 6 concludes.

$$
q ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}} \equiv \frac {\sum_ {i} p _ {o , i} q _ {o , i}}{p _ {o} ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}}}\tag{21}
$$

This normalization converts a project's multi-element revenue stream into a single NdPr-equivalent production metric, effectively capturing the contribution of all reported rare earth elements into a common NdPr-based quantity. $^{38}$ The net present value of a representative project can be written as:

$$
\mathrm{NPV} (p) = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left(p q ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}} + \mathrm{Rev} ^ {\mathrm{nREE}} - \mathrm{OPEX}\right) \frac {1}{(1 + r) ^ {t}} - \mathrm{CAPEX}\tag{22}
$$

where OPEX denotes total cash operating costs, CAPEX measures initial capital expenditures, $Rev^{nREE}$ represents annual revenues from non-REE minerals (e.g., gallium), r is the discount rate, and T is the project life. Prices and quantities are assumed constant over time. Setting NPV = 0 and solving for p yields the incentive price:

$$
p ^ {*} = \frac {\mathrm{CAPEX} + \Omega \cdot (\mathrm{OPEX} - \mathrm{Rev} ^ {\mathrm{nREE}})}{\Omega \cdot q ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}}}\tag{23}
$$

where $\Omega\equiv\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{(1+r)^{t}}$ is the annuity factor. The incentive price $p^{*}$ thus defines the minimum NdPr oxide price at which projected discounted cash flows recover the initial capital outlay, making the project economically viable.

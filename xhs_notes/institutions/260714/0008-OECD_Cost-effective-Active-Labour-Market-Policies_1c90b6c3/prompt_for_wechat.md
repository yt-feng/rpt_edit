## Abstract

While the primary purpose of Active Labour Market Policies (ALMPs) is to improve labour market outcomes, these policies are increasingly expected to additionally address social barriers that hinder labour market integration of those furthest from work. All such effects need to be taken into account to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of ALMPs credibly and comprehensively, and thus guide investments into ALMPs in the context of increasing fiscal pressures across the OECD and the EU. This paper maps the integration of social outcomes goals into ALMPs across OECD and EU countries. It reveals significant gaps in countries' capacity to link data on social outcomes with data from unemployment registers, systematically monitor and conduct counterfactual impact evaluations (CIE) and comprehensive cost-benefit analyses (CBA) considering social outcomes. Strengthening data infrastructure and expanding CIE and CBA to include social outcomes are essential to better understand the full value of ALMPs and support evidence-based policymaking.

# Executive summary

While the primary purpose of Active Labour Market Policies (ALMPs) is to improve access to employment, these policies are increasingly expected to address additionally the wider social barriers preventing those furthest from the labour market from securing stable and good-quality jobs. As fiscal pressures tighten across the OECD, countries need comprehensive evidence on which programmes deliver the greatest value for money, not only in terms of employment and earnings, but also through broader social benefits. Yet monitoring and evaluation practices, when carried out, remain largely focused on labour market outcomes and have not kept pace with the broadening ambitions and potential role of ALMPs.

This paper presents a cross-country overview of ALMPs that pursue social objectives alongside employment goals, the data countries use to monitor and evaluate social outcomes of ALMPs, and the approaches underpinning comprehensive cost-benefit analysis (CBA). It draws on responses to a dedicated questionnaire completed by OECD and EU countries in the second half of 2025.

The analysis shows that social objectives are now widely embedded in ALMP design. The mapping exercise identified more than 200 ALMPs with explicit social objectives across 37 OECD and EU countries in 2025, targeting in particular the long-term unemployed, persons with disabilities and young people, but also migrants, low-skilled and older workers, and individuals facing health problems, addictions or care responsibilities. Strengthening jobseekers' sense of agency, including motivation, self-confidence and autonomy, emerges as the most common social objective, while mental and physical health are more often addressed through specialised interventions.

Despite the broad range of social objectives pursued by ALMPs, monitoring and evaluation remain heavily skewed towards employment outcomes. While more than half of OECD and EU countries routinely conduct counterfactual impact evaluations (CIEs) of employment outcomes, only around one in ten do so for social outcomes. Roughly half of countries routinely link administrative data on health, education or migration to unemployment registers, while less established outcomes, such as social participation, remain particularly difficult to capture. Where evidence does exist, ALMPs generally show positive effects on social skills and quality of life, although impacts vary across target groups and intervention types.

Accounting for these broader effects is essential when assessing the value for money of ALMPs, through comprehensive CBAs. Nevertheless, such CBAs remain relatively rare across responding countries, with only 20% of countries reporting having dedicated CBA frameworks for ALMPs, and fewer than one in five conducting CBAs regularly. Countries most frequently cite gaps in data coverage and quality, particularly regarding non-monetary outcomes (e.g., health) and the costs of delivering ALMPs, as well as methodological challenges, as the main barriers to conducting CBAs.

Four priorities stand out to address these gaps: (i) integrating social outcomes more systematically into monitoring and evaluation frameworks of ALMPs; (ii) strengthening data interoperability and inter-institutional co-operation to better leverage administrative data; (iii) developing dedicated methodological frameworks for the CBA of ALMPs; and (iv) improving the collection of ALMP cost data. These aspects are at the core of a new EC-OECD project on counterfactual impact evaluation of ALMPs, which aims to broaden the traditional scope of evaluation, promote the use of CBA to assess cost-effectiveness, and support countries in strengthening data collection, data linkage, evaluation frameworks and evidence-informed policymaking.

## 1 Introduction

Active Labour Market Policies (ALMPs) aim to enhance matching of jobseekers with vacancies and improve employment opportunities for people facing difficulties entering or remaining in the labour market. They focus primarily on achieving employment-related outcomes, such as job placement, skills development and labour market integration. While these objectives remain central, ALMPs are increasingly expected not only to improve employment prospects, but also to address a wider range of social challenges faced by jobseekers, inactive people and people at risk of job loss. To reliably guide investments into ALMPs, all these different effects need to be accounted for in the evaluations of effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of ALMPs. The need to strengthen such comprehensive evaluations is increasingly important across OECD and EU countries in the context of tightened fiscal resources.

The need to address social outcomes through ALMPs is reinforced by broader structural trends. Demographic changes and persistent labour and skills shortages have increased the urgency of supporting the labour market integration of underrepresented groups and mobilise their untapped potential (OECD, 2025[1]; European Commission, 2025[2]). For people facing multiple barriers and disadvantages, employment outcomes are often not sufficient for assessing their short-term progress as it may take longer for them to find a job and retain it. Moreover, for these groups, participation in ALMPs can serve as a stepping stone to further participation in other, perhaps more intensive, programmes (OECD/Department of Social Protection, Ireland/EC-JRC, 2024[3]). Therefore, recognising interim social outcomes, such as improved self-confidence and autonomy, social participation and social skills, health and well-being, is essential for supporting groups further from the labour market. Strengthening these outcomes and recognising progress along these social dimensions can increase participants' engagement in ALMPs, reduce dropout rates and support transitions into work that are more stable and long-lasting.

Beyond traditional employment outcomes, ALMPs often generate additional social benefits (OECD, 2021[4]). These benefits include improvements in individual outcomes, such as self-confidence, social participation, health and well-being, as well as wider societal impacts, including reductions in crime rates, higher educational attainment for children, and lower early school dropout rates. To comprehensively understand and evaluate ALMPs, it is therefore crucial to consider not only employment-related impacts but also their broader effects on social outcomes.

Beyond the need to address labour shortages and boost employment in a cost-effective manner, social outcomes matter in their own right. There is a growing recognition that long-term unemployment and inactivity can generate a range of social challenges that, if unaddressed, can undermine individual health and well-being. Prolonged detachment from the labour market is associated with loss of motivation and confidence, social isolation, deteriorating mental and physical health, and increased vulnerability to poverty, stigma or other forms of social exclusion.

# ABSTRACT/RÉSUMÉ

## Non-compete clauses and productivity: New cross-country firm-level evidence

Restraint clauses that prevent workers from joining or starting a competing firm – commonly known as non-compete clauses – are traditionally justified to protect legitimate business interests. Yet, such clauses may suppress job mobility, business dynamism and competition, with adverse consequences for productivity growth. This paper presents the first harmonised cross-country evidence on the link between non-compete clauses and productivity performance. Merging novel data on industry-country-level prevalence of non-compete clauses with firm-level data from Orbis, we find that a higher non-compete prevalence is associated with weaker productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and slower knowledge diffusion. The estimates translate into meaningful aggregate productivity losses (1.9% for a 10-percentage-point increase in non-compete incidence). While the economic “bite” of non-competes on productivity seems larger in countries where firms have greater freedom to deploy them, it remains negative in countries where non-competes are more tightly regulated. This underscores the idea that non-compete clauses can exert “chilling effects” even when they are unlikely to be upheld by courts, raising questions about whether current regulatory frameworks are fit for purpose.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past 20 years, the productivity slowdown has been underpinned at the microeconomic level by lower rates of job reallocation, firm entry and knowledge diffusion (OECD, 2025[1]; Shambaugh, Nunn and Liu, 2018[2]; OECD, 2025[3]). One factor that could partly explain these headwinds is the spread of post-employment restraint clauses, which prevent workers from joining (or starting) a competing firm (non-compete clauses) and disclosing confidential information; or firm-to-firm no-poaching agreements (Andrews and Garnero, 2025[4]). While a growing body of evidence highlights the negative effects that non-compete clauses can have on wages and job mobility, it is unclear whether these effects simply reflect a redistribution of value from workers to firms, or whether non-compete clauses also depress productivity and thus shrink the size of the overall economic pie.

To fill this gap, this paper presents new harmonised cross-country evidence on the link between non-compete and related clauses and productivity performance. To this purpose, novel data on the share of workers bound by non-compete and related clauses at the industry level – sourced from a recent OECD-Bocconi survey of firms in 15 countries – is merged with cross-country firm-level balance sheet data from Orbis. Consistent with existing evidence showing that non-compete clauses can stifle economic dynamism, we first present descriptive evidence from new OECD surveys which shows that: i) non-compete clause prevalence is surprisingly high (OECD, 2026[5]); and ii) a meaningful share of all employees have been stopped from moving jobs or starting a new business due to having a non-compete clause. In turn, we explore the potential for non-compete and related clauses to distort two key structural features of well-functioning economies: productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and knowledge diffusion. While causality is difficult to pin down, we find clear evidence of a robust negative relationship between the prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level and the aforementioned structural drivers of aggregate productivity growth.

We augment the state-of-the-art dynamic reallocation methodology (Foster, Grim and Haltiwanger, 2016[6]; Decker et al., 2020[7]) and find that a higher prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level is associated with a lower propensity for labour to flow to more productive firms, at the expense of less productive firms. The baseline results are robust to a battery of robustness tests, including controlling for labour market tightness, workforce composition, product and labour market regulations and the use of other restraint agreements (see below). On average across countries, a 10-percentage-point increase in the share of the workforce bound by non-compete clauses is associated with an estimated $17\%$ reduction in the strength of productivity-enhancing labour reallocation. While the economic "bite" of non-competes on labour reallocation is somewhat larger in countries where firms have greater freedom to deploy such clauses $(-20\%)$ , it remains negative in countries where regulatory frameworks place more restrictions on firms' use of non-competes $(-14\%)$ .

Drawing on neo-Schumpeterian growth theory – whereby aggregate productivity growth is influenced by knowledge transfer from frontier to laggard firms (Aghion and Howitt, 1998[8]) – we then show that a higher prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level is associated with weaker technological convergence of laggard firms to the global productivity frontier. A 10-percentage-point increase in non-compete prevalence is linked to about a 5% reduction in catch-up growth, implying a 1.8-percentage-point decline in annual firm-level labour productivity growth for the median non-frontier firm. While the estimated economic “bite” of non-competes on knowledge diffusion is also larger in countries where firms have greater freedom to deploy such clauses, these results are less robust to the inclusion of additional institutional controls than the reallocation results.

Taken together, these results suggest that non-compete clauses – by directly impeding the ability of workers to join (or start) a competing firm – stifle the reallocation of talent to productive firms and the spread of leading ideas. To understand what they imply for aggregate productivity performance, we conduct a simulation exercise utilising a well-established aggregate productivity decomposition that distinguishes between within-firm productivity growth and the productivity gains arising from labour reallocation across continuing firms (Foster, Haltiwanger and Krizan, 2001[9]). The results suggest that a 10-percentage-point increase in the prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level is associated with a decline in the level of aggregate (business sector) labour productivity of 1.9%, with smaller impacts (1.5%) in countries where such clauses are more difficult to deploy. While this figure should not be taken too literally given that we have not identified a purely causal relationship, it is nonetheless illustrative of the potential for non-compete clauses to have material consequences for aggregate productivity. Moreover, these figures do not account for other mechanisms through which non-compete clauses may depress aggregate productivity, such as reduced firm entry and competition.

These findings have important policy implications for at least three key reasons.

1. The spread of non-compete clauses could have played a role in the productivity slowdown and the accompanying decline in economic dynamism by amplifying frictions that restrict the mobility of productive resources as well as competition in factor and product markets. The productivity effects associated with a relatively modest change in non-compete prevalence are – as expected – smaller than the productivity impacts of larger-scale reforms, but still material. This suggests that recent regulatory interventions to restrict the use of non-compete clauses in selected OECD countries are a potential productivity-enhancing structural reform. The evidence that non-compete clauses may reduce productivity even in countries where they are more tightly regulated supports previous findings in the literature that such clauses can have a “chilling effect” on job mobility, as workers often perceive non-compete clauses as valid and act accordingly, irrespective of the likelihood that courts would uphold them. Policy approaches that focus only on the formal regulatory framework of non-compete clauses are therefore unlikely to fully address their economic effects.

Previous literature on the economic effects of non-compete clauses has mainly documented their impact on job mobility and wages.

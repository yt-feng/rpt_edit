## Executive Summary

Lesotho has sustained a high level of public investment over many years, but this has not translated into durable infrastructure or strong growth outcomes. Despite capital spending averaging around 10 percent of GDP, well above regional and low-income country peers, economic growth has remained weak and volatile, and GDP per capita remains among the lowest in the region. At the same time, the public capital stock has steadily eroded, declining sharply since the early 2000s, even as infrastructure gaps remain large. The central challenge facing Lesotho is therefore not the volume of public investment, but the effectiveness with which investment spending is planned, allocated, and implemented.

High public investment in Lesotho has been supported by periods of favorable financing conditions rather than by strong public investment institutions. Large and volatile Southern African Customs Union (SACU) transfers, together with capital grants - most notably for the Lesotho Highlands Water Project - have provided substantial fiscal space for investment without requiring sustained domestic revenue mobilization or extensive debt financing. While this has enabled high levels of capital spending, it has also weakened incentives to strengthen project preparation, budgeting discipline, and implementation capacity. As a result, increases in public investment have not been accompanied by commensurate improvements in infrastructure quality or durability.

Infrastructure outcomes in Lesotho remain weak relative to the scale of investment, reflecting low efficiency and persistent implementation bottlenecks. Access to electricity and basic services has improved only gradually, and service quality remains uneven, despite sustained capital spending. Lesotho's public investment efficiency gap is estimated at around 49 percent (2019), indicating that the country derives substantially less infrastructure output from its capital stock than more efficient peers. These weak outcomes are closely linked to low budget execution: on average, only about half of budgeted capital spending has been executed in recent years. Persistent under-execution has delayed project completion, weakened service delivery, and contributed to the erosion of the public capital stock, making execution failures the most immediate and binding constraint on the impact of public investment.

The assessment follows the standard PIMA approach, which evaluates 15 key public investment management “institutions” across the three stages of the investment cycle (planning, allocation, and implementation). Each institution is scored on both institutional design (the existence of rules, procedures, and organizational arrangements) and effectiveness (how well these arrangements work in practice), and the results are summarized to highlight the main strengths, weaknesses, and reform priorities (see Figure 1 and Annex 2).

These weak outcomes reflect broad-based shortcomings across the public investment management framework, with both institutional arrangements and their implementation remaining weak. The design of laws is stronger than its effective implementation, but the core issue encompasses both. While elements of the formal architecture for planning, budgeting, and implementing public investment are in place, they are incomplete, unevenly applied, and constrained by limited capacity across ministries. As a result, projects often enter the budget without being fully prepared, prioritized, or costed; funding is not predictable or sufficiently protected during implementation; and there is limited active management of the investment portfolio once projects are underway. Rather than a narrow gap between rules and practice,

Lesotho faces a system-wide weakness across the investment cycle, which helps explain persistently low execution and the limited translation of high investment spending into completed, durable infrastructure.

Compared with Sub-Saharan African and low-income developing country peers, Lesotho's public investment management framework underperforms somewhat both in design and in practice. While coordination mechanisms and elements of budget coverage are comparable to regional norms, effectiveness is weaker in national and sectoral planning, multi-year budgeting, project selection, availability of funding, portfolio management, and project implementation. These gaps are most pronounced in the downstream stages of the investment cycle, where weaknesses in execution, oversight, and active portfolio management limit the impact of public investment. As a result, despite investment levels that exceed those of many peers, Lesotho derives less infrastructure output from its spending than countries with stronger implementation capacity.

There are three key interconnected findings coming out of this report:

\- First, low budget execution is the most immediate and binding constraint on the impact of public investment: on average, only about 53 percent of budgeted capital spending has been executed over the past three fiscal years, despite reallocations and available fiscal space.

Second, weaknesses are evident across the investment cycle rather than concentrated in a single stage. In particular, projects often enter the budget without being fully appraised or costed; medium-term capital ceilings differ markedly from final budget allocations; and the release of funds to ongoing projects is not timely or predictable, with payments subject to cash rationing, delays, and the accumulation of arrears, resulting in stop-start implementation and repeated delays.

Third, limited institutional capacity and weak enforcement of existing processes constrain the system's ability to correct these problems in-year, including through effective portfolio monitoring and timely reallocation.

The proposed reform agenda focuses on strengthening execution and funding predictability through reforms to project preparation and budgeting to ensure that public investment translates more reliably into completed, durable infrastructure. The recommendations therefore focus on improving infrastructure outcomes by (i) strengthening the planning framework and project pipeline, (ii) establishing a firmer basis for appraisal and transparent project selection, (iii) improving multiyear capital budgeting and disclosure of total project costs to better fund ongoing projects, and (iv) tackling the most immediate execution constraints through operational procurement, improved cash and commitment controls, and stronger monitoring and performance-based reallocations—underpinned by enhanced audit transparency and better asset information.

Addressing the key issue of under execution means raising Lesotho's absorption capacity. In practice, this requires a sequenced focus on the binding constraints that most directly prevent budgeted funds from being converted into completed projects. Near-term priorities should therefore center on getting procurement fully operational and improving funding predictability for ongoing projects. Next, strengthening appraisal and selection and improving planning will raise “quality at entry” and reduce the risk of future stoppages. Finally, stronger portfolio monitoring, clearer performance-based reallocation procedures, and enhanced transparency and asset information will help identify blockages early, enable timely course-correction, and shift resources toward projects that can be implemented.

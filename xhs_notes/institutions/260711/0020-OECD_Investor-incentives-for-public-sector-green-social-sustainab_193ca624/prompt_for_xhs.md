## Abstract

This report aims to contribute to an improved understanding of how investors view public sector green, social, sustainability and sustainability-linked (GSSS) bonds, and to strengthen development actors' approach to supporting issuances in developing countries. It draws from the insights shared by respondents to the 'OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds' and subsequent consultations with investors and other market players. It explores the factors influencing investor interest and risk appetite for GSSS bonds, and expectations on future market developments. Considering these findings, it provides policy considerations and concrete action plans to help donors, issuing governments in developing countries and other market players increase the attractiveness of public sector GSSS bond issuances and investments in developing countries for investors. Building on those insights, the paper points to several options for future research.

# Executive summary

The global bond market will be critical to meeting unprecedented investment needs. Globally, the fixed income market reached USD 145.1 trillion in 2024 compared to the USD 126.7 trillion equity market, and borrowing from sovereigns and corporates continues to rise (SIFMA Research, 2025 $^{[1]}$ ; OECD, 2025 $^{[2]}$ ). As part of this asset class, green, social, sustainability and sustainability-linked (GSSS) bonds are powerful instruments to drive financing – especially from the private sector – towards sustainable development, by linking scale, sustainable impact and long-term duration. GSSS bonds can therefore be a particularly important part of the solution to the alarming annual USD 4 trillion Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) financing gap (OECD, 2025 $^{[4]}$ ). For developing countries, GSSS bonds also hold advantages – including diversifying the investor base, signalling commitments to sustainability, and building a track record for further issuances.

Yet GSSS bond issuances in developing countries remain rare. They are a small share of total bond issuances: in 2024, GSSS bonds accounted for only 5% of annual emerging market issuance (IFC-Amundi, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ). In 2025, only 17% of the overall green, social and sustainability bond market was issued in developing countries (dropping to 5% when not including the People's Republic of China). This trend is also true in the sustainability-linked bond market: in 2025, 24% of the total issued amount came from issuers in ODA-eligible countries, dropping to 17% when excluding the People's Republic of China). While recognising that debt instruments are not appropriate for all types of entities, these figures indicate that there is room for GSSS bond issuances to grow significantly across developing countries.

Effectively scaling GSSS bond issuances in developing countries therefore requires more issuances coming from developing countries. Previous OECD reports in the GSSS bond series have focused on how donors of the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) can enable GSSS bond issuances in developing countries (Dembele, Schwarz and Horrocks, 2021 $^{[7]}$ ; OECD, 2022 $^{[8]}$ ; OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). However, effectively scaling issuances also rests on greater investor demand towards these regions and instruments. This paper therefore aims to cast light on how investors view public sector GSSS bonds, especially from developing countries. It draws from the insights shared as part of the ‘OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds’ as well as from follow-up consultations with investors and other market players. The focus is on factors influencing investor interest and risk appetite for GSSS bonds, and expectations on future market developments. While recognising that the uncertain – and changing – geopolitical and macroeconomic environment influences investors’ perception of risk and of GSSS bonds, their insights remain crucially important, including for ensuring that actions by providers of development finance concretely respond to their needs and focus on practical solutions.

Based on the above, the paper proposes the following policy considerations on how to strengthen donors' approaches to GSSS bonds in developing countries:

\- Support the development of harmonised and interoperable locally-adapted standards, principles and taxonomies.

\- Provide technical assistance (TA) for the implementation of budgetary reporting standards to identify eligible expenditures.

\- Prioritise developing sustainability-focused bond mandates for domestic funds.

\- Deliver technical assistance for post-issuance disclosure and impact measurement.

\- Consider the potentially unexpected implications of blended finance approaches on GSSS bond issuances.

Recognising the importance of other stakeholders in creating a strong GSSS bond market, the paper also presents the following considerations:

\- Public sector issuers in developing countries need to prioritise liquidity buffers and flexibility in the issuance strategy.

\- Other market players need to increase the inclusion of developing country GSSS bond issuances in benchmark indices.

## The OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds: aims and scopes

The annual financing gap for achieving the 2030 sustainable development goals (SDGs) now stands at an alarming USD 4 trillion (OECD, 2025 $^{[4]}$ ). The scale and urgency of the challenge is clear – and will require an increase in all sources of finance. The global bond market is a powerful way to mobilise capital, including from the private sector, towards sustainable development and resilience, due to its size (in 2024, the outstanding global fixed income market stood at USD 145.1 trillion compared to the USD 126.7 trillion global equity market capitalisation) (SIFMA Research, 2025 $^{[1]}$ ) and long-term focus of its investors. At the same time, despite the complex macroeconomic and geopolitical context, borrowing levels continue to reach historic highs: in 2026, governments and corporations are expected to borrow USD 29 trillion from markets. This represents a 17% increase compared to 2024 (OECD, 2026 $^{[3]}$ ). This all increases the relevance of the bond market – for issuers, investors and policymakers alike. Within this, green, social, sustainability and sustainability-linked (GSSS) bonds are particularly relevant: they link scale with impact, and have received strong interest from issuers and investors alike (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). In recent years, the market has shown resilience and robustness (Luxembourg Green Exchange, 2025 $^{[11]}$ ; BNP Paribas Asset Management, 2026 $^{[12]}$ ). This paper builds on the insights shared as part of the 'OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds' (hereafter "OECD-LuxSE survey") to contribute to an improved understanding of how investors view public sector GSSS bonds, especially from developing countries.

The GSSS bond group can be further divided into two sub-categories – green, social and sustainability (GSS) bonds and sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs) – which differ based on their underlying mechanisms. $^{1}$ Both instruments hold significant advantages for developing country issuers – including diversifying the investor base (also internationally), signalling commitments to sustainability, and building a market and track record for further issuances from the private sector (World Bank Treasury, 2022 $^{[13]}$ ). GSS bonds also concretely drive financing towards sustainable projects, while providing transparency around this. SLBs, on the other hand, incentivise behaviour towards sustainability targets, while increasing accountability towards these. As such, GSS bonds and SLBs are complementary instruments, and they can be used depending on issuers' ultimate financing needs (OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). In a context of increased pressure and urgency to progress towards climate action and the SDGs, these factors make them particularly important for public sector issuers. $^{2}$

Yet the reality is that issuances are not occurring in the countries or regions that have the greatest financing needs, and which could therefore benefit the most from these issuances (see (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ), (OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ) and Box 1.1). Here, it is important to note that this doesn't necessarily reflect a lack of interest in GSSS bonds from issuers. GSSS bonds are debt instruments, and this means that they are not appropriate for all entities. Developing country issuers may face additional constraints when entering this space (see Box 1.2). Factors relating to the issuer's macroeconomic environment or institutional setting may also inhibit capital market transactions – ranging from prevailing debt distress, low capital market development, missing regulation or lacking pipelines of sustainable assets (OECD, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ). Still, there is room for developing countries to increase their issuance of GSSS bonds, which represent only 5% of annual emerging market bond issuance (IFC-Amundi, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ). The OECD series on GSSS bonds outlines concrete recommendations for members of the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) (referred to hereafter as donors) to effectively overcome challenges in issuing GSSS bonds in developing countries (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). A crucial starting point of these recommendations is that their use must be carefully assessed and evaluated.

## Box 1.1 Data trends in the GSSS bond market

The GSSS bond market has been in constant evolution since its birth in 2007 (EIB, 2022 $^{[14]}$ ). It faced very significant annual growth until 2022, where challenging market conditions resulted in a decrease in issuances (although this market downsize was less pronounced than in the overall bond market). Since then, the market picked back up again – reflecting its resilience and robustness (BNP Paribas Asset Management, 2026 $^{[12]}$ ). As of 2025, it continues to be mostly composed of use-of-proceeds instruments, and green bonds remained the dominant instrument, accounting for 61% of the market in 2025. In the same year, social bond issuances made up 12%, while sustainability bond issuances had another strong year (increasing to 23% from 22% in 2024). In contrast, SLBs continued to struggle: in 2025 they made up 3% of the market (down from 4% in 2024).

Despite the potential that GSSS bonds hold for financing sustainable development, issuances from ODA-eligible countries, which have the greatest financing needs, account for a small share of the total market. In 2025, only 17% of the overall green, social and sustainability (GSS) bond market was issued in developing countries (dropping to 5% when not including the People's Republic of China). This trend is also true in the SLB market: in 2025, only 24% of the total issued amount came from issuers in ODA-eligible countries, dropping to 17% when excluding the People's Republic of China. As of 2025, Europe continues to dominate the SLB market. At the same time, despite the first sovereigns entering the SLB market as issuers in 2022, public sector players have so far remained reluctant to enter this space. As of Q1 2026, four sovereigns have issued SLBs: Chile, Uruguay, Thailand and Slovenia.

# China's K-Shaped Economy Is Not a Recovery — It Is a Structural Divergence That Demands a Barbell Investment Strategy

The headline numbers for China's May credit data looked reassuring. New total social financing came in at RMB 2,029 billion, 19 percent above consensus. M1 growth accelerated to 5.5 percent, beating expectations by half a percentage point. On the surface, liquidity appeared to be improving, and the economy seemed to be gaining traction. But these figures are deceptive. They mask a deeper structural divergence that is reshaping the Chinese economy in ways that most investors have not yet fully priced in.

The reality is that China is experiencing a K-shaped economy, not a synchronized recovery. One branch of the K is rising — driven by government bond issuance, export-oriented manufacturing tied to the AI super-cycle, and service-sector activity that is outpacing official gauges. The other branch is falling — weighed down by household deleveraging, contracting consumer credit, and a manufacturing sector that is losing momentum. The aggregate data is smoothing over these opposing forces, and investors who rely on top-line numbers alone will misallocate capital.

This divergence is not temporary. It reflects a fundamental reordering of China's growth model, where the state is stepping in to fill the credit gap left by the private sector, and where external demand is outperforming domestic consumption. For investors in Chinese financials, this environment calls for a barbell strategy that balances exposure to institutions benefiting from state-led credit expansion and formal capital channel growth, while avoiding banks that are overly exposed to household and small-business lending.

Three findings from the May data reveal the true shape of this K, and each carries specific implications for portfolio construction.

## The Credit Impulse Is Weakening Because Government Bonds Are Replacing, Not Stimulating, Private Sector Borrowing

The composition of May's TSF tells a clear story about who is borrowing and who is not. Government bonds contributed RMB 1.2 trillion to new TSF, accounting for 60 percent of the monthly total. Without this issuance, headline TSF would have been flat to negative. Corporate bond financing improved 14.6 percent year on year, but that was a partial offset to the persistent weakness in corporate long-term loans, which actually contracted by RMB 20 billion in May. Shadow banking continued to shrink.

This is not a credit expansion; it is a credit substitution. The government is borrowing to sustain aggregate financing growth because the private sector is unwilling or unable to do so. The consequence is that the credit impulse — the three-month change in TSF relative to quarterly GDP — is weakening on a month-on-month basis. When the state is the marginal borrower, the multiplier effect on economic activity is lower than when corporations or households take on debt for investment or consumption.

The so-what for investors is straightforward. Banks that rely heavily on corporate lending for revenue growth will face continued headwinds. Loan books are growing in nominal terms, but the quality of that growth is deteriorating. More than 100 percent of new RMB loans in May came from discounted bills, which carry lower yields and shorter durations. This is a defensive maneuver by banks to meet regulatory lending targets without taking on credit risk. It is not a sign of healthy demand.

The strategic question that follows is whether the government can sustain this pace of bond issuance without crowding out private investment further. The report data does not answer this directly, but the historical pattern suggests that prolonged state-led credit expansion eventually depresses private sector borrowing costs in a way that discourages risk-taking. Investors should watch the trajectory of corporate bond yields relative to government bond yields as a leading indicator of whether this substitution effect is intensifying.

## The Apparent Improvement in M1 Is Low Quality and Masks a Precautionary Cash Hoarding by Corporations

M1 growth accelerated to 5.5 percent in May, which was above consensus and would normally be interpreted as a sign that corporations are becoming more willing to deploy cash for transactions and investment. A closer reading of the data suggests otherwise. The Purchasing Managers' Index for new orders declined 0.7 percentage points month on month to 49.9, slipping back into contraction territory. Corporations are not holding more cash because they are optimistic about demand; they are holding more cash because they are uncertain about the outlook.

This is precautionary liquidity, not transactional liquidity. The distinction matters because the two have very different implications for bank profitability and economic activity. Transactional liquidity drives fee income, loan demand, and deposit turnover. Precautionary liquidity sits idle in current accounts, earning minimal interest and generating no multiplier effect in the real economy.

The M2-M1 spread narrowed in May, which some analysts interpret as a positive signal that term deposits are being activated. But the narrowing in this cycle is happening for the wrong reason. M1 is rising because corporations are hoarding cash, not because they are spending it. M2 is stable because households are still saving at elevated rates despite declining deposit growth in absolute terms. The household deposit-to-debt ratio chart in the report shows that household deposits have been running well above household debt since 2022, and the gap has only widened. This is a balance-sheet repair dynamic, not a prelude to consumption-led recovery.

For investors, the implication is that the improvement in monetary aggregates is not yet translating into improved earnings visibility for banks that depend on loan growth and fee income from corporate activity. The quality of liquidity matters more than the quantity. Until the PMI new orders index crosses back above 50 on a sustained basis, the rise in M1 should be treated with skepticism.

## The Divergence Between External and Domestic Demand Is Creating Two Separate Operating Environments for Financial Institutions

The May export data was striking. Customs exports grew 19.4 percent year on year, the strongest print since February and well above the consensus estimate of 15 percent. The drivers were concentrated in renewable energy and AI-related goods, reflecting the global investment cycle in data centers, grid infrastructure, and semiconductor supply chains. On the other side of the K, core CPI dipped 0.1 percent month on month to 1.1 percent, with softness in both services and core goods. The producer price index continued to rise, but the gap between purchase prices and ex-factory prices remained wide, indicating that downstream sectors are unable to pass through input cost increases.

This divergence is visible in the gap between the RatingDog PMI and the official NBS PMI. The RatingDog index, which tends to capture smaller and more export-oriented firms, has been running above the official manufacturing PMI since early 2026. The spread widened further in May, suggesting that the firms benefiting from the AI and green energy export cycle are operating in a different demand environment than the broader manufacturing base that serves domestic construction and infrastructure.

For banks, this creates a two-tier credit environment. Banks with strong exposure to export-oriented supply chains and the Yangtze River Delta region are likely to see better loan demand and asset quality than banks concentrated in inland provinces or heavily exposed to property and local government financing vehicles. This is why a barbell strategy that pairs a high-dividend, internationally oriented state-owned bank with a high-quality regional bank in a dynamic export hub makes analytical sense.

The report identifies Bank of China and Bank of Ningbo as the top picks in this framework. Bank of China benefits from its offshore franchise and cross-border business lines, which align with the expanding formal capital connect channels that the regulatory agenda is promoting. Bank of Ningbo benefits from its position in Zhejiang province, a manufacturing and export powerhouse that is disproportionately benefiting from the AI and renewable energy export cycle.

## The Regulatory Push Toward Formal Capital Channels Makes HKEx a Structural Beneficiary of the K-Shape

The State Council's recent outbound investment regulations and the CSRC's agenda to clean up cross-border stock trading are often interpreted as restrictive measures. That reading is incomplete. The intent is not to restrict capital outflows; it is to channel them through official, regulated conduits such as QDII, the Southbound Stock Connect, and the Wealth Management Connect. The goal is to bring offshore capital flows onto balance sheets where they can be monitored, taxed, and managed.

This regulatory direction is a direct consequence of the K-shaped economy. As domestic investment opportunities in property and infrastructure become less attractive, household savings are seeking offshore diversification. The authorities want this to happen through formal channels rather than through informal cross-border arrangements that create regulatory blind spots and capital control leakage. Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing is the natural beneficiary of this trend because it operates the infrastructure through which these formal channels flow.

The strategic logic is clear. Every incremental dollar that moves from informal cross-border trading into Southbound Connect generates transaction volume and settlement revenue for HKEx. As the connect schemes expand to include more products, more currencies, and more investor categories, the exchange's revenue base becomes more diversified and more resilient to domestic credit cycles. This is a long-duration growth story that is independent of the near-term trajectory of China's GDP growth.

The unresolved question is how quickly the regulatory transition will occur. The report's data does not provide a timeline for the expansion of connect schemes, and the pace of regulatory change in China is notoriously difficult to predict. Investors should monitor the growth rate of Southbound Connect turnover relative to total Hong Kong market turnover as a proxy for how fast this structural shift is happening.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: How Long Can the Government Sustain the Credit Substitution Without Undermining Bank Profitability

The report provides a clear diagnosis of the K-shaped economy and a well-reasoned set of investment recommendations. But it leaves one critical question open. Government bond issuance is currently supporting TSF growth, and banks are buying these bonds as part of their asset allocation. This creates a circular flow where the government borrows, banks buy the debt, and the government spends the proceeds on infrastructure and social programs that may or may not generate sufficient economic returns to service the debt.

If the government continues to crowd out private sector credit demand for an extended period, banks will face a structural decline in net interest margins as they shift from higher-yielding corporate loans to lower-yielding government bonds. The current barbell strategy works because Bank of China and Bank of Ningbo have offsetting strengths that mitigate this risk. But for the banking sector as a whole, the sustainability of this model depends on whether private sector credit demand eventually recovers or whether the government is willing to accept permanently lower bank profitability in exchange for financial stability.

The report does not model this scenario, and investors should develop their own stress tests. A useful framework is to track the ratio of government bond holdings to total bank assets over time. If this ratio rises above a certain threshold, it may signal that the credit substitution has become structural rather than cyclical, which would have negative implications for bank valuations across the board.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the K-Shaped Economy

The report's findings can be translated into a three-step decision framework for investors in Chinese financials.

First, assess your exposure to credit quality. Banks that are heavily weighted toward household mortgages, small business loans, and local government financing vehicles face the highest risk of asset quality deterioration in a K-shaped environment. The household deleveraging trend is not reversing, and the property sector remains under pressure. Avoid banks with high exposure to these segments unless they have demonstrated the ability to maintain credit costs below 1 percent through multiple cycles.

Second, evaluate your exposure to net interest margin dynamics. Banks that rely on corporate lending for the majority of their interest income will face margin compression as the government's credit substitution depresses loan yields. Banks with diversified income streams, including fee income from cross-border business, wealth management, and capital market services, are better positioned to maintain profitability even if net interest margins decline.

Third, position for the regulatory direction of travel. The authorities are committed to expanding formal capital connect channels, and HKEx is the primary infrastructure beneficiary. Direct exposure to HKEx provides a hedge against the domestic credit cycle while offering participation in the long-term growth of China's capital account liberalization. For investors who prefer bank exposure, institutions with strong cross-border franchises and regional concentration in export-oriented provinces offer the best risk-reward profile.

# India's Balance of Payments Is Stronger Than the Rupee Suggests — And That Divergence Holds the Real Story

The Indian rupee has been under pressure, but the country's underlying balance of payments position tells a very different story. In the first quarter of calendar year 2026, India recorded a balance of payments surplus of $7.2 billion, driven by a current account surplus of $7 billion — a result that surprised most forecasters. The apparent weakness of the INR, in other words, is not a signal of fundamental external vulnerability. It is a reflection of precautionary dollar demand amid geopolitical uncertainty in the Middle East, combined with speculative positioning that has temporarily disconnected the currency from its fundamentals.

This divergence matters because it changes how investors should think about India's external risk profile. The conventional narrative — that higher oil prices and geopolitical stress will inevitably widen India's current account deficit and pressure the rupee — is incomplete. The evidence from the first quarter of 2026 suggests that the structural adjustments under way in India's external sector are more powerful than many assume. Lower oil intensity, more price-sensitive import volumes, policy tools to manage gold imports, and a comprehensive set of Reserve Bank of India measures to attract capital inflows are collectively reshaping the external outlook. The result is a balance of payments trajectory that is significantly more favorable than either market pricing or consensus forecasts currently reflect.

This article argues that investors should reassess India's external vulnerability. The risk is not that India's balance of payments deteriorates sharply, but that markets are slow to recognize the improvement — and that the eventual repricing of the INR could be more orderly, and more limited in direction, than many expect.

## The Structural Decline in Oil Intensity Means Higher Prices Will Not Translate Proportionally into a Wider Deficit

The most important structural change in India's external position is the steady decline in oil intensity over the past three decades. Oil consumption per unit of GDP has fallen by roughly 90 percent since the early 1990s, reflecting improvements in energy efficiency, the electrification of transportation, and a gradual shift toward less energy-intensive services-led growth. This is not a cyclical phenomenon; it is the result of decades of policy choices, technological change, and economic transformation.

The implication for the current account is direct and often misunderstood. When oil prices rise, the mechanical impact on India's import bill is partially offset by a decline in import volumes. The relationship between Brent crude prices and net crude import volumes is non-linear. Below $75-$80 per barrel, higher prices are associated with rising import volumes. Above that threshold, the relationship reverses: price increases lead to lower volumes. Post-pandemic, this price sensitivity has increased. A 10 percent rise in oil prices now corresponds to roughly a 6 percent decline in net import volumes, compared with a smaller response in the pre-pandemic period.

This elasticity matters because it introduces a natural stabilizer into the current account. At Brent prices around $90 per barrel, as projected for calendar year 2026, the volume response will meaningfully reduce the trade balance impact. The mechanism operates through two channels: domestic fuel price pass-through, which reduces consumption demand, and the broader economic drag from higher energy costs, which slows activity and thus oil demand. Historical evidence shows that every 10 percent increase in domestic fuel prices reduces petrol and diesel consumption growth by roughly 3 percent over the following 12 months. With domestic fuel prices already adjusted upward this year, the demand response is already under way.

The so-what for investors is straightforward. The traditional rule of thumb that every $10 increase in oil prices widens India's current account deficit by 0.4 to 0.5 percent of GDP is no longer reliable. The structural decline in oil intensity, combined with greater price sensitivity of demand, means the elasticity is lower. This does not mean higher oil prices are irrelevant for India's external balance. It means the magnitude of the impact is smaller than historical relationships would suggest — and that the market's reflexive concern about oil shocks may be overstated.

## Gold Import Duties Have Historically Worked — But the Mechanism Has Limits That Investors Need to Watch

Gold imports have been a recurring source of external stress for India, averaging between 1 and 3 percent of GDP during previous episodes of geopolitical uncertainty. In the first quarter of 2026, gold imports were tracking at roughly 2.2 percent of GDP, levels comparable to prior stress episodes. The government's response has been predictable but effective: an increase in import duties in May 2026, following a pattern used repeatedly over the past decade.

The historical evidence is clear. Gold import duty hikes have consistently led to a decline in import volumes, with the impact beginning to materialize within one to two months and the full effect — a decline of 40 to 50 percent — unfolding over five to six months. The mechanism is straightforward: higher duties increase the cost of official gold imports, reducing retail demand and encouraging inventory drawdowns. The 2013 duty hike, for example, was followed by a sharp and sustained decline in gold imports that lasted for several quarters.

There are, however, two important caveats that investors should consider. First, the current environment is unusual because gold prices are at record highs and geopolitical uncertainty remains elevated. Gold's role as a store of value during periods of stress means that demand may be less price-sensitive than in normal times. The wedding season from November to February could also provide a temporary boost to jewellery demand that partially offsets the duty impact. Second, higher official duties create an incentive for unofficial imports, which would not appear in the balance of payments data but would still represent a leakage of foreign exchange. The magnitude of this channel is difficult to quantify but is worth monitoring.

The strategic implication is that gold import duties are a useful but imperfect policy tool. They can reduce official imports in the near term, but they do not eliminate the underlying demand for gold as a hedge against uncertainty. Investors should expect gold imports to moderate in the second half of 2026, but the adjustment may be less complete than in previous cycles. The risk is that sustained geopolitical stress keeps gold demand elevated despite higher duties, creating a persistent drag on the current account that is not fully captured in current forecasts.

## The RBI's Capital Inflow Measures Are Not a Repeat of 2013 — And That Is the Point

The Reserve Bank of India has announced a comprehensive set of measures to attract dollar inflows, including concessional forex swap rates for banks and quasi-sovereign entities, exemptions on interest and capital gains tax for foreign portfolio investors in government securities, and a new foreign currency non-resident deposit scheme with full hedging support. These measures are often compared to the 2013 "taper tantrum" response, but the comparison is misleading.

In 2013, the RBI was reacting to an acute external crisis. The current account deficit had widened to nearly 5 percent of GDP, the rupee was under severe pressure, and the central bank was forced to take emergency measures to stem capital outflows. The 2026 context is fundamentally different. India's current account deficit is projected at 1.3 percent of GDP for calendar year 2026, down from earlier forecasts of 2.0 percent. The balance of payments is in surplus. The RBI's measures are not a crisis response; they are a preemptive strategy to lock in a more favorable external position and reduce depreciation pressure on the rupee.

The scale of potential inflows is significant. The FCNR (B) deposit scheme alone could generate $30 to $50 billion in inflows in calendar year 2026, based on the 2013 experience. External commercial borrowings could add another $5 to $15 billion. Foreign portfolio investment in government securities is likely to increase as the expanded Fully Accessible Route brings more long-duration bonds into the eligible universe. In total, the RBI's measures could generate roughly $60 billion in additional capital inflows over the next 18 months — enough to shift the balance of payments from deficit to surplus across both calendar year 2026 and fiscal year 2027.

The so-what for investors is that the INR's depreciation pressure should ease significantly, but this does not imply material appreciation. The RBI is likely to absorb renewed dollar inflows through reserve accumulation and by unwinding its short forward book. The central bank's objective is not to engineer a stronger rupee but to reduce volatility and rebuild reserves. For investors, the implication is that the INR is likely to trade in a narrower range than current forward pricing suggests, with the risk of sharp depreciation substantially reduced.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Remittance Inflows and the Risk of Unofficial Gold Imports

The report provides a compelling case for a more favorable balance of payments outlook, but it leaves two important questions unresolved. First, the strength of remittance inflows in the first quarter of 2026 — a record $41 billion, or 4 percent of GDP — appears to have been driven by precautionary transfers from non-resident Indians in the Gulf region amid heightened geopolitical uncertainty. The report assumes a moderation in remittances over the remainder of the year, consistent with slower growth in GCC economies. But if geopolitical tensions persist or escalate, precautionary transfers could remain elevated, providing an unexpected boost to the current account. Conversely, if the situation stabilizes quickly, remittances could normalize faster than expected, creating downside risk to the current account forecast.

Second, the report acknowledges the risk that higher gold import duties could increase unofficial imports but does not quantify this channel. In previous episodes of duty hikes, the gap between official and unofficial gold imports has been difficult to measure but has often been significant. If unofficial imports rise substantially, the reported current account deficit would understate the true external drain. Investors should monitor gold price differentials between domestic and international markets as a proxy for unofficial flows.

These open questions are not flaws in the analysis; they reflect genuine uncertainty about the trajectory of geopolitical risk and its second-order effects. The report's framework is robust, but investors should recognize that the balance of payments outlook is conditional on assumptions about remittance behavior and the effectiveness of gold import controls that cannot be tested until more data become available.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position for India's External Shift

For investors trying to translate this analysis into actionable positioning, three questions provide a useful framework.

First, what is your view on oil prices? If you expect Brent to remain above $90 per barrel for an extended period, the report's analysis suggests the current account impact will be smaller than historical models predict, but it will still be negative. The appropriate response is to reduce exposure to sectors that are sensitive to oil prices and to favor domestic demand stories over export-oriented ones. If you expect oil prices to decline, the improvement in India's external position could be more pronounced than currently forecast.

Second, what is your view on the INR? The report argues that depreciation pressure should ease, but material appreciation is unlikely. For investors with a medium-term horizon, this suggests that hedging INR exposure is less urgent than it was six months ago, but that outright long INR positions are unlikely to generate significant returns. The more interesting trade may be in INR volatility: if the RBI succeeds in reducing currency fluctuations, options strategies that benefit from lower realized volatility could be attractive.

Third, what is your view on Indian fixed income? The expansion of the Fully Accessible Route for government securities and the tax exemptions for foreign portfolio investors create a more favorable regulatory environment for foreign investment in Indian bonds. For investors who are underweight Indian fixed income, the improving balance of payments outlook and the RBI's commitment to attracting inflows provide a rationale for increasing allocation. The risk is that global interest rates remain high, limiting the appeal of Indian bonds on a carry basis.

The unifying theme across these questions is that India's external position is improving in ways that markets have not fully priced. The conventional narrative of vulnerability to oil shocks and geopolitical stress is outdated. The structural changes in oil intensity, the policy tools available to manage gold imports, and the RBI's proactive capital inflow strategy collectively create a more resilient external profile than at any point in the past decade.

The report makes a persuasive case that India's balance of payments outlook is more favorable than current market pricing suggests. The structural decline in oil intensity, the increased price sensitivity of oil import volumes, the historical effectiveness of gold import duties, and the scale of the RBI's capital inflow measures all point in the same direction: India's external vulnerability is lower than consensus believes.

But the report also leaves important questions unanswered. How durable is the remittance boost? Will higher gold duties simply shift imports to unofficial channels? Can the RBI's capital inflow measures sustain inflows beyond the initial wave? These are not rhetorical questions; they are the analytical frontier for investors who want to move beyond the consensus view.

EN_A: So the rupee has been under pressure, but this report says India’s external fundamentals actually look pretty solid. What’s going on?
EN_B: That’s exactly the puzzle. Despite the Middle East conflict and weaker capital inflows, India posted a 7.2 billion dollar balance of payments surplus in the first quarter of this year.
EN_A: Wait, a surplus? Then why is the rupee still so weak?
EN_B: The report argues it’s mostly precautionary dollar demand — companies hoarding dollars because of geopolitical uncertainty, not because the underlying position is deteriorating.
EN_A: So the big question this report tries to answer is — has the rupee’s weakness been exaggerated?
EN_B: Exactly. It reassesses India’s external balances through three lenses: oil, gold, and capital flows. The conclusion is that depreciation pressure should ease, but don’t expect a big rally.
EN_A: Let’s start with oil. India is a huge importer. With prices this high, the current account should be hurting, right?
EN_B: Surprisingly, the report finds that India’s oil intensity has been falling for decades. And when Brent goes above 80 dollars a barrel, import volumes actually start to drop.
EN_A: So higher prices mean less volume? That’s a pretty powerful offset.
EN_B: Yes. The report estimates that for every 10 percent rise in oil prices, net import volumes fall by about 6 percent. Higher fuel prices at the pump reduce domestic demand, and that helps contain the overall import bill.
EN_A: That makes sense. So the report actually lowered its current account deficit forecast for this year from 2 percent of GDP to 1.3 percent?
EN_B: Right. And that’s also thanks to record high remittances and strong services exports. The first quarter actually showed a current account surplus.
EN_A: What about gold? India is famous for its gold demand, especially during times of uncertainty.
EN_B: The report points out that policymakers have historically used import duties to curb gold imports during stress periods. They just hiked duties in May, and history suggests volumes will start to decline within one to two months.
EN_A: But with gold prices at record highs, wouldn’t that just push demand into unofficial channels?
EN_B: That’s one of the open questions the report flags. It doesn’t fully explore how much smuggling might increase. So the near-term impact of the duty is promising, but the longer-term effect is still uncertain.
EN_A: And on the capital flow side, what has the central bank done?
EN_B: Quite a lot. The report details measures like concessional swap rates for banks to raise dollar funding, and expanding the eligible bond universe for foreign investors. They estimate these could bring in about 60 billion dollars of additional inflows.
EN_A: 60 billion? That sounds like a lot. Does that mean the rupee will appreciate?
EN_B: Not necessarily. The report expects the central bank to use most of those inflows to build reserves and unwind its short forward book. So depreciation pressure eases, but significant appreciation is unlikely.
EN_A: So the rupee is basically being managed?
EN_B: That’s the takeaway. But there’s a big unresolved question here — what if global risk appetite suddenly improves and capital floods in? Can the central bank really absorb it all?
EN_A: That’s a good point. So for someone trying to figure out where the rupee is headed, what’s still missing from this report?
EN_B: It doesn’t give a clear timeline. It lays out the logic for why pressure should ease, but it doesn’t say “in the next three months, this is what will happen.” And the sustainability of the gold duty impact is another open question.
EN_A: So the real value of this report is not a simple prediction, but a framework for understanding the structural shifts in India’s external position.
EN_B: Exactly. And if you want the detailed scenarios, including the 2027 projections, the full report has all of that in the appendix.

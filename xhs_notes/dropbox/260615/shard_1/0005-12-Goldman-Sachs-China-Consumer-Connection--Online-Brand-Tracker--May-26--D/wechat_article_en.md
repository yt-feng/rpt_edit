# China's 618 Reveals a Consumer Market That Is Not Slowing Uniformly—It Is Restructuring Around Platform Subsidies and Brand Polarization

The dominant narrative surrounding China's consumer sector in mid-2026 is one of weakness. Headline data from the May online brand tracker appears to confirm this: across Tmall, Taobao, and JD combined, categories ranging from White Goods at -31% year-on-year to Beer at -22% and Pet Foods at -22% paint a picture of broad-based contraction. The casual observer would conclude that the Chinese consumer has simply stopped spending. That conclusion would be strategically dangerous.

The May data, when read with the necessary granularity, tells a far more complex story. The aggregate numbers obscure a market that is not merely shrinking but actively restructuring around three powerful forces: the aggressive injection of platform and local government subsidies, a sharp divergence between domestic and multinational brand performance within the same categories, and a fundamental shift in channel dynamics that renders single-platform data misleading. The real insight from this report is that the 618 shopping festival is not a simple demand event—it is a stress test that reveals which brands have the margin structure, supply chain agility, and platform relationships to thrive in a subsidy-driven environment. For investors and executives, the question is no longer whether the consumer is weak. The question is whether your brand is positioned to capture the pockets of growth that exist even within a declining aggregate.

## The Beauty Category Is Not Recovering—It Is Being Artificially Supported by Subsidies, and the Divergence Between Local and MNC Brands Tells the Real Story

The most striking data point in the May tracker is the performance of the Beauty category. On the surface, the numbers appear contradictory. On Tmall, Taobao, and JD combined, Beauty GMV declined 4% year-on-year in May. Yet when Douyin is added to the calculation, Beauty GMV grew at 14% year-on-year, accelerating from 11% in April. This is not a recovery in consumer demand. This is a subsidy-driven reallocation of spending.

The report explicitly notes that the acceleration was "partially helped by a softer Women's Day in 1Q, along with increasing subsidies from platforms and local governments." This is the critical caveat. The growth is not organic demand improvement; it is the result of financial engineering by platforms and local authorities to stimulate consumption during a specific promotional window. The question for any brand operating in this category is whether the growth is sustainable once the subsidies are withdrawn. Historical patterns from previous 618 and Double Eleven events suggest that demand pulled forward by subsidies often leads to a subsequent trough.

The divergence between local and multinational brands within Beauty is even more instructive. Local brands that rebounded—Forest Cabin at 110% year-on-year, Botanee at 43%, Shanghai Jahwa at 27%—appear to have been the primary beneficiaries of these subsidies. Meanwhile, multinational giants Estee Lauder at 36% and L'Oreal at 16% also posted strong growth, but their performance was driven by different dynamics: Estee Lauder in the higher segment and L'Oreal through dermocosmetics. The losers in this environment are the mid-tier brands that lack the scale to compete on subsidy absorption or the premium positioning to maintain pricing power.

The strategic implication is clear. Brands operating in Beauty must make a binary choice. They can compete on subsidy absorption, which requires thin margins and high volume, or they can compete on brand equity and product differentiation, which requires maintaining pricing power even as the market distorts. The middle ground is becoming untenable. The report's data on June 1-8 Douyin performance reinforces this: western MNCs like L'Oreal and Estee Lauder at 50%+ year-on-year, while local brands are mixed, with Mao Geping catching up at 60%+ and Giant accelerating on an easier base. This is not a uniform recovery. It is a market sorting winners from losers in real time.

## The Sportswear Category Exposes the Danger of Reading Single-Platform Data in an Omni-Channel World

The Sportswear category presents a different but equally instructive analytical challenge. On Tmall alone, Sportswear declined 11% year-on-year in May, a sharp deceleration from 6% growth in April and 26% growth in 1Q26. A surface-level reading would suggest a category in trouble. Yet the report flags a critical methodological issue: "brands have been executing omni-channel strategies in both online (e.g., emerging channels like Dewu) and offline, suggesting the sales data collected by data vendors may not reconcile with actual growth."

This is not a minor caveat. It is a fundamental challenge to how investors and analysts should interpret the tracker data. If brands are shifting volume to channels not captured by the primary data vendors, then the apparent decline in Sportswear on Tmall may simply reflect a channel shift rather than a demand decline. The report's recommendation to "combine May - Jun sales to assess overall performance" is sound, but it does not fully address the underlying problem: the data infrastructure for tracking Chinese consumer spending is becoming fractured.

Within the category, the brand-level divergence is stark. Outdoor names such as Arc'teryx and Descente, as well as niche premium brand Lululemon, saw deceleration in May, partially on a high base. Meanwhile, adidas, Li Ning, Anta, Fila, Bosideng, and Salomon accelerated from April, "possibly on 618 shopping festival promotion." This pattern suggests that the 618 promotion is not lifting all boats equally. Brands with strong platform relationships and the ability to offer compelling promotional mechanics are capturing share, while premium outdoor brands that may be less willing to discount are seeing growth slow.

The strategic takeaway for Sportswear investors is that top-line data from a single platform is no longer sufficient for decision-making. Companies that appear to be underperforming on Tmall may actually be executing successful omni-channel strategies that are invisible to the tracker. Conversely, brands that appear to be growing on Tmall may be doing so at the expense of profitability through aggressive discounting. The report does not provide profitability data, but the inference is unavoidable: the 618 promotion cycle is compressing margins for many brands, and the winners will be those who can use the promotion to acquire customers without destroying their brand equity.

## The Broader Consumer Slowdown Is Real, But It Is Masking Structural Shifts in Dairy, IMF, and White Goods That Have Different Drivers

While the Beauty and Sportswear categories offer nuanced stories of divergence and channel shift, the broader consumer categories present a more straightforward picture of weakness—but even here, the drivers are not uniform. Dairy declined 9% year-on-year on Tmall/Taobao/JD combined in May, improving from a -33% decline in 1Q26 but still negative. IMF declined 19% year-on-year, worsening from -11% in 1Q26. White Goods declined 31% year-on-year, widening from -15% in 1Q26.

These are not small declines. They represent a genuine contraction in consumer spending on essential and durable categories. However, the drivers differ significantly. Dairy weakness is likely structural, reflecting ongoing price competition and capacity oversupply in the domestic dairy industry. IMF weakness may reflect demographic trends and a declining birth rate, which is a multi-year headwind that no promotional event can reverse. White Goods weakness is more cyclical, reflecting the pull-forward of demand during the pandemic and the ongoing property market downturn, which suppresses demand for major appliances.

The report's category-level data reveals that the weakness is not uniform even within these struggling categories. On Tmall, Dairy value declined 21% year-on-year in May, but volume declined only 6%, implying that average selling prices fell 17%. This is a market where consumers are still buying, but they are trading down to cheaper options. In White Goods, the story is different: Tmall value declined 50% year-on-year, suggesting that consumers are deferring purchases entirely rather than trading down. The difference matters for investment strategy. In Dairy, the opportunity may lie in value-oriented brands that can capture volume share. In White Goods, the only strategy is to wait for the property cycle to turn.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Subsidy-Driven Growth and the True Profitability of 618 Winners

For all its granularity, the report leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important is the sustainability of the subsidy-driven growth observed in Beauty. The report notes that the acceleration was "partially helped by... increasing subsidies from platforms and local governments." But it does not quantify the magnitude of these subsidies or assess their impact on brand profitability. A brand that grows 110% year-on-year by absorbing platform subsidies may be generating negative contribution margins on the incremental volume. The question for investors is whether this growth is value-creating or value-destroying.

The second unanswered question concerns the accuracy of the tracker data in an increasingly fragmented channel environment. The report acknowledges that "brands have been executing omni-channel strategies... suggesting the sales data collected by data vendors may not reconcile with actual growth." But it does not provide a framework for adjusting the data or estimating the magnitude of the channel shift. For investors relying on this tracker to make portfolio decisions, this is a material limitation. A brand that appears to be declining on the tracker may actually be growing if it is shifting volume to Dewu, Douyin, or offline channels that are not fully captured.

The third question is about the second-order effects of the 618 promotion cycle on brand positioning. When a premium brand like Estee Lauder grows 36% year-on-year in a declining category, is it gaining market share permanently, or is it simply pulling forward demand that will be lost in subsequent months? The report does not provide cohort analysis or repeat purchase data that would allow investors to distinguish between these scenarios. The same question applies to local brands like Forest Cabin, whose triple-digit growth may reflect one-time subsidy benefits rather than sustainable competitive advantage.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Subsidy-Driven 618 Environment

Given the complexity of the data and the limitations of the tracker, investors and executives need a structured framework for interpreting the May results and positioning for the remainder of 2026. The following framework synthesizes the report's insights into actionable questions:

**Step 1: Determine whether your category is subsidy-dependent or structurally driven.** Beauty and Sportswear are clearly subsidy-dependent categories where 618 promotions are creating artificial demand spikes. Dairy, IMF, and White Goods are more structurally driven by demographics, capacity, and the property cycle. The appropriate strategy differs fundamentally. In subsidy-dependent categories, the focus should be on share capture within the promotional window and on ensuring that the cost of acquiring customers through subsidies does not exceed their lifetime value. In structurally driven categories, the focus should be on cost reduction and capacity management, as no amount of promotion can reverse demographic or cyclical headwinds.

**Step 2: Assess your brand's position on the divergence spectrum.** The report consistently shows that within each category, performance is diverging between winners and losers. In Beauty, the winners are premium MNCs and subsidy-absorbing local brands, while mid-tier brands are being squeezed. In Sportswear, the winners are brands with strong platform relationships and promotional flexibility, while premium outdoor brands are decelerating. The question for each brand is: which side of the divergence am I on? If the answer is the losing side, the strategy must change before the next promotional cycle.

**Step 3: Audit your channel data infrastructure.** The report's warning about omni-channel strategies not being captured by data vendors is a red flag for any investor relying on third-party data for decision-making. Companies should be able to reconcile their own internal sales data with the tracker data and explain any discrepancies. If a brand appears to be underperforming on the tracker but is actually growing in aggregate, that is a potential investment opportunity. If a brand appears to be outperforming on the tracker but is actually losing share in channels not captured, that is a potential risk.

**Step 4: Model the post-subsidy scenario.** The most important question that the report does not answer is what happens to demand after the 618 subsidies are withdrawn. Investors should model two scenarios: one in which the subsidy-driven growth is sustained through repeat purchases and customer retention, and one in which the growth reverses as consumers revert to their pre-promotion spending patterns. The gap between these scenarios is the risk premium that should be applied to brands that are heavily dependent on promotional volume.

## The Unresolved Analytical Question That Should Drive Your Research Agenda

The most important unresolved question from this report is whether the subsidy-driven growth in Beauty and Sportswear represents a genuine shift in consumer preferences or a temporary distortion that will reverse in the second half of 2026. The answer has profound implications for portfolio construction. If the growth is genuine, then local brands like Forest Cabin and Proya that are capturing share during 618 may be building lasting competitive advantages. If the growth is a distortion, then the apparent winners of May and June may be setting themselves up for a difficult second half when the subsidies are withdrawn and demand normalizes.

The report's data on June 1-8 Douyin performance offers a partial answer: western MNCs continue to grow strongly at 50%+, while local brands are mixed. This suggests that the MNCs may have more sustainable growth drivers, such as product innovation and brand equity, while the local brands are more dependent on the promotional environment. But this is inference, not conclusion. The full answer will require data from July and August, when the post-618 hangover becomes visible. Investors who can get ahead of this question will have a significant advantage in positioning for the second half of the year.

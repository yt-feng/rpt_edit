EN_A: So there is this new report on Asia Pacific banks covering Hong Kong and ASEAN. What is the one big takeaway you got from it?
EN_B: The big takeaway is that these banks are at a structural inflection point. The return profiles and growth drivers vary massively across markets.
EN_A: Structural inflection point? Can you break that down?
EN_B: Sure. Some markets like Indonesia have very low credit penetration. Private debt to GDP is below 50 percent. Loan growth has been strong. Others like Thailand have household debt at 87 percent of GDP and an aging population. Loan growth has slowed to just 2 to 5 percent.
EN_A: So Indonesia is a growth story and Thailand is a structural problem?
EN_B: Exactly. Indonesian banks rebuilt after the 1998 crisis and have grown loans at an 8 percent CAGR. Their ROE was 18.4 percent in 2025, the highest in ASEAN. Thailand is different. ROE is only 9.2 percent. Asset quality issues have been recurring since 2012.
EN_A: What about Singapore and Malaysia? Are they mature markets?
EN_B: Singapore is definitely mature. Loan growth is only 3 to 5 percent. But its banks are global leaders in wealth management and digitalization, especially DBS. And Singapore is a leader in digital assets. Malaysia is also relatively mature. Loan growth is 5 to 6 percent, driven by mortgages. Asset quality has been stable.
EN_A: And Hong Kong banks? The report covers HSBC, Standard Chartered, and BOCHK. How are they different?
EN_B: Very different. HSBC is a global network play. It targets a return on tangible equity above 17 percent from 2026 to 2028. Standard Chartered is in a turnaround. Its ROTE improved to 11.9 percent in 2025, aiming for 15 percent by 2028. BOCHK is Hong Kong focused. Its cost-to-income ratio is only 24 percent, and it has very high capital ratios.
EN_A: So every market is different. How should investors think about picking banks?
EN_B: The report highlights capital returns as a key theme. Look at Indonesia. Banks like BRI have a dividend yield of 13.5 percent for 2026 and a payout ratio of 90 percent. Singapore banks have yields around 4.5 percent. That reflects different capital allocation strategies.
EN_A: So high dividend yield banks are more attractive?
EN_B: Not necessarily. High yield can mean limited growth opportunities or excess capital. Indonesian banks have high yields but also low credit penetration and strong growth potential. It is a balance between returning capital and growing. Thai banks with high yields might reflect structural growth challenges.
EN_A: What other key themes does the report cover?
EN_B: Wealth management is a big one, especially in Singapore and Hong Kong. And the emergence of digital assets. Singapore is already a leader there. But the report does not fully answer how digital transformation will impact cost efficiency, or how asset quality will hold up in a downturn.
EN_A: Asset quality. Does the report give a clear answer on that?
EN_B: Not fully. For Thailand, the report notes recurring credit issues since 2012, but it is not clear if credit quality is now on sounder footing. That still needs to be validated. For the Philippines, banks have managed credit risks well historically, but their cost-to-income ratio is the highest in the region, which hurts long-term returns.
EN_A: So what are the open questions that make the full report worth reading?
EN_B: Two big ones. First, can loan growth in Indonesia and the Philippines accelerate further given very low credit penetration? Second, how will net interest margins for Hong Kong banks evolve as interest rates change? The full report has much more detailed analysis on these.

ZH_A: 最近我看到一份关于中国基础材料行业的研究报告，里面提到了不少有意思的发现。你觉得这份报告最核心的判断是什么？

ZH_B: 简单来说，报告认为在中国，铝和铜是最值得关注的，其次是锂，最后才是钢铁。核心逻辑是，房地产需求依然疲软，但制造业和出口表现出了韧性，而供应端在铝和铜上依然受限。

ZH_A: 房地产需求疲软，这个大家都有感觉。但你说制造业和出口有韧性，这具体体现在哪里？报告是怎么说的？

ZH_B: 报告提到，他们刚结束了一次中国实地调研。比如在铝方面，尽管国内需求看起来有点软，社会库存比较高，但下游的铝材加工企业订单增长非常强劲。四月份的订单量比往年同期高出了20%到30%。

ZH_A: 等一下，国内需求软，但下游加工企业订单却很好。这不矛盾吗？这到底意味着什么？

ZH_B: 这恰恰是报告要回答的一个关键问题。高库存可能是个“假象”。报告给出了几个解释。一个原因是，今年铝价涨了不少，下游需要时间消化，不太愿意自己囤库存。另一个更重要的原因是，税务检查变严了，以前藏在贸易商手里的“隐形库存”被迫变成了可见的社会库存。

ZH_A: 所以库存高不代表没人要，而是库存被“挤”出来了。那接下来呢？铝价会怎么走？

ZH_B: 报告认为市场会保持建设性。关键看两点。第一，因为国内外有价差，铝材出口的预期很强，这能帮助消化库存。报告引用数据说，未来几个月铝材出口可能达到每月65万吨以上，接近历史最高水平。第二，供应端有收缩的迹象。调研后发现，有些省份的冶炼厂开工率在下降，因为政府正在严查违规超产。

ZH_A: 违规超产？这个有意思。以前不是一直有产能天花板吗？怎么还能超产？

ZH_B: 对，这就是报告里提到的细节。有些企业通过“产能置换”的方式，在旧产能还没完全关停的情况下，就开了新生产线。这次检查就是针对这种不合规行为。所以，供应天花板依然有效，甚至可能更紧。这让即将到来的传统消费淡季，可能跟往年不太一样。

ZH_A: 明白了，铝的逻辑是“需求有韧性，供应有约束”。那铜呢？报告里怎么说的？

ZH_B: 铜是市场上很受追捧的品种。报告的结论是，长期看多逻辑没变，但短期价格的主要驱动力来自供应端，而不是需求。比如，大家担心中东局势会影响硫磺供应，进而影响铜生产，但报告调研发现，大型矿企利润高，能承担更高的采购成本，所以影响不大。

ZH_A: 所以供应端的故事还在继续。那需求端呢？新能源和AI这些不是一直在讲吗？

ZH_B: 没错，市场对电网、可再生能源和AI带来的铜需求增长是有共识的，但这个预期还没有完全反映到价格里。报告指出，去年底以来铜价的上涨，主要是由供应中断推动的。一个更有意思的长期视角是，越来越多的国家开始重视关键矿产和供应链安全，这可能导致全球市场出现部分分割，比如美国开始战略囤货。未来，铜的战略重要性可能会在定价中扮演更重要的角色。

ZH_A: 这确实是个很宏大的叙事。那跟铝和铜相比，锂和钢铁的情况是不是就没那么乐观了？

ZH_B: 对，报告把锂排在铝和铜之后。对于锂，短期内供需格局还算稳定，但中期看法比较分化。比如，报告调研的公司对2026年的锂需求很有信心，尤其是储能，预期增长至少60%。但对于2027年下半年，大家都没什么把握。

ZH_A: 为什么2027年的能见度这么低？

ZH_B: 报告提到了几个不确定性。一个是2026年的储能需求可能被提前透支了。另一个是，如果储能项目上得太多，可能会导致电价峰谷价差缩小，影响项目的投资回报率。在供应端，报告确认了津巴布韦的锂矿出口在恢复，同时很多公司正在重启之前暂停的矿山。这意味着即使江西的环保停产持续，中期供应还是会增加。

ZH_A: 所以短期没问题，但中期供应压力不小。那对于锂价，调研下来大家怎么看？

ZH_B: 报告里提到，大部分公司认为每吨15万元人民币的价格有支撑，下游也能接受。但市场需要测试20万元这个关口，因为这会影响到储能的成本，以及是否会刺激更多矿山复产。报告还提到了钠离子电池，它正在成为现实，但短期内量不大。它和锂电池的成本平价是关键，这对应的长期锂价区间大概在12万到15万元。

ZH_A: 最后说说钢铁吧。报告里用了“not turning around”这个词，听起来很悲观。

ZH_B: 确实，钢铁是报告最不看好的。需求持续缓慢下滑，这个没有争议。房地产依然疲软，汽车和家电在2025年补贴政策退坡后也缺乏亮点。唯一能支撑的是基建，因为2026年是“十五五”规划的第一年，会有政府主导的项目启动。但这不足以弥补其他板块的疲弱。

ZH_A: 那钢铁的供应端呢？不是一直在讲产能置换和供给侧改革吗？

ZH_B: 报告认为，供应自律是个长期斗争。虽然国家出台了更严格的产能置换政策，比如把置换比例从1.25:1提高到1.5:1，鼓励兼并重组，但市场对此的效果存疑。一个核心问题是，政府对国企的影响力大，但主要的央企和省级国企已经基本完成整合了。而民企之间的并购，更多是市场行为，需要时间。所以，供应端的改善不会很快。

ZH_A: 听起来，钢铁行业的基本面确实最差。那报告里有没有提到一个我们一直在聊的话题——房地产？它到底什么时候见底？

ZH_B: 报告专门和一家全国性的房产中介做了交流。他们的判断是，2026年住宅销售面积可能还会下降7%，见底的量大概在每年6亿平方米左右。一线和热门二线城市的销售和价格确实稳住了，但这主要是因为放松限购，而不是基本面好转。问题是，除了北上深，全国已经没有限购了，政策空间非常有限。

ZH_A: 所以低线城市的压力还是很大。那政府会不会出大规模刺激政策？

ZH_B: 报告认为大概率不会。一个原因是，中国经济在房地产下行中依然表现出韧性，比如出口就很好，这可能是政府希望看到的“脱钩”。另一个原因是，过去几年支持政策的效果都是暂时的，没有改变大趋势。从性价比角度看，财政资金花在其他蓬勃发展的行业上可能更有效。所以，只要不出现断崖式下跌或系统性风险，政府可以容忍一个温和的调整。

ZH_A: 这个结论挺有启发的。听完你的分析，我大概明白了为什么报告更看好铝和铜。那最后，这份报告里有没有哪些问题，是你觉得特别想知道答案，但报告没有完全展开的？

ZH_B: 有的。比如，报告提到了铝材出口的强劲增长，但美国加征关税和全球贸易壁垒对这些出口的持续性会有多大影响？报告没有给出明确的量化分析。另一个是关于锂的中期供需。虽然报告指出2027年能见度低，但并没有给出一个具体的过剩量级预测。这些悬念，可能就需要我们自己去读完整的报告，或者等待下一次调研来解答了。

ZH_A: 好，那我们今天就先聊到这里。如果你想获取这份报告的完整版，或者加入我们的讨论社群，欢迎在节目详情页查看。感谢收听，我们下期再见。

EN_A: You just read this fascinating report on China's basic materials sector. What's the single biggest takeaway you got from it?

EN_B: The report has a clear pecking order. It prefers aluminum and copper over lithium, and lithium over steel. The main reason is that while property demand remains weak, manufacturing and exports are holding up. And on the supply side, aluminum and copper still face real constraints.

EN_A: Property is weak, we all know that. But what does "manufacturing and exports are holding up" actually look like? Give me a concrete example from the report.

EN_B: Take aluminum. The report is based on a recent field trip. They found that while domestic demand looks soft and social inventories are high, downstream fabricators are seeing huge order growth. April was 20 to 30 percent higher than previous years.

EN_A: Wait, soft domestic demand but strong orders for fabricators? That sounds contradictory. What's going on?

EN_B: That's exactly the question the report tries to answer. The high inventory might be a bit of a mirage. One reason is that downstream buyers are hesitant to build their own stockpiles because prices have gone up this year. But the more interesting reason is tighter tax inspections. Some "hidden" inventory held by traders is now showing up as visible social inventory.

EN_A: So the inventory isn't necessarily a sign of weak demand, it's just inventory being flushed out. What happens next for aluminum prices?

EN_B: The report is constructive. Two key factors. First, because of the price gap between China and the LME, there's a strong expectation for aluminum product exports to pick up and drain the inventory. The report cites data suggesting exports could hit over 650,000 tons per month, near record highs. Second, on the supply side, they saw smelter utilization rates actually drop in some provinces after their trip. That's reportedly due to government inspections on overproduction, especially from non-compliant capacity swaps.

EN_A: Capacity swaps? I thought there was a strict cap on aluminum capacity. How can there be overproduction?

EN_B: That's the nuance. Some producers started new potlines through capacity swaps before fully shutting down the old ones. That's what the inspections are targeting. So the supply ceiling is still intact, and it might even be getting tighter. That's why the report thinks the upcoming slow season could be different this year.

EN_A: So for aluminum, the logic is resilient demand plus constrained supply. What about copper? The report seems pretty bullish on that too.

EN_B: Copper is a well-loved commodity. The long-term bull case is unchanged. But the report finds that the recent price strength has been driven more by supply disruptions than by demand. For example, there was a worry about sulfur supply from the Middle East war affecting copper production. But their checks show big miners can afford to pay for supply, so the impact is minimal.

EN_A: So the supply story is still the main driver. What about the demand side? Everyone talks about EVs, grid, and AI.

EN_B: Right, the solid demand outlook from those sectors is consensus, but it hasn't really been priced in yet. The report points out that the price rally since late last year has been mostly about supply issues. And those issues aren't improving. Aging mines, production recovery from major disruptions taking time. Here's a bigger picture thought from the report: more countries are focusing on critical minerals and supply security. This could lead to a partial segmentation of the global market, like stockpiling in the US. Some expect this strategic importance to start playing a bigger role in pricing.

EN_A: That's a big, macro narrative. So where does that leave lithium and steel? The report seems less excited about those.

EN_B: Exactly. Lithium is ranked below aluminum and copper. The near-term supply-demand balance is okay, but the medium-term view is mixed. For example, companies they visited are confident about 2026 lithium demand, especially for energy storage, expecting at least 60% year-on-year growth. But visibility for 2027, especially the second half, is very low.

EN_A: Why is 2027 so uncertain?

EN_B: A couple of reasons. One is that 2026 ESS demand might be front-loaded. Another is that a surge in ESS projects could narrow the peak-valley price arbitrage for electricity tariffs, hurting project returns. On the supply side, the report confirms that Zimbabwe exports are recovering, and companies are actively restarting suspended mines. So even with the Jiangxi disruption, medium-term supply is set to increase.

EN_A: So near-term is fine, medium-term pressure is building. What about lithium prices? What did they hear on the ground?

EN_B: Most companies they visited believe the price is supported at 150,000 RMB per ton and acceptable to downstream. But the market needs to test the 200,000 level. The key concerns there are the impact on ESS returns from a cost perspective, and whether it triggers more mine restarts. The report also mentions sodium-ion batteries. They are becoming a reality, but volumes are small. Cost parity with lithium batteries is key, and that implies a long-term lithium price range of roughly 120,000 to 150,000 RMB.

EN_A: And finally, steel. The report uses the phrase "not turning around." That sounds pretty grim.

EN_B: It is. Demand is slowly declining, no dispute there. Property is still weak. Autos and home appliances lack highlights after the 2025 trade-in subsidies. Infrastructure can be supportive because 2026 is the first year of the 15th Five-Year Plan, but that's not enough to offset weakness elsewhere. On the supply side, discipline is a long-term fight. China just tightened capacity swap rules, but there's skepticism about how effective they will be.

EN_A: Why the skepticism? Isn't the government getting tougher?

EN_B: The report explains that the government's influence is mainly on state-owned enterprises. But most major consolidations among SOEs at the central and provincial levels are already done. Mergers among private companies are largely market-driven, and that takes time. So supply-side improvement won't happen quickly.

EN_A: That paints a pretty clear picture. Let me ask you about the elephant in the room. The report also covers property. What's the outlook there?

EN_B: They spoke with a national property agent. Their estimate is that residential GFA sales will still fall 7% in 2026. They think the bottom could be around 600 million square meters per year. Sales and prices have stabilized in top-tier cities, but that's mainly due to relaxed purchase restrictions, not a fundamental improvement. And since only Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen still have restrictions, there's not much policy room left.

EN_A: So lower-tier cities are still struggling. Does the report expect a massive stimulus to change that?

EN_B: No, they don't. The report's view is that China is actively trying to reduce its reliance on property. The economy has shown resilience despite the downturn, partly because exports are doing well. That might be what the government wants. Also, past support policies have only had temporary effects. From a cost-effectiveness perspective, the government might prefer to spend fiscal money on other booming industries. So, as long as there's no sharp acceleration in the decline or a systemic risk, a moderated correction can be tolerated.

EN_A: That's a really interesting conclusion. So, after hearing all this, I get why the report prefers aluminum and copper. One last thing: what questions did the report leave you with? What would you want to read the full report to find out?

EN_B: Definitely. One big one is about aluminum exports. The report is bullish on them, but how sustainable is that growth given rising trade barriers and tariffs? The report doesn't fully quantify that risk. Another is the medium-term lithium balance. The report says 2027 visibility is low, but it doesn't give a specific surplus or deficit forecast. Those are the kinds of details that the full report probably dives into.

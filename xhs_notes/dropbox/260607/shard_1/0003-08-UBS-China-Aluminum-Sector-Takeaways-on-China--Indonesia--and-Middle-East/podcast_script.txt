ZH_A: 最近市场对铝板块关注度很高，我们手头有一份某外资投行的最新研报，内容非常扎实。我想问问你，这份报告最核心的判断是什么？
ZH_B: 报告的核心判断是，全球铝供应格局正在经历一轮深刻的重塑，但方向并不一致。中国受限于产能天花板，增长非常缓慢；印尼虽然有大规模扩产计划，但实际落地面临很多执行风险；而中东地区则因为原料短缺和设施损坏，出现了超预期的供应中断。
ZH_A: 所以这其实是一个全球性的“此消彼长”的故事？
ZH_B: 可以这么理解。但关键在于，每个地区的“消”和“长”背后，逻辑完全不同。报告里重点分析了这三个地区，而且给出了很多我们平时看不到的细节。
ZH_A: 那我们先从中国开始。最近市场很担心中国铝产能过剩，这份报告怎么看？
ZH_B: 报告请了行业专家来解读。专家预计2026年中国铝产量是4550万吨，只比2025年增长2%。这个增速其实很慢。所谓的“过剩”，更多是结构性的。
ZH_A: 结构性的？具体是什么意思？
ZH_B: 主要有四个原因。第一，一些多年前建设的、没有拿到最终审批的产能，现在还在运行。第二，产能置换过程中，新产能先投产了，老产能却没关停，导致阶段性超产。第三，提高电流强度来增产的做法很有限，因为企业担心第二年考核压力大，还会缩短电解槽寿命。第四，一些小的技术升级确实提高了单产。
ZH_A: 那这些“不合规”的产能，政府会管吗？
ZH_B: 报告指出，地方政府因为税收贡献大，没有动力去强制关停。而中央层面，政策重心可能会转向通过碳合规来逐步“收编”这些产能。这意味着，未来的合规产能上限可能会从目前的4500万吨左右，提高到大约4600万吨，和实际运行产能基本对齐。
ZH_A: 这很有意思。那印尼呢？大家都很关注印尼的铝产能扩张。
ZH_B: 印尼的故事听起来很宏大，但执行风险非常高。专家预计2026年和2027年印尼分别能新增220万吨和200万吨的产能。但报告里列出了两大关键约束。
ZH_A: 哪两大约束？
ZH_B: 第一是税收优惠。很多冶炼厂还没拿到免税资格，包括进口关税、出口税和所得税。没有这些优惠，利润就被压缩了，企业不愿意卖。比如，Juwan和Adaro在2025年已经积累了大约10万吨的未销售库存。如果Adaro在2027年之前都拿不到批准，2026年可能还会再增加30万吨库存。
ZH_A: 所以产能是建起来了，但产品卖不出去？
ZH_B: 对，这是一个很现实的问题。第二个约束是电力和设备。电厂建设需要两年以上，中国的发电设备交货周期长达四年，电解槽也要6到12个月才能到货。这些都是硬瓶颈。
ZH_A: 那中东的情况呢？报告说那里出现了供应中断？
ZH_B: 是的，而且规模很大。报告预计中东2026年的铝产量会减少大约220万吨，受影响的产能高达350到380万吨。原因有两个：一是原料短缺，比如Alba、Jebel Ali和伊朗的产能都受到了影响，恢复需要3到6个月。二是更严重的设施损坏，比如Al Taweelah和Qatalum，恢复时间可能超过12个月。
ZH_A: 这意味着中东的供应缺口短期很难补上？
ZH_B: 没错。而且物流也受限，红海和海湾港口的氧化铝进口只能维持部分运营，铝锭出口也受到限制。专家估计，自中断以来，该地区的库存已经累积到了110到120万吨。
ZH_A: 听完这三个地区的分析，我最大的感受是：全球铝的供应弹性其实比市场想象的要低很多。
ZH_B: 完全同意。中国的产能天花板是硬的，印尼的产能增长是“纸面上”的，而中东的供应中断又是实实在在的。这份报告的价值就在于，它把这些复杂的因素拆解得非常清楚。

EN_A: We’ve been getting a lot of questions about the aluminum sector lately. I have a new report from a global investment bank here. What’s the single biggest takeaway?
EN_B: The main takeaway is that the global aluminum supply picture is undergoing a significant shift, but not in a uniform way. China’s growth is capped and very slow. Indonesia has big expansion plans, but faces serious execution risks. And the Middle East is seeing unexpected and large-scale supply disruptions.
EN_A: So it’s a story of regional divergence?
EN_B: Exactly. And the logic behind each region is completely different. The report dives deep into these three areas and gives a lot of granular detail you don’t usually see.
EN_A: Let’s start with China. There’s been a lot of concern about overproduction there. What does the report say?
EN_B: The report brought in an industry expert. The expert estimates China’s aluminum output will reach 45.5 million tons in 2026, only a 2% increase from 2025. That’s a very slow pace. The so-called ‘overproduction’ is more structural.
EN_A: Structural? What does that mean specifically?
EN_B: There are four main reasons. First, some old, non-compliant capacity built years ago is still running. Second, capacity swaps aren’t synchronized, so new capacity starts before old capacity shuts down. Third, operators are cautious about pushing amperage too high, because it hurts performance targets next year and shortens pot life. Fourth, incremental upgrades have slightly boosted productivity.
EN_A: So what does this mean for government regulation?
EN_B: The report points out that local governments have little incentive to enforce cuts because these plants generate significant tax revenue. At the central level, the policy focus may gradually shift toward formalizing this legacy capacity through carbon compliance. That means the authorized capacity ceiling could eventually rise to around 46 million tons, which is roughly in line with current operating levels.
EN_A: That’s a fascinating insight. What about Indonesia? Everyone is watching the capacity ramp there.
EN_B: Indonesia’s story sounds big, but execution risk is very high. The expert expects 2.2 million tons of new capacity in 2026 and 2 million in 2027. But the report highlights two major constraints.
EN_A: What are they?
EN_B: First, tax incentives. Many smelters haven’t secured tax-exempt status on import duties, export taxes, and corporate income tax. Without those, margins are squeezed, so they don’t want to sell. For example, Juwan and Adaro have already built up about 100,000 tons of unsold inventory in 2025. If Adaro doesn’t get approval through 2027, that could add another 300,000 tons of inventory in 2026.
EN_A: So capacity is being built, but the product can’t be sold profitably?
EN_B: Exactly. It’s a very real problem. The second constraint is power and equipment. Power plants take over two years to build, delivery of Chinese generating equipment can take four years, and pot delivery takes six to twelve months. These are hard bottlenecks.
EN_A: And the Middle East? The report mentions supply disruptions there.
EN_B: Yes, and on a large scale. The report estimates Middle East aluminum output will decline by about 2.2 million tons in 2026, with 3.5 to 3.8 million tons of capacity affected. The disruptions come from two sources: raw material shortages at sites like Alba and Jebel Ali, which need 3 to 6 months to restart, and more severe facility damage at Al Taweelah and Qatalum, which could take over 12 months to recover.
EN_A: So the supply gap from the Middle East won’t be filled quickly?
EN_B: That’s right. Logistics are also constrained. Limited alumina inflows through the Red Sea and Gulf ports only support partial operations, and aluminum exports are restricted. The expert estimates regional inventories have built up to 1.1 to 1.2 million tons since the disruptions began.

# The Capex Supercycle Demands a New Valuation Lens: Why EV/EBITDA Now Matters More Than P/E for Medium-Term Investors

The U.S. equity market is entering a capital expenditure supercycle of historic proportions, and the valuation tools that served investors well during the past decade are losing their signal strength. Aggregate S&P 500 capex is projected to grow 40% year-over-year in 2026, the fastest pace in at least 35 years, and is on track to absorb roughly 50% of operating cash flows. This is not merely a cyclical uptick. It is a structural shift that places the current investment environment squarely alongside the telecom and internet buildout of the mid-to-late 1990s and the energy and housing boom of the mid-2000s. For investors with a multi-year horizon, the central question is no longer whether AI-driven spending will generate returns, but how to price the risk that even good investment can overshoot.

The answer lies in rethinking which valuation metric deserves primacy. During periods of elevated capex, the price-to-earnings ratio becomes a less reliable guide to medium-term returns, because it is distorted by leverage, tax shields, and depreciation policies that shift as companies ramp up spending. Enterprise value to EBITDA, by contrast, more directly reflects what investors are paying for the operating asset base, and it aligns naturally with the multi-year timeline of a capex cycle. Historical evidence from the last three decades shows that during high-capex regimes, EV/EBITDA sorting exhibits a stronger linear relationship with forward three-year total returns than P/E does, and the cheap-expensive return spread delivered by EV/EBITDA is wider. This is not an academic observation. It is a practical signal that should shape portfolio construction today.

The immediate implication is that investors who rely on P/E alone to judge market or sector valuations may be underestimating the risks embedded in current prices. S&P 500 EV/EBITDA is currently more elevated than P/E when compared to its own long-run history, and that gap matters. The companies undertaking the bulk of the spending, the hyperscalers and chipmakers in Communication Services, Technology, and Discretionary sectors, are also the ones whose enterprise values have expanded most dramatically. Their balance sheets are being redeployed as capex commitments rise, with net debt to EBITDA shifting from negative territory to positive, and capex to EBITDA climbing above 1x for the first time in years. The margin for error is narrowing, and the burden of proof is shifting from narrative to earnings delivery.

This does not mean a bearish call on U.S. equities over the next twelve months. The near-term earnings backdrop remains supportive, particularly within the broader Tech complex, and valuations have yet to retake recent median levels. The EV/EBITDA signal has limited timing power over horizons of one year or less. But for investors managing capital with a three- to five-year lens, the current setup demands greater selectivity and a willingness to look past headline multiples. The capex supercycle is creating winners and losers that P/E alone cannot distinguish. EV/EBITDA offers a more honest picture of what is being paid for the underlying asset base, and it should become a standard reference point in any medium-term equity strategy discussion.

## The Scale and Concentration of This Capex Cycle Are Unprecedented, and History Warns That Even Productive Investment Can Overshoot

The numbers are striking. Aggregate S&P 500 capex is projected to grow 40% year-over-year in 2026, the fastest pace in at least 35 years. TMT capex as a share of total S&P 500 capex is projected to approach 50% this year, a step change even relative to the late-2010s public cloud buildout. To put that in context, during the peak of the 1990s telecom buildout, TMT capex never exceeded 30% of the total. The current cycle is not just larger in absolute terms; it is far more concentrated in a handful of sectors. Communication Services is growing capex at 75% year-over-year, Technology at 65%, and Discretionary at 48%. The rest of the market, from Energy to Materials to Healthcare, is growing at single-digit rates or declining.

This concentration has two important consequences. First, it means that the fate of the broader market is increasingly tied to the investment decisions of a small group of companies whose spending is driven by a single technological bet: that AI infrastructure will generate long-run returns. Second, it means that when the inevitable questions about over-investment arise, the adjustment will be concentrated in the very sectors that have driven market returns. History offers a sobering parallel. The 1990s telecom buildout ultimately proved productivity-enhancing, but it also led to a period of painful consolidation and value destruction that lasted years. The mid-2000s energy and housing boom followed a similar pattern. In both cases, the payoff was long-dated, and the interim period was marked by significant drawdowns for investors who paid peak multiples.

The key insight is not that this cycle will end the same way. The underlying technology may be more transformative, and the companies involved are generally more disciplined than their predecessors. But the structural dynamics are similar. When capex intensity reaches these levels, the marginal return on invested capital becomes the critical variable, and it is inherently difficult to forecast. The risk is not that AI fails, but that the scale of investment overshoots the pace of adoption, creating a period of excess capacity and falling returns. That is the scenario that EV/EBITDA is better equipped to capture than P/E, because it reflects the full cost of the asset base, including the debt used to finance it, rather than just the earnings generated after depreciation.

## EV/EBITDA Gains Signal Strength During High-Capex Regimes Because It Filters Out the Distortions That P/E Embeds

The empirical case is straightforward. Over the last 30 years, during periods when trailing twelve-month capex-to-EBITDA was above the median, sorting the S&P 500 into quintiles by EV/EBITDA produced a monotonic decline in forward three-year total returns from the cheapest to the richest quintile. The same monotonicity breaks down for P/E, where mid-quintiles tend to outperform the tails. This pattern suggests that P/E embeds cycle-specific convexities that distort its predictive power when investment spending is elevated. The cheap-expensive return spread delivered by EV/EBITDA sorting is also wider, by a margin of roughly 100 percentage points versus 60 percentage points for P/E during high-capex periods.

Why does this happen? The explanation is structural, not statistical. When a company is in the middle of a major capex program, its earnings are depressed by higher depreciation and, in some cases, by increased interest expense if it uses debt to finance the spending. P/E, which divides price by earnings, mechanically rises even if the underlying business is unchanged, because the denominator shrinks. EV/EBITDA, by contrast, adds back depreciation and excludes the effects of capital structure, giving a clearer picture of what the market is paying for the operating cash flows generated by the asset base. For a multi-year investor, this distinction matters because the payoff from capex typically materializes over three to five years, which is exactly the horizon over which EV/EBITDA sorting shows its strongest relationship with returns.

The practical implication for today is that the current elevation in EV/EBITDA relative to P/E should be taken seriously. As of late May 2026, trailing twelve-month EV/EBITDA for the S&P 500 stood at roughly 16.6x, while P/E was around 28.2x. Both are high by historical standards, but the gap between them is wider than normal, and it is being driven by the sectors that are spending the most. Technology Hardware, Capital Goods, and Semiconductors all rank in the 80th to 100th percentile of their own history for EV/EBITDA, while their P/E ranks are similar but not identical. This suggests that the market is pricing in a significant premium for the future cash flows these assets are expected to generate, and that premium leaves less room for error.

## The Signal Is Inherently Medium-Term, and Near-Term Conditions Remain Constructive, Creating a Strategic Tension for Investors

It is important to be precise about what the EV/EBITDA signal does and does not imply. Over shorter horizons, including one year or less, the relationship between EV/EBITDA and forward returns is much less significant. This makes intuitive sense. Capex cycles take time to play out, and the market often continues to reward spending during the early and middle stages, even as valuations become stretched. The signal becomes most useful when the cycle matures and the gap between expectations and reality becomes apparent. That is why the current EV/EBITDA elevation argues for selectivity and caution for multi-year investors, not for an immediate shift to a defensive posture.

The near-term setup, in fact, remains broadly supportive. Next-twelve-month earnings estimates are still rising, particularly within the broader Tech complex, and valuations have not yet retaken the median levels seen earlier in the cycle. The combination of rising EPS expectations, strong secular growth in AI-related end markets, and a still-favorable macroeconomic backdrop leaves the near-term outlook constructive. The tension is between the short-term momentum and the medium-term risk. Investors who ignore the EV/EBITDA signal entirely risk being caught offside when the cycle turns, while those who overreact to it risk missing further upside in the near term.

This is not a contradiction. It is a recognition that different valuation tools are suited to different time horizons, and that the current environment requires a dual-lens approach. For tactical allocation over the next six to twelve months, P/E and earnings momentum remain relevant. For strategic positioning over three to five years, EV/EBITDA offers a more reliable guide to the risks embedded in current prices. The challenge is that most institutional frameworks are built around the former, not the latter, and the shift in emphasis required by the capex supercycle is not yet reflected in mainstream portfolio construction.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Beneficiaries and Spenders, and the Timing of the Inflection

The analysis raises two questions that the available data does not fully resolve. First, the current elevation in EV/EBITDA is being shaped as much by the beneficiaries of AI capex, such as technology hardware and semiconductor companies that sell into the buildout, as by the companies undertaking the spending itself. This creates a compositional effect that complicates the signal. The beneficiaries may be able to sustain higher multiples because their returns on capital are improving in real time, while the spenders may face a different trajectory. The report does not provide a clear framework for distinguishing between these two groups within the EV/EBITDA lens, and that distinction is critical for stock selection.

Second, the timing of the inflection point remains uncertain. History suggests that capex peaks tend to coincide with late-cycle dynamics, but the current cycle is unusual in that it is being driven by a single technological theme rather than by broad economic expansion. Consensus estimates call for a deceleration in spending growth sometime in 2027 or 2028, but the market may begin to price that deceleration well before it materializes. The EV/EBITDA signal can indicate that valuations are elevated, but it cannot pinpoint when the reversion will occur. For that, investors need to incorporate additional inputs, including capacity utilization rates, customer adoption curves, and the competitive dynamics within individual industries.

These are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are open questions that any serious investor should grapple with. The value of the EV/EBITDA framework is that it forces those questions to the surface, rather than allowing them to be obscured by the comfort of a single, familiar multiple. The next step is to build a decision framework that incorporates both the signal and its limitations.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Capex Supercycle

For investors who accept the premise that EV/EBITDA gains signal strength during high-capex regimes, the logical next step is to translate that insight into a structured approach to portfolio construction. The framework has three layers.

First, at the macro level, use the aggregate EV/EBITDA of the S&P 500 as a strategic risk gauge. When the metric is above the 75th percentile of its historical range, as it is today, reduce the weight of tactical bets that depend on multiple expansion and increase the emphasis on earnings delivery. This does not mean reducing equity exposure mechanically, but it does mean being more disciplined about entry points and more willing to take profits on positions that have benefited from valuation re-rating rather than fundamental improvement.

Second, at the sector level, compare EV/EBITDA ranks to P/E ranks to identify where the valuation signal is most divergent. Sectors where EV/EBITDA is significantly more elevated than P/E, such as Technology Hardware and Capital Goods, warrant closer scrutiny. The premium embedded in enterprise value may be justified by future cash flows, but it also implies a lower margin for error. Conversely, sectors where EV/EBITDA is less elevated than P/E, such as Software and Services, may offer more attractive risk-reward profiles, because the market is not pricing in the same level of optimism about future asset returns.

Third, at the stock level, use EV/EBITDA as a cross-check on any position with a multi-year holding period. For companies that are undertaking significant capex, compare their EV/EBITDA to their historical range and to their peer group. If the multiple is elevated relative to both, ask whether the expected return on invested capital is high enough to justify the premium over a three- to five-year horizon. If the answer is uncertain, reduce position size or require a wider margin of safety before adding.

This framework does not replace traditional valuation analysis. It supplements it, by adding a dimension that is specifically relevant to the current environment. The capex supercycle is not a temporary phenomenon. It is a structural shift that will shape corporate returns and market outcomes for years. The valuation tools that worked in the past may not be sufficient for the future, and the investors who adapt early will have a significant advantage.

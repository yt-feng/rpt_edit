# AI Infrastructure Demand is Not Cyclical — It is Structural, and Greater China Hardware is the Primary Beneficiary

The dominant narrative in technology hardware investing has shifted from whether AI spending will continue to how deep and wide the infrastructure buildout will extend. A detailed investor presentation on Greater China technology hardware makes a clear case: the AI capex cycle is not moderating. It is broadening. The report, focused on Taiwan and select China-based hardware suppliers, argues that the current valuation disconnect between earnings growth potential and market pricing creates a compelling opportunity for investors willing to look beyond near-term noise. The central thesis is that AI infrastructure demand is transitioning from a concentrated buildout among a few hyperscalers to a more distributed, multi-generational investment cycle that will sustain demand for components, interconnects, testing equipment, and mechanical systems through at least 2027.

What makes this moment strategically important is the convergence of multiple technology transitions happening simultaneously. Network switches are upgrading to higher speeds. Data interconnects are moving from copper to optical solutions. Power density in server racks is increasing dramatically, requiring new testing and thermal management capabilities. And the industrial automation cycle is showing early signs of a broad-based recovery that complements the AI-driven demand. The report identifies specific companies positioned to capture these trends, but more importantly, it provides a framework for understanding why the hardware supply chain is entering a period of sustained, structural growth rather than a typical cyclical upswing.

## The AI Infrastructure Buildout is Entering Its Second Phase, and It is More Capital Intensive Than the First

The first phase of AI infrastructure investment was characterized by hyperscalers building out GPU clusters at unprecedented scale. That phase is not ending, but it is evolving. The report indicates that the next phase will be defined by three concurrent developments: the ramp of next-generation GPU platforms, the expansion of ASIC-based AI servers, and the geographical dispersion of data center capacity. Each of these developments requires more hardware per unit of compute, not less.

Consider the implications for network switches. The report highlights that network switches will be the major revenue driver for key suppliers in 2026, with AI accelerator revenue picking up in the second half of the year. This is not merely about volume growth. It is about a structural upgrade cycle. As AI training clusters grow larger, the network fabric connecting them must become faster and more efficient. The transition from 400G to 800G switch architectures, and eventually to 1.6T, represents a multi-year upgrade cycle that benefits suppliers of high-speed switches, optical transceivers, and the underlying components.

The same logic applies to data interconnects. The report notes that copper data interconnects will continue growing on increasing adoption among customers and specification upgrades, while power interconnects will be driven by server shipment growth and higher dollar content from next-generation GPU platforms. This is a critical insight. The content per server is increasing, not just the number of servers. For investors, this means that even if server shipment growth moderates, revenue growth for component suppliers could accelerate due to higher value per unit.

What the report does not fully answer is how quickly the optical transition will occur. While copper interconnects remain dominant for short-reach applications within data centers, optical solutions are expected to gain more traction in 2027. The report mentions that optical offerings will benefit from enhanced capability and customer relationships from recent acquisitions, but the timing and magnitude of revenue contribution remain uncertain. This creates a meaningful analytical question: will the optical transition happen faster than expected as bandwidth demands outpace copper's physical limitations, or will copper maintain its position longer due to cost and reliability advantages? The answer has significant implications for which suppliers benefit most in the 2027-2028 timeframe.

## The Testing and Measurement Segment is a Hidden Lever on AI Infrastructure Spending

One of the most underappreciated aspects of the AI infrastructure buildout is the testing and measurement requirement. As server rack power density increases, the need for power testing equipment grows disproportionately. The report identifies power testing as a key growth driver, riding on the increasing power density of server racks and capacity expansions among major customers.

This is not a niche opportunity. Every new GPU platform requires a new testing cycle. Every increase in rack power density requires validation. Every transition to advanced packaging requires new metrology tools. The report outlines multiple growth drivers in the semiconductor and photonics testing business, including new GPU system-level testing cycles, metrology tools for advanced packaging, photonics testing for transceivers and co-packaged optics, and potential revenue from final test handlers and burn-in ovens.

The strategic insight here is that testing equipment has a different demand profile than component manufacturing. While component demand can be lumpy and dependent on specific product cycles, testing demand is more persistent. Each new generation of technology requires more testing, not less. The report's price target for the testing equipment supplier implies significant upside, based on a valuation that already reflects strong growth expectations. The question for investors is whether the testing opportunity is fully priced in or whether the market is underestimating the duration and breadth of the testing cycle.

The report also raises an important question about the competitive dynamics in testing. As AI chips become more complex and specialized, testing requirements become more customized. This favors suppliers with deep engineering capabilities and close customer relationships. But it also means that the barriers to entry are rising, which could lead to consolidation or pricing power for established players. The report does not explicitly address this competitive dynamic, but it is a logical implication of the trends it describes.

## The Industrial Automation Recovery Adds a Second Growth Engine Beyond AI

While AI infrastructure dominates the narrative, the report identifies a separate but complementary growth driver: the industrial automation cycle. The report describes the current phase as the "early innings of a broad-based upcycle," supported by both top-down indicators such as manufacturing PMIs and bottom-up data from industry players.

This is strategically important because it diversifies the investment thesis. If AI spending were to moderate unexpectedly, the industrial automation recovery could provide a buffer. Conversely, if the automation cycle accelerates, it could amplify the overall growth trajectory for companies exposed to both trends. The report specifically highlights that technology sectors are continuing to bolster demand, including semiconductors, components, and assembly, while battery orders in China remain solid.

The report identifies two companies in the automation space that are well-positioned: one benefiting from share gains and incremental contribution from linear guides, and the other seeing margin expansions supported by higher utilization and price hikes. Both are rated overweight with significant upside to current price targets based on forward earnings multiples that are below historical averages.

The analytical question this raises is whether the automation recovery is sustainable or whether it is a temporary cyclical bounce. The report's framework suggests it is structural, driven by technology sector demand and China's battery manufacturing buildout. But investors should consider the risk that a global economic slowdown could delay or diminish the automation recovery. The report's price targets assume a continued recovery, and any deviation from that path would affect the investment case.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Positioning

Based on the report's analysis, investors should evaluate their hardware exposure through three lenses. First, is the company's revenue growth driven by volume or by content per unit? Companies that benefit from increasing content per server, such as those supplying higher-value interconnects, switches, or testing equipment, have more durable growth profiles than those dependent on unit volume growth alone.

Second, does the company have exposure to multiple technology transitions simultaneously? The report identifies companies that benefit from network upgrades, power infrastructure expansion, testing requirements, and the automation recovery. Companies with diversified exposure across these trends have lower risk of being disrupted by a single technology shift.

Third, what is the valuation relative to the growth duration? The report provides price targets based on forward P/E multiples that are below historical averages for many of the companies it covers. This suggests that the market is not fully pricing in the multi-year growth opportunity. However, investors should consider whether the growth is sustainable beyond 2027, when some of the current technology transitions may mature.

The report does not fully answer the question of what happens after the current investment cycle peaks. It implicitly assumes that AI infrastructure spending will continue to grow, but it does not provide a framework for when or how the cycle might end. This is the most important unresolved question for long-term investors. The current opportunity is compelling, but the duration of the cycle is the key variable that will determine whether today's investments generate attractive returns.

## The KOSPI’s Correction Was Caused by Mechanical Flows, Not Earnings Deterioration

Three distinct flow factors drove the 27% peak-to-trough decline, and none of them reflected a change in the underlying earnings power of Korean companies. First, foreign investors sold KRW158 trillion year-to-date, driven by portfolio risk-management rules. When an individual stock’s weighting exceeds a position limit—commonly 10% of a fund—or when Korea’s weighting in the MSCI EM benchmark rises beyond a portfolio’s target allocation, institutional investors are forced to reduce exposure. This mechanical selling intensified at two key thresholds: when the KOSPI reached 7,500 in mid-May and again when it hit 9,000 in late June. The selling was not discretionary; it was algorithmic rebalancing.

Second, institutional support from domestic pension funds weakened. The National Pension Service increased its domestic equity allocation from 14.9% to 20.8%, but that allocation has now reached practical limits relative to its overall fund size. The NPS cannot keep buying at the same rate, and its reduced marginal demand removes a stabilizing force that had previously absorbed retail and foreign volatility.

Third, the rapid growth of leveraged ETFs and newly launched single-stock leveraged products created a volatility feedback loop. Korea’s total ETF market holds approximately KRW450 trillion in AUM, of which leveraged ETFs account for KRW27 trillion, or roughly 6% of the total. Retail investors hold an estimated 85% of these leveraged ETF positions, and their returns have been significant: leveraged ETFs have lost 53% since the KOSPI peaked on 22 June, compared to the KOSPI’s 22% decline over the same period. The South Korean government announced a first round of measures to tighten investment qualifications for these products on 16 July, and we expect further restrictions in the second half of 2026. The forced liquidations of retail leveraged positions (Figure 11 in the original report) have added to the downward pressure, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that is now nearing its end.

Corporate fundamentals have not changed. The KOSPI’s 2026F ROE is 27%, and its P/E of 7.0x implies the market is pricing in a permanent impairment of earnings that the data does not support. The correction was a flow event, not a fundamental one.

## Corporate Buybacks Will Become the Dominant Source of Equity Demand, Replacing Foreign and Institutional Flows

The most important structural shift in the Korean equity market over the next three years is the explosion in corporate share buybacks. The buyback ratio—buybacks as a percentage of net profit—jumps from 8.8% in 2025 to 13.5% in 2026F, 23.7% in 2027F, and 26.0% in 2028F.

Approximately 90% of this buyback activity will come from the two large semiconductor companies, which together account for the bulk of KOSPI net profit and free cash flow. This is not a one-time event. The buyback programs are structural, driven by the companies’ own capital allocation policies and supported by government pressure for improved shareholder returns.

The implications for market demand are straightforward. Foreign investors sold KRW158 trillion in the first half of 2026. If our buyback estimates are correct, corporate buybacks alone will absorb KRW116 trillion in the second half of 2026, KRW274 trillion in 2027, and KRW328 trillion in 2028. This is not a replacement of foreign flows; it is a superior replacement, because corporate buybacks are price-insensitive in the short term and accretive to earnings per share over the long term. A company buying back its own stock at 7.0x P/E and 1.7x P/BV is making a capital allocation decision that directly increases the value of remaining shares. Foreign selling, by contrast, is mechanical and often unrelated to valuation.

The risk to this view is that buybacks fall short of our estimates. If the semiconductor companies delay or reduce their programs, the demand shortfall would be significant. But the direction is clear: Korean companies are moving from a regime of negligible buybacks to one where buybacks become a material source of equity demand.

## Leveraged ETF Deleveraging Is a Temporary Reset, Not a Structural Impairment

The most misunderstood element of the current correction is the role of leveraged ETFs. Many observers interpret the 53% decline in leveraged ETF returns as evidence of systemic risk or retail speculation run amok. In reality, it is a mechanical consequence of a product structure that amplifies both upside and downside. The leveraged ETF market in Korea is KRW27 trillion, or 6% of total ETF AUM. Retail investors hold 85% of these positions. When the KOSPI fell 22%, leveraged ETFs designed to deliver 2x daily returns fell by approximately 53%, as compounding and volatility decay magnified losses.

The government’s response—restricting investment qualifications for single-stock leveraged ETFs—is appropriate and likely to be followed by further tightening in the second half of 2026. The forced liquidations of retail margin positions (Figure 11) have been notable, but they are a reset, not a permanent loss of market capacity. Once the deleveraging is complete, the retail investor base will re-enter the market with lower leverage and a healthier risk profile. The KOSPI volatility index at 96.9 was an extreme reading that reflected market sentiment, not a structural change in market risk.

The key insight is that leveraged ETF deleveraging is a one-time event. It does not change the long-term earnings trajectory of Korean companies, nor does it alter the calculus for corporate buybacks. If anything, the lower share prices created by forced selling have made buybacks more attractive for corporate treasurers. The market is effectively transferring shares from forced sellers to corporate buyers at current prices.

## Policy Catalysts in the Second Half of 2026 Will Reinforce the Shift Toward Shareholder Returns

The Korean government has signaled a series of policy measures in the second half of 2026 that will directly support the buyback-driven re-rating thesis. The most important is the publication of a low-PBR company list, expected around November 2026. This list will identify companies with price-to-book ratios below one and will encourage them to cancel treasury shares, increase dividends, and dispose of non-core assets. For a market where many large-cap companies trade at P/BV of 1.7x despite 27% ROE, the low-PBR list is a direct catalyst for corporate action.

We also expect stricter duplicate-listing rules and KOSDAQ reform. These measures are designed to improve shareholder protection, reduce parent-company discounts, and strengthen overall market quality. The tax reform proposal announced in late July 2026 includes higher tax incentives for long-term domestic equity investment through ISAs and pension accounts, reforms to inheritance and gift tax that reduce controlling shareholders’ incentive to suppress share prices, and enhanced tax credits for AI, semiconductors, and other strategic industries.

Taken together, these policies create a regulatory environment that rewards the same behaviors we are already seeing from large-cap companies: buybacks, treasury-share cancellation, and improved capital efficiency. The government is not just encouraging shareholder returns; it is building a framework that encourages companies to act.

The risk is that the low-PBR list becomes a compliance exercise rather than a catalyst for change. If companies simply disclose low P/BV without committing to specific actions, the market impact will be limited. But the combination of government enforcement, activist campaigns, and the sheer scale of buyback programs from the semiconductor sector suggests that this cycle is different from previous attempts at corporate governance reform in Korea.

## Decision Framework: How to Position for the Buyback-Driven Re-Rating

For those observing Korean equities at current levels, the key question is not whether the market is attractively valued—it is, by many conventional metrics—but whether the structural drivers of demand are sufficient to overcome the mechanical selling that has dominated the past two months. We believe they are, and we offer the following framework for consideration.

First, focus on companies with large and executable buyback programs. The two semiconductor companies are the obvious candidates, but the buyback wave will extend to other large-cap names as the low-PBR list and tax reforms take effect. Companies with strong free cash flow, low leverage, and a stated commitment to shareholder returns are the most likely to benefit.

Second, monitor the pace of leveraged ETF deleveraging and forced liquidations. The KOSPI volatility index at 96.9 was an extreme reading, and as forced selling subsides, the market should stabilize. The government’s tightening of leveraged ETF qualifications will reduce the velocity of future volatility spikes, even if it does not eliminate them entirely.

Third, watch foreign flows as a secondary signal, not a primary driver. Foreign investors will not return to Korea in force until the KOSPI’s composition or valuation changes the risk-reward for global mandates. But the absence of foreign buying is not a reason to avoid the market if corporate buybacks are large enough to absorb the selling. Our estimates suggest they are.

Fourth, consider the downside risks explicitly. Our buyback estimates could prove too optimistic if the semiconductor companies delay their programs. Foreign selling could accelerate if the KOSPI rises above 7,500–9,000 without an upgrade to earnings or ROE, as mechanical rebalancing would resume. Retail investors could rebuild leverage too quickly, creating the conditions for another volatility event. These risks are real, but they are reflected in the current valuation. At 7.0x 2026F P/E and 1.7x P/BV, the market is discounting significant uncertainty.

The Korean equity market is in the middle of a transition from a liquidity-and-leverage-driven rally to a fundamental recalibration supported by corporate buybacks and government policy. The correction from 9,115 to 6,691 was notable, but it has reset expectations and created entry points that are rare in developed markets. The next re-rating will be slower, more structural, and more sustainable than the one that preceded it.

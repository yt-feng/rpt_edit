EN_A: So what is the big question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It wants to figure out which companies are genuinely vulnerable when currencies swing, which ones are just seeing a translation effect on paper, and which ones might be confusing a currency tailwind with an actual improvement in their business.
EN_A: What is the first key insight?
EN_B: The report maps out currency exposure for each company. For example, Jeronimo Martins gets over 70 percent of its revenue from foreign currencies, mainly the Polish zloty and the Colombian peso. Ahold Delhaize has about 60 percent of its revenue in US dollars. Carrefour gets roughly 20 percent of its revenue from Brazil.
EN_A: What does that mean? Is big exposure always bad news?
EN_B: Not necessarily. The key is whether it is a translation effect or a real economic impact. Take Ahold Delhaize. A stronger dollar will mechanically boost its euro-denominated earnings. But if its US volumes and margins are shrinking, that currency tailwind can mask a weakening underlying business. So investors need to separate the two.
EN_A: So you cannot just look at the headline FX impact. What is the second insight?
EN_B: The report shows that the link between currencies and stock prices is very different across companies. Carrefour’s share price is highly correlated with the Brazilian real against the euro. That is partly because Brazil contributes a third of its profit, but also because the real itself acts as a proxy for Brazilian macro risk.
EN_A: What does that mean for investors?
EN_B: It means you have to be careful not to confuse a currency-driven stock rally with a fundamental turnaround. The report warns that Carrefour’s cash and carry business in Brazil is still facing structural competition. Volumes and margins are likely to stay capped, even if the currency is moving in the right direction.
EN_A: What about Ahold Delhaize? Its dollar exposure is huge. Does the stock move with the dollar?
EN_B: Surprisingly, the report finds that the correlation is weaker than you might expect. Investors tend to focus more on Ahold’s defensive fundamentals and stable cash returns rather than short-term currency swings. That is another case where the surface-level link does not tell the full story.
EN_A: Are there any questions the report leaves open that are worth exploring?

For example, Carrefour operates in Argentina, which is classified as a hyperinflationary economy. How much protection does hedging really provide there? The full report goes deeper into that.
EN_A: What is the other open question?
EN_B: The report lists companies with almost no FX exposure, like Colruyt, Domino‘s and Greggs. But it does not fully answer whether that lack of exposure gives them a competitive advantage when the rest of the sector is being tossed around by currencies.

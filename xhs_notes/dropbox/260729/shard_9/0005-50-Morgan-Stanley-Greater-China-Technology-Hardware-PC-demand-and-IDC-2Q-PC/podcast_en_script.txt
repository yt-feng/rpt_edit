EN_A: So there is this research report from a global investment bank that digs into the PC and server supply chain. What was your biggest takeaway?
EN_B: The PC market is going through a strange phase where volumes are shrinking but prices are rising. Meanwhile, servers are in a completely different world — supply is still running way behind demand, and we are seeing one of the tightest supply environments in history.
EN_A: Shrinking volumes but rising prices? That sounds contradictory. How is that possible?
EN_B: The report cites IDC data. PC shipments in Q2 were 66.2 million units, down 5 percent year on year. That is the first decline in nine quarters. But Intel reported a 15 percent revenue increase in its client segment, driven entirely by higher average selling prices and a richer product mix.
EN_A: So it is not demand pulling the market up. It is vendors pushing up prices.
EN_B: Exactly. And the ODM makers are prioritizing high-end models. What this means is the PC market is splitting — low-end shipments are shrinking, while high-end products are propping up revenue and margins. But the report does not fully answer how long this pricing power can last.
EN_A: And what about the server side? You said supply is still behind demand.
EN_B: Intel said on the earnings call that the industry is facing one of the most severe supply constraints in its history. Logic, wafers, memory, substrates — everything is tight. And for the first time, Intel admitted it has made prepayments to substrate suppliers to secure capacity.
EN_A: Prepayments? That is a big signal.
EN_B: It is. The report says this confirms the supply environment has tightened further in the past three months. Substrate suppliers are in a seller's market and can keep raising prices. That is why the report lists several substrate-related companies as key beneficiaries.
EN_A: What about AI servers? Did the report say anything about the CPU to GPU ratio?
EN_B: There is a striking data point. Intel now says the CPU to GPU ratio is near one to one. Last quarter, for inference workloads, they suggested it was one to three or four. That shift is huge. It implies general server demand could be much stronger than expected.
EN_A: So server ODM makers could see another year of high growth in 2027?
EN_B: The report estimates 20 to 30 percent plus year-on-year growth, driven more by supply than demand. But here is the question the report does not fully answer — if supply bottlenecks do not ease, how much of that growth can actually be realized?
EN_A: So the real question this report is trying to answer is — in a world where PCs are weak and servers are hot, which parts of the supply chain have real pricing power and structural opportunity?
EN_B: Exactly. It gives a clear framework. On the PC side, watch for premiumization and pricing power. On the server side, focus on substrates, ABF carriers, and connectors — the bottleneck parts. The report also lists specific companies and their ratings.
EN_A: Are there any questions the report leaves open?
EN_B: Two. First, how long can PC price increases hold? If demand drops further and channel inventory builds up, prices could come under pressure. Second, will prepayments for substrates become the new normal? That could change supplier cash flows and bargaining power. Those details are in the full report.

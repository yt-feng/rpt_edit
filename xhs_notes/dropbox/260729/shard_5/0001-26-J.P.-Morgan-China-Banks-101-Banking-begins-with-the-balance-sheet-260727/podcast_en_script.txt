EN_A: I just read a report called “China Banks 101.” It sounds like a textbook for beginners. What did you make of it?
EN_B: It really is a textbook-style piece. It tries to answer one very basic but core question — how do banks actually make money, and what’s changing in their profitability when you look at the data.
EN_A: So what’s the real question this report is asking? It’s not just about which bank stock to buy, right?
EN_B: Exactly. It’s more like: when you look at a bank’s financials, which metrics actually matter? The report starts with the balance sheet basics, then uses a DuPont analysis to break down how banks in different Asian markets generate returns.
EN_A: DuPont analysis sounds complicated. Can you explain it in one sentence?
EN_B: Sure. It breaks down a bank’s return on equity into pieces — is it making money from interest margins, from fees, or by controlling costs? The report compares eleven markets from China to India, Indonesia to Australia.
EN_A: So what’s the first big insight?
EN_B: Net interest margins are shrinking across the board. Take China — it dropped from nearly 2% in 2017 to a projected 1.47% by 2025. That means banks have less room to profit from lending.
EN_A: So what do banks do then? They can’t just sit still.
EN_B: That leads to the second insight. Banks are shifting toward non-interest income. In China, that share went from 30% in 2017 to a projected 29% in 2025 — not a huge jump, but the trend is clear. Taiwan is more dramatic, from 41% to 44%.
EN_A: And what about cost control? I saw something called the cost-to-income ratio.
EN_B: Right, that’s the third key point. China’s cost-to-income ratio moved from 38% to about 36% forecast for 2025 — fairly stable. But India went from 47% to 52%, showing more cost pressure as it expands.
EN_A: So overall, Asian banks are diverging in profitability?
EN_B: Very much so. Indonesia’s ROA is still high at 2.57% forecast for 2025, while China’s is only 0.65%. But the report also warns that high returns can come with higher risk, like credit costs.
EN_A: Credit costs — that sounds like loan loss provisions.
EN_B: Exactly. The report shows China’s credit cost dropping from 1.2% in 2017 to 0.8% in 2025, meaning asset quality is improving. But Hong Kong went the other way, from 0.3% to 0.9%.
EN_A: Are there any questions the report doesn’t fully answer? You said “but” earlier.
EN_B: Yes, a few. For instance, how far will net interest margin compression go? The report gives forecasts, but doesn’t fully explore what happens if the rate environment shifts suddenly. Also, is non-interest income growth sustainable? Does it rely on wealth management or trading? That’s not fully unpacked.

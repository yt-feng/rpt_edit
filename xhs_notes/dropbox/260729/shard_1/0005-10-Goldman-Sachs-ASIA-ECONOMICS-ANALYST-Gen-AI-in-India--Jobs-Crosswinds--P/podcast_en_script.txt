EN_A: So this research report looks at how generative AI might reshape India’s labor market. What’s the big takeaway?
EN_B: The main finding is that AI is more of a complement than a substitute for India’s non-farm workforce. They estimate about 9 to 17 percent of tasks could be automated, but only 8 to 12 percent of jobs are at real risk of being replaced.
EN_A: That’s actually not that scary. What about the rest?
EN_B: Right. About 42 to 48 percent of non-farm jobs are more likely to be augmented by AI. Think doctors getting help with diagnostics, teachers using personalized tools, financial analysts automating basic modeling. AI frees them up for higher-value work.
EN_A: So it’s not a simple “AI takes jobs” story. Which sectors are most vulnerable?
EN_B: The risk is concentrated in services, especially IT outsourcing, postal services, telecoms — anything with repetitive, codifiable tasks like customer support, data entry, basic compliance checks. Manufacturing and construction are less affected because they involve more physical work.
EN_A: So white-collar workers doing routine tasks are at higher risk, while blue-collar workers are safer?
EN_B: Exactly. It’s counterintuitive. The report groups occupations: clerical support and technicians face the highest substitution risk, while manual workers and machine operators might even see job growth, because physical and interpersonal tasks are hard to automate.
EN_A: What about the upside for India’s economy?
EN_B: The baseline estimate is that AI adoption could boost annual labor productivity growth by about 0.4 percentage points over a decade, with a range of 0.1 to 0.8. The key is that productivity gains come from broad-based deployment, not just substitution.
EN_A: So there’s a sequencing issue?
EN_B: Yes. If firms prioritize using AI to augment workers first, productivity improves sooner and labor disruption comes later. That sequencing matters a lot.
EN_A: India has something unique called global capability centers — those are R&D and back-office hubs for multinationals. How do they fit in?
EN_B: The report finds that while India’s top six IT firms cut about 64,000 net jobs over three years, the broader tech sector including GCCs added about 700,000 jobs, reaching 4.4 million. So GCCs might cushion some of the substitution risk.
EN_A: What are the main barriers to AI adoption in India?
EN_B: Infrastructure is a big one. India’s data center capacity is only about 1 gigawatt, which is 1 percent of the global total. Scaling AI will need massive investment in computing power, electricity, water, and digital infrastructure.
EN_A: Any external risks?
EN_B: Yes. Rising protectionism in the US and EU — restrictions on cross-border data, digital services, and foreign AI models — could hurt India’s services exports.
EN_B: The report doesn’t fully answer what new jobs AI will create, because that’s outside its scope. Also, AI capabilities are improving much faster than previous tech cycles. How that acceleration changes the employment picture is still an open question.

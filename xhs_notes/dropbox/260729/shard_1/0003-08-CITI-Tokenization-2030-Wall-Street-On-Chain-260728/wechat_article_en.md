# Tokenization Is Not a Technology Story: It Is a Market Structure Transformation That Demands Strategic Positioning Now

The global tokenized asset market, currently valued at approximately $17 billion, is projected to reach $5.5 trillion by 2030 in a base case scenario, with a bull case of $8.2 trillion and a bear case of $2.7 trillion. This is not a speculative forecast about blockchain adoption. It is a structural projection about where financial intermediation is heading, driven by three converging forces: the integration of tokenization into core market infrastructure by incumbents such as DTCC, NYSE, and Nasdaq; the emergence of regulated on-chain settlement money through stablecoins and tokenized deposits; and improving regulatory clarity across major jurisdictions.

The significance of this shift is often misunderstood. Tokenization is not about replacing traditional finance with crypto-native systems. It is about the gradual, uneven, but ultimately irreversible reconfiguration of how assets are issued, traded, settled, and serviced. The institutions that will capture value in this new landscape are not necessarily the most technologically advanced. They are the ones that understand the strategic implications of hybrid operating models, the emergence of vertically integrated "Structural Orchestrators," and the need to manage a messy, multi-year transition where tokenized and legacy systems run in parallel.

This is not a technology story. It is a market structure story. The strategic question for financial institutions is not whether to adopt tokenization, but how to position themselves across issuance, distribution, settlement, and custody layers before the architecture solidifies.

## Public Market Securities, Not Private Assets, Will Drive the First Wave of Tokenization

The conventional narrative has long held that tokenization's natural fit is private markets—illiquid assets such as real estate, private equity, and venture capital, where fractionalization and programmability could unlock liquidity. The data suggests otherwise. Citi Institute's projections indicate that growth will be led by public market securities, particularly U.S. equities and U.S. Treasuries, not by private market instruments.

The reasoning is structural, not speculative. Private markets face fundamental constraints that public markets do not. Secondary market liquidity is thin by design. Valuation is episodic and negotiated, not continuous and transparent. Regulatory frameworks for digital representations of private securities remain fragmented across jurisdictions. These are not problems that tokenization can solve through technology alone. They require coordination among issuers, investors, regulators, and market makers—a coordination that has proven slow to materialize.

Public market securities, by contrast, already have deep liquidity, established price discovery mechanisms, and clear regulatory treatment. The marginal benefit of tokenization for these assets is not liquidity creation but operational efficiency: faster settlement, reduced reconciliation costs, 24x7 trading capability, and programmable compliance. These are improvements that can be implemented within existing market structure, not disruptions that require rebuilding it.

The implication for institutional strategy is clear. Institutions should prioritize tokenization initiatives in liquid, high-volume asset classes where operational efficiency gains are measurable and regulatory clarity is highest. Private market tokenization, while strategically interesting, should be treated as a second-phase opportunity that depends on the maturation of infrastructure and standards.

## Hybrid Operating Models Will Dominate the Transition, Creating Operational Complexity Before Efficiency Gains Materialize

The most common framing of tokenization is a binary one: either markets remain on legacy infrastructure or they shift entirely on-chain. This framing is misleading. The transition will be gradual, and the dominant operating model for the foreseeable future will be hybrid—tokenized and legacy systems operating in parallel, with interoperability layers connecting them.

The E-ZPass tollbooth analogy, cited in the report by T. Rowe Price's Head of Digital Assets Strategy, captures the dynamic precisely. When automated toll collection was introduced, it did not replace manual tollbooths overnight. Roads had to accommodate both systems simultaneously, adding lanes and complexity before the efficiencies of automation could be fully realized. Tokenization will follow the same pattern. Institutions will need to maintain legacy infrastructure while building tokenized capabilities, manage dual settlement systems, and ensure that assets can move between on-chain and off-chain environments without friction.

This hybrid period introduces real operational risks. Data reconciliation becomes more complex, not less. Legal frameworks must accommodate both digital and traditional representations of ownership. Compliance workflows must operate across two environments with different rules and timings. The efficiency gains that tokenization promises—instant settlement, reduced counterparty risk, automated corporate actions—will only materialize once the hybrid phase is substantially complete and critical mass of liquidity has moved on-chain.

The strategic implication is that institutions should invest in interoperability infrastructure now, not wait for full tokenization. The ability to manage hybrid operations efficiently will be a competitive differentiator. Those that treat tokenization as a binary switch will be caught in the complexity of the transition without capturing its benefits.

## The Emergence of "Structural Orchestrators" Will Reshape Competitive Dynamics Across Capital Markets

Tokenization introduces a structural change that many institutions underestimate: the potential for vertical integration across issuance, distribution, and settlement. In traditional capital markets, these functions are largely separated by design. Issuers use investment banks for underwriting. Exchanges handle trading. Central securities depositories manage settlement and custody. Each layer has its own economics and its own set of specialized intermediaries.

Tokenization, because it operates on shared programmable infrastructure, enables institutions to control multiple layers simultaneously. An institution that issues a tokenized security can embed compliance, settlement, and servicing logic directly into the asset. It can distribute the asset through its own platforms, settle it using its own on-chain money, and manage custody through its own digital wallet infrastructure. This is not theoretical. The report identifies the emergence of "Structural Orchestrators"—institutions that seek to control both asset issuance and settlement rails to capture a larger share of the transaction lifecycle.

The competitive implications are profound. Traditional post-trade intermediaries—custodians, clearing houses, settlement agents—face structural pressure as settlement becomes faster, more automated, and embedded into the asset itself. New market participants, including stablecoin issuers and blockchain-native infrastructure providers, are positioning themselves at the intersection of issuance and settlement. Traditional asset managers and banks must decide whether to build these capabilities themselves, partner with emerging orchestrators, or accept a narrower role in the value chain.

The strategic response is not obvious. Building vertically integrated capabilities requires significant investment in technology, compliance, and liquidity management. But the cost of inaction may be higher. Institutions that cede control of the settlement layer to new entrants risk becoming commoditized distributors of assets they no longer control end-to-end.

## Digital Money Is the Foundational Enabler, and Its Evolution Will Determine the Pace of Tokenization

Earlier tokenization efforts failed to scale for many reasons, but one was critical: there was no regulated on-chain cash to settle transactions. Tokenized securities could be issued and traded, but settlement still required fiat rails, introducing the same delays, costs, and counterparty risks that tokenization was supposed to eliminate.

This constraint is now easing. Citi Institute projects stablecoin issuance value could reach $1.9 trillion by 2030. Major banks are developing tokenized deposit infrastructure. Central bank digital currencies are advancing in Europe, India, and Mainland China. The coexistence of stablecoins, tokenized deposits, and potentially CBDCs provides the liquidity foundation required for tokenized securities to scale, enabling atomic Delivery-versus-Payment settlement where asset and cash transfer occur simultaneously and irrevocably.

The composition of on-chain money will vary by jurisdiction. In the United States, the report expects a mix of stablecoins and tokenized deposits. In Europe, India, and Mainland China, the policy focus is on CBDCs and tokenized deposits, not stablecoins. This creates a fragmented settlement landscape where cross-border tokenized transactions may require interoperability between different forms of on-chain money.

The strategic implication is that institutions should invest in multi-currency, multi-form on-chain money capabilities, not lock into a single settlement asset. The ability to settle tokenized securities in stablecoins, tokenized deposits, or CBDCs depending on jurisdiction and counterparty preference will be a source of competitive advantage. Institutions that wait for a single standard to emerge will find themselves constrained by the choices of others.

## A Decision Framework for Institutional Strategy

The report's analysis translates into a clear decision framework for financial institutions evaluating their tokenization strategy.

First, assess asset class readiness. Prioritize liquid public market securities where regulatory clarity is highest and operational efficiency gains are measurable. Treat private market tokenization as a second-phase opportunity contingent on infrastructure maturity.

Second, invest in hybrid operations. Build the interoperability infrastructure needed to manage tokenized and legacy systems in parallel. The ability to reconcile data, settle across environments, and maintain compliance continuity during the transition will be a competitive differentiator.

Third, define your position in the value chain. Decide whether to build vertically integrated capabilities spanning issuance, distribution, and settlement, or to specialize in a single layer and partner for the rest. The emergence of Structural Orchestrators means that institutions that do not control settlement rails risk being disintermediated.

Fourth, build multi-form on-chain money capabilities. Invest in the ability to settle tokenized securities in stablecoins, tokenized deposits, and CBDCs. The fragmentation of on-chain money across jurisdictions means that flexibility, not standardization, will be the source of competitive advantage.

Fifth, monitor regulatory divergence. Greater regulatory clarity supports scalability, but divergent regional rules risk fragmenting markets and increasing compliance costs. Institutions should build compliance frameworks that can adapt to multiple regulatory regimes, not optimize for a single one.

The tokenization of financial assets is not imminent, but it is inevitable. The institutions that position themselves strategically now will capture disproportionate value as the transition accelerates from 2026 onward. Those that wait for full clarity and standardization will find themselves reacting to a market structure that others have already shaped.

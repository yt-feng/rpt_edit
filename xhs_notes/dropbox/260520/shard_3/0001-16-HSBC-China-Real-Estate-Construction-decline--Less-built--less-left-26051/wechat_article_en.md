# China’s Housing Market Is Not Recovering on Sentiment Alone: Supply Tightening Is the Structural Driver That Investors Are Underestimating

The debate over China’s property sector has settled into a familiar and unproductive binary: is the recent rally driven by genuine fundamental improvement or by temporary policy-induced sentiment? For investors who have watched multiple false dawns since 2021, skepticism is rational. But focusing on this question misses the more important structural shift already underway. The data now show that absolute broad inventory in China’s housing market has declined by approximately 19% over 2023-2025, driven not by a demand surge but by a sustained and measurable contraction in new construction supply. This is not a cyclical blip. It is the early formation of a new supply-demand equilibrium that will fundamentally alter the pricing power, margin structure, and competitive dynamics of the sector for years to come.

The implications are strategic, not tactical. Investors who treat this as another sentiment-driven bounce risk missing the point that the supply pipeline is thinning in a way that historically precedes durable price stabilization. The recovery will not be uniform across cities, developers, or product segments. But the direction of travel is clear: less built means less left to sell, and that arithmetic is beginning to favor developers with premium land banks and strong execution in top-tier cities. This article unpacks the mechanics of the supply squeeze, identifies where the data still leave open questions, and provides a framework for investors to position for the next phase of the cycle.

## The Decline in Broad Inventory Is Real, but the Headline Turnover Metrics Are Misleading

The most common objection to the supply-tightening thesis is that inventory turnover has not improved. On the surface, that is correct. National turnover ratios have moved in the wrong direction, and completed homes for sale actually increased in 2025. For an investor scanning the headlines, this looks like continued oversupply. But this interpretation conflates two different inventory categories and misses the structural shift in composition.

Broad inventory, which includes undeveloped land, projects under construction without sales permits, and completed or near-completed units, has fallen sharply from approximately 3.7 billion square meters in 2023 to roughly 3.0 billion square meters by end-2025. The primary driver is that area sold has consistently exceeded new starts, rapidly drawing down the stock of units that are under construction but not yet approved for sale. This category alone shrank by 57%. Meanwhile, undeveloped land holdings declined by only 9%, and narrow inventory (completed homes and units with sales permits) fell by a modest 8%.

The headline turnover metric is being dragged down by a compositional shift: as the pipeline thins, a larger share of remaining inventory is older, harder-to-sell completed units that require more time to clear. This is not a sign of demand weakness. It is a lagging indicator of the supply squeeze that has already occurred. The market is clearing the easy-to-sell units first, leaving a tail of distressed stock that will take two to three years to absorb. Investors who focus only on turnover are looking at the wrong variable.

## Completed Homes Remain a Near-Term Headwind, but the Pipeline Is Thinning Faster Than Expected

The rise in completed homes for sale in 2025 is the most visible source of bearish sentiment. Developers across the board reported higher completed inventory, with some state-owned enterprises seeing completed stock rise to over 30% of total inventories. This is a real and near-term challenge. Clearance of these units will require price discounts and marketing effort, and it will weigh on margins for the next one to two years.

However, the critical insight is that this is a stock problem, not a flow problem. With new starts falling dramatically and fewer projects converting into completions, the flow of new completed units entering the market is declining. The stock of completed homes is elevated today precisely because it was not sold earlier, but the rate at which new units are being added to that stock is decelerating. Once the existing overhang is cleared, the market will face a much tighter supply environment.

The data from 19 cities that recorded inventory declines of 10% or more in the past twelve months supports this view. These are not uniformly top-tier cities. They include second- and third-tier locations such as Jiaxing, Changchun, and Zhengzhou. The pattern is broad-based. The supply squeeze is not confined to Shenzhen or Shanghai. It is a national phenomenon, though it is more advanced in cities where developers cut starts earlier and more aggressively.

## Idle Land Buybacks Could Accelerate the Supply Contraction by an Additional 5-10%

One of the most underappreciated variables in the supply equation is the role of government-led idle land repurchases. In the first quarter of 2026, local governments issued RMB 48 billion in special bonds for land buybacks, a 56% year-on-year increase. The announced plans are substantial. Chongqing has targeted land repurchases equivalent to 16% of the area sold between 2019 and 2024. Guangdong, Hubei, and Hunan have announced targets in the 10-15% range.

But execution has been uneven. The national average for announced buyback targets stands at roughly 5.5% of land sold in the reference period, but actual bond issuance and land acquisition have lagged behind announcements. If more provinces and cities lift their targets toward 10%, the broad inventory could fall by an additional 5-10%. This is not a marginal adjustment. It is a policy lever that could accelerate the supply contraction by a full year or more.

There is also a qualitative dimension. Some repurchased land parcels are being resold with significantly reduced gross floor area. A Shenzhen parcel originally acquired by Shimao was repurchased and subsequently resold to CR Land with a 35% reduction in GFA. This means that even when land re-enters the market, the effective supply is lower. The combination of buybacks and planning revisions is a powerful supply-side tool that investors have not fully priced in.

## The Investor Dilemma: Why Headline Inventory Turnover Has Worsened Even as Supply Tightens

This is the section where the report leaves a meaningful open question. If supply is tightening, why has inventory turnover not improved? The answer lies in the secondary market. Price-sensitive buyers are increasingly opting for cheaper resale units, while primary market launches are geared toward upgraders seeking better quality. This mix shift depresses new home sales volume even as overall market liquidity improves.

The constructive interpretation is that stronger resale liquidity gives buyers clearer exit options and helps stabilize price expectations. But the data do not yet confirm that confidence has returned at scale. The secondary market is absorbing demand that would otherwise go to primary sales, and until the resale market clears its own overhang, the primary market turnover will remain suppressed.

Investors need to watch for an inflection in secondary market pricing and transaction velocity. Once resale volumes stabilize and prices stop declining, the primary market will benefit from a healthier demand base. The report expects this inflection in 2026, but the timing depends on income growth, credit availability, and policy support. ## A Decision Framework for Positioning in the Next Phase of the Cycle

For investors trying to translate this analysis into portfolio decisions, the following framework is useful. The supply squeeze creates three distinct investment opportunities, each with different risk-return profiles.

First, developers with strong premium pipelines in top-tier cities are best positioned to benefit from the tightening supply of high-quality units. As inventory of desirable new homes shrinks, pricing power improves, and sell-through rates for premium projects are already rising in cities like Shenzhen. The report identifies CR Land and C&D International as preferred names, with improved visibility for earnings recovery from 2027.

Second, developers with large land banks in cities where buyback programs are active may see balance sheet relief and potential value realization. The repurchase of idle land at or near original cost can provide a liquidity cushion and reduce the drag from non-earning assets. This is a more speculative opportunity, as execution risk is high and the pace of buybacks remains uneven.

Third, the broader market recovery will benefit the entire ecosystem, but the timing and magnitude will vary by city tier and product segment. Investors should focus on cities where inventory has already declined by double digits and where new starts have been cut most aggressively. These markets will reach equilibrium first and will likely see the earliest price stabilization.

The key risk to this framework is that demand fails to recover in line with supply contraction. If income growth remains weak or credit conditions tighten further, the supply squeeze alone will not be enough to drive a durable recovery. Investors should monitor consumer confidence, mortgage approval rates, and secondary market pricing as leading indicators.

## Why This Analysis Matters Now: The Window for Positioning Is Narrowing

The supply squeeze has been building for two years, but it is only now becoming visible in the aggregate data. The decline in broad inventory is large enough to matter, and the pipeline for new supply is thinning at an accelerating rate. Investors who wait for confirmation from turnover metrics or completed home clearance will likely miss the early phase of the recovery.

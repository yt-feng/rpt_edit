# The Long Capitulation Is Complete: Positioning Now Biases Toward a Selloff, Not a Rally

The United States Treasury market has crossed a structural threshold. After months of grinding attrition, long-duration holders have capitulated across the curve. Out-of-the-money longs have been closed in virtually every contract from two-year notes to thirty-year bonds. What remains is a market dominated by shorts, most of which sit comfortably in the money. This is not a neutral reset. It is a repositioning that carries a clear directional bias: rates are more likely to sell off further than to rally.

The conventional narrative has been that capitulation precedes reversal. When the last long gives up, the argument goes, the market has nowhere to go but up. That logic may hold in equity markets where mean reversion is a statistical regularity. In fixed income, however, the dynamics are different. The residual out-of-the-money longs that remain are not a contrarian signal of exhaustion. They are a mechanical drag on price, a structural overhang that tilts the path of least resistance toward higher yields. The report from a leading global investment bank makes this explicit: the net OTM position is long, and that keeps a selloff bias intact.

Why now matters is that this positioning shift is coinciding with a fundamental repricing of Fed expectations. The market is no longer debating the timing of cuts. It is pricing the possibility of hikes. Asset managers, who had been the backbone of long-duration exposure, are adjusting accordingly. They are not merely reducing duration. They are actively building short positions across the curve. The combination of stretched CTA shorts, active managers underweight Treasuries and overweight spreads, and a fund flow pattern that favors short-duration products creates a configuration that is historically associated with continued bearish momentum.

The question for institutional investors is no longer whether the market is oversold. It is whether the structural forces now in place are strong enough to overcome the gravitational pull of higher yields drawing in new buyers. The answer, based on the evidence, is that they are.

## The Futures Proxy Reveals a Market Where Residual Longs Are a Headwind, Not a Tailwind

The most analytically useful tool in the report is the futures positioning proxy, which decomposes open interest into four categories: in-the-money longs, out-of-the-money longs, in-the-money shorts, and out-of-the-money shorts. The current snapshot is striking. Across the curve, in-the-money shorts dominate. Out-of-the-money longs are residual, concentrated in the two-year, ten-year, and thirty-year contracts. In-the-money longs are virtually nonexistent.

This configuration matters because of how it interacts with dealer hedging and price dynamics. Out-of-the-money longs are positions that are losing money. Their holders face a choice: hold and hope for a reversal, or close and realize the loss. The longer rates stay elevated, the more pressure builds on these residual longs to exit. Each closure requires the dealer on the other side to unwind the hedge, typically by selling more Treasuries or buying back short positions. The net effect is a self-reinforcing drag on price.

The proxy also generates a return signal based on the average z-score of the net OTM position over the last five days relative to the past three months. That signal is currently negative across every contract from two years to thirty years. A negative signal means the net OTM position is long, and the implied bias is for rates to sell off. There is no ambiguity in this reading. The market is positioned for higher yields, and the positioning itself is part of the mechanism that makes that outcome more likely.

## CTAs Are Stretched Short, but the Next Move Depends Entirely on Volatility

Commodity Trading Advisors have been a dominant force in the recent selloff. The report shows that across multiple lookback models, CTAs are short in both the two-year and thirty-year contracts. The short is particularly stretched at the long end, where all lookback models agree on the direction. This is not a diversified short. It is a concentrated, momentum-driven bet that has been building for weeks.

The critical insight from the report is that the next move for CTAs is not a function of price level but of volatility. If futures continue to decline on low volatility, shorts can and will grow. The trend-following algorithms will add to positions, extending the selloff. If volatility increases, however, the calculus shifts. The risk of a sharp reversal rises, and CTAs will pull back their shorts to manage that risk. The market is therefore in a regime where the path of yields is contingent on the path of volatility, not the other way around.

This creates an interesting asymmetry for institutional investors. A low-volatility grind higher in yields is the most dangerous scenario for anyone positioned for a rally. It invites further short selling from systematic strategies and extends the duration of pain for residual longs. A volatility spike, by contrast, could trigger a rapid but temporary reversal as CTAs unwind. The report does not take a view on which scenario is more likely, but the framework is clear: volatility is the swing factor, and investors should be watching VIX and MOVE indices as closely as they watch the ten-year yield.

## Active Managers Are Reinforcing the Bearish Bias by Staying Underweight Treasuries and Overweight Spreads

The positioning of active benchmark Agg funds tells a complementary story. These funds remain underweight U.S. Treasuries and overweight mortgage-backed securities and investment-grade credit. The overweight in IG has been growing over the past month, driven by the outperformance of spreads relative to Treasuries since the start of April.

This is not a passive allocation decision. It is an active bet that credit risk will continue to be rewarded relative to duration risk. And so far, that bet has paid off. IG and MBS have outperformed Treasuries, reinforcing the tilt. The risk, however, is that this trade becomes crowded. If the selloff in Treasuries accelerates, the relative performance of spreads will come under pressure. The spread overweight will then become a source of vulnerability rather than strength.

The report also notes that active funds are relatively neutral on the curve. This is important because it means the bearish bias is coming from outright duration underweight, not from a curve steepening or flattening bet. The positioning is simple: less duration, more credit. That is a defensible posture in a rising rate environment, but it leaves active managers exposed to a scenario where the selloff is driven by a repricing of term premium rather than a shift in monetary policy expectations.

## Fund Flows Are Contradictory: Inflows Accelerate Even as Duration Shortens

One of the most intriguing tensions in the report is the divergence between positioning and flows. Even as positioning washes out and longs exit, higher yields are drawing money back into fixed income funds. The report shows that U.S. fixed income funds took in $18 billion in a single week, twice the twelve-week average pace. Inflows were led by Agg, short government, and IG funds. Long government funds were the lone category to see outflows.

This pattern signals a shift toward shorter weighted average maturity. Investors are willing to add fixed income exposure, but they want less duration risk. They are buying the yield, not the price appreciation. This is consistent with a market that has repriced to levels that are attractive for carry but not yet attractive for capital gains.

The question this raises is whether the inflow momentum can sustain itself. If yields continue to rise, the carry argument becomes even more compelling, and inflows could accelerate further. That would create a floor under prices, limiting the downside. If yields stabilize, the inflow momentum may fade as investors wait for a clearer signal on the Fed. The report does not resolve this tension, but it identifies it as the key dynamic to watch in the weeks ahead.

## The Japanese Factor and the Unresolved Question of Intervention Funding

The report devotes significant attention to Japanese investor behavior, and for good reason. Japanese private investors sold $14 billion of U.S. Treasuries in March, following $18 billion in February. The selling was broad-based, driven by banks, pensions, and life insurers. In recent weeks, however, foreign private bond holdings have been inflecting higher, suggesting some dip buying.

The more consequential question is whether suspected Japanese yen intervention has been funded by U.S. Treasury sales. The report estimates intervention flows of approximately $72 billion, with implied UST sales of $40 to $50 billion. The data so far shows little evidence of sustained official liquidation. Foreign RRP, a proxy for official sector cash, has been little changed. The widening in front-end swap spreads since late April is also inconsistent with market-moving official sales.

But the report leaves a critical question open: if further intervention is required, at what scale does it become consequential for the UST market? The analysis suggests that a figure closer to $100 billion would be the threshold. That scale aligns with custodial declines observed at the start of the Iran conflict, which likely contributed to a pullback in broader demand. If intervention flows reach that level, it would pose a headwind to long front-end spread positions and could amplify the selloff.

The unresolved question is whether the funding of the intervention is simply unclear or whether it is being done through channels that do not show up in the data. The upcoming Treasury International Capital data release should clarify which regions drove March sales. Until then, the risk of intervention-driven selling remains a tail risk that cannot be dismissed.

## A Decision Framework for Institutional Investors: Three Scenarios and Their Implications

The report provides the raw material for a decision framework, but it does not synthesize one. That is the task for the reader. Based on the evidence, three scenarios emerge, each with distinct implications for positioning.

Scenario one is a low-volatility grind higher. In this scenario, yields continue to rise gradually, CTAs add to shorts, residual longs capitulate, and active managers maintain their underweight to Treasuries. The path of least resistance is higher yields, and the optimal positioning is short duration with a flattening bias. This scenario favors investors who are already short and are willing to ride the trend.

Scenario two is a volatility-driven reversal. In this scenario, a catalyst such as a weaker economic data print or a dovish Fed surprise triggers a spike in volatility. CTAs cover shorts, residual longs get a reprieve, and yields rally sharply. The optimal positioning is to be long convexity, either through options or through a long-duration position that benefits from a rapid decline in yields. This scenario favors investors who are positioned for mean reversion and are willing to accept the risk of being early.

Scenario three is a regime shift driven by foreign selling. In this scenario, Japanese intervention scales up to $100 billion or more, and the funding is done through UST sales. The market absorbs a large supply shock, term premium rises, and yields sell off across the curve. The optimal positioning is to be short the front end and long volatility. This scenario is the least probable but the most consequential.

The report does not assign probabilities to these scenarios. That is the reader's job. What the report provides is the analytical architecture to think through each one. The key is to recognize that the current positioning is not neutral. It is biased toward scenario one, but the optionality to pivot to scenario two or three should be priced into any portfolio.

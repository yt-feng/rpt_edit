# Inflation Scare is Reshaping the Rotation: The Energy-Led Rebalancing Has Only Just Begun

The market narrative shifted last week, and it was not a subtle pivot. Global equities declined by 0.6%, a seemingly modest move that masks a significant internal rotation. The headline number is not the story. The story is the collapse of rate-sensitive sectors and the surge in energy stocks, a pattern that tells us the inflation scare is no longer a theoretical risk—it is the dominant force driving capital flows. While the earnings cycle remains robust and the global news pulse is improving, the rising probability of central bank rate hikes is creating a fault line that will determine winners and losers for the remainder of the year. The old playbook of buying growth at any price is being retired. The new playbook rewards sectors with pricing power and real asset exposure, and it punishes those that borrowed against a low-rate future.

This is not a cyclical hiccup. It is a structural re-rating of risk. The data from the latest high-frequency monitor reveals a market that is beginning to price in a regime where inflation is sticky, not transitory. The best-performing sector last week was Energy, up 4.2%, while the worst were Utilities and Materials, both down 3.0%, and Real Estate, down 2.9%. This divergence is the market's way of voting on which business models can survive, and even thrive, in a higher-for-longer rate environment. Energy companies benefit from rising commodity prices; utilities and real estate suffer from higher discount rates and financing costs. The message is clear: the market is rotating from duration-sensitive assets into inflation beneficiaries.

Why now? Because the data is catching up with the fear. For months, markets dismissed inflation as a supply-chain artifact. Those excuses are wearing thin. The move in energy is not just about oil prices; it is about a broader recognition that input costs are rising across the board, and central banks are running out of patience. The report notes that "an increasing risk is that rising inflation prompts central banks to raise rates." This is the key sentence. It is not a forecast; it is a risk assessment that the market is now acting upon. Investors who ignore this signal risk being caught on the wrong side of a major regime change.

## The Momentum Shift Favors Real Assets Over Growth Narratives, Confirming a Regime Change

The Triple Momentum framework—which ranks sectors and themes by a combination of earnings momentum, price momentum, and news sentiment—provides the clearest evidence of this shift. The strongest momentum is now concentrated in Semis, Energy, and Tech Hardware. This is not a random cluster. These are sectors that combine strong earnings growth with exposure to real economic demand and, in the case of Energy, direct commodity leverage. The weakest momentum belongs to Software and Luxury Lifestyle. Software, once the darling of the low-rate era, is now in a negative momentum trap: its high valuation multiples are vulnerable to rising discount rates, and its earnings revisions are losing steam. Luxury Lifestyle suffers from a different problem: it is a proxy for wealth effects and consumer confidence, both of which are threatened by rising rates and slowing global demand.

The thematic data reinforces this point. Among the 12 themes tracked, Space (+4.3%) and Renewable Energy (+1.5%) were the best performers, while Nuclear Energy (-7.2%), Gold (-6.8%), and Luxury Lifestyle (-5.4%) were the worst. The gold decline is particularly instructive. Gold is often seen as an inflation hedge, but it is also a rate-sensitive asset with no yield. In an environment where real rates are rising, gold loses its appeal. The market is not buying a generic "inflation" trade; it is buying specific sectors where inflation translates directly into revenue and earnings growth. Space and Renewable Energy benefit from government spending commitments and long-term structural demand that is less sensitive to short-term rate moves. Nuclear Energy, by contrast, is a capital-intensive sector that requires cheap debt to be viable.

The implication for portfolio construction is profound. The traditional 60/40 portfolio, which relies on bonds to cushion equity drawdowns, is under severe stress. If inflation forces rates higher, bonds will not provide the hedge they once did. The market is telling us that diversification must now come from factor exposure, not just asset class labels. Sectors with strong Triple Momentum are acting as the new ballast.

## The Earnings Cycle is Strong, But It is No Longer a Universal Tailwind

It would be a mistake to conclude that the earnings environment is deteriorating. It is not. The report states clearly that "the global earnings cycle remains strong." The problem is that strong earnings are no longer sufficient to lift all boats. The market is becoming highly discriminating. Companies that beat earnings expectations are being rewarded, but only if their guidance and business models are compatible with a rising rate environment. Companies that miss or provide cautious guidance are being punished severely.

This is where the "So What" becomes critical. A strong earnings cycle used to be a tide that lifted all ships. Now it is a tide that lifts only those ships built for choppy waters. Energy companies are reporting strong earnings because oil and gas prices are high. Tech hardware companies are reporting strong earnings because of AI-driven demand for chips and infrastructure. But software companies, which rely on subscription models and long sales cycles, are seeing their margins squeezed by higher labor costs and slower enterprise spending. The earnings revision ratio, which measures the balance of upgrades versus downgrades, is likely diverging sharply between these groups.

The data on individual stocks is telling. The highest Triple Momentum Rank among large, liquid global stocks includes Bloom Energy, Renesas Electronics, Samsung SDI, SEMCO, Murata, STMicroelectronics, Intel, and AMD. This list is dominated by energy and semiconductor names. There is not a single pure-play software company on it. This is not a coincidence. The market is voting with its capital, and it is voting for tangible assets, production capacity, and pricing power. Investors who are still overweight tech and growth without regard to these factors are taking on uncompensated risk.

## The Regional Divergence is a Warning Signal for Global Investors

The regional performance data reveals a market that is splitting along lines of economic structure. The US was the best-performing region last week, up 0.2%, while Emerging Markets lagged significantly, down 2.5%. Europe also underperformed, falling 2.0%. On a month-to-date basis, the picture is even more stark. Asia Pacific ex-Japan is the best-performing region MTD, up 4.7%, while Europe is the worst, down 1.5%. Year-to-date, Asia Pacific ex-Japan leads with a 19.1% gain, while Europe is barely positive at 1.4%.

This divergence is not random. It reflects the different inflation profiles and policy responses across regions. The US economy is relatively insulated from energy price shocks because it is a net energy producer. Its inflation problem is more demand-driven, which gives the Fed a clearer path to hiking. Europe, by contrast, is an energy importer. Rising energy prices hit European consumers and businesses directly, depressing growth and making the ECB's job much harder. Emerging Markets are caught in a vice: they face higher import costs and tighter global financial conditions, which force their central banks to hike aggressively or see their currencies collapse.

The strategic implication is that a global portfolio cannot be managed with a single macro view. The inflation scare is a global phenomenon, but its transmission mechanisms are local. The US may be able to tolerate higher rates without a recession; Europe may not. Emerging Markets may face a full-blown crisis if capital flows reverse. The Triple Momentum framework is useful here because it captures these regional differences through price and earnings data. But the framework is only as good as the user's ability to interpret it. The report provides the raw signals; the investor must provide the judgment about which regions are likely to sustain their momentum and which are at risk of a reversal.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Duration of the Inflation Scare

The report is excellent at identifying the current state of the market and the momentum signals. It is less clear on the most critical question: how long will this inflation scare last? Is this a multi-quarter rotation, or is it a prelude to a more severe downturn? The report notes the risk of rate hikes, but it does not model the probability of a policy error. If central banks hike too aggressively, they could tip the global economy into recession. In that scenario, even the currently strong sectors like Energy and Semis would eventually suffer, as demand collapses.

The data on themes is also ambiguous. Space and Renewable Energy are performing well, but they are small, volatile themes. Are they leading indicators of a broader shift, or are they speculative pockets that will reverse when liquidity tightens further? The Triple Momentum framework shows that Gold has the strongest momentum on a combined basis, yet it was a poor performer last week. This suggests a tension between the medium-term signal and the short-term price action. Resolving that tension requires a deeper analysis of the macro drivers that the report does not fully explore.

Another open question is the role of AI. The AI theme is still positive, with a price momentum rank of 52 and an earnings momentum rank of 57. But it is not breaking out. The market may be starting to question the speed of AI monetization. If rates rise, the discount rate applied to future AI earnings will increase, making those long-duration cash flows less valuable. The report provides the data to ask this question, but it does not answer it.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Inflation Scare

For readers who want to translate this report into actionable strategy, the following framework is useful. It is based on the insight that the market is now pricing two distinct scenarios, and the portfolio must be positioned to handle both.

First, assess your portfolio's exposure to "duration." Duration, in this context, refers to the sensitivity of a stock's valuation to changes in interest rates. High-duration assets are those with high price-to-earnings ratios, long-dated cash flows, and low current earnings. This includes most software companies, many biotech firms, and growth-oriented consumer discretionary names. Low-duration assets are those with high current earnings, strong free cash flow, and pricing power. This includes energy companies, commodity producers, and select industrials. The report's Triple Momentum data is a direct proxy for this duration sensitivity. The sectors with strong momentum are low-duration; the sectors with weak momentum are high-duration.

Second, use the regional data to gauge your geographic risk. If you are overweight Europe, you are implicitly betting that the inflation scare will abate quickly and that the ECB will not need to hike aggressively. If you are overweight Asia Pacific ex-Japan, you are betting on a different outcome, one where regional growth remains robust despite global headwinds. The report's data does not tell you which bet is correct, but it forces you to be explicit about your assumption.

Third, monitor the earnings revision ratio closely. This is the canary in the coal mine. If the ratio starts to deteriorate across the board, it means the earnings cycle is breaking, and the current rotation will turn into a broad sell-off. If the ratio remains strong in low-duration sectors while weakening in high-duration ones, the rotation is healthy and likely to continue. The report provides the framework for this monitoring, but the investor must check the data weekly.

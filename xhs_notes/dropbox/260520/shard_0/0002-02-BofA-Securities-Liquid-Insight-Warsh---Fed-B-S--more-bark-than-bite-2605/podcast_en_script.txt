EN_A: Kevin Warsh just got confirmed as the new Fed Chair. The big question everyone is asking is what he will do with the Fed balance sheet.
EN_B: That is the key question. Our core take is this: Warsh is all bark and almost no bite. The size of the balance sheet is unlikely to shrink much, and the composition changes will have minimal market impact.
EN_A: What do you mean by all bark and no bite? He has been a vocal critic of the Fed balance sheet for years.
EN_B: He has. But criticizing is one thing. Actually cutting the balance sheet is another. The report focuses on two things: size and composition. Size is hard to reduce. Composition will shift, but it won't really matter for markets.
EN_A: So why is size so hard to reduce? The Fed already finished quantitative tightening.
EN_B: Right, by the fourth quarter of 2025, the balance sheet was normalized. Now its size is determined by liabilities. The Fed has three big liabilities: currency in circulation, the Treasury General Account, and bank reserves.
EN_A: So to shrink further, Warsh has to cut one of these three?
EN_B: Exactly. Currency is exogenous, the Fed cannot control it. The Treasury has shown no interest in reducing its cash balance. That leaves reserves as the only real option.
EN_A: How does he cut reserves? Just tell banks to hold less?
EN_B: There are two paths. One is bank-unfriendly: cap or tier reserves, making banks less liquid. That would reduce risk-taking, slow the economy. Warsh is unlikely to go that route.
EN_A: What is the other path?
EN_B: Bank-friendly deregulation. For example, let banks pre-pledge collateral to the Fed discount window. That expands their high-quality liquid assets, so they need fewer reserves. We estimate a reserve drop of 200 to 500 billion dollars, but it will be slow.
EN_A: What about the composition side? What will Warsh do there?
EN_B: He will likely speed up the shortening of the Fed's Treasury portfolio duration. The Fed is already letting MBS roll off and reinvest in shorter-dated Treasuries. Warsh could push maturing coupon reinvestments into 2 to 3 year notes.
EN_A: That would shorten the average duration of the whole Treasury market. That should matter, right?
EN_B: It would, but only if the Treasury does not offset it. The report argues the Treasury is unlikely to adjust its issuance. If that is right, the market impact is basically zero.
EN_A: So the bottom line is that Warsh will not really move the needle on the balance sheet?
EN_B: That is the view. Size changes are modest. Composition changes are neutral. Neither will tighten financial conditions. So there is no reason for Warsh to cut rates because of the balance sheet.
EN_A: The report also talks about a key question: does Warsh support ample reserves or scarce reserves?
EN_B: That is the most important question. We believe he will support ample reserves. The reason is simple: ample reserves are easier to manage, keep the banking system flush with cash, reduce money market volatility, and support easier financial conditions.
EN_A: But Warsh is a hawk. Why would he support ample reserves?
EN_B: Because President Trump cares more about easy financial conditions than the size of the balance sheet. And Warsh will listen to Trump. Plus, the entire Fed leadership supports the ample reserve regime. There is a great quote from Fed Governor Waller: you do not want banks searching under sofa cushions for money every night. That is incredibly inefficient and stupid.
EN_A: The report also introduces a blue sky idea: setting the bank standing repo rate equal to the interest on reserves. What is that?
EN_B: It is a novel approach. Banks could borrow cash from the Fed using Treasury or agency collateral at a rate equal to what they earn on reserves. That way, they do not need to hold as large a reserve buffer.
EN_A: How do you get banks to actually use this tool?
EN_B: You have to reduce the stigma. Right now, if a bank uses Fed liquidity tools, the market thinks something is wrong. The report suggests the Fed stop publishing weekly regional reserve distribution data. That would make it harder for the street to figure out which bank is borrowing.
EN_A: If this blue sky idea actually happened, would it be a big deal?
EN_B: It could be more impactful than the conventional paths. But the report is clear: this is a blue sky idea. It is not widely discussed yet.

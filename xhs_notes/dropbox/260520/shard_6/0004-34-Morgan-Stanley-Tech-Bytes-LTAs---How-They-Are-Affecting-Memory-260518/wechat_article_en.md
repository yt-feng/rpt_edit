# Long-Term Agreements Are Reshaping Memory from a Cyclical Commodity into a Contracted Earnings Stream, and the Market Has Not Yet Priced the Re-Rating

The memory industry has long been defined by a punishing rhythm: boom-driven overinvestment, supply gluts, price collapses, and then the slow crawl back to recovery. Investors have historically priced memory stocks at a persistent discount to reflect this cyclical risk, accepting that peak earnings are unsustainable and troughs are inevitable. That structural assumption is now being challenged by a shift in how the largest customers and suppliers are contracting with one another.

Long-term agreements spanning three to five years, backed by prepayments, pricing floors, and financial guarantees, are transforming the relationship between hyperscaler cloud providers and memory manufacturers. These are not the soft, renegotiable commitments of past cycles. They are binding, non-cancellable, non-refundable contracts that effectively convert a portion of future revenue into secured, predictable cash flows. If this framework holds, the memory industry may be entering a regime where earnings visibility, margin protection, and free cash flow durability become materially higher than in any prior cycle.

The implications for valuation are significant. A global investment bank report has raised its price target for SK hynix by more than 50 percent, reflecting not just stronger near-term earnings but a fundamental reassessment of how the company should be valued. The argument is straightforward: if memory earnings become less cyclical, the P/E multiple applied to those earnings should expand. Yet the market has been slow to adjust. The structural shift in contracting is happening now, but the valuation re-rating appears to be in its early stages.

This article examines how LTAs are changing the economics of memory, why this cycle differs from past attempts at contracting, what the evidence from company disclosures tells us, and where the most important unresolved questions remain. It also provides a decision framework for investors trying to assess which memory companies are best positioned to benefit from this transformation.

## LTAs Are No Longer Soft Commitments; They Now Function as Financial Insurance for Both Suppliers and Customers

The critical distinction between past long-term agreements and the current framework is the degree of binding power. In prior semiconductor cycles, LTAs were often structured as best-effort volume commitments with limited penalties for non-fulfillment. Customers could walk away during a downturn. Suppliers could reallocate capacity during a shortage. The agreements provided some demand visibility but offered little real protection against cyclical volatility.

The current generation of LTAs is fundamentally different. According to the report, these agreements typically span three to five years and include specific pricing mechanisms such as floors, collars, or fixed formulas. More importantly, they involve substantial upfront payments from customers to suppliers. In some cases, hyperscalers have committed prepayments covering 50 percent of 2027 demand and 100 percent of 2028 demand. These prepayments are not refundable. They function as a financial guarantee that the customer will take delivery of the contracted volume, because walking away means forfeiting capital already deployed.

This structure changes the incentive dynamics of the entire supply chain. For suppliers, the prepayment provides upfront capital that can be used to fund capacity expansion with lower risk. For customers, the prepayment secures supply in an environment where memory has become a critical bottleneck for AI infrastructure build-out. Both parties have skin in the game, and the cost of breaking the agreement is high enough to make it a genuine commitment.

The report cites specific evidence from company disclosures. Micron announced a five-year Strategic Customer Agreement, which it described as a first in its history. SK hynix has stated that customer requests for medium- to long-term supply commitments have significantly increased, and that it is reviewing various structural options for multiyear agreements. Samsung has confirmed that it has already signed finalized multiyear contracts with some customers, noting that these agreements present a higher level of binding commitment than existing supply contracts based on mutual trust.

Perhaps the most striking evidence comes from SanDisk, which has rebranded its LTA strategy as New Business Models. The CEO stated that customers are putting up billions of dollars of collateral through various financial instruments that will survive for the life of the contracts. If a customer fails to meet its purchasing obligations, that financial commitment immediately transfers to the supplier as compensation. This is not a penalty clause. It is a financial guarantee that fundamentally alters the risk profile of the supplier's revenue stream.

## The Structural Shift in Customer Behavior Is Driven by Memory Scarcity, Not by Supplier Bargaining Power

It would be easy to interpret the rise of LTAs as a sign that memory suppliers have gained pricing power at the expense of their customers. That interpretation is too simplistic. The real driver is a structural imbalance between the growth of AI-driven memory demand and the physical constraints on supply expansion.

The report estimates that the AI semiconductor market will grow 50 to 60 percent through 2030. DRAM supply growth, by contrast, is likely to be capped at around 30 percent in bit shipments per annum, constrained by significant bottlenecks in EUV lithography tool availability from 2027 onward. The report flags the risk of sold-out conditions at ASML, given unprecedented capacity shortages for both advanced logic and DRAM manufacturing.

This supply-demand imbalance is not a temporary phenomenon. It reflects the reality that building new memory fabrication capacity takes years, requires enormous capital expenditure, and depends on a limited supply of advanced manufacturing equipment. Hyperscalers planning AI infrastructure build-outs over the next five years cannot afford to rely on spot markets or quarterly negotiations to secure the memory they need. They must lock in supply now, even if it means accepting pricing terms that protect supplier margins.

The report makes clear that hyperscalers are no longer procuring memory opportunistically. They are securing long-term supply to support AI infrastructure build-outs. This is a structural realignment of the customer base away from traditional demand drivers such as PCs and mobile phones toward data centers. Data center customers have longer planning horizons, higher visibility into their own demand, and a much lower tolerance for supply disruption. They are willing to pay for certainty.

For memory suppliers, this shift creates an opportunity to transform their business model. Instead of building capacity on speculative forecasts and then hoping demand materializes, they can now build capacity against confirmed orders backed by financial guarantees. This eliminates a significant portion of inventory risk and improves mass production efficiency. The report argues that even if there is excess supply from capacity expansion, long-term contracted volumes buffer both pricing and margin, reducing the severity of memory downturns.

## The Re-Rating Playbook Already Exists in Other Industries, and Memory Stocks Are Starting to Follow It

Investors skeptical of the LTA thesis often point out that the memory industry has attempted to reduce cyclicality before, and those attempts have failed. The report addresses this skepticism not by arguing that memory will become completely non-cyclical, but by providing a concrete playbook for how structural improvements in earnings visibility and free cash flow can lead to sustained valuation re-rating.

The most powerful analogy in the report is Apple. The report conducts a value attribution analysis showing that buybacks and dividends together accounted for approximately 70 percent of Apple's excess compounded return versus the S&P 500 over the past decade. Apple's stock outperformed not because its earnings grew faster than the market, but because it consistently returned capital to shareholders through buybacks, reducing the share count and increasing per-share earnings.

The report is careful not to argue that memory stocks will replicate Apple's share price performance. The point is more specific: as LTAs reduce cyclicality and free cash flow yields rise, memory companies like SK hynix and Samsung could develop a more durable capital return story. Sustained free cash flow generation, combined with management commitment to shareholder returns, can support both higher dividend payouts and share buybacks. This, in turn, drives P/E re-rating because investors are willing to pay a higher multiple for earnings that are more predictable and more likely to be returned to shareholders.

The report also provides a second analogy from the Japanese shipping industry during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Shipping rates increased more than threefold during the pandemic, and stocks traded at very low P/E multiples because the market expected mean reversion. When freight rates eventually fell back 90 percent from peak and forward earnings dropped almost 80 percent, the stocks did not collapse. Instead, they re-rated upward because shipping companies used their pandemic-era cash generation to establish much higher and more sustainable dividend payout ratios. Investors began valuing the stocks based on their dividend yield rather than their cyclical earnings power.

The parallel to memory is instructive. If LTAs allow memory companies to generate sustained free cash flow through the cycle, and if management teams commit to returning that cash to shareholders, the market may eventually re-rate memory stocks on the basis of their capital return potential rather than their cyclical earnings volatility. The report's exhibits show that both Samsung and SK hynix are projected to generate significantly higher free cash flow and free cash flow yields over the 2026 to 2028 period, precisely as LTAs begin to take full effect.

## The Report Leaves Critical Questions Unanswered, Particularly Around LTA Pricing Terms and Enforcement Mechanisms

For all the detail the report provides on the structure and implications of LTAs, it is notably candid about what it does not know. The report states explicitly that companies are unlikely to disclose much detail of LTAs because of non-disclosure agreements. It also acknowledges that specific terms vary among different suppliers and customers. Some agreements set only floor pricing or minimum margin protections. Others include more comprehensive pricing mechanisms.

This lack of transparency creates genuine analytical uncertainty. Without knowing the exact pricing formulas embedded in these contracts, it is difficult to assess how much margin protection suppliers truly have. A floor price that is set below the supplier's cost of production provides little real protection. A pricing collar that adjusts with market conditions may still expose suppliers to significant downside if demand softens.

Similarly, the enforcement mechanisms for these contracts remain opaque. The report distinguishes current LTAs from past take-or-pay penalties, arguing that prepaid revenue deals would be much harder to renegotiate. But the legal and practical enforceability of these contracts has not been tested in a downturn. If a major hyperscaler faces financial pressure or a strategic shift away from AI investment, will it honor a multiyear contract with billions of dollars of committed volume? The report's view is based on evidence that hyperscaler customers are thinking differently, but that evidence is circumstantial.

Another unresolved question is how LTAs will affect the non-AI memory market. The report notes that more than 50 percent of supplies over the next two to three years will likely be bound through LTAs, with additional upside potential from non-AI customers. But if the largest customers lock up the majority of available supply, smaller customers in the PC, mobile, and consumer markets may face tighter allocation and higher prices. This could create a two-tier market in which LTAs protect margins on contracted volume while spot market prices become more volatile for everyone else.

Finally, the report does not fully address the risk that LTAs could lead to overinvestment. If suppliers build capacity based on confirmed LTA demand, and if that demand proves to be overstated or front-loaded, the industry could still face an oversupply problem once the contracted volumes are fulfilled. The report argues that LTAs reduce the risk of overbuilding because capex is aimed at confirmed demand rather than speculative forecasts. But confirmed demand today is not the same as sustained demand five years from now. The risk of a demand cliff after the LTA period expires is real, and the report does not provide a framework for assessing it.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Assess LTA Penetration, Pricing Protection, and Capital Return Commitment

Given the complexity and uncertainty around LTAs, investors need a structured approach to evaluating which memory companies are best positioned to benefit from this structural shift. Based on the report's analysis, three factors are most important.

First, assess LTA penetration as a percentage of total revenue. Companies that have signed a higher proportion of their capacity under long-term contracts will have greater earnings visibility and lower cyclical risk. The report indicates that SK hynix and Samsung are both actively pursuing LTAs, with Samsung confirming it has already signed finalized contracts. SanDisk's New Business Models framework suggests a similarly aggressive approach. Investors should monitor disclosures around the percentage of capacity committed under LTAs and the duration of those commitments.

Second, evaluate the quality of pricing protection in the contracts. Not all LTAs are created equal. Contracts with fixed pricing or tight pricing collars provide more margin protection than contracts with only floor pricing or minimum margin guarantees. Contracts that include prepayments or financial collateral provide stronger enforcement mechanisms than contracts that rely solely on penalties. Investors should look for evidence that suppliers are securing pricing terms that protect their margins even in a downturn.

Third, assess management's commitment to returning free cash flow to shareholders. As the report's Apple and shipping analogies demonstrate, sustained free cash flow generation only leads to valuation re-rating if management actually returns that cash to shareholders through buybacks and dividends. Memory companies have historically been inconsistent on this front, preferring to reinvest cash into capacity expansion during upcycles and conserve cash during downturns. The LTA framework may change this dynamic by providing more predictable cash flows and reducing the need for speculative capex. But investors should look for concrete evidence of capital return commitments, such as dividend payout ratio targets or share buyback authorizations.

Companies that score highly on all three factors are likely to see the most significant P/E re-rating as the market gradually recognizes that their earnings are less cyclical than history suggests. Companies that score poorly may still benefit from the industry-wide shift toward LTAs, but the valuation benefit will be less pronounced.

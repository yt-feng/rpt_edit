# China's Infant Formula Market Is No Longer a Battle of Domestic vs. Foreign—It Is a Battle of Trust, Science, and Freshness

The conventional narrative that China's infant formula market is a zero-sum contest between domestic champions and multinational giants is no longer sufficient to explain what is happening on the ground. According to the sixth annual proprietary survey of 1,200 Chinese parents and caregivers conducted by a global investment bank's research team, the market has entered a more nuanced phase. The data, collected across Tier 1 through Tier 5 cities, reveals a structural shift that rewards companies capable of simultaneously mastering three distinct dimensions: scientific credibility, supply chain transparency, and consumer trust recovery. The winners will not be defined by their headquarters location but by their ability to execute on these three fronts.

The timing of this shift matters. The survey was conducted in the aftermath of a series of high-profile infant formula recalls that disrupted consumer confidence in certain multinational brands. These disruptions created a natural experiment, exposing which companies had built the resilience to retain customers and which had not. The results are sobering for some and encouraging for others. The survey's 11 key findings, when synthesized, point to a single governing insight: the market's center of gravity is moving away from brand heritage and toward demonstrable product attributes that parents can verify.

This article interprets the survey's findings through a strategic lens, drawing out the implications for investors and industry observers. It does not attempt to summarize every data point. Instead, it builds an argument about where the industry is heading and what questions remain unanswered.

## The Recall Shock Has Accelerated a Pre-Existing Trend Toward Domestic Brands, but the Real Story Is About Switching Behavior, Not Nationalism

The survey's most immediately striking finding is the extent of stock shortages for recalled brands. Among respondents who currently use or previously purchased Danone or Nestle Wyeth products, 84 percent reported that these labels were unavailable due to recalls. Within this group, 47 percent specifically noted that Nestle stock was harder to find while Danone stock levels remained unchanged. This asymmetry is consistent with the fact that Nestle infant formula was recalled in China, while Danone's products were not.

The more consequential finding, however, is what happened to the customers who switched. A clear majority—63 percent—of respondents who used to buy Danone or Nestle Wyeth switched to a Chinese brand. Only 35 percent moved to other international brands. This is not a story of consumers suddenly embracing nationalism. It is a story of availability driving behavior. When shelves are empty, parents do not wait. They buy what is available, and in many cases, that meant domestic products.

This pattern has direct implications for individual companies. For a2 Milk, the survey suggests that any potential tailwind from competitor recalls was largely a missed opportunity. The company faced its own supply constraints during the same period, which prevented it from capturing switchers. Worse, the survey indicates that consumers who switched are not necessarily returning. The implication is clear: supply chain reliability is now a competitive differentiator, not just an operational metric.

For Health and Happiness, the picture is more favorable. The company's premium and super-premium products, produced in France and Denmark, align well with the demand for safety and quality that intensified after the recalls. The survey supports the view that H&H is a meaningful beneficiary of this switching dynamic, particularly given its early-mover advantage in HMO-added products and its rapid online channel growth.

For Feihe, the recall event itself may not have been a direct growth driver, but the company benefits from a broader structural trend that the survey confirms: the improving perception of domestic brand quality. Feihe's positioning as "most suitable for Chinese babies" and its investment in a fresh ingredient traceability system, launched in January 2026, speak directly to the attributes that consumers now prioritize.

## Nutritional Science Has Overtaken Brand Heritage as the Primary Purchase Driver, and This Changes the Competitive Landscape Permanently

The survey data reveals a significant shift in what drives consumer choice. In 2025, brand and nutritional value or scientific benefit were equally influential factors. In 2026, nutritional value has clearly pulled ahead, with brand falling to second place. Country of origin, which was once a top-three consideration, has dropped out of the top three entirely.

This is not a minor adjustment in consumer preferences. It represents a fundamental reordering of the industry's competitive dynamics. For years, multinational brands relied on the perception that foreign equals superior. That perception has been eroding, as the survey's longitudinal data shows. The percentage of respondents who prefer foreign brands has declined to its lowest point since the survey began in 2020. Meanwhile, the proportion with no preference has grown to 55 percent.

The implication is that companies can no longer coast on brand equity alone. They must invest in the science behind their products and communicate that investment effectively. This is particularly relevant for human milk oligosaccharides, or HMOs, which the survey identifies as very important to consumers. Companies that were early to market with HMO-added products, such as Feihe and H&H, have a structural advantage. Those that lag in product innovation will find it increasingly difficult to justify premium pricing.

The survey also shows that consumers are increasingly open to foreign brands that are manufactured in China. In 2025, only 24 percent of respondents said they would be more likely to buy a foreign brand if it were manufactured in China. In 2026, that figure jumped to 50 percent. This suggests that the stigma around China-based production is fading, provided the product delivers on nutritional science. For multinationals, this opens a strategic option: localize production to improve freshness and supply chain responsiveness without necessarily sacrificing the brand's international cachet.

## Freshness and Traceability Have Become Non-Negotiable Consumer Demands, Creating a New Axis of Competition

One of the survey's more subtle but strategically important findings relates to consumer attitudes toward expiration dates and product freshness. The survey indicates heightened scrutiny of these attributes, which puts pressure on companies with long, complex global supply chains. For a multinational brand that ships products from Europe to China, the time from production to shelf can be measured in months. For a domestic brand with local production, that timeline can be measured in weeks.

Feihe's fresh ingredient traceability system, launched in January 2026, is a direct response to this consumer demand. The system allows consumers to verify core ingredient production dates via QR code, effectively turning supply chain transparency into a marketing asset. This is not a gimmick. It addresses a real and growing consumer concern that the survey data confirms.

For H&H, which relies on European production, the freshness challenge is more acute. The company's global supply chain model has been well managed historically, but the survey suggests that consumer expectations are rising. Companies that cannot demonstrate freshness will face an uphill battle, regardless of their brand strength or scientific credentials.

This trend also has implications for the online channel, which continues to grow in importance. The survey shows that online expansion is a key driver for both Feihe and H&H, with the latter reporting that online sales accounted for 29 percent of revenue in 2025. Online channels amplify the importance of freshness because consumers can more easily compare products, read reviews, and check production dates. Companies that excel in e-commerce logistics will have an advantage over those that treat online as an afterthought.

## The Birth Rate Narrative Is Overly Simplistic: Premiumization, Category Expansion, and Zodiac Cycles Create Offsets That Investors Are Underestimating

The dominant bear case for China's infant formula market has been the declining birth rate. It is a powerful and intuitive argument: fewer babies mean less demand for formula. The survey, however, provides evidence that this narrative is incomplete. Several factors are partially decoupling individual company performance from the macro birth rate.

First, premiumization continues to drive value growth even as volume declines. Feihe's super-premium product mix, which accounted for 68 percent of sales in 2025, is a direct beneficiary. Consumers are trading up, not trading down. Second, category expansion into products for older children and adults is creating new revenue streams. Both Danone and Nestle have scale in pediatrics and older demographics, which the survey identifies as a long-term buffer against infant population decline.

Third, the survey provides a nuanced view of zodiac cycles. The next two zodiac years, Horse in 2026 and Goat in 2027, appear to be incrementally positive from a birth rate perspective. This is not a forecast of a baby boom, but it does suggest that the worst of the demographic headwinds may be behind the industry. The survey also cautions against assuming that an increase in marriages will translate to more births in the near term, which is a useful reminder that demographic trends are slow-moving and difficult to predict.

For investors, the implication is clear: company-level analysis must go beyond the birth rate. The companies that are investing in premiumization, product innovation, and category expansion will outperform those that are simply hoping for a demographic recovery.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Durability of Switching Behavior and the Cost of Winning Back Lost Customers

The survey provides a snapshot of consumer behavior at a specific point in time, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most critical is the durability of the switching behavior observed after the recalls. When consumers switched from Danone or Nestle Wyeth to domestic brands, did they switch permanently, or will they return once the recalled brands are back on shelves?

The survey cannot answer this question definitively, but it offers clues. The improving perception of domestic brand quality, combined with the declining importance of country of origin, suggests that the switching may be more permanent than temporary. If consumers have tried domestic brands and found them to be of equal or superior quality, the barrier to switching back is high. The cost of winning back lost customers, in terms of marketing spend and promotional discounts, could be significant.

A second unanswered question relates to the supply chain constraints that affected a2 Milk. The survey confirms that a2 was unable to capitalize on competitor recalls due to its own supply issues, but it does not quantify the long-term impact on market share. The company's recent downgrade to Sell by the covering analyst suggests that the market is already pricing in a difficult recovery. But the survey data raises a deeper question: even when supply constraints are resolved, can a2 win back the customers who switched to domestic brands? The answer is not obvious.

Third, the survey does not fully address the role of government policy. The report mentions childcare subsidies and improved financial health as macro tailwinds, but the specific impact of these policies on formula demand is not quantified. As the industry navigates a period of demographic transition, policy support could become a more important variable.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask About Every Infant Formula Company

Based on the survey's findings, investors should evaluate infant formula companies using a three-part framework. The first question is about scientific credibility: Does the company have a credible and demonstrable investment in nutritional science, particularly in areas like HMOs that consumers now prioritize? Companies that can answer yes have a structural advantage. Those that cannot will face pricing pressure.

The second question is about supply chain transparency: Can the company demonstrate freshness and traceability in a way that consumers can verify? The survey shows that this is becoming a non-negotiable attribute. Companies with local production or short supply chains have an inherent advantage. Companies with long, complex global supply chains must invest in technology and logistics to close the gap.

The third question is about switching dynamics: Is the company gaining or losing customers as a result of the recent recalls and the broader shift in consumer preferences? This is not a static question. The answer depends on the company's specific positioning, its product portfolio, and its execution in the online channel. Companies that are gaining switchers, such as H&H, have a tailwind. Companies that are losing switchers, or that missed the opportunity due to supply constraints, face a headwind that may persist for several quarters.

This framework is not exhaustive, but it captures the most important variables that the survey identifies. Investors who apply it will be better positioned to distinguish between companies that are benefiting from structural trends and those that are merely riding transient tailwinds.

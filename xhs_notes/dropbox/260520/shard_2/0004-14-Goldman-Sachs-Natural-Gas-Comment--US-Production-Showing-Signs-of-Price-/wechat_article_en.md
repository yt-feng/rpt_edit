# Henry Hub Is No Longer a One-Way Bet: Price Sensitivity in Haynesville Changes the Natural Gas Balance

The natural gas market has entered a new phase where the relationship between price and production is no longer linear, and that shift carries profound implications for anyone positioning in the sector. Henry Hub has pushed back above $3 per mmBtu for the first time since late March, but the most important story is not the weather-driven rally or the sympathy move with European gas. It is the fact that US dry gas production, particularly from the Haynesville shale, is showing clear signs of price sensitivity after a year in which most market participants assumed it would not. This behavioral change matters because it directly challenges the prevailing view that US supply will remain relentless at any price level, and it introduces a new variable into the balance equation for 2026 and 2027.

The conventional wisdom entering this year was that Haynesville producers would deliver solid volume growth regardless of Henry Hub pricing. That assumption has been tested and found wanting. In the first five months of 2025, when prompt prices averaged $3.70, Haynesville production rose by 800 million cubic feet per day sequentially from December through May. In the same period this year, with average prices at $3.19 and several weeks below $3, production has declined by 200 million cubic feet per day. The contrast is stark, and it reveals a market that is more responsive to price signals than many expected. This is not a trivial observation. It suggests that the inventory of drilled-but-uncompleted wells, producer balance sheets, and capital discipline are now binding constraints in a way they were not a year ago.

The implications extend beyond summer storage injections. If Haynesville production can moderate under lower prices, then the downside risk to Henry Hub in 2027, when the Permian associated gas ramp is expected to add 3.4 billion cubic feet per day, may be less severe than the consensus fear. But the market has not yet priced this nuance. The challenge for investors is to distinguish between temporary weather-driven volatility and a structural change in supply behavior. The data from the Haynesville suggests the latter is underway, but the evidence is still incomplete.

## The Haynesville Production Response Is the Most Relevant Driver for the Balance, Not Weather or Storage

Weather forecasts and European gas prices have captured headlines, but they are transitory factors that obscure the underlying structural shift. The correlation between Henry Hub and cooling degree day forecasts is real, but it is a short-term relationship that tells little about the medium-term balance. Similarly, the Hormuz disruption and the rally in European TTF gas have created sympathy moves, but the US market is fundamentally insulated from those events by its own supply dynamics and LNG export capacity constraints. The real action is in the Haynesville, where the year-over-year change in production behavior is the clearest signal that the market is becoming price sensitive.

The data is unambiguous. In 2025, Haynesville production grew by 800 million cubic feet per day from December to May. In 2026, over the same period, it declined by 200 million cubic feet per day. Even taking the strongest month of the year so far, April, production was only up 200 million cubic feet per day from December, compared to a 700 million cubic feet per day increase in the prior year. Maintenance is a seasonal factor, but it likely affected both periods similarly. The difference is price. Producers had a strong incentive to bring wells online when prompt prices were above $3.70. They have no such incentive when prices hover near $3.00 or dip below.

This price sensitivity matters because it changes the supply response function for the entire US gas market. If the Haynesville, which is the most price-sensitive dry gas play, can throttle back production when prices are low, then the market has a self-correcting mechanism that limits downside risk. That mechanism was assumed to be broken, but the data shows it is alive. The question is how durable it will be as we move into the winter and 2027.

## A Tight Winter Is Unlikely, but the Summer 2027 Congestion Risk Is the Real Analytical Frontier

The report makes a deliberate point of stating that it is not concerned about a tight winter even if Haynesville production stays flat through October. This is a critical distinction. The logic rests on two pillars. First, the Haynesville rig count has increased significantly in the first quarter of 2026, which means incremental wells can be brought to market by the fourth quarter if needed. Second, the Permian associated gas production is expected to rise substantially this winter as 4.5 billion cubic feet per day of new pipeline capacity comes online in the second half of 2026.

The combination of these factors means that the winter 2026-2027 balance is likely manageable even under a conservative Haynesville outlook. The real risk lies further out, in the summer of 2027. That is when the Permian ramp is expected to reach its peak, with December 2027 Permian gas production projected at 27.8 billion cubic feet per day, an increase of 3.4 billion from current levels. That volume of associated gas, if it materializes, could push the market into congestion territory, particularly if it coincides with a full recovery in Haynesville production.

The report's base case forecast for 2027 Henry Hub is $3.50 per mmBtu, but it acknowledges that the risks are skewed to the downside because of this congestion risk. The key insight, however, is that a more disciplined Haynesville growth path can help limit those downside risks. If producers continue to show price sensitivity, the supply response will be more measured, and the market will clear at a higher price than it would under a scenario of relentless Permian growth combined with aggressive Haynesville drilling.

This is where the analytical framework becomes most valuable. The market is not facing a single path. It is facing a range of outcomes that depend on how producers behave. The report provides the data to calibrate those outcomes, but it does not resolve the uncertainty. That is the frontier for investors.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Durability of Price Sensitivity and the Lag Structure of the Rig Count

The report establishes that Haynesville production has become price sensitive, but it leaves open several critical questions. The first is whether this sensitivity is structural or cyclical. It could be that producers are simply reacting to a temporary period of low prices and will return to aggressive drilling once Henry Hub stabilizes above $3.50. Alternatively, it could reflect a permanent shift in capital discipline driven by shareholder pressure, balance sheet repair, or a recognition that the long-term demand outlook for natural gas is uncertain given the pace of renewable energy deployment and the potential for policy changes.

The second unanswered question is the lag between price changes and production changes. The report shows that production has responded within a few months, but the magnitude and speed of that response could change as the rig count adjusts. The first quarter rig count increase in the Haynesville suggests that producers are preparing to bring more supply to market later this year. If that materializes, the price sensitivity observed in early 2026 may prove to be a temporary phenomenon rather than a lasting feature of the market.

The third open question is the interaction between Haynesville and Permian associated gas. The report treats them as separate supply sources, but they are linked through the broader gas market. If Permian associated gas grows faster than expected, it could depress prices and force Haynesville producers to cut back further. Conversely, if Haynesville remains disciplined, it could support prices and encourage more Permian drilling. The feedback loop is complex and not fully modeled.

These are not weaknesses of the analysis. They are inherent uncertainties that any serious investor must grapple with. The report provides the framework and the data to understand the dynamics, but it does not pretend to have all the answers. That is precisely why the full report is worth reading.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Use the Haynesville Signal to Position for 2027

The report's insights can be translated into a practical decision framework. The starting point is to recognize that the market is not tight today and is unlikely to become tight this winter. The summer 2026 strip at $3.17 and the prompt near $3.00 do not reflect a market that is congested, nor do they reflect a market that is under-supplied. The report's forecast of $3.50 for the summer is within a range that balances the market, but it does not provide a strong directional signal.

The real opportunity lies in positioning for the 2027 outlook. The key variable to monitor is the Haynesville rig count and production trajectory over the next six months. If the rig count continues to rise and production recovers to the levels implied by the report's base case, then the downside risk to 2027 prices increases. Investors should consider hedging or reducing long exposure to Henry Hub under that scenario. If, however, the rig count stabilizes or declines, and production remains below the base case, then the downside risk is limited and the $3.50 forecast may prove conservative.

The second variable to track is the Permian pipeline capacity additions. The 4.5 billion cubic feet per day of new capacity expected in the second half of 2026 is a game changer. It will unlock significant associated gas volumes that are currently constrained. The timing and magnitude of those flows will determine how much supply hits the market in 2027. Investors should pay close attention to the pace of pipeline construction and the actual flow data once the capacity comes online.

The third variable is the weather, but it should be treated as a noise factor rather than a signal. Short-term weather forecasts drive price volatility, but they do not change the structural balance. Investors should avoid overreacting to weather-driven moves and instead focus on the production and pipeline data that will determine the medium-term outlook.

The decision framework can be summarized as follows: if Haynesville production remains flat or declines through the summer, the 2027 downside risk is limited and long positions are attractive. If Haynesville production recovers to the base case trajectory, the downside risk is significant and hedging is warranted. The report provides the data to make that assessment, but the judgment requires ongoing monitoring.

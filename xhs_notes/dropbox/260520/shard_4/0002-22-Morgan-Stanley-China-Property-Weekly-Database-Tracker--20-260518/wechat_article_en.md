# China's Property Market Is Not Recovering Uniformly: The Secondary Market Is Leading a Structural Shift That Primary Sales Cannot Mask

The most recent weekly data from a major global investment bank covering China's property sector through mid-May 2026 reveals a market that appears to be healing, but only if you look at the wrong numbers. Weekly primary registered unit sales across 50 cities increased 5% year-on-year, while secondary sales surged 49%. This is not a recovery. This is a structural rebalancing of where and how housing transactions are occurring, and it carries profound implications for developers, policymakers, and investors who continue to anchor their expectations on new-home sales as the primary signal.

The temptation is to read the headline improvement in primary sales as evidence that policy stimulus is finally working. Tier 1 cities saw primary sales rise 5% year-on-year, Tier 2 cities posted a 6% gain, and even Tier 3 cities managed a modest 1% increase. Year-to-date, however, primary sales remain down 15% year-on-year, a figure that reveals the underlying weakness beneath the weekly noise. The real story is not the modest uptick in new-home transactions, but the explosion in secondary market activity that has now reached nearly 50% year-on-year growth in a single week.

This divergence between primary and secondary markets is not a temporary statistical anomaly. It is the clearest signal yet that China's property market is entering a new phase where inventory dynamics, buyer preferences, and price expectations are fundamentally different from the pre-downturn era. For investors, the question is no longer whether the market is recovering, but whether the recovery that is happening is actually investable.

## The Secondary Market Surge Reveals a Fundamental Shift in Buyer Behavior That Primary Sales Data Cannot Explain Away

Secondary registered unit sales across 10 monitored cities increased 49% year-on-year for the week ending May 17, accelerating sharply from the 17% growth recorded the previous week. Tier 1 cities led the charge with a 65% year-on-year jump, while Tier 2 cities posted a 40% gain. These are not marginal improvements. They represent a doubling or tripling of growth rates within a single week, suggesting that a tipping point in buyer psychology has been reached.

The conventional explanation for this surge is that policy easing has unlocked pent-up demand. Lower mortgage rates, relaxed purchase restrictions, and down-payment reductions have made housing more accessible. But if that were the full story, primary sales should have responded with equal vigor. They did not. The 5% primary sales growth, while positive, is anemic compared to the secondary market's acceleration. This gap tells us that buyers are not simply returning to the market; they are returning to a specific segment of the market that offers something primary sales cannot.

What secondary transactions offer is immediacy and price discovery. In a market where developer defaults remain a real concern, completed homes with clear titles and physical inspection possibilities carry a lower risk premium than off-plan purchases from potentially distressed developers. The secondary market also reflects genuine market-clearing prices, whereas primary sales are often influenced by developer pricing strategies, government price caps, and bulk transaction distortions. The surge in secondary activity suggests that buyers have concluded that the best deals are not with developers but with existing homeowners who are willing to negotiate.

## The Six-City Asking Price Index Contradicts the Volume Recovery and Signals That Price Discovery Is Still Underway

The Centaline six-city secondary asking price tracking index stood at 18.5% in the latest week, declining from 19.4% in the prior week. This index measures the discount from asking prices at which transactions are being negotiated, and its movement is arguably more important than the volume data. A declining discount rate would suggest that sellers are gaining pricing power as demand returns. An increasing discount rate would indicate that sellers still need to cut prices to attract buyers.

The current reading of 18.5% represents a slight tightening from the prior week, but it remains at levels that historically signal a buyers' market. More importantly, the direction of the index is not yet clearly established. It has fluctuated in a narrow range over recent weeks, suggesting that the market is searching for a new equilibrium price rather than finding one. The secondary volume surge is happening at discounted prices, and the discount is not narrowing fast enough to indicate that the market has bottomed in price terms.

This creates a tension that investors must resolve. High transaction volumes at stable or slightly declining discounts could indicate that the market is clearing efficiently, with willing buyers and sellers meeting at mutually acceptable prices. But it could also indicate that the volume recovery is being driven entirely by price concessions, meaning that any stabilization in prices would cause volumes to collapse again. The data available does not yet allow us to distinguish between these two scenarios, but the burden of proof should be on those who argue that a sustainable price recovery has begun.

## The Absence of New Project Launches Is the Most Overlooked Signal in the Weekly Data

The report notes that no newly launched projects were monitored last week. This is an extraordinary data point that deserves far more attention than it typically receives. In a normal market, weekly new project launches provide the pipeline of supply that drives primary sales. When launches stop, primary sales are sustained only by inventory liquidation, which is inherently finite and often distressed.

The absence of new launches can be interpreted in two ways. The optimistic interpretation is that developers have successfully reduced inventory to manageable levels and are now waiting for better pricing conditions before bringing new supply to market. The pessimistic interpretation is that developers lack the financial capacity or land banks to launch new projects, and that the primary sales we are seeing represent the final liquidation of legacy inventory.

The year-to-date primary sales decline of 15% supports the pessimistic view. If developers were preparing for a recovery, they would be accelerating launches to capture improving demand. Instead, the market is seeing the opposite. This suggests that the primary market is shrinking not because demand is absent, but because supply is constrained by developer balance sheets. The secondary market is filling the gap, but secondary transactions do not generate revenue for developers. For investors in property stocks, the primary market is the relevant metric, and it remains deeply troubled.

## The Report Does Not Fully Address How the Divergence Between Primary and Secondary Markets Will Resolve

The weekly database tracker provides invaluable granular data, but it leaves a critical analytical question unanswered: how will the current divergence between primary and secondary markets resolve itself? There are three plausible scenarios, and each has dramatically different implications for investors.

In the first scenario, secondary market activity eventually pulls primary sales higher. As existing homeowners sell their units, they generate cash that can be used for home upgrades or new purchases. This "chain effect" is common in mature housing markets and could provide a second wind for primary sales once developer confidence returns. In this scenario, the current weakness in primary sales is a lagging indicator, and a synchronized recovery is possible within 12 to 18 months.

In the second scenario, the secondary market cannibalizes primary sales permanently. Buyers who would have purchased new homes from developers instead choose existing homes because they offer better value, lower risk, and immediate occupancy. Developers are forced to compete on price, compressing margins and delaying new project viability. In this scenario, the property sector undergoes a structural contraction, and the days of high-volume primary sales are over.

In the third scenario, both markets weaken as the secondary surge exhausts itself. The current secondary volume may be driven by a temporary wave of distressed sellers who are exiting the market, and once this inventory is absorbed, activity could decline sharply. Primary sales would then face an even more difficult environment because the buyer pool has been depleted. This is the most bearish scenario and the one that the current data cannot rule out.

The report provides the data to frame these scenarios but does not offer a clear analytical framework for weighting them. Investors must do that work themselves, and the answer depends on factors that the weekly data cannot capture: developer financing conditions, local government land sales, and household income expectations.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Use the Secondary Market as a Leading Indicator, Not a Comfort Signal

For investors trying to navigate this market, the weekly database offers a decision framework that is more useful than simply tracking primary sales trends. The key is to treat the secondary market as a leading indicator for primary market health, not as a substitute signal.

The first rule is to monitor the ratio of secondary to primary transaction volumes. When this ratio is rising, it indicates that buyers are preferring existing homes over new ones. A stable or declining ratio would indicate that the primary market is regaining its competitive position. Currently, the ratio is rising sharply, and investors should not interpret this as bullish for developers.

The second rule is to track the asking price discount index in conjunction with secondary volumes. If volumes are rising and discounts are narrowing, the market is experiencing genuine price discovery and demand improvement. If volumes are rising but discounts are stable or widening, the volume is being driven entirely by price cuts, and the recovery is fragile. The current data shows stable to slightly narrowing discounts, which is mildly positive but not yet conclusive.

The third rule is to watch for new project launches. The absence of launches is a red flag that cannot be ignored. Until developers resume launching new projects at scale, the primary market is effectively in runoff mode. Investors should require evidence of new launches before upgrading their view on the sector.

The fourth rule is to segment by tier. Tier 1 cities are performing significantly better than Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities in both primary and secondary markets. A recovery that is concentrated in Tier 1 is not a national recovery, and investors should be wary of extrapolating top-tier performance to the broader market.

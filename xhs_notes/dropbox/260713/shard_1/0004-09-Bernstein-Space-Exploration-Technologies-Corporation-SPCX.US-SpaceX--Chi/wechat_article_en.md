## China's Booster Landing Is a Necessary First Step, Not a Competitive Threat to SpaceX's Near-Term Dominance

The Long March 10B landing represents a genuine technical achievement, but it must be placed in context. Prior Chinese attempts—including the Long March 10A and Long March 12A—had failed. This success brings China to the point where SpaceX was roughly a decade ago, when Falcon 9 first demonstrated booster recovery. The Chinese program now faces a series of harder steps: demonstrating that the recovered booster can be refurbished and relaunched, building a production line capable of supporting high launch cadence, and moving from partial to full reusability.

The financial implications are straightforward. Partial reuse, where only the first stage is recovered, still requires a new second stage for every launch. That caps the economic efficiency gains and limits the scalability of launch operations. SpaceX's Falcon 9 operates on exactly this model, and it required years of manufacturing refinement to reach 165 launches in a single year. China has not yet demonstrated that it can manufacture second stages at the rate needed to support even a fraction of that volume. The Long March 10's payload capacity of 140 metric tons to low earth orbit is larger than Starship V3's 100 metric tons, but that advantage is irrelevant if the rocket cannot be launched frequently enough to exploit it.

The more important comparison is architectural. Starship is designed for full reuse of both stages, a capability that SpaceX has not yet fully demonstrated but is actively pursuing. Full reuse changes the economics of launch entirely: it eliminates the need to manufacture a new second stage for each flight, which is the single largest cost driver in a partially reusable system. If SpaceX achieves full reuse, it can increase launch rates by an order of magnitude relative to any partially reusable competitor. That is not a marginal improvement. It is a structural shift that would make the launch cost per kilogram of Starship fundamentally lower than anything the Long March 10 could achieve, even if China successfully demonstrates first-stage reuse.

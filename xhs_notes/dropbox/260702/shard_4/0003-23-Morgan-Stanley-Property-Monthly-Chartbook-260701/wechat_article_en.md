# European Property Stocks Are Priced for a Recovery That Has Not Yet Arrived

The European property sector is flat year to date. While the broader MSCI Europe index has returned 9 percent in euros, property stocks have delivered zero. Even including dividends, total shareholder return for property stands at just 3 percent, compared to 11 percent for equities overall. This divergence is not a temporary lag. It is a signal that the market is pricing a specific set of assumptions about interest rates, rental growth, and capital values — assumptions that may or may not hold.

The sector now trades at a 32 percent discount to net asset value, nearly double the historical average of 17 percent. That discount is not uniform. It reflects a market that is making sharp distinctions between regions, subsectors, and individual companies. UK property stocks have outperformed continental Europe in June and year to date. Retail and logistics are the best-performing subsectors. Residential and self-storage are lagging. The dispersion in NAV valuations has increased materially in recent months, and it remains above the 10-year average.

What does this mean for investors? It means the easy narrative — that property is cheap and therefore attractive — is insufficient. A 32 percent discount to NAV can be a value trap if NAV itself is falling. The question is not whether property stocks are cheap. The question is whether the underlying assets are being marked correctly, and whether the market’s assumptions about future cash flows are realistic.

This article examines the report’s data to build a decision framework. It identifies where the market is most optimistic, where it is most pessimistic, and what investors should watch next. It also highlights several open questions that the monthly chartbook does not fully answer — questions that are essential for anyone considering a position in European property.

## The 32 Percent NAV Discount Masks a Market That Is Making Sharp Regional and Subsector Distinctions

A single average discount to NAV tells a misleading story. The report shows that the spread between UK and continental European NAV discounts has widened. UK stocks trade at a smaller discount than their continental peers. Within continental Europe, the dispersion is even more pronounced. German residential, Paris offices, and Stockholm offices face the most severe markdowns. Logistics and retail in certain markets trade much closer to book value.

This dispersion is not random. It reflects the market’s view of future capital growth. The report includes direct property market assumptions for 2026 through 2028. For the UK, analysts forecast average capital value growth of 3 percent in 2026. For continental Europe, the forecast is just 1 percent. Within continental Europe, the range is wide: Madrid offices are expected to see 2 percent growth, while German offices are expected to decline by 2 percent and Stockholm offices by the same amount.

The logic is straightforward. Markets where rents are growing and vacancy is falling command higher valuations. Markets where office demand is structurally impaired, due to remote work or oversupply, are being penalized. The NAV discount is not a sentiment indicator. It is a rational response to divergent fundamental trajectories.

The implication for investors is clear. A portfolio that buys European property stocks indiscriminately, based solely on the average discount, is taking on significant hidden risk. The discount must be decomposed by region and subsector. Only then does it become a useful signal.

## UK Property Is Outperforming Continental Europe Because Its Recovery Story Is More Credible, Not Because It Is Cheaper

UK property stocks are up 4 percent year to date in local currency, compared to UK equities up 5 percent. That is not a dramatic outperformance, but it is significant relative to continental Europe, where property stocks have been flat to negative. The report’s direct property assumptions explain why.

For UK retail, the report forecasts capital growth of 3 percent in 2026, rising to 4 percent in 2027. For London offices, the forecast is also 3 percent in 2026 and 4 percent in 2027. UK industrial and logistics, which had been the star performer in recent years, is expected to grow at a more modest 2 percent in 2026 before accelerating to 4 percent in 2028.

Compare this to continental Europe. Paris offices are expected to see zero capital growth in 2026 and 2027. German offices are expected to decline by 2 percent in 2026 and 1 percent in 2027. Stockholm offices are also expected to decline. The only bright spots are Madrid offices, German residential, and average logistics — each forecast at 2 percent growth in 2026.

The UK story is one of yield stabilization and modest rental growth. The continental story, outside of a few pockets, is one of continued yield expansion and stagnant rents. This difference is not cyclical. It reflects structural factors: the UK office market has adjusted more quickly to post-pandemic demand patterns, while continental European markets, particularly in Germany and France, are still working through oversupply.

The report’s data on office vacancy rates supports this view. London vacancy rates, while elevated, have stabilized. Paris and Frankfurt vacancy rates continue to drift higher. The gap in prime office rents between London and continental cities has widened, with London commanding a significant premium.

For investors, the UK premium is not a valuation anomaly. It is a rational reflection of better fundamentals. But it also means that the UK discount to NAV, while narrower than in continental Europe, may not offer as much upside if interest rates remain higher for longer.

## The Dispersion in Valuation Metrics Is at an All-Time High for EPS Yields, Signaling That the Market Has Lost Confidence in Earnings Visibility

The report tracks two key measures of valuation dispersion: price to NAV and adjusted EPRA EPS yield. The NAV dispersion has increased materially in recent months and remains above the 10-year average. That is noteworthy, but not unprecedented. What is truly striking is the EPS yield dispersion, which the report states is at an all-time low.

This is a counterintuitive finding. NAV dispersion is high, but EPS yield dispersion is low. How can both be true?

The answer lies in what each metric measures. NAV reflects the marked-to-market value of assets. EPS yield reflects the earnings power of those assets. When NAV dispersion is high but EPS yield dispersion is low, it means that the market is broadly agreeing on the earnings outlook for the sector, but disagreeing sharply on the value of the underlying assets.

This is consistent with a market that is uncertain about interest rates. If investors expect rates to remain elevated, they will discount future cash flows more heavily, compressing NAVs. But if they also expect that companies can maintain earnings through operational discipline, EPS yields will remain stable.

The low EPS yield dispersion suggests that analysts and investors have converged on a narrow range of earnings expectations. That convergence may be misplaced. If interest rates move unexpectedly, or if rental growth disappoints, the consensus could break quickly. The all-time low in EPS yield dispersion is a risk signal, not a comfort.

For investors, the implication is that the market is not pricing a wide range of outcomes. It is pricing a narrow band of expectations. That leaves the sector vulnerable to surprises in either direction.

## The Report’s Direct Property Assumptions Offer a Decision Framework, but They Also Raise Unanswered Questions About the Path of Interest Rates

The report provides detailed assumptions for rental growth, yield shift, and capital growth across multiple subsectors and regions. These assumptions are not forecasts in the traditional sense. They are inputs that analysts use to derive NAV estimates and target prices. But they can also serve as a decision framework for investors.

Here is how to use them.

First, identify the subsectors and regions where the assumptions imply a clear catalyst. For example, UK retail is expected to see 3 percent rental growth and zero yield shift in 2026, followed by yield compression of 7 basis points in each of the next two years. That combination implies capital growth of 3 percent initially, accelerating to 4 percent. If these assumptions hold, UK retail stocks offer a clear path to NAV recovery.

Second, identify where the assumptions are most fragile. German offices are expected to see negative capital growth of 2 percent in 2026 and 1 percent in 2027, with yield expansion of 13 basis points each year. That is a severe outlook. If German office rents stabilize sooner than expected, the downside may be limited. But if vacancy continues to rise, the assumptions could prove optimistic.

Third, compare the assumptions to market pricing. If a stock trades at a 40 percent discount to NAV, but the underlying assumptions imply only 2 percent annual capital growth, the discount may be justified. If the assumptions imply 4 percent growth, the discount may be excessive.

The report does not fully answer one critical question: what happens if interest rates do not decline as expected? The assumptions for capital growth implicitly assume that yields will either stabilize or compress. If the 10-year bond yield in Germany or the UK rises by another 50 basis points, those assumptions would need to be revised downward. The report’s data on EBITDA/EV yield spreads over swap rates shows that the margin has compressed, but it remains positive. That margin is the cushion against higher rates. If it narrows further, the sector becomes more vulnerable.

## The Open Question That the Report Does Not Fully Address Is Whether the Recovery in Transaction Volumes Will Materialize

The report includes a reference to a separate note on transaction activity: the European Property Transaction Tracker, which noted a slowdown in the first quarter of 2026. Transaction volumes are a leading indicator for the sector. When volumes are low, price discovery is impaired, and NAV estimates become less reliable. When volumes pick up, it signals that buyers and sellers are converging on prices, which typically supports valuations.

The report does not provide a forecast for transaction volumes. It notes the slowdown but does not project when it will reverse. This is a meaningful gap. If transaction volumes remain low through the second half of 2026, the current NAV discounts may persist or even widen. If volumes recover, the discounts could close quickly as buyers step in to acquire assets at marked-down prices.

Several factors will determine the path of transaction volumes. The cost of debt is the most important. If financing costs remain elevated, leveraged buyers will struggle to achieve their return hurdles. Equity capital, however, is abundant. Private equity firms and institutional investors have raised significant dry powder for real estate. The question is whether they will deploy it at current prices, or wait for further declines.

The report’s data on capital availability is referenced but not detailed in the parsing provided. This is one area where the full report would offer additional insight. Investors should watch for signs that transaction activity is picking up, particularly in the UK, where the fundamental story is strongest.

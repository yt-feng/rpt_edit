# The AI Value Chain Cannot Sustain Its Current Divergence: The Semiconductor-Hyperscaler Performance Gap Must Converge, and Investors Need to Prepare for Both Paths

The most striking feature of the AI trade over the past nine months is not the magnitude of returns, but the asymmetry of who has captured them. Since last September, semiconductor stocks—the chip and memory makers that supply the physical backbone of artificial intelligence—have dramatically outperformed the hyperscalers that actually deploy and monetize that infrastructure. This is not a neutral market phenomenon. It is a structural tension that, left unresolved, threatens the very foundation of the AI investment thesis.

The divergence matters because it exposes a fundamental question that most market participants are only beginning to grapple with: who actually captures the economic value created by AI? If the suppliers of compute continue to extract a disproportionate share of returns at the expense of the cloud platforms and model providers that pay for that compute, the entire cycle risks breaking. The hyperscalers cannot sustain infinite capital expenditure growth without corresponding revenue and profit improvement. The semiconductor companies cannot sustain their premium valuations if their largest customers eventually cut orders.

This is not a hypothetical scenario. The data already shows the pressure building. Hyperscaler stock prices have been flattish over the past year despite strong earnings growth, implying a significant derating and an increase in their cost of equity. Their credit spreads have widened relative to semiconductor companies, raising their cost of debt. Short interest in hyperscalers has been rising steadily since last October, with a further steep increase in May and June of this year. The market is already pricing in the risk that the current distribution of AI value capture is unsustainable.

The core analytical challenge for investors is that the available evidence points in two very different directions. A global investment bank's house view leans positive, anticipating that hyperscalers will eventually demonstrate sufficient monetization to justify their spending and close the performance gap from above. But the consensus of bottom-up analysts tells a different story, projecting a sharp deceleration in hyperscaler capital expenditure growth from 2027 onward. If that consensus proves correct, the semiconductor trade could face a severe and sustained correction.

Investors cannot afford to wait for clarity. The divergence is already affecting positioning, risk appetite, and sectoral flows. The question is not whether the gap will close, but how—and what that means for portfolios that have become increasingly concentrated in the semiconductor-led AI trade.

## The Semiconductor Outperformance Has Created a Positioning Vulnerability That Magnifies the Downside Risk

The ratio of market capitalization to sales for the semiconductor sector within the MSCI ACWI has risen to levels that demand scrutiny. This is not simply a valuation metric; it is a measure of how much investor capital has been allocated to the semiconductor trade relative to the underlying economic activity of those companies. When this ratio becomes elevated, it signals that the market has priced in expectations that may be difficult to sustain without continued acceleration in fundamental performance.

What makes this positioning particularly fragile is the concentration of ownership. The semiconductor trade has become a crowded consensus. Many institutional portfolios that began the cycle underweight AI infrastructure have spent the past year chasing performance, adding exposure to chip makers as a way to participate in the AI theme without the complexity of evaluating individual cloud platforms or software beneficiaries. This has created a situation where the marginal buyer of semiconductors is increasingly momentum-driven rather than fundamentally anchored.

The risk is not that semiconductor companies will suddenly report poor earnings. The risk is that any signal that hyperscalers are reconsidering their capex plans—whether from earnings calls, industry conferences, or regulatory developments—could trigger a rapid reassessment of the entire AI value chain. And because semiconductor positioning is so extended, the resulting correction could be disproportionately violent. The short interest data for semiconductors ex-memory has been rising in May and June, suggesting that some sophisticated investors are already hedging against this outcome.

This is the classic structure of a crowded trade that has worked too well for too long. The outperformance itself has created the conditions for a reversal. Investors who have ridden the semiconductor wave need to ask themselves whether they have a clear catalyst for the next leg higher, or whether they are simply hoping that the current trajectory continues indefinitely.

## The Hyperscaler Capex Trajectory Is the Single Most Important Variable, and the Consensus View Implies a Hard Landing

The numbers are stark. According to consensus analyst projections compiled by Bloomberg, the five largest US hyperscalers—Google, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle—are on track to spend approximately $758 billion in capital expenditure in 2026, representing 100% year-over-year growth. This is an extraordinary ramp, unprecedented in scale and speed. But the consensus also projects that growth decelerates sharply to 22% in 2027, then to 7% in 2028, and to just 1-2% in 2029 and 2030.

Taken at face value, this trajectory implies that the hyperscalers are front-loading their AI infrastructure buildout, and that the peak of the investment cycle is already visible. If the consensus is correct, the semiconductor companies that have benefited from this spending surge will face a dramatic slowdown in demand growth within the next 12 to 18 months. The implications for semiconductor earnings, margins, and valuations are severe.

The global investment bank's own analysts project stronger capex growth for 2027, estimating approximately $1,150 billion versus the consensus of $925 billion. This is a meaningful difference, representing roughly 24% more spending. But even this more optimistic view still implies a deceleration from the 100% growth rate of 2026. The question is not whether growth slows, but how much.

The critical insight for investors is that the consensus view is already embedded in market expectations for hyperscaler earnings and, by extension, for semiconductor demand. If the consensus proves too pessimistic, the positive surprise could drive a rotation back into hyperscalers and support semiconductor valuations. If the consensus proves accurate or even optimistic, the downside for semiconductors could be substantial.

This creates a strategic dilemma. The most important variable for the AI trade is the one that is hardest to forecast with confidence. Hyperscaler capex plans are not static; they are influenced by competitive dynamics, technological developments, regulatory changes, and the pace of AI adoption among enterprise customers. No analyst can predict with certainty how these factors will evolve.

## AI Compute Pricing Is the Leading Indicator That Most Investors Are Ignoring

If hyperscaler capex is the most important variable, AI compute pricing is the most informative leading indicator. The price that hyperscalers can charge for access to AI compute capacity—whether through cloud instances, API calls, or dedicated infrastructure—determines their ability to generate returns on their massive capital investments. If compute pricing holds firm or rises, hyperscalers can maintain or improve their profit margins, justifying continued spending. If compute pricing continues to decline, the economics of the entire buildout come into question.

The data from the Compute Desk's Hopper US Index, which tracks on-demand and reserved pricing for renting NVIDIA Hopper GPUs from US neocloud providers, shows that after persistent downward pressure through the end of 2025, there were encouraging signs of improvement in April and May of this year. However, that improvement appeared to subside somewhat in June. Similarly, the LLM Token Expenditure Index, which tracks the cost of accessing large language model outputs, had been rising from late February to the end of May before declining in June.

These are early signals, and they are not yet conclusive. But they deserve far more attention than they currently receive from the broader market. Most investors focus on headline earnings reports, product announcements, and macroeconomic data. The pricing of AI compute is a micro-level metric that directly reflects the supply-demand dynamics of the most critical input in the AI economy.

The pattern that should concern investors is the following: compute pricing has been under structural pressure for most of the past year, with only brief periods of stabilization or improvement. This suggests that the supply of AI compute capacity is growing faster than demand, at least at current price levels. If this continues, hyperscalers will find it increasingly difficult to achieve the returns on invested capital that their stock prices imply. And if returns disappoint, the rational response is to reduce future capex.

The positive scenario—the one the global investment bank's house view favors—requires that AI adoption accelerates sufficiently to absorb the growing supply of compute capacity at stable or improving prices. This is possible, particularly as enterprise AI use cases mature and as AI-driven revenue streams become more visible. But it is not guaranteed.

## The Short Interest Data Reveals a Market That Is Pricing in Different Risks for Different Parts of the AI Value Chain

The short interest data provides a granular view of how sophisticated investors are positioning across the AI ecosystem. The patterns are revealing and, in some cases, counterintuitive.

For hyperscalers, short interest as a percentage of shares outstanding has been on a rising trend since last October, with a further steep increase in May and June. This is consistent with the narrative that investors are concerned about the sustainability of hyperscaler returns on their massive capex programs. The market is already expressing skepticism about the ability of these companies to monetize their investments.

For semiconductors excluding memory, short interest has been rising more recently during May and June, but it remains below the levels seen a year ago. This suggests that while some investors are beginning to hedge semiconductor exposure, the trade is not yet universally questioned. There is still a base of conviction that semiconductor companies will continue to benefit from AI demand regardless of what happens to hyperscaler margins.

For the JPM AI adoption beneficiaries basket, short interest remains rather contained since early April and stands below its level at the start of the year. This is an interesting data point. It suggests that the market is not yet pricing in significant risk for companies that are positioned to benefit from AI adoption—software platforms, data analytics firms, enterprise AI tools. If anything, this part of the AI value chain appears to be attracting less skepticism than the infrastructure layer.

For the JPM AI vulnerable basket, short interest has been rising steadily since the start of 2025 and stands significantly above the levels of the other three groups. This makes intuitive sense. Companies whose business models are threatened by AI—those in traditional software, business process outsourcing, or content creation—are seeing increased bearish positioning.

The overall picture is one of nuanced differentiation. The market is not treating AI as a monolithic theme. It is distinguishing between hyperscalers, which face skepticism about returns; semiconductors, which still command significant conviction; AI beneficiaries, which are largely uncontested; and AI vulnerable companies, which are increasingly being shorted.

What is missing from this picture is a clear signal about the direction of the semiconductor-hyperscaler divergence. The short interest data shows rising concern about hyperscalers and some concern about semiconductors, but it does not tell us whether the performance gap will close through hyperscaler outperformance or semiconductor underperformance.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the AI Value Chain Divergence

Investors need a framework for making portfolio decisions in the face of this uncertainty. The following approach is designed to be systematic rather than prescriptive, recognizing that different investors have different risk tolerances, time horizons, and conviction levels.

First, assess your exposure to the semiconductor trade relative to your broader AI allocation. If you have allowed semiconductor positions to grow through price appreciation without rebalancing, you may have a larger concentration than you intend. The first step is measurement. Calculate the percentage of your AI-related holdings that are in semiconductor stocks versus hyperscalers versus AI beneficiaries. If the semiconductor allocation is significantly higher than it was six months ago, you have a concentration that needs to be evaluated.

Second, identify your primary scenario. Do you believe that hyperscaler monetization will accelerate, justifying continued capex growth and closing the performance gap from above? Or do you believe that the consensus view of a sharp capex deceleration is more likely, implying that semiconductor outperformance has peaked? Your answer to this question should drive your positioning.

If you favor the positive scenario, your portfolio should be tilted toward hyperscalers and AI beneficiaries. These are the parts of the value chain that would benefit most from accelerating monetization. You may also want to maintain semiconductor exposure, but with the understanding that the relative outperformance of semiconductors is likely to narrow.

If you favor the negative scenario, you should reduce semiconductor exposure and consider hedging strategies. The short interest data suggests that the market is already pricing in some of this risk, but not all of it. A sharp deceleration in hyperscaler capex would likely trigger a significant correction in semiconductor stocks, and the crowded positioning in that trade would amplify the downside.

Third, monitor the leading indicators. AI compute pricing is the most important real-time signal. If pricing stabilizes or improves, it supports the positive scenario. If pricing continues to decline, it supports the negative scenario. Similarly, pay attention to hyperscaler earnings calls and capex guidance. Any signal that companies are reassessing their spending plans should be taken seriously.

Fourth, consider the macro backdrop. US money creation is on track to step up from $1.6 trillion in 2025 to $1.8 trillion in 2026, driven by US banks' willingness to expand their balance sheets and the Federal Reserve's modest balance sheet expansion. This liquidity should provide support to US financial assets, particularly equities. However, cash allocations among non-bank investors are low, which creates vulnerability to negative shocks. The positive liquidity backdrop does not eliminate the risk of a sector-specific correction in semiconductors.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer Yet

The report provides a rigorous framework for thinking about the AI value chain divergence, but it leaves several important questions unresolved. The most critical is the timing of any convergence. The performance gap has persisted for nine months, and the conditions that have sustained it—record hyperscaler capex growth, strong semiconductor earnings, and investor enthusiasm for the AI theme—show no immediate signs of reversing. The report identifies the risk of a correction but does not provide a catalyst or a timeline.

A second unresolved question is the role of AI model providers and enterprise users in the value chain. The report focuses primarily on hyperscalers and semiconductor companies, but the ultimate return on AI investment depends on whether end users—businesses and consumers—actually derive sufficient value from AI applications to justify the spending. This is the hardest variable to forecast and the one that will ultimately determine whether the current investment cycle is sustainable.

A third question is the impact of regulatory and geopolitical developments. The AI industry is increasingly subject to government scrutiny, export controls, and national security considerations. Changes in the regulatory environment could dramatically alter the economics of AI infrastructure investment. The report does not address this dimension in detail.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are inherent uncertainties that investors must navigate. The value of the report is that it identifies the key variables and provides a framework for monitoring them.

## The Next Phase of the AI Trade Will Test Conviction

The AI investment theme has been remarkably durable. It has survived tariff shocks, geopolitical tensions, interest rate uncertainty, and multiple corrections in broader equity markets. The reason is simple: the potential of AI is real, and the spending to capture that potential is unprecedented.

But durability is not the same as inevitability. The current divergence between semiconductors and hyperscalers is a structural tension that must be resolved. The resolution could be benign—hyperscalers demonstrate monetization, margins improve, and the performance gap closes through broad-based AI value creation. Or it could be painful—capex decelerates, semiconductor earnings disappoint, and the AI trade experiences a significant correction.

Investors who have benefited from the semiconductor-led rally should not assume that the trend will continue indefinitely. The data suggests that the risks are rising, even if the timing of any resolution remains uncertain. The most prudent approach is to reassess positioning, identify leading indicators, and prepare for both scenarios.

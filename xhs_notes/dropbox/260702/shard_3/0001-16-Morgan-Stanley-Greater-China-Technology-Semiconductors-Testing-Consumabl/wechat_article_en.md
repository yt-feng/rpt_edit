# Testing Consumables Are About to Get More Expensive: Why Probe Card and Test Socket Vendors Finally Have Pricing Power

The semiconductor testing consumables market is approaching an inflection point that most investors are not pricing in. Rising precious metal costs and a structural shortage in test pin capacity are creating conditions for a sustained price increase in probe cards and test sockets—a dynamic that will transfer pricing power from semiconductor manufacturers back to testing equipment suppliers. This is not a cyclical blip. It is a structural shift in the bargaining dynamics of the semiconductor testing value chain, and it will have material implications for the earnings trajectories of leading testing consumables vendors over the next 12 to 18 months.

The market has been fixated on the front-end semiconductor equipment cycle and the AI-driven demand for advanced packaging. But the testing consumables segment, which sits at the intersection of process control and final yield, has been quietly building a supply-demand imbalance that now demands attention. A global investment bank report from late June 2026 makes the case that probe card and test socket vendors are likely to raise prices, passing on cost increases to customers in a manner similar to what has already occurred in other segments of the semiconductor industry. The argument is grounded in observable supply chain dynamics: precious metal prices have been climbing, and test pin capacity is severely constrained.

The implications extend beyond near-term earnings. A price increase in testing consumables would confirm that the semiconductor industry's pricing power is no longer concentrated solely in foundries and memory makers. It would signal that the testing infrastructure—long considered a commoditized afterthought—is becoming a bottleneck that commands economic rent. For investors, this means that the valuation frameworks used for testing consumables companies need to be recalibrated. The current multiples, which appear elevated on trailing earnings, may prove to be entry points if earnings compound at rates that current consensus estimates do not capture.

## The Combination of Rising Precious Metal Costs and Test Pin Shortage Creates a Rare Window for Price Increases

The case for price increases in testing consumables rests on two independent but reinforcing pressures. The first is the rising cost of precious metals. Probe cards and test sockets rely on materials such as palladium, platinum, and gold for their contact pins and interface structures. These metals have been on a sustained upward trajectory, driven by supply constraints and demand from other industrial applications. Testing consumables vendors cannot absorb these input cost increases indefinitely, especially when their margins are already under pressure from previous rounds of pricing concessions.

The second and more structurally significant factor is the severe shortage in test pin capacity. Test pins are the microscopic contact elements that connect the testing equipment to the semiconductor die or package. They are precision components that require specialized manufacturing processes and significant lead times to produce. The current shortage is not a temporary mismatch between supply and demand. It reflects a multi-year underinvestment in testing pin capacity, combined with a sudden surge in demand driven by the ramp of AI and high-performance computing chips. These chips require more extensive testing, more pins per device, and higher reliability standards, all of which consume pin capacity at a rate that outstrips the industry's ability to add new supply.

When input costs rise and capacity is constrained, the textbook outcome is price increases. What makes this moment unusual is that testing consumables vendors have historically struggled to pass through cost increases. Their customers—OSATs, integrated device manufacturers, and foundries—have significant purchasing power and have pushed back against price hikes in the past. But the current combination of rising costs and capacity scarcity has shifted the balance. Vendors can now credibly threaten to allocate limited capacity to customers who accept price adjustments, creating a dynamic where price increases become not just possible but probable.

## The AI and HPC Ramp Is Creating Demand That Testing Infrastructure Was Not Built to Handle

The demand shock driving the testing consumables shortage originates from a single source: the explosive growth in AI and high-performance computing chips. These chips are larger, more complex, and require more extensive testing than any previous generation of semiconductors. The testing requirements for AI accelerators and HPC processors include not only functional testing but also parametric testing, burn-in, and system-level testing. Each of these steps consumes test pins and probe card cycles, and the cumulative demand is far exceeding the capacity that was built during the previous cycle.

What is less understood is that the testing intensity per chip is increasing even as chip volumes grow. AI chips are not tested at the same rate as conventional logic chips. They are tested more times, at more temperature points, and across more operating conditions. This means that the testing consumables market is growing faster than the underlying chip market, and the growth rate is accelerating as AI chips move from development to high-volume production. The global investment bank report notes that June results for leading testing consumables vendors are likely to be very strong, driven by robust AI and HPC ramp. This is not a one-quarter phenomenon. It is the beginning of a multi-year cycle where testing demand grows faster than supply.

The incremental contribution from display driver ICs adds another layer of demand. The DDIC market has been in a recovery phase, and as smartphone and display panel volumes increase, so does the need for probe cards and test sockets for these devices. While DDICs are lower-value testing applications compared to AI chips, they consume capacity that could otherwise be allocated to higher-margin testing. The combination of high-value AI testing and volume-driven DDIC testing is creating a demand profile that leaves little room for excess capacity.

## MPI and WinWay Are Positioned to Capture the Upside, but the Market Is Still Pricing in the Old Earnings Trajectory

The two companies most directly exposed to this testing consumables pricing cycle are MPI Corporation and WinWay Technology. Both are leading suppliers of probe cards and test sockets, and both have been cited in the global investment bank report as likely beneficiaries of the pricing environment. MPI's share price has lagged recently, weighed down by weak sentiment around co-packaged optics technology, which is a separate and unrelated product line. The market has conflated MPI's CPO exposure with its core testing business, creating a valuation discount that does not reflect the underlying demand dynamics.

The investment bank's analysis suggests that MPI's testing demand remains robust regardless of the technology choices made by customers in the optical engine space. Insertion test demand for AI and HPC chips does not depend on whether the final interconnect technology uses CPO or an alternative approach. The testing requirements are determined by the chip architecture and the need to ensure yield, not by the packaging technology. This distinction is critical. Investors who are avoiding MPI because of CPO uncertainty are missing the structural growth in its core testing business.

WinWay, meanwhile, is beginning to ramp new capacity to fulfill a strong backlog. The company has been capacity-constrained, and the addition of new manufacturing lines starting in June should allow it to convert backlog into revenue. The earnings growth trajectory for WinWay is steep, with the investment bank projecting a 111% earnings CAGR from 2025 to 2028. At 39 times 2027 estimated earnings, the valuation is not cheap, but it becomes more reasonable when measured against the growth rate. The question for investors is whether the market will re-rate these companies as the pricing power narrative becomes more widely understood.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: How Durable Is the Pricing Power, and Will New Supply Erode It?

The global investment bank report makes a compelling case for near-term price increases, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most critical of these is the durability of the pricing power. If testing consumables vendors raise prices now, how long will it take for new capacity to come online and erode that pricing power? The report notes that new supply takes time to ramp, but it does not specify the lead times or the magnitude of capacity additions that are in the pipeline.

The second unresolved question is the elasticity of demand. How much price increase can testing consumables vendors push through before customers begin to seek alternatives or redesign their testing strategies? Large OSATs and foundries have engineering teams that can optimize test flows and reduce the number of test insertions. If prices rise too quickly, customers may invest in testing efficiency improvements that permanently reduce the demand for probe cards and test sockets. The report does not model this behavioral response.

The third question is about the competitive dynamics within the testing consumables industry. If MPI and WinWay raise prices, will competitors follow, or will they hold prices steady to gain market share? The pricing power of any individual vendor depends on the capacity utilization of the entire industry, not just that vendor's own factory. If some vendors choose to compete on price rather than follow the market leaders, the pricing cycle could be shorter and less profitable than the report implies.

These open questions do not invalidate the thesis, but they do suggest that the investment case requires monitoring. The pricing power is real, but it is not permanent, and the timing of the reversion matters for valuation.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Evaluate Testing Consumables Exposure

For investors considering exposure to testing consumables, the key is to distinguish between companies that have structural pricing power and those that are merely riding a cyclical wave. The following framework can help make that distinction.

First, assess the company's exposure to precious metal costs. Companies that have long-term supply contracts or hedging programs are better positioned to manage input cost volatility. Companies that purchase metals on the spot market will see margins squeezed between price increases and customer pushback, even if they eventually pass through costs.

Second, evaluate the company's test pin capacity relative to its order book. Companies that are running at high utilization rates and have long lead times for new capacity have the most pricing power. Companies with excess capacity or short lead times will find it harder to raise prices because customers can switch suppliers more easily.

Third, consider the customer concentration. Companies that serve a diversified base of OSATs, foundries, and IDMs have more pricing power than companies that depend on a single large customer. A concentrated customer base gives the buyer leverage in price negotiations, especially if the customer also supplies the testing consumables vendor with raw materials or technology.

Fourth, look at the technology differentiation. Companies that offer proprietary probe card designs or specialized test socket solutions have pricing power that commodity suppliers do not. The testing of AI and HPC chips requires high-precision, high-reliability components, and suppliers that can deliver those components consistently have a competitive moat.

Fifth, monitor the capacity announcements from the industry. If multiple testing consumables vendors announce capacity expansions simultaneously, it could signal that the supply shortage will be resolved sooner than expected. Conversely, if capacity additions are slow and lumpy, the pricing cycle could extend for several years.

## The Market Has Not Yet Priced In the Full Earnings Upside

The most important conclusion from the global investment bank report is that the earnings trajectories for MPI and WinWay are likely to exceed current consensus estimates. The market is still pricing these companies based on historical growth rates and historical margins. If testing consumables prices increase by even a mid-single-digit percentage, the impact on earnings will be substantial because the incremental revenue flows through to operating income with high operating leverage.

MPI's current valuation of 43 times 2027 estimated earnings may appear expensive, but it must be measured against a 105% earnings CAGR from 2025 to 2028. A company growing earnings at that rate can support a higher multiple than a company growing at 15%. The key question is whether the growth is sustainable, and the answer depends on the durability of the pricing power and the demand from AI and HPC.

WinWay's valuation is similar: 39 times 2027 estimated earnings against a 111% earnings CAGR. The risk is that the market has already priced in a successful ramp of new capacity and a continuation of strong demand. If either of those assumptions fails, the downside could be significant. But the reward for being correct is a multi-year compounder that the market has not yet fully appreciated.

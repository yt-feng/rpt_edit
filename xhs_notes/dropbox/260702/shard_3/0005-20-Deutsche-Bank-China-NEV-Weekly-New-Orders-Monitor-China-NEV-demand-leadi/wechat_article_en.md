# China’s NEV Weekly Order Data Reveals a Market That Is No Longer Rising Together

The most important signal in China’s new energy vehicle market right now is not the headline sales number for the month. It is the divergence in weekly order flow among the leading automakers. According to the latest weekly new orders data through June 28, 2026, the market is entering a phase where aggregate demand growth masks a brutal redistribution of market share. This is not a cyclical pause. It is a structural sorting event in which winners are consolidating their positions while challengers face mounting pressure to prove their models are sustainable.

The data, drawn from a global investment bank’s weekly monitor, covers the ten key NEV players in China. In the week of June 22-28, total orders across these tracked OEMs fell by a meaningful margin compared to the prior week. But the aggregate number is misleading. Several companies posted strong sequential gains, while others saw double-digit declines. The dispersion is too wide to be explained by seasonal noise or a single promotional event. It suggests that consumer preferences are shifting in ways that reward specific product strategies and punish others.

This is the moment for investors and strategists to stop looking at the NEV market as a single growth story and start analyzing it as a portfolio of differentiated business models. The weekly order data is the most granular leading indicator available, and it is telling a story that monthly delivery numbers will only confirm weeks later. The market is not slowing down. It is recalibrating. And the companies that understand which signals matter most will be the ones that survive the coming shakeout.

## The Week-on-Week Order Data Exposes a Market That Is No Longer Moving in Sync

The headline figure for the week ending June 28 shows a clear sequential decline. BYD, the industry leader, recorded 70,600 new orders, down 34% from the prior week’s 106,800. Tesla’s orders fell 11% to 11,200. NIO dropped 20% to 11,600. XPeng fell 19% to 7,300. Xiaomi’s orders declined 15% to 6,100. On the surface, this looks like a broad-based softening. But the pattern changes dramatically when you look at the other side of the table.

Li Auto posted 9,900 new orders, a 67% week-on-week surge. Geely, including its Zeekr and Galaxy brands, recorded 28,300 orders, a 17% increase. Leap Motor added 15,000 orders, up 6%. Even HIMA, the alliance that includes AITO, managed 10,800 orders, down only 7% from an already strong prior week. The contrast could not be starker. While some of the most visible names in the industry are losing momentum, a smaller group of players is accelerating.

The so-what here is critical. The market is not experiencing uniform demand weakness. It is experiencing a shift in where demand is flowing. The companies that gained share this week are not necessarily the cheapest or the most heavily marketed. They are the ones that have executed on specific product niches. Li Auto’s extended-range SUVs continue to resonate with family buyers who want electric driving without range anxiety. Geely’s multi-brand strategy is giving it coverage across price points. Leap Motor is proving that a focused, cost-efficient approach can generate consistent order flow even in a crowded segment.

This divergence means that aggregate market forecasts are becoming less useful. The question for investors is no longer “how many NEVs will China sell next month.” It is “which companies are winning the orders that exist.”

## Year-on-Year Comparisons Confirm That Structural Winners Are Emerging, Not Just Cyclical Ones

The year-on-year comparison in the data adds another layer of conviction. For the companies that had comparable data, the results are telling. NIO’s orders were up 132% versus the same week last year. Li Auto was up 30%. HIMA was up 8%. BYD was down 14%. Tesla was down 3%. XPeng was down 22%. Xiaomi’s comparison is distorted by a massive order week in the prior year, but the raw numbers suggest a normalization rather than a collapse.

The pattern here is consistent with the week-on-week data but even more revealing. The companies that are growing year-on-year are not the ones with the highest brand recognition or the longest history. They are the ones that have made specific strategic pivots. NIO’s surge reflects the successful launch of its lower-priced Onvo brand, which has expanded its addressable market without cannibalizing its premium lineup. Li Auto’s consistent growth shows that its product roadmap is aligned with what Chinese families actually want. HIMA’s modest but positive growth indicates that the Huawei-backed ecosystem is gaining traction, albeit more slowly than some hoped.

The year-on-year declines for BYD and Tesla are the most significant signals. These are the two largest players in the global NEV market, and both are seeing order erosion relative to last year. For BYD, the decline may reflect a normalization after an extraordinary growth period, but it also raises questions about whether the company can maintain its dominance as competitors close the gap in technology and cost. For Tesla, the decline is more concerning because it comes despite aggressive price cuts and a well-established brand. The Chinese market may be telling Tesla that its product cycle is aging and that local competitors are offering more compelling options at similar or lower prices.

The implication is clear. The NEV market in China is no longer a rising tide that lifts all boats. It is a zero-sum game in which order growth for some comes at the expense of others. The companies that are winning are those that have adapted their product strategy to the specific needs of the Chinese consumer in 2026, not those that are resting on past success.

## The Data Also Raises Questions That the Report Does Not Fully Answer

While the weekly order data is invaluable as a real-time signal, it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most obvious is the sustainability of the order flows. A single week of strong orders could be driven by a promotional event, a new model launch, or even a data anomaly. The report provides a snapshot, not a trend. Without several more weeks of data, it is impossible to know whether Li Auto’s 67% jump is the start of a new growth trajectory or a one-time spike.

Another open question is the profitability of the orders being captured. The data tracks order volume, not revenue or margin. A company like Leap Motor may be generating solid order numbers, but if those orders come from low-margin vehicles, the financial outcome may be less attractive than the volume suggests. Conversely, NIO’s 132% year-on-year growth may be driven by lower-priced models that compress margins. The order data is a necessary but insufficient input for investment decisions. It must be combined with pricing, cost, and margin analysis to produce a complete picture.

A third gap is the lack of granularity on model mix. The data aggregates orders at the brand or group level, but within each company, the performance of individual models can vary dramatically. For example, BYD’s overall decline may be driven by weakness in its Dynasty series while its Ocean series holds steady. Without model-level data, it is difficult to diagnose the root cause of the order shifts or to predict how they will evolve.

These gaps are not flaws in the report. They are inherent limitations of any high-frequency indicator. The value of the weekly order data is that it raises the right questions and forces investors to look beyond the monthly delivery headlines. But the answers require deeper analysis, including channel checks, pricing surveys, and financial model updates.

## A Decision Framework for Using Weekly Order Data in Investment Strategy

For investors and strategists who want to use this data effectively, the key is to treat it as a directional signal rather than a precise forecast. The weekly order numbers are noisy. They are influenced by holidays, promotional campaigns, and even weather. But when the noise is filtered out, the signal is powerful. The following framework can help translate the data into actionable insights.

First, focus on divergence rather than aggregates. The most important information in this week’s data is not the total order number. It is the spread between the best and worst performers. When the spread widens, it indicates that market share is shifting. Investors should overweight companies that are consistently on the positive side of the spread and underweight those that are consistently on the negative side.

Second, compare order trends to delivery trends. The weekly order data is a leading indicator. It should be compared to monthly delivery numbers to identify discrepancies. If orders are rising but deliveries are flat, it may indicate production constraints. If orders are falling but deliveries are stable, it may indicate a backlog being worked through. The gap between orders and deliveries is itself a valuable signal.

Third, use year-on-year comparisons to filter out noise. Week-on-week volatility is high. Year-on-year comparisons smooth out seasonal effects and provide a clearer picture of underlying demand trends. A company that is growing orders year-on-year in a flat market is gaining share. A company that is declining year-on-year in a growing market is losing it.

Fourth, triangulate with pricing and incentive data. Order volume alone does not tell the full story. If a company is generating strong orders through aggressive discounts, the volume may come at the expense of profitability. Investors should monitor whether order growth is accompanied by stable or improving pricing. If not, the volume may be unsustainable.

Finally, watch for inflection points. The most valuable use of weekly data is to identify when a trend is changing. A company that has been declining for weeks and suddenly posts a strong week may be at an inflection point. Conversely, a company that has been growing and suddenly stalls may be losing momentum. The weekly data is most useful not for confirming what you already know, but for catching changes early.

## The Full Picture Requires Granular Data and Continuous Monitoring

This analysis is based on a single week of data from a single source. It is enough to identify patterns and raise questions, but it is not enough to draw firm conclusions. The real value of the weekly order monitor is realized over time, as the data accumulates and trends become clearer. Investors who want to use this information effectively need access to the full historical series, the underlying charts, and the analyst commentary that accompanies each week’s release.

The report from which this data is drawn includes detailed charts for each major automaker, showing the trajectory of weekly orders over multiple weeks and months. These charts reveal patterns that are invisible in a single week of numbers. They show whether a company is in a sustained uptrend, a cyclical downturn, or a period of consolidation. They also show how each company’s order flow responds to model launches, price changes, and competitive actions.

For serious investors, the weekly order monitor is not a one-time read. It is a tool that must be tracked continuously, with each new data point adding to the picture. The companies that are gaining share today may not be the same ones that gain share next quarter. The only way to stay ahead is to monitor the data in real time and adjust your thesis as the evidence accumulates.

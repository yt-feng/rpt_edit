# The Strait of Hormuz Crisis Exposed a Dangerous Asymmetry in Global Fertilizer Risk: Nitrogen Is a Ticking Clock, Phosphate Is a Slow Erosion

The reopening of the Strait of Hormuz in mid-June brought immediate relief to fertilizer markets, but this relief is deceptive. The crisis was not a single event with a clean resolution; it was a stress test that revealed two fundamentally different risk profiles embedded in the global fertilizer system. One is acute and time-sensitive. The other is chronic and cumulative. Investors and agricultural strategists who treat the Hormuz disruption as a resolved incident are missing the deeper structural vulnerability that remains.

Roughly one-third of global fertilizer trade flows through the Strait of Hormuz. When the route was disrupted in the second quarter, the market reacted sharply. Urea prices spiked roughly 50 percent at the peak, only to retrace as flows resumed. Yet the price recovery masked a dangerous asymmetry. For nitrogen fertilizers, the timing of the disruption was mercifully benign. For phosphates, the timing was anything but. And the divergence between these two commodity classes is not just a market footnote; it is a strategic distinction that should reshape how agricultural supply chains are managed, hedged, and invested in.

The report from a global investment bank makes this distinction clear, but its implications extend far beyond the immediate data. The real question is not whether the Strait of Hormuz will close again. It is whether the global food system has built sufficient resilience into its fertilizer procurement cycles to absorb a disruption that hits at the wrong moment. The answer, as this analysis will show, is deeply unsettling.

## Nitrogen Fertilizers Face a Narrow Window of Catastrophic Exposure That the Market Is Not Pricing Correctly

Nitrogen fertilizers are the most time-critical input in global grain production. Corn, wheat, and rice all require nitrogen application each season for optimal yields. There is no substitution in the short term, and there is no stockpiling that can bridge a multi-month supply gap. When nitrogen is not available at planting time, yields drop immediately and irreversibly.

The second quarter disruption at Hormuz occurred during a seasonal lull. Northern Hemisphere farmers had largely completed their procurement in the first quarter, before the Strait closed. The market absorbed the shock with a price spike that subsequently faded. But this was luck, not resilience. The critical procurement season for major nitrogen importers falls in the third quarter, when Brazil prepares for its corn planting, India secures fertilizer for rice and sugarcane, and the European Union gears up for winter wheat.

If a similar disruption re-emerges in Q3, the consequences would be materially worse. Farmers would face a choice between paying dramatically higher prices for whatever supply remains or reducing application rates. For nitrogen, reduced application is not a gradual degradation; it is an immediate production loss. The market is currently pricing the Hormuz risk as a binary event that has passed. In reality, it is a recurring threat with a seasonal trigger. The probability of a Q3 disruption may be low, but the impact asymmetry is extreme. The market is not discounting this asymmetry because it is focused on the average, not the tail.

The implication for strategic planners is clear. Nitrogen procurement contracts should be structured with explicit disruption clauses tied to seasonal windows. Inventory buffers should be built ahead of Q3, not averaged across the year. And hedging strategies should overweight Q3 exposure relative to the rest of the year, even if current prices do not signal elevated risk. The market is not pricing the seasonality of disruption correctly, and that mispricing creates both risk and opportunity.

## Phosphate Fertilizers Create a Slow-Burning Crisis in Brazilian Soybean Production That Is Already Visible in the Data

Phosphate fertilizers operate on a different timescale. Unlike nitrogen, phosphate can be drawn down from soil reserves in the short term without an immediate yield penalty. This creates a dangerous illusion of safety. A farmer who under-applies phosphate in one season sees no visible damage. The soil bank covers the deficit. But repeated under-application depletes those reserves, gradually weakening soil quality, reducing crop resilience, and eventually lowering productivity in a way that is difficult to reverse.

Brazilian soybean farmers are the canary in this coal mine. Brazil accounts for 62 percent of global soybean exports and depends on imports for roughly 80 percent of its phosphate fertilizer needs. The June-July procurement window is critical, as September planting approaches. Yet by mid-June, Brazilian soybean growers had secured only about 68 percent of their expected fertilizer demand, compared to a historical average of roughly 75 percent. Even after the Strait reopened, phosphate prices have not declined significantly. Affordability has been constrained since the 2022 Russia-Ukraine disruption, and the latest shock has kept farmers under persistent pressure.

The data point that should concern investors most is not the current procurement gap, but its cumulative effect. Brazilian soybean farmers have been operating under affordability pressure for multiple seasons. Each season of under-application reduces the soil phosphate bank by a small margin. Over several years, those margins add up to a structural decline in soil fertility. The risk is not a sudden collapse in Brazilian soybean production, but a gradual erosion of yield potential that becomes increasingly difficult and expensive to reverse.

This is a slow-moving crisis that does not trigger the same market alarms as a price spike. But it is arguably more consequential for long-term supply dynamics. If Brazilian soil quality deteriorates meaningfully, the global soybean market would face a supply shock that cannot be quickly remedied by higher prices or alternative suppliers. The investment implication is that soybean prices should carry a structural premium reflecting this cumulative soil degradation risk, a premium that is currently absent from forward curves.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question It Does Not Fully Answer: How Much Soil Bank Has Already Been Drawn Down?

The global investment bank report provides an excellent framework for understanding the immediate dynamics of fertilizer disruption. It distinguishes clearly between the acute risk for nitrogen and the chronic risk for phosphates. It identifies the specific procurement windows and the current shortfall in Brazilian phosphate coverage. What it does not answer, and what the market desperately needs to understand, is the cumulative stock of soil phosphate reserves that have been depleted over the past two seasons.

The concept of a soil bank is well understood in agronomy, but it is rarely quantified in a way that feeds into commodity price models. We know that Brazilian farmers have been under financial pressure since 2022. We know that phosphate affordability has deteriorated. We know that procurement rates have been below historical averages. But we do not have a clear, aggregated estimate of how much soil phosphate has been drawn down across Brazil's major soybean-producing regions.

This is the missing variable that connects the current fertilizer disruption to future production outcomes. If the soil bank remains adequate, the current procurement gap is a temporary concern that can be corrected in the next season. If the soil bank has been significantly depleted, the gap represents a permanent loss of productive capacity that will manifest in lower yields over the next two to three years, regardless of what happens to fertilizer prices.

The report implicitly calls for this analysis by highlighting the cumulative nature of phosphate risk. A natural next step for research would be to model the trajectory of Brazilian soil phosphate reserves under different scenarios of fertilizer affordability and procurement rates. Until that analysis is available, investors are flying partially blind on one of the most important structural risks in global agriculture.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating Fertilizer Risk: Three Distinct Strategies Based on Time Horizon and Commodity Type

The asymmetry between nitrogen and phosphate risk demands a differentiated approach to investment and procurement strategy. A single fertilizer hedging or investment strategy is almost certainly suboptimal because it fails to account for the fundamentally different risk profiles of these two commodity classes.

For nitrogen, the strategy should be event-driven and seasonally concentrated. The primary risk is a Q3 disruption that coincides with peak procurement. The appropriate response is to build inventory buffers ahead of Q3, structure procurement contracts with force majeure protections that are seasonally specific, and hedge Q3 price exposure more aggressively than other quarters. The cost of this approach is the carrying cost of inventory and the premium for seasonal hedges. But these costs are small relative to the potential loss from an unhedged Q3 disruption.

For phosphate, the strategy should be structural and cumulative. The primary risk is not a single disruption but a multi-year erosion of soil fertility driven by persistent affordability constraints. The appropriate response is to invest in long-term soil health monitoring, develop relationships with suppliers that offer multi-year contracts rather than spot procurement, and price the risk of cumulative yield decline into long-term soybean positions. This is not a trading strategy; it is a portfolio construction approach that accounts for a slow-moving but compounding risk.

For diversified agricultural investors, the framework suggests a barbell approach. On one side, maintain tactical flexibility and event-driven hedging for nitrogen exposure. On the other side, build structural positions that benefit from the gradual tightening of phosphate-constrained supply, particularly in soybeans. The middle ground of generic commodity exposure is the least defensible position, as it fails to capture either the tactical opportunity in nitrogen or the structural opportunity in phosphates.

The framework also highlights a broader lesson for commodity investors. Not all supply disruptions are alike. Some are acute shocks that resolve quickly and leave no lasting damage. Others are chronic pressures that accumulate quietly and transform market structure over multiple years. The ability to distinguish between these two types of risk, and to allocate capital accordingly, is the difference between a reactive commodity strategy and a genuinely strategic one.

# US Exceptionalism Is Back, but the Regime Has Changed: Higher Volatility, Thinner Patience, and a New Macro Normal

The dominant narrative emerging from Washington is not one of caution, but of conviction. US financial exceptionalism, fueled by an unprecedented wave of AI-led capital expenditure, easy financial conditions, and a deregulatory impulse, is being framed as the structural foundation for a new "Golden Age." The thesis is clear: the US economy can run hot, absorb geopolitical shocks like the Iran conflict, and still deliver upside growth risk. Yet beneath this confident surface lies a far more complex and fragile reality. The regime has shifted. Inflation is structurally firmer, term premia are structurally higher, and daily market moves are outsized and becoming normalized. The old playbook of expecting rates to revert to pre-pandemic levels is dead. The key question is not whether the US can maintain its exceptionalism, but at what cost and for how long.

This is not a return to the Great Moderation. It is a new regime defined by fatter tails—where supply shocks are more frequent, fiscal dynamics matter more, and the patience of both markets and policymakers is demonstrably thinner. The report from a global investment bank, based on meetings with policymakers, official creditors, and independent experts in Washington, reveals a consensus that the macro environment has fundamentally changed, even as the immediate outlook for US growth appears robust. The strategic implication for investors is profound: positioning for a world where the old certainties of low inflation, stable rates, and predictable geopolitics are no longer available.

## The AI-Driven Growth Story Is Real, but Its Distributional and Inflationary Consequences Are the Next Fault Line

The most powerful force sustaining US exceptionalism is the AI capex and data-center buildout. This is not a speculative narrative. The data is concrete: hyperscaler companies are guiding to approximately $730 billion in capex this year, with projections exceeding $900 billion by 2027, and total AI-related investment to the end of the decade could reach $5.5 trillion. The impact on earnings is already visible, with consensus earnings growth revised up roughly 20% over the next two years. AI data center debt issuance has surpassed $300 billion, making it the largest "sector" in the US High Grade corporate bond market. This is a structural shift in the composition of US capital formation and corporate finance.

The productivity potential is equally significant. The World Bank estimates that if AI delivers a transformative productivity uplift, global potential growth could move back toward 2010 levels, making the 2030s the strongest period of global growth in decades. The range of estimates is wide—from a modest 0.2-0.3 percentage point increase in the near term to a 2.75 percentage point boost over the long term—but the direction is unambiguous. The US is the primary beneficiary, and this concentration of gains reinforces its financial exceptionalism relative to Europe, the UK, and emerging markets.

However, the politics of AI have arrived as the next major fault line. The report flags backlash risks building around income inequality, datacenter siting disputes, and questions of regulatory authority and jurisdiction. As the share of capital in national income rises with each technological wave, distributional risks become unavoidable. The labor share is expected to decline, and inequality is likely to increase unless mitigated by policy. This is not a distant concern. It is a live political issue that will shape the regulatory environment and potentially the pace of AI deployment. The question is whether the productivity gains will be broad enough to offset the social and political friction they generate.

## The New Macro Regime Is Structurally Higher Inflation, Higher Term Premia, and a Fed That May Need to Hike

The report's second major insight is that the macro regime has normalized at a higher level of inflation and volatility. This is not a temporary phenomenon. Supply-side shocks from trade fragmentation, geopolitical conflict, and AI investment are all inflationary in the near term. The World Bank projects global headline inflation to pick up to 4% this year, and the share of economies with inflation above target remains elevated. The era of the Great Moderation, where central banks could reliably guide inflation back to target with minimal disruption, is over.

The Federal Reserve is now operating in a fundamentally different environment. The June dot plot shows nine members anticipating rate hikes this year, with six looking for two or more moves. Core inflation in 2026 is projected at 3.3%, moving to 2.5% in 2027, but the path is uncertain. The FOMC's focus has shifted decisively toward inflation, and the removal of forward guidance—a policy advocated by Fed Chair Warsh—is seen as potentially contributing to structurally higher volatility. The absence of any communication means the loss of an asymmetric easing signal, leaving markets to navigate without the traditional anchor.

The strategic implication is that "higher for longer" is now the base case, and the risk is tilted toward earlier and more aggressive rate action than many expect. The report notes that most investors see the risk of earlier Fed hikes than the JPM forecast of the second half of 2027. The real neutral rate, absent a reduction in inflation, may require 50-100 basis points of tightening to reassert a restrictive stance. This is a critical input for any portfolio construction. The days of expecting a quick return to accommodative policy are gone.

## Geopolitical Risk Is Real but Contained for the US Economy, While the Security Umbrella Weakens

The Iran conflict, while serious, has not derailed the US economic narrative. The report highlights that the US economy is far less vulnerable to geopolitical oil price shocks than in the past. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas shows that the response of US real GDP growth to an oil price shock is only one-twentieth of what it would have been in 1980. The US has transitioned from a major net oil importer to a net oil exporter, providing a significant buffer. Oil prices are below January 2025 levels, and the JPM commodities team forecasts Brent averaging $86 per barrel in the third quarter of 2026 and declining to $64 per barrel by 2027.

However, the geopolitical consequences extend beyond the immediate economic impact. The report reveals a more lasting consequence: a weaker US security umbrella in the Middle East and higher regional defense spending. The UAE, which absorbed a disproportionate share of Iranian missile and drone fire, is wary of US negotiating tactics. Washington's offer to Tehran of a $300 billion reconstruction package has reinforced Gulf doubts about whether the US will follow through on security commitments when it matters. Allies bearing risk without being consulted, followed by deals that undercut the rationale for the war, is a source of friction that will shape how willingly Gulf states cooperate with Washington on the next phase of Iran policy.

The strategic takeaway is that while the US economy can absorb the direct shock, the geopolitical architecture that has underpinned global stability is eroding. This creates a more fragmented and costly security environment, particularly for the Gulf states, and introduces a persistent tail risk for global energy markets. The question of who bears the cost of regional stability is now open.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Fiscal and Debt Dynamics

The report is remarkably candid about the unsolvable nature of US fiscal dynamics. Debt-to-GDP ratios are rising significantly in both advanced and emerging economies, reducing fiscal space and increasing vulnerability to higher interest rates. A speaker noted that borrowing costs rise sharply once debt-to-GDP passes roughly 25-30%, making higher debt sovereigns much more vulnerable to financing stress. In emerging markets, debt-to-GDP has increased from about 35% historically to around 75%, while debt service ratios have risen from roughly 6-7% to about 10-11%.

Yet the report also notes that fiscal and debt concerns remain notably underweighted in market pricing. The prevailing view is that elevated rates reflect the inflationary regime more so than clear fiscal and institutional risks. This is a critical gap in the analysis. The report does not fully answer what happens when the market begins to price in the fiscal risk premium more aggressively. The speakers suggested that members of Congress are keen to solicit feedback from the private sector on how best to thread the needle between political viability and real change, but no clear path forward was identified. The question remains: at what level of debt-to-GDP and interest rates does the market force a fiscal adjustment, and what would that adjustment look like?

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Navigating the New Regime

For investors, the report provides a clear framework for decision-making. The first axis is the growth-inflation mix. The base case is for above-trend US growth driven by AI investment, combined with structurally higher inflation that keeps the Fed on a tightening bias. This favors assets that benefit from real economic expansion—such as US equities, particularly in the AI and technology sectors—but requires caution on duration-sensitive fixed income. The risk of a mid-cycle tightening by the Fed, involving 50-100 basis points of rate increases, is real and should be hedged.

The second axis is geopolitical and fiscal risk. The US security umbrella is weakening, and fiscal dynamics are deteriorating, but these risks are not yet priced. Investors should consider adding tail-risk hedges for geopolitical shocks, particularly in energy markets, and should monitor the evolution of US Treasury demand as foreign buyers become more price-sensitive. The dollar debasement trade is off the immediate radar, but the structural drivers of dollar strength—strong demand for USD financial assets—could shift if fiscal credibility erodes.

The third axis is the distributional and political consequences of AI. The backlash risks are building, and regulatory uncertainty is likely to increase. Investors should factor in the possibility of new rules around data center siting, energy consumption, and labor market impacts. The AI supercycle is real, but its path will be shaped by political forces that are only beginning to mobilize.

The report leaves several open questions that are essential for any serious investor to consider. How will the Fed balance the need to contain inflation with the risk of triggering a recession in a high-debt environment? Will the productivity gains from AI be broad enough to offset the distributional frictions? And what happens when the market finally demands a fiscal reckoning? These are not questions with easy answers, but they define the strategic landscape for the next several years.

# Store-Level Economics, Not Store Count, Will Determine Wanchen's Long-Term Value

The dominant narrative in China's value retail sector has long been about speed: who can open the most stores fastest. Fujian Wanchen Food has been a leading protagonist in this story, with net store openings accelerating from roughly 4,100 in 2025 to already exceeding that full-year figure in just the first half of 2026. Yet the most important signal from recent management meetings is not the pace of expansion. It is the deliberate, disciplined shift toward store-level health as the primary operating metric, even when that means tempering growth. This shift, if sustained, fundamentally changes the risk profile and valuation logic for the company.

Observers have been conditioned to believe that China's value retail story is a winner-take-most land grab, where the largest network wins through scale advantages in procurement, logistics, and brand recognition. Wanchen's management is now making a different argument: that the real competitive moat lies not in store count but in per-store economics, franchisee profitability, and the ability to maintain positive same-store sales growth while scaling. The evidence from the first half of 2026 supports this thesis. Despite unfavorable weather and weak consumption sentiment, average per-store GMV has turned positive year-on-year, and same-store sales growth trends are even stronger once new store dilution is removed. This is not a company merely riding industry tailwinds. It is a company demonstrating that operational refinement can offset macro headwinds.

Why does this matter now? Because the market is pricing Wanchen's stock at 14 times 2026 estimated earnings and 12 times 2027 estimates, a significant discount to its closest peer and to its own historical multiples. This discount reflects skepticism that rapid store growth can continue without degrading unit economics. Management's latest disclosures directly challenge that skepticism. They show that new stores are still achieving payback within two years, that franchisee confidence remains high with existing operators opening second and third stores, and that the company is actively managing the trade-off between opening pace and per-store performance. The question for observers is whether this disciplined approach can persist as competition intensifies and as the company experiments with new formats.

The answer will determine whether Wanchen trades like a structurally compounding business or like a cyclical growth story that eventually hits a wall. This article unpacks the key operating data, the strategic logic behind management's decisions, and the unresolved questions that will shape the company's trajectory over the next 12 to 24 months.

## The Company Has Already Demonstrated That Store Growth and Per-Store Health Can Coexist, But Only With Deliberate Operational Discipline

The most striking data point from the recent corporate day is that Wanchen completed more store openings in the first half of 2026 than in all of 2025. This was not a sudden acceleration driven by aggressive subsidies or relaxed standards. Management explicitly stated that the pace is being dynamically balanced with store unit economics, and that the 2-year payback period remains intact for new locations. This is a meaningful achievement. In a retail environment where many chains have seen payback periods stretch to three years or more, maintaining a 2-year threshold while nearly doubling the annualized opening rate suggests that the company's site selection process is becoming more, not less, rigorous.

The mechanism behind this is worth examining. Wanchen's management has implemented tight internal monitoring of per-store GMV on both a monthly and daily basis. Rent-to-sales ratios are controlled through active three-party negotiations with landlords and franchisees. The company is not simply pushing stores into any available location. It is concentrating roughly 70 percent of new openings in its stronghold markets of the Yangtze River Delta and the Shanhe Four Provinces, where brand recognition, supply chain density, and consumer familiarity are highest. This geographic concentration reduces the risk of opening a store that fails to reach breakeven, because the company already understands the demand patterns and logistics costs in these regions.

The implication for observers is clear: the company's growth is not reckless. It is being executed within a framework that prioritizes store-level returns over raw store count. This is precisely the kind of discipline that separates sustainable compounders from one-cycle growth stories. However, the question remains whether this discipline can be maintained as the company inevitably pushes into less familiar geographies. Management has acknowledged that the long-term expansion runway depends on penetration in prefecture-level cities within existing stronghold markets. That is a manageable challenge. But the eventual move into new provinces will be a much harder test.

## Positive Same-Store Sales Growth at Scale Is the Single Most Important Metric for Evaluating Wanchen, and It Is Holding Up Better Than Expected

Same-store sales growth is the most reliable indicator of whether a retail concept is gaining or losing relevance. For a company adding stores at a rapid clip, reported per-store GMV can be dragged down by the inclusion of new stores that have not yet ramped to maturity. Management addressed this directly, noting that same-store sales growth trends are even more positive than average per-store GMV, because they remove the dilution from new locations. This is a critical distinction. It means that the core store base is not just holding up; it is improving.

What is driving this resilience? Management pointed to three operational levers. First, disciplined site selection ensures that new stores are placed in locations with sufficient foot traffic and demographic support. Second, curated category expansion is adding new product lines that drive incremental traffic. Cold-chain products, for example, now account for roughly 3 percent of total GMV, and the broader "new category" bucket contributes about 5 percent. These additions are not just margin-accretive; they are traffic-accretive, bringing in customers who might otherwise shop at convenience stores or supermarkets for chilled and frozen items. Third, sharper store operations, including improved inventory management and membership engagement, are driving higher purchase frequency among existing customers.

Membership data reinforces this picture. Registered members have grown from 190 million at end-2025 to over 200 million, with growth outpacing store expansion. Active members maintain a stable purchase frequency of three times per month and a monthly contribution of roughly 90 renminbi per member. The absolute level of active members has also increased. This suggests that the company is not just adding new customers through new stores; it is deepening engagement with its existing customer base. In a weak consumption environment, that is a meaningful vote of confidence in the value proposition.

The strategic implication is that Wanchen's business model is proving resilient to macro headwinds. But resilience is not immunity. If consumer sentiment deteriorates further, or if competitors escalate price wars, same-store sales could come under pressure. Management expressed confidence that pricing competition remains rational, but this is an external factor that the company cannot fully control.

## Profitability Expansion Is Not Automatic; It Depends on Continued GPM Improvement and Logistics Leverage, Both of Which Are Underway But Not Guaranteed

Wanchen's management maintained its full-year target of slight net profit margin expansion for the retail business, compared to roughly 5 percent in fiscal 2025. This target is achievable, but it is not baked in. It depends on two distinct drivers: gross profit margin expansion and operating expense leverage.

On the gross margin side, management sees long-term headroom driven by deepening scale efficiencies and procurement optimization. The company is investing in private label expansion, with SKU count more than doubling from 30-plus in fiscal 2025 to a broader portfolio in 2026, aiming for full-category coverage by year-end. However, management is positioning private label not as a margin-accretive play at this stage, but as a tool to deliver better customer value and product differentiation. This means that private label may initially compress gross margins before eventually improving them, as the company gains scale and supplier bargaining power.

On the operating expense side, logistics efficiency is the key variable. The company is investing in more warehouses to support faster replenishment as the outlet network expands. Despite these investments, management expects the overall logistics expense ratio to decline year-on-year, driven by improved fulfillment efficiency. This is plausible but not certain. If store growth accelerates further or if the geographic footprint becomes more dispersed, logistics costs could rise faster than expected.

The net profit margin story is therefore one of gradual improvement, not step-change expansion. For a company trading at 14 times forward earnings, the market is already pricing in some margin improvement. The risk is that improvement comes more slowly than expected, or that competitive pressures force the company to invest more aggressively in price or promotions, compressing margins. Observers should watch the trajectory of GPM and logistics ratios closely in the next two quarters to assess whether the margin thesis is on track.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Wanchen's New Format Experiments Will Affect Capital Allocation and Franchisee Economics

Wanchen is actively testing three new store formats: a discount supermarket concept, a full-category hard discounter called Huishengjia, and a convenience store format. The hard discounter has roughly 10 stores in Guangdong, and the convenience store is a single pilot in Changsha. Management described these as experiments to unlock new growth levers, which is the right strategic posture. But the report leaves several critical questions unanswered.

First, what is the capital intensity of these new formats relative to the core snack-and-beverage model? If the hard discounter requires significantly more inventory investment, longer store build-out times, or higher rent costs, it could dilute return on invested capital even if it generates higher absolute revenue. Second, how will franchisee economics differ across formats? The core model works because franchisees can achieve payback within two years with relatively low investment. If new formats require higher franchisee investment or offer longer payback periods, the company may struggle to attract quality operators. Third, what is the cannibalization risk? If Wanchen opens a full-category discounter near an existing snack store, it could pull traffic and sales from the older format without generating net incremental value.

Management is monitoring the high-growth fresh snacks niche but remains cautious due to cold chain costs, spoilage ratios, and prime location requirements. This caution is appropriate. But the broader question is whether these new formats are genuinely additive to the company's growth trajectory or whether they represent a distraction from the core business. The report provides data on the current scale of these experiments but does not offer a framework for when management would decide to scale, pause, or abandon each format. This is a meaningful gap that observers should probe in future meetings.

## A Decision Framework for Observers: Three Questions That Determine Whether Wanchen Is a Compelling Study at Current Levels

Given the information available, observers should evaluate Wanchen against three criteria that will determine whether the current valuation is attractive or whether the stock deserves a discount.

First, can same-store sales growth remain positive through the next 12 months? This is the most important leading indicator. If same-store sales turn negative, it would suggest that the core concept is losing relevance or that competition is eroding the value proposition. The company's data through the first half of 2026 is encouraging, but the macro environment remains fragile. Observers should track monthly per-store GMV trends and compare them to management's full-year guidance of roughly 390,000 renminbi in average monthly GMV.

Second, is the competitive environment genuinely rational? Management noted that both industry leaders remain disciplined on store-opening subsidies, with total subsidy levels down year-on-year in the first half of 2026. This is a positive signal. But rational competition can break down quickly if one player decides to grab market share aggressively. Observers should monitor subsidy levels, store opening announcements from competitors, and any signs of price wars in key markets.

Third, can the company expand net profit margins while continuing to invest in new formats and logistics infrastructure? The margin expansion target is modest, but it requires simultaneous improvement in gross margin and operating leverage. If logistics costs rise or if private label investment compresses margins more than expected, the earnings growth story could disappoint. Observers should focus on the trajectory of the retail business net profit margin, excluding minority interests, and compare it to the company's own guidance.

If the answer to all three questions is positive, Wanchen's current valuation of 14 times forward earnings looks attractive relative to its earnings growth rate of roughly 84 percent in 2026. If any of these questions produce a negative answer, the stock's discount to peers is justified, and observers should wait for a better entry point.

## The Unresolved Questions That Make This Story Worth Following

The most intriguing aspect of Wanchen's current position is not what management has disclosed, but what remains ambiguous. The company is executing well on its core strategy, but the long-term trajectory depends on factors that are inherently uncertain. How quickly will private label scale to a point where it meaningfully improves gross margins? Will the hard discounter format prove scalable beyond a handful of pilot stores? Can the company maintain franchisee enthusiasm as it pushes into smaller cities where unit economics may be less favorable? These are not weaknesses in the company's strategy. They are the natural uncertainties of a business that is still in the early stages of its growth cycle.

For serious observers, these uncertainties create opportunity. The market tends to extrapolate recent trends linearly, either assuming that good performance will continue indefinitely or that any sign of deceleration signals a structural problem. Wanchen's management is providing enough data to challenge both extremes. The store opening data is strong, but the emphasis on store health suggests that management is aware of the risks. The same-store sales growth is positive, but management is not claiming that it will accelerate. The margin outlook is constructive, but the company is investing for the long term rather than maximizing short-term profit.

This balanced, data-driven approach is precisely what observers should want from a growth company. It does not guarantee that the stock will rise. But it does mean that the company is being run with a clear understanding of the trade-offs involved in scaling a retail network. That understanding, more than any single data point, is the foundation for long-term value creation.

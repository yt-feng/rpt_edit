## Inflection Point: From Pilots to Commercial Scale

The global robotaxi industry is entering a decisive phase, shifting from limited pilots to early commercialization. Advances in artificial intelligence, falling hardware costs, and improving regulatory clarity are enabling real-world deployment, with several operators already running fully driverless services around the clock in select cities. As a result, the key question for investors has moved beyond technological feasibility toward identifying which players can scale most efficiently and profitably. This transition mirrors the early days of ride-hailing and sets up a potentially large first-mover advantage.

## New Analysis: A US\$1 Trillion Opportunity with Improving Economics

This report builds a differentiated, bottom-up view of the robotaxi ecosystem, estimating a total addressable market of roughly US\$1 trillion across mobility services, vehicles, software, and adjacent industries. We forecast a global fleet of approximately 2.5 million robotaxis by 2035, with the US and China accounting for the majority of adoption. Crucially, detailed unit economics analysis shows that costs are already below US\$0.50 per kilometer in some cities in China and trending lower globally, supporting the potential for 30–40% margins at scale. We also introduce a “Robotaxi 30” framework to map the competitive landscape and business models across regions and value chains.

Industry restructuring: AV gleaming, EV dim, ICEV's outlook turning grim
Robotaxi diffusion is structurally positive for technology enablers/operators and related components (sensors, compute, by-wire systems, and software), more neutral-to-mixed for NEVs, and potentially another push for the ICEV-to-NEV transition on a broader global basis. In addition, broader robotaxi availability could also have longer-term implication on the ownership of private passenger vehicles, shifting value from traditional vehicle sales toward autonomous fleets, mobility platforms, and service-based models.

## Core Insight: A Platform Shift with Emerging Winners

Robotaxis represent a structural platform shift rather than just an extension of the auto sector, with value creation spanning OEMs, ride-hailing platforms, AI leaders, and semiconductor companies. Cost deflation – particularly driven by China's supply chain – acts as a key catalyst for adoption and intensifies competitive pressure globally. At the same time, early signs of winner-takes-most dynamics are emerging, as scaled operators benefit from data advantages and cost efficiencies. Market leadership is beginning to concentrate among a small group of companies, while the broader ecosystem evolves toward partnership-driven “co-opetition.”

## Catalysts: Scaling, Cost Parity, and AI Progress

Near-term catalysts include the expansion of driverless operations into new cities and the scaling of partnerships between autonomous technology providers and ride-hailing platforms. Over the medium term, further declines in hardware costs and improving utilization rates should drive robotaxis toward cost parity with traditional ride-hailing globally. At the same time, advances in end-to-end AI models and data accumulation are likely to strengthen the competitive positioning of early leaders, establishing barriers to entry and pushing industry consolidation.

## Risks: Regulation, Execution, and Adoption Curve

Despite the strong long-term outlook, several risks remain. Regulatory progress may be uneven across regions, particularly outside the US and China, potentially delaying deployment. Safety concerns or adverse incidents could slow consumer adoption and invite stricter oversight. There is also a risk that unit economics improve more slowly than expected due to lower utilization or higher costs, while the capital intensity of scaling fleets may weigh on near-term returns. Finally, global expansion could prove more complex than anticipated, given differences in infrastructure, policy, and consumer behavior.

# Investment Thesis – Robotaxi Ecosystem has Arrived; the Real Race Can Begin

## Setting the Scene

A decade in the making. Try launching Uber or Didi in 2009. No smartphones, no mobile payments, no GPS, no app store. Even with flawless vehicles and drivers, it would have failed on arrival. For the past decade, robotaxis faced a similar reality. The vehicles were not capable, the tech wasn't ready, and the world around them remained deeply circumspect of the prospect.

That ecosystem has finally materialized. Robotaxis are moving from pilot programme to prime time, propelled by four converging forces: 1) A flood of well-capitalized, diverse players. 2) End-to-end AI breakthroughs. 3) Collapsing hardware/training costs. 4) Clearer, more constructive regulations amid intensifying global competition.

The journey is just beginning and substantial hurdles remain, but we are at an inflection point. Consumer skepticism towards robotaxis still runs high, but perception is shifting as autonomous ride-hailing moves from concept to commercial reality. The gap between public perception and industry momentum is narrowing - driven by "first ride effect", continuous tech advancement, and the accelerating pace of real world deployment, in scale.

From 2026, the race stops being about proving it can be done, and becomes about who can move the furthest, the fastest, and the cheapest. Credible players, such as Waymo (covered by Brian Nowak), Baidu Apollo (covered by Gary Yu), WeRide (covered by Tim Hsiao), and Pony.ai (NC), are already running 24/7 fully driverless fleets across a growing list of cities.

Is regulation holding back robotaxi commercialization? Conflicts between consumer demand for autonomous mobility and regulators' focus on safety, liability, data governance, and urban traffic management are creating challenges for OEMs and robotaxi operators in major markets like China. While robotaxi technology may advance faster than regulatory frameworks at times, we believe enforceable standards are essential for scaling the industry responsibly. Clear rules should help build public trust, reduce operational uncertainty, and support the emergence of global leaders.

The Autonomy Inflection. MS Auto and Internet teams have jointly developed a proprietary global robotaxi model with high conviction – we expect unit economics (UE) breakeven by 2028, scaled commercial operations by 2030. By 2035, we see a 2.5mn-vehicle global fleet, serving daily demand at the scale of the population of S. Korea, France or Germany.

In our view, this is the transition that matters – from proof-of-concept to a genuine transportation utility. In the next decade, the robotaxi fleet will be no longer a collection of pilot programs but a distributed mobility grid. The US and China are gaining an upper hand in the near-term with respect to technological advances and fleet rollout, while Europe and broader Asia will play an increasingly important role in shaping the industry landscape over the longer term.

## Four Powerful Catalysts Shifting Robotaxi Rollout into High Gear

## Catalyst 1: Diverse Players Accelerated by Al Breakthroughs

End-to-end AI architectures are simultaneously lowering barriers to entry and raising performance ceilings. The transition from modular, rule-based autonomy stacks toward unified neural networks trained through world-model simulation - complemented by closed-loop reinforcement learning that exposes systems to the consequences of their own decisions - is dramatically compressing development timelines. The result is a

broader competitive field entering the space with commercially viable capabilities, accelerating both fleet deployment and the data flywheel upon which autonomous performance depends.

Globally, this Al wave has democratized access to L4-competitive technology in a way that pure hardware cost deflation alone never could. In the US, Waymo's (covered by Brian Nowak) hybrid Al-plus-rule-based stack and Tesla's (covered by Andrew Percoco) vision-only neural network approach represent two distinct approaches to long-run architecture - and both are maturing simultaneously. In China, WeRide (covered by Tim Hsiao), Pony.ai, and Baidu Apollo (covered by Gary Yu) have each crossed meaningful competency thresholds enabled by scale, with Chinese players benefiting from a regulatory environment that, unlike Europe, allows much broader testing permissions. In the Middle East, where operational design domains are less topographically complex and weather windows are broad, the Al maturity bar to commercial deployment has proven meaningfully lower – one reason WeRide was able to achieve unit economics breakeven in Abu Dhabi ahead of most other markets globally.

We highlight five categories of players: L4 pure-plays, OEM adjacents, ride-sharing platforms, solution providers, and tech heavyweights. Each are applying AI in ways that reflect their underlying competitive positioning.

L4 pure-plays have the deepest algorithmic investment and the longest track record in driverless miles.

OEM adjacents have the broadest data corpus from consumer vehicle fleets, providing training signal diversity that purpose-built robotaxi operators cannot easily replicate.

Internet/tech heavyweights bring compute infrastructure and consumer distribution at scale.

We believe convergence of these capabilities is why 2026 feels categorically different from the hype cycles seen in both 2018 and 2021.

Catalyst 2: Technology Maturation Meeting Commercial Scalability

Technological maturation is colliding with commercial scalability, driven by a fundamental shift toward end-to-end AI architectures. Leading robotaxi operators are demonstrating performance improvements measured in miles-per-intervention that would have seemed implausible two years ago. This evolution is complemented by advanced reinforcement learning techniques. Robotaxi players are exploring closed-loop training methods that more easily generalize, rather than relying solely on traditional behavior cloning methods. These improvements are directly reflected in MPI enhancements across major players.

Fleet operational data from Waymo's cumulative L4+ miles globally, Pony.ai's city-wide deployments across China's four megacities, and WeRide's multi-continent operations are now generating the training signal volumes necessary for genuine generalization beyond geofenced competence. The distinction matters significantly for the medium-term competitive picture in our view – a system that can generalise can handle novel-edge cases without explicit programming, which is the prerequisite for expanding into new cities and geographies without proportional safety engineering investment.

In the US, Waymo's (covered by Brian Nowak) expansion to Washington DC and other snow markets represents geographic generalization corridors into markets with

meaningfully different driving environments.

In China, the maturation is particularly visible in the metrics that matter commercially. Pony.ai's 7th-generation robotaxi deployed to Guangzhou in late 2025 has hit the city-level unit economics breakeven threshold that the company had targeted for years. Baidu Apollo's system has cleared the same threshold in Wuhan. These are not demo metrics – they are production KPis from fleets generating tens of thousands of rides monthly.

In Europe and the rest of the world, maturation is arriving at a lag but the pace is accelerating. For example, Uber (covered by Brian Nowak) is partnering with WeRide, Pony.ai, Momenta and Wayve across Europe, the Middle East, and several other regions. Baidu's Apollo Go and Lyft will deploy autonomous ride-hailing services across Europe while WeRide and Pony.ai are both expanding in SEA as well.

## Catalyst 3: A Structural Change in Total Cost of Operation

Robotaxi total cost of ownership has declined sharply over the past three years, driven by operating leverage and continuous optimization of robotaxi vehicle BoMs. We project a further 20% reduction in operating cost per kilometer over the next three years, supported by ongoing sensor and hardware cost deflation, AI-driven software efficiencies, improving remote-assistance productivity ratios, and higher utilization as operational design domains expand.

The profitability implications are transformative. L4+ robotaxi deployment can meaningfully reshape the ride-hailing cost structure, unlocking vehicle-level net margins of more than 30% globally at scale and more than 40% in developed markets, where comparable taxi fares are significantly higher.

In China, operating cost improvements have been particularly pronounced. Chinese OEM partners can manufacture purpose-built robotaxi platforms for US\$35–40k per vehicle in 2026, representing a meaningful discount to Western equivalents and a fraction of the US \$150k+ unit costs that characterized many early robotaxi deployments in the 2018-20 period. In several Chinese cities, operators have already demonstrated pathways to operating breakeven, supported by a highly competitive cost base, low electricity prices, and a relatively favorable insurance framework for autonomous vehicles.

Globally, the total cost of ownership equation is evolving along a different trajectory. Labor costs are higher, but so is revenue per ride. The key variable remains hardware cost deflation. As fleet sizes expand from several thousand vehicles to tens of thousands, LiDAR, compute, and sensor costs should decline along manufacturing learning curves, narrowing the operating cost gap with Chinese operators. We believe global operators could see more meaningful hardware cost inflection over the next five years.

For emerging robotaxi markets, particularly developed markets such as Europe, operating economics are more complex. Higher vehicle acquisition and registration costs, stricter data localization requirements that increase cloud and compute capex, and a fragmented regulatory landscape all raise the cost of scaling across multiple cities. That said, the revenue side of the equation is also more attractive, as taxi fares in cities such as London, Paris, and Munich rank among the highest globally. As a result, while European robotaxi economics may structurally lag China by three to five years, they offer some of the strongest long-term margin potential globally once the market reaches maturity.

## Catalyst 4: A Strategic Priority – Constructive Regulatory Progress as Global Competition Intensifies

The major economies are increasingly engaged. Tesla is advancing Cybercab, while Waymo continues to strengthen its position in the US. Europe, the Middle East, and ASEAN together represent a taxi and ride-hailing fleet of roughly 4 million units. Even 25% penetration implies around 1 million incremental L4 vehicles – an opportunity large enough to define competitive positioning for the next decade. As a result, regulatory frameworks, permitting regimes, and local procurement policies are accelerating out of strategic necessity rather than enthusiasm.

Global collaboration is the new competition. Beyond technological advancement, the pursuit of cost-effective solutions and scalable operations is key. Consistent BoM reduction and rapid scaling will rely significantly on Chinese vehicle solutions, which should cost around US\$35–40k per vehicle in 2026. Higher vehicle utilization and lower operating costs, in turn, depend heavily on strategic tie-ups with local ride-sharing partners that provide demand aggregation, consumer trust, and regulatory relationships.

Consequently, we expect a growing number of partnerships, such as WeRide–Uber in the Middle East and WeRide–Grab in Southeast Asia, to form over the next two to three years. Recent examples include Pony.ai's collaboration with Qatar's Mowasalat to begin L4 robotaxi testing in Doha, Momenta's partnership with Uber to roll out L4 robotaxis in Munich in 2026, and Ant Group-backed Hello's plan to launch its HR1 robotaxi in 2026, teaming up with Horizon Robotics and targeting a fleet of 50,000 units across 10 or more cities by 2027.

Across these developments, a consistent structure is emerging. Chinese technology and cost discipline paired with local platform reach and regulatory credibility. This is not a transitional model, but the foundation of the global robotaxi supply chain. The pace of deal formation over the next two to three years will be critical in establishing competitive advantages.

## The Path to Sustained Profitability: A Scale Game

Unit economics analysis divorced from operational context is analytically meaningless. Robotaxi profitability depends on three interdependent variables – fleet utilization, operational domain coverage, and order frequency – each of which improves non-linearly with scale. The compounding nature of this relationship explains why early-mover advantages in this industry are so durable and why investors should weigh demonstrated operational scale as heavily as technology metrics when assessing competitive positioning.

We believe L4+ robotaxi players can achieve vehicle-level net margins of 30% or more at scale. In developed markets, where labor and other operating costs account for a larger share of the total, we estimate robotaxis could unlock net margins exceeding 40% by 2030. The profit margin will then be distributed among different participants – fleet owners, shared mobility platforms, and technology enablers – to fund hardware and software R&D, operating licenses, and potentially taxation. Our projections point to a restructuring of both the cost stack of the traditional taxi business and the profit redistribution channel.

In the US, the unit economics trajectory of bellwethers such as Waymo is the key metric to watch, as early adopters are willing to pay for novelty and reliability. As volume scales, pricing power may moderate, but the structural elimination of labor costs in the US should more than offset any yield compression at reasonable utilization rates. The critical near-term metric is rides per vehicle per day, as each additional ride beyond the initial utilization threshold flows through at near-100% incremental margin, given the predominantly fixed cost structure of a robotaxi fleet.

In China, Pony.ai has disclosed that it achieved city-level vehicle breakeven in Guangzhou and Shenzhen in late 2025, while Baidu Apollo reached the same threshold in Wuhan. Our modeling indicates that average daily revenue of Rmb450–500 (\~US\$75) per vehicle represents the breakeven threshold on a broader operational basis. This translates to approximately 23–25 orders per day at prevailing Guangzhou fare levels – a target that is achievable under current operational area coverage and increasingly routine as ODDs expand.

Globally, WeRide has achieved unit economics breakeven for its robotaxi fleet in Abu Dhabi by removing safety drivers from vehicles and targets expansion to 300+ vehicles by year-end. The Abu Dhabi achievement is instructive because it demonstrates breakeven in a market with genuinely different operating characteristics – wider roads, lower traffic complexity, and higher average fares driven by affluent expatriate and tourist demographics – at a smaller fleet size than China’s Tier 1 deployments. This has important implications for Middle East expansion economics and suggests the region may reach sustainable profitability faster than consensus assumes.

L2+ and L4 - two parallel universes or a single destination? Upgrading from L2+ to L4 will not be easy. Players have followed an evolutionary path from ADAS toward L3 and later L4, with numerous iterations in both hardware and algorithms. While L2+ players could theoretically evolve into L4 operators, running an autonomous ride-hailing fleet involves much more than offering an L4 technical solution. The sophisticated ride-hailing ecosystem – complex fleet logistics, regulatory relationships, fleet maintenance operations, dynamic dispatching, and consumer acquisition – gives incumbent robotaxi operators a significant economic moat that pure technology players are likely to underestimate.

Where could we be wrong? Regulatory hurdles remain a primary concern, as robotaxi services are subject to evolving, jurisdiction-specific regimes governing testing, deployment, data privacy, cybersecurity, and insurance. Safety concerns persist, with many consumers still questioning the reliability of autonomous systems in complex scenarios. Unit economics may not materialize as quickly as expected due to uncertainties in robotaxi pricing, vehicle utilization, and hardware costs. High upfront R&D capital requirements and prolonged development cycles could also erode profitability for players that lack the balance sheet depth to absorb extended pre-scale periods.

Broader Ecosystem Impact

Building on our prior research, the Bluepaper From Horsepower to Brainpower – AI Takes the Wheel, we delve deeper into the dynamics of the L4 robotaxi ecosystem, and examine the broader cross-sector and regional implications. Growing adoption of robotaxis not only opens a wealth of direct investment opportunities but also presents intriguing second-derivative plays tied to insurance, transportation, utilities, manufacturing, and semiconductors.

## Auto Insurance: A Business Model in Transition

The robotaxi's impact on insurance is one of the most underappreciated second-order investment stories in the sector. Chinese insurers are developing L3/L4-specific products to meet new demands from robotaxi companies, and the current pricing models – built on actuarial tables derived from human-driven vehicle accident rates – are fundamentally inadequate for autonomous fleet underwriting. Current robotaxi insurance in China is typically structured as a commercial vehicle product with manual underwriting adjustments, reflecting the absence of actuarial data for autonomous fleet risk. As Waymo's 200mn L4 miles by year end, Pony.ai's Guangzhou deployment data, and WeRide's Abu Dhabi operations generate the statistical base for proper actuarial modelling, we expect the insurance market will reprice dramatically.

If L4 robotaxis demonstrate superior safety performance relative to human-driven vehicles, which the available evidence suggests they do in their operational design domains, the current pure risk premium is expected to sustain a downward trend over the long-term in a non-linear pattern, along with potential new risk exposures and insurance demand. This deflation benefits robotaxi operators directly by reducing one of their material fixed costs per vehicle. It may also impact incumbent auto insurers that face the prospect of an accelerating business structure transformation of their premium base as consumers shift from privately owned vehicles to robotaxi services. The European insurance market, where per-vehicle premiums are highest and profit pools are most concentrated among incumbent carriers, faces the most significant structural impact from this dynamic.

## Car Ownership and Oil Consumption – Longer-Term but Real

Widespread L4 deployment could meaningfully reduce consumer appetite for private vehicle ownership, particularly if autonomous services prove more cost-effective and convenient at the price points we project for 2030. The car ownership impact is most plausible in dense urban environments, precisely the markets where robotaxi penetration will be highest. A household that currently owns two vehicles primarily for commuting and errand-running has a credible economic alternative to car ownership if robotaxi fares fall below the marginal cost of driving and owning a personal vehicle.

## Oil Demand Implications are Twofold

The shift from private ownership reduces the total vehicle fleet over time, and the robotaxi fleet is predominantly electric, meaning each vehicle-mile that migrates from human-driven petrol or diesel to autonomous electric represents a permanent oil demand reduction. The timeline of this impact is long – operating over decades rather than years – but the directional signal is unambiguous and relevant for energy sector investors thinking about the 2030+ demand picture. The Middle East markets most aggressively adopting

robotaxis are also the world's largest oil producers, an irony that has not been lost on sovereign wealth funds from Abu Dhabi and Riyadh that are simultaneously investing in autonomous mobility and managing national oil company revenues.

## Auto Manufacturing – Platform Disruption

The rise of robotaxis will have significant implications for manufacturing volumes, vehicle design philosophy, and supply chain dynamics across the global auto industry. Robotaxi platforms require meaningfully different design specifications than consumer vehicles: commercial-grade durability for higher utilization cycles; interior configurations optimized for passenger comfort without a driver seat; sensor mounting infrastructure integrated into body panels; and compute chassis capable of supporting 2,000+ TOPS of processing power. These specifications do not map cleanly to existing consumer vehicle platforms, requiring either purpose-built robotaxi vehicles or extensive modification of existing platforms.

Chinese OEM manufacturers, e.g. BAIC-Bluepark, GAC, XPeng, and emerging robotaxi-specialist manufacturers are best positioned to capture the purpose-built robotaxi vehicle market given their cost structures and their existing supply relationships with robotaxi operators. Western OEM manufacturers, with higher labour costs and less flexible production systems, are more likely to participate through modified existing platforms or through licensing manufacturing capacity to technology-led robotaxi operators. The manufacturing winner in robotaxis will ultimately be determined by cost and delivery speed rather than brand prestige, which will be a fundamentally different competitive framework than consumer automotive.

## Automotive Semi – A Meaningful Upgrade Cycle

Mainstream L4 robotaxis currently run on 2,000+ TOPS of computing power, typically implemented through dual Thor configurations from Nvidia (covered by Joseph Moore). This represents a meaningful upgrade from the approximately 500 TOPS average that characterises L2+ passenger vehicles – a four-to-one improvement that flows directly to Nvidia's automotive revenue line and to the compute-adjacent supply chain, including memory, power management ICs, and thermal management systems. Looking forward, we expect robotaxi compute requirements to double to 4,000+ TOPS by 2030, if not earlier, as autonomous systems incorporate more complex sensor fusion, longer planning horizons, and higher-fidelity simulation loops. The semiconductor content per robotaxi vehicle at 4,000 TOPS is several multiples of the content per conventional passenger vehicle, making robotaxi fleet proliferation a meaningful revenue driver for auto semiconductor suppliers at scale.

## Key Question 1: How Quickly Could Global Robotaxis Ramp Up?

Global robotaxi penetration is not a single curve but four distinct adoption trajectories that diverge meaningfully on timing, speed, and ultimate endpoint. Treating them as a monolith risks substantial forecasting error. The US and China are the sprint markets; Europe is the marathon; the rest of the world – anchored by the Middle East and ASEAN – is the wildcard that could outperform expectations if regulatory hospitality proves durable and Chinese vehicle cost structures are deployed effectively.

Our global forecast assumes a 2.5mn unit fleet size by 2035, with US and China accounting for approximately 70% – the remainder distributed across Europe, the Middle East, and RoW. This growth is supported by expanding operational design domains (ODDs) in each region, improving MPI metrics, and the continued entry of credible robotaxi makers/fleet operators in markets beyond China and the US. Average daily orders per vehicle reach 16-25 globally in relatively mature markets such as China, European and the US by 2035, consistent with current ride-sharing utilization benchmarks that robotaxi operators are targeting.

Europe's robotaxi story is the most nuanced of the major markets and the one most prone to underestimation. # Key Question 2: What Are the Different Revenue Models?

Given the global robotaxi industry remains in its early commercial stages and no universal revenue model has proven to be the most effective across all markets yet, we analyze the key players along the value chain and the current revenue models adopted by major players in each geography. The revenue model question is not purely academic. The structural position a company occupies in the robotaxi stack determines its addressable margin pool, its capital requirements, and its long-term competitive sustainability:

\- Robotaxi platforms: They develop and maintain the L4+ AD solution, and they are also responsible for obtaining all the licenses required for commercial operations.

\- Fleet owners: They purchase and manage robotaxi fleets, and often lease them out to robotaxi platforms, or ride-sharing platforms for operations.

\- Ride-sharing platforms: They often partner with robotaxi players as the latter can leverage their large, existing user base.

• EV OEMs: They manufacture customized EVs for robotaxis

\- Suppliers: They supply X-by-Wire, cameras, LiDARs, chips to robotaxis.

## Revenue Model #1: Self-Operation (Primarily Domestic China, Early US)

This is the common business model for Chinese robotaxi start-ups in their early stages, where they own and operate the robotaxi fleet themselves. The capital intensity is highest under this model, but so is the control over the consumer experience and the data generated from operations. Without partnerships with ride-sharing platforms, customer acquisition costs are elevated. Robotaxi fleet operators must offer promotions and undercut conventional ride-hailing players to attract consumers and build brand awareness. Given the high costs of vehicle depreciation, insurance, maintenance, charging, parking, and remote monitoring, reaching sustainable unit economics breakeven under this model requires higher utilization than the asset-light alternatives, making it more appropriate for mature markets with established consumer demand.

In the US, the self-operation model benefits from higher per-ride revenue that partially offsets the capital intensity, and it's important to have strong balance sheet support for the extended pre-scale period. Robotaxi heavyweights' choice to own and operate rather than wholesale its technology to fleet partners reflects both the strategic importance of owning the direct access to consumers and their assessment that technology licensing alone would be insufficient to achieve the operational excellence required to pioneer and lead the market in an early stage of tech/service rollout.

## Revenue Model #2: Strategic Partnerships with Fleet Owners (Domestic China, Scaling)

This has become the dominant business model for robotaxi platforms in the current phase, where they partner with third-party fleet owners operating under an asset-light structure.

Third-party fleet owners acquire and manage the robotaxi vehicles, bearing vehicle depreciation, insurance, maintenance, and related costs. In return, they receive leasing revenue from robotaxi operators, who typically retain approximately 20% of robotaxi operational revenue for providing the autonomous driving software licensing. Robotaxi players bear remote monitoring and software maintenance costs. In some cases, they also partner with ride-sharing platforms, where the platform charges about 10% for customers acquired through their channels. Of note, profit-sharing and/or licensing fee arrangements vary widely across regions, depending on factors such as scale and bargaining power.

This model is particularly attractive in the current period because it allows robotaxi operators to scale their deployed fleet without proportional balance sheet growth, directing capital toward technology development rather than vehicle ownership. The fleet owner ecosystem in China, comprising leasing companies, local government investment vehicles, and ride-hailing subsidiaries, has developed rapidly to fill this capital need. We expect this model to remain the primary scaling mechanism for Chinese operators through 2028, with the self-operation model becoming more relevant as operators accumulate sufficient capital from operations to fund fleet ownership directly.

## Revenue Model #3: Strategic Partnerships with Ride-Sharing Platforms (Overseas)

This is the primary business model for robotaxi platforms in overseas markets, where they partner with both ride-sharing platforms and third-party fleet owners. Robotaxi players recognize revenue through three streams. Robotaxi vehicle sales to fleet owners, licensing fees from ride-sharing platforms, and profit sharing with ride-sharing platforms as operations scale to profitability. They bear remote monitoring and software maintenance costs. For ride-sharing platforms like Uber, this structure typically involves the platform recognizing the full robotaxi operation revenue, then remitting around 60% to the robotaxi operator as a licensing fee and a separate portion to fleet owners as leasing revenue. We note that the licensing fee and/or leasing rate arrangements differ widely across regions, depending on factors such as scale and bargaining power.

This model has proven its viability in Abu Dhabi, where WeRide achieved unit economics breakeven under precisely this structure. The key advantage of the model in overseas markets is the platform's consumer distribution capability – Uber's app reach in the Middle East, ASEAN, and Europe eliminates the consumer acquisition problem that makes self-operation so capital-intensive in new markets. For Uber, the model provides a path to structurally remove the driver earnings component from the take-rate equation and converts a two-sided marketplace into effectively a technology licensing and demand aggregation business.

In Europe, the licensing fee model is likely to be adapted to reflect local data ownership requirements. European data localization regulations under General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) create a compliance overhead for Chinese operators that is less burdensome in the Middle East or ASEAN, and the licensing fee structure may need to incorporate provisions for European data processing that increase operating costs relative to other markets. We believe this is one reason the European margin trajectory lags China and the Middle East by roughly 2-3 years even at equivalent utilization levels.

\- PICC P&C's leadership in spearheading the Technical Standard for the Determination of Insurance Compensation in Intelligent Connected Vehicle Traffic Accidents underscores its role in industry rule-setting and public governance, as well as its ability to interface with traffic police, judicial authorities, and MIIT-related systems.

## Case Study: From ICE to NEV: How Insurers and Regulators Responded

China's auto insurance market underwent four rounds of reform during 2015–20. We have seen several key policy developments:

\- Regulators guided premium rates and commissions lower and expanded coverage to improve customer benefits.

\- For insurers, regulators capped expense loading ratios, increased expected loss ratios, and required “BXHY” (or filing-implementation consistency, meaning insurers’ filed expense and commission assumptions must remain strictly consistent with actual execution) to improve expense structure and avoid irrational competition.

\- Regulators reformed no-claim discount factors and expanded autonomous pricing coefficients, benefiting low-risk customers with more discounts and insurers with more flexible pricing.

## The higher the automation level, the more legal and commercial liability shifts from the driver to the system and operating entity, but the process will take time.

Currently, we are still in the assisted-driving period (NEV or L2), where the system provides assistance and legal liability typically remains with the driver.

As the market gradually shifts into L3 with conditional autonomous driving, more liability will move from the driver to the system, although controversy could remain. For example, based on the current industry draft, under activated L3 automation, liability hinges on driver response to takeover requests: if the driver retakes control and an accident occurs, the driver bears full liability; if the driver fails to take over, liability shifts to the vehicle manufacturer. Where automation is not activated, or required software updates are not installed, liability remains with the driver, a principle that may also extend to certain L4 scenarios. However, based on China's current regulatory and legal framework, L3 autonomous driving does not alter the outward liability structure of motor insurance, which remains driver-centric, although economic risk exposure has begun to shift internally toward the vehicle and the autonomous-driving system. At L3, insurers pay first under driver-centric insurance and recover later from OEMs or system providers.

Over time, the majority of liability could shift to the operator under L4/L5, while motor insurance contracts and liability insurance scales further.

\- Commercial third-party liability insurance remains the primary first-loss cover on the individual side at L3, but as drivers gradually exit the control loop from L4 onward, its relevance to individuals should diminish materially and largely disappear by L5, in our view, with liability increasingly transferred to OEMs, system providers, or operators.

\- Own-damage insurance should persist throughout the automation continuum, but evolve from a high-frequency accident product into a low-frequency, high-value asset protection product.

\- Driver and passenger insurance remains relevant up to L3, but will likely become increasingly marginal at higher automation levels as in-cabin safety improves structurally.

\- In parallel, system/algorithm liability insurance should enter the insurance stack at L3 and become a core product at L4–L5, covering algorithm failures, system defects, and highly correlated tail risks, among others.

# Key Question 5: What's The Potential Impact on Job Markets?

From a broader perspective, we think robotaxis should not be viewed solely as a labor replacement technology. Instead, they represent a restructuring of labor demand within the transportation sector. More importantly, labor shortages may ultimately become the dominant issue across many regions over the next two decades. As a result, robotaxis may be viewed as a mechanism for supplementing declining labor supply through technology and capital investment. The objective is not simply to reduce labor costs, but to maintain transportation capacity and economic activity despite shrinking labor pools.

## 1. How soon and how many drivers could robotaxis actually affect?

Under an extreme scenario in which robotaxis fully replace conventional taxi and ride-hailing services, millions of professional drivers globally could be impacted. In reality, however, robotaxi deployment is likely to be gradual due to several factors:

\- Deployment remains limited to specific geofenced areas, and licensing/regulatory approvals are likely to be phased in over time.

\- Service coverage between operating zones is often fragmented and cross-regional operation takes time.

\- Extreme weather and traffic conditions may still require human intervention.

\- Fleet deployment requires substantial capital investment. Therefore, adoption rates will vary significantly across cities and countries.

\- Charging and operational infrastructure must be built out.

As a result, even if autonomous driving technology continues to improve, meaningful robotaxi penetration is likely to occur over a 10-20 year timeframe rather than through an abrupt replacement of existing drivers.

## 2. What new jobs could robotaxis create?

Robotaxis are not simply "driverless vehicles." They require an entirely new ecosystem of fleet management, maintenance and operational support. Compared with traditional ride-hailing services, robotaxi operators will need to build centralized operational capabilities across multiple functions, including:

Fleet operations, such as vehicle scheduling specialists or energy management personnel.

Remote safety support that requires remote assistance operators, safety monitoring specialists, incident response personnel, etc.

Vehicle maintenance from maintenance technicians.

Vehicle preparation from cleaning staff.

Infrastructure operations from charging station operators.

Software and data services such as AI model maintenance and cloud operations.

## 3. How much of the job loss could be offset?

Robotaxis are unlikely to create one new job for every driver displaced. Otherwise, automation would generate little productivity benefit. However, assuming a future fleet of 1 million robotaxis, we could create additional jobs as follows:

i) Remote operators – Depending on technological maturity, they may require:
Early stage: 1 operator per 10 vehicles, implying 100,000 operators
Scaling stage: 1 operator per 30 vehicles, implying 33,000 operators
Mature stage: 1 operator per 100 vehicles, implying 10,000 operators

ii) Maintenance and Servicing: Robotaxis are expected to operate at significantly higher utilization rates than privately owned vehicles. In addition to traditional maintenance, fleets will require LiDAR calibration, camera calibration, radar diagnostics, and compute system maintenance. Assuming one technician can support 20 vehicles, this would create 50,000 maintenance personnel every 1 million robotaxis.

iii) Cleaning and Vehicle Preparation: Assuming each vehicle receives daily cleaning and preparation, and one employee services 20 vehicles per day, 50,000 cleaning and preparation personnel would be needed.

iv) Charging Infrastructure Operations: Assuming one charging hub per 50 robotaxis and three employees per charging hub, this would imply approximately 20,000 charging hubs and 60,000 charging-related personnel.

v) Fleet Dispatch and Management: Assuming one dispatcher oversees 100 vehicles, approximately 10,000 dispatch and fleet management personnel would be needed.

These numbers haven't include the hardware/software engineers for developing new vehicles and autonomous driving models. Even under a mature robotaxi scenario, a fleet of 1mn units of vehicles could still support more than 150-200k direct jobs. Therefore, while the net employment impact is likely negative from a static perspective, the magnitude of job losses may be significantly smaller than commonly assumed. In addition, some of the new roles created within the ecosystem could command higher compensation levels, reflecting their specialized skill requirements.

## 4. Why might labor disruption be smaller than expected?

A common assumption is that millions of drivers replaced by robotaxis will become millions of unemployed. In reality, large-scale robotaxi deployment will likely take more than a decade. Across many countries, professional drivers tend to be middle-aged or older. As a result, a meaningful portion of the current workforce may leave the industry through natural retirement before robotaxi adoption reaches scale.

Meanwhile, robotaxis' lower fares and increased service availability could stimulate new transportation demand, including late-night travel, elderly mobility, suburban transportation, and daily commuting. As a result, robotaxis could first reduce future driver hiring needs rather than trigger large-scale layoffs of the existing workforce.

## 5. Robotaxis could help fill labor gaps rather than create unemployment

We believe this could ultimately become one of the most important long-term investment considerations. Admittedly, many major economies already face demographic headwinds, including declining fertility rates, aging populations, and slower labor force growth. This trend may be particularly relevant in Japan, South Korea, China, and parts of Europe, where population aging is expected to become increasingly pronounced over the coming decades.

## Key Risks and Where We Could Be Wrong

## Regulatory Fragmentation

Regulatory hurdles remain the primary concern across all markets. Robotaxi services are subject to evolving, jurisdiction-specific regimes governing testing, deployment, data privacy, cybersecurity, and insurance. In China, the permitting process can be rigorous, with operators typically required to complete at least one million kilometers of accident-free testing before obtaining approval. As a result, expansion into new cities can face meaningful delays. In Europe, the absence of a pan-EU framework means each national regulatory authority must independently develop its approach, creating a multi-year licensing timeline that will constrain the pace of European scaling. In the US, the interplay between federal and state authority over AV regulation creates ongoing uncertainty that could slow permitting even in markets where technology readiness is not the constraint. A major safety incident in any leading market could trigger moratoriums that set back the entire global industry.

Of note, China paused the issuance of new autonomous driving permits in April following concerns related to a technology incident in March. The pause covers fleet expansions, new pilot programs, and extensions into additional cities, and authorities have not announced a timeline for resumption. That said, we view the suspension as a temporary setback rather than a policy reversal. Beijing continues to regard robotaxis as an important component of its broader autonomous driving strategy, which suggests a constructive long-term policy stance. In addition, the remaining challenges appear increasingly engineering-related and therefore potentially more tractable over time. Finally, we believe China is unlikely to cede its position in the global race toward Level 4 autonomous driving, particularly as development continues to advance internationally regardless of the timing of domestic permit approvals.

## Safety Concerns and Consumer Scepticism

Consumer perception remains a significant headwind, and perception can shift rapidly in adverse directions following safety incidents. The first-ride effect, where consumers who experience an autonomous ride typically become strong advocates, is a powerful positive force for adoption, but it requires consumers to take that first ride despite their pre-existing scepticism. Marketing robotaxi services to a mainstream consumer base that has been conditioned by negative media coverage of autonomous vehicle accidents is a genuine commercial challenge that pure technology metrics do not capture. A high-profile accident involving a fully driverless robotaxi in any major market could freeze adoption across multiple geographies simultaneously, creating asymmetric downside risk that justifies a more conservative penetration assumption than the central case.

## Unit Economics Disappointment

Unit economics may take longer to materialize than expected due to uncertainties around robotaxi pricing, vehicle utilization rates, hardware costs, and the productivity of remote monitoring operations. Any delay in expanding ODD coverage or achieving the consumer adoption required to support high utilization could negatively affect operational efficiency. This risk is particularly relevant for pure-play operators without the balance sheet support of a larger parent technology company, as weaker-than-expected unit economics could lead to capital withdrawal and fleet contraction.

## Capital Requirements and Competitive Attrition

The upfront R&D capital requirements and prolonged development cycles of L4 autonomous driving remain formidable barriers. Even operators that have demonstrated commercial viability in select markets face the ongoing cost of technology development, fleet maintenance, regulatory compliance across multiple jurisdictions, and the operational overhead of running 24/7 fleets in demanding urban environments. Players without access to sustained capital would be at risk before the industry reaches the scale at which autonomous ride-hailing becomes self-financing.

# Implications for the Europe Market

L4+ robotaxi rollout in Europe

Europe seems to be lagging behind. The European market is evolving more cautiously than the US or China so far, with just an embryonic operation in Croatia using BAIC cars and Waymo planning to start in London this year. Outside Europe, Mercedes has launched a robotaxi pilot in Abu Dhabi in a JV with Momenta (Chinese advanced driver-assistance player) and Lumo (local partner), using S-Class test vehicles and the idea is to scale the offering to other regions after validation.

Stellantis is also pursuing a partnership approach, collaborating with external autonomy specialists including Pony.ai, NVIDIA and Uber to accelerate Level 4 capabilities without bearing the full development burden internally. ## Implications for the Korea Market

By Young Suk Shin/Chan Park

## L4+ robotaxi rollout in Korea

From pilot-driven to ecosystem-building phase: Korea's robotaxi industry is transitioning from a pilot-driven phase to an ecosystem-building phase. For much of the past decade, efforts focused on demonstrating autonomous driving technology in limited environments such as Pangyo, Sangam, and parts of Seoul. The government's current objective is more ambitious: achieving commercial Level 4 autonomous driving by 2027 while building a competitive domestic autonomous mobility industry.

The government's approach has evolved from supporting isolated pilot projects to creating conditions for large-scale deployment. Earlier autonomous driving initiatives focused on proving technological feasibility within tightly defined operating zones. More recently, policy has shifted toward expanding operating domains, simplifying testing frameworks, and encouraging collaboration among industry participants.

The centerpiece of this effort is Gwangju, Korea's first citywide autonomous driving pilot zone. The objective is not simply to test autonomous vehicles but to create a realistic urban environment where companies can accumulate operational experience, generate driving data, and validate commercial service models. More broadly, policymakers view autonomous driving as a strategic industry that will influence Korea's competitiveness in the future automotive sector, much as batteries and semiconductors have done today.

Team Korea approach: Unlike the US model, where Tesla and Waymo are emerging as the two leading robotaxi operators, or the Chinese model, where several large players are deploying fleets at scale, Korea is pursuing a collaborative ecosystem approach. A layered ecosystem is emerging, with different participants specializing in distinct parts of the value chain. Vehicle manufacturers provide the platform, autonomous driving companies supply software, fleet operators manage vehicles, and mobility platforms such as Kakao connect services with customers.

This structure reflects Korea's broader industrial model, where large corporations, technology specialists, and government institutions often collaborate to develop strategic industries. Rather than producing a single Korean equivalent of Waymo, Korea appears to be building an interconnected ecosystem designed to accelerate commercialization while spreading risk across multiple participants.

## Key players and their strategies:

\- Hyundai Motor Group is the central industrial player. Through Hyundai, Kia, 42dot, and Motional, the group is building capabilities across vehicle manufacturing, software-defined vehicles, autonomous driving software, and mobility services. Hyundai's strategy is to remain relevant as value creation shifts from hardware toward software and autonomous mobility platforms. While its manufacturing scale provides a significant domestic advantage, its key challenge remains accumulating real-world operating experience comparable to global leaders such as Waymo.

\- Kakao Mobility occupies a different but potentially powerful position. Through

Kakao T, it already operates one of Korea's largest transportation platforms and serves as the interface between mobility providers and consumers. Rather than developing autonomous driving technology itself, Kakao's strategy is to become the primary distribution and dispatch platform for future autonomous fleets. If robotaxi adoption accelerates, customer ownership may prove just as important as vehicle ownership.

\- Autonomous a2z has emerged as one of Korea's leading independent autonomous driving software companies. Its strategy is to become a preferred supplier of autonomous driving technology rather than compete as a fleet operator. This allows it to partner with multiple vehicle manufacturers and service providers while focusing resources on perception, planning, and vehicle-control technologies.

\- RideFlux is pursuing a broader commercialization strategy by combining passenger transportation with autonomous logistics services. This approach may enable the company to generate revenue earlier while continuing to develop its core autonomous driving capabilities. Its expansion into freight applications reflects a pragmatic view that logistics may commercialize faster than large-scale robotaxi deployment.

## Key risks and barriers: Despite strong policy support, several challenges could slow commercialization.

\- The most immediate issue is the continued reliance on safety drivers and remote supervision. Robotaxi economics improve significantly only when vehicles can operate with minimal human intervention. Until then, operating costs remain elevated and profitability remains uncertain.

\- Scale is a second challenge. Autonomous driving systems improve through real-world data collection, and Korean fleets remain small relative to those operating in the US and China. This creates a risk that Korean firms fall further behind global leaders in both data accumulation and system performance.

\- Commercial viability remains another open question. Even if autonomous vehicles operate safely, fleet economics must ultimately compete with traditional taxi services. High sensor costs, maintenance requirements, remote operations, and ongoing software development create significant cost burdens that remain largely unproven at scale.

\- Finally, regulatory and liability issues remain unresolved. As vehicles move toward driverless operation, responsibility for accidents may shift among manufacturers, software providers, operators, and insurers. Establishing clear accountability frameworks will be essential before widespread deployment becomes possible.

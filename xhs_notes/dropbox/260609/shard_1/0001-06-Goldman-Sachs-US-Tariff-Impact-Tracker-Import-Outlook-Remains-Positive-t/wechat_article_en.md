# Import Data Is Surging Past Liberation Day, But the Real Test for Supply Chains Begins in June

The weekly data from the US Tariff Impact Tracker is delivering a clear and counterintuitive signal: containerized imports from China to the United States are not collapsing. They are, in fact, accelerating. Laden vessel flows from China to the US rose sequentially by 1% in the most recent week and registered a 3% year-over-year gain. More strikingly, the Port of Los Angeles is expected to see TEU volumes increase by 6% next week and 4% two weeks out, with year-over-year comparisons of plus 21.5% and plus 34% respectively. These are not the numbers of a trade war freezing global commerce. They are the numbers of a system that has learned to adapt, front-run, and hedge against policy uncertainty.

The conventional narrative holds that tariffs destroy trade. But what the data reveals is something more nuanced: tariffs create volatility, and volatility creates opportunity for those who can move faster than the policy cycle. The surge we are observing is partly a function of "lapping Liberation Day"—the anniversary of the April 2, 2026 tariff escalation—but it is also evidence of genuine restocking. Shippers are not retreating; they are recalculating. The question that matters now is whether this positive momentum can survive the summer.

The controlling insight from this report is that the import outlook remains positive through the end of May, but the sustainability of that trend hinges on how shippers behave in June. The data through early June shows strong year-over-year comparisons, but these are easy comps. The real analytical challenge is to determine whether the current flow represents a one-time pull-forward of demand or the beginning of a structural reset in supply chain strategy. The answer will determine not just freight rates, but the earnings trajectory of the entire transport sector.

## The Liberation Day Lapping Effect Masks a Deeper Restocking Cycle That Could Reshape Inventory Strategy

The most immediate observation from the weekly data is that year-over-year comparisons are flattered by the low base created by Liberation Day. But that is only part of the story. When we look at the sequential data—week-over-week changes in laden vessels and TEUs—the picture is one of genuine demand, not just statistical noise. The Port of Los Angeles is projecting double-digit year-over-year growth for the next two weeks. That is not a base effect; that is a restocking cycle.

Shippers are behaving as if they expect the current tariff regime to be the new normal, and they are adjusting their inventory buffers accordingly. The report notes that effective tariff rates have, in some cases, come down relative to the peak of uncertainty, and that is creating a window for restocking. The logic is straightforward: if you are a retailer or manufacturer facing a known tariff cost, the rational response is to bring in as much product as possible before the next policy shift. This is not panic buying; it is strategic inventory management.

The implication for investors is that the current import surge is likely to persist through the end of May and into early June, but it may fade if the administration provides no clarity on the post-150-day tariff framework under Section 122. The report's authors explicitly flag this uncertainty as a risk to medium- and longer-term freight planning. The restocking cycle is real, but it may be finite. The question is whether it will be followed by a demand vacuum or a new equilibrium.

## Trade Uncertainty Is Now a Structural Feature, Not a Cyclical Bug, and It Is Reshaping Freight Pricing in Unexpected Ways

The report makes a critical distinction between two types of uncertainty: near-term and medium-term. In the near term, the uncertainty is about which countries will benefit from lower effective tariff rates and how quickly manufacturing can shift. The data suggests that Asia ex-China is already seeing some benefit, with vessel growth from that region matching China's on a year-over-year basis. This is consistent with a "China Plus One" strategy that has been discussed for years but is now being executed in real time.

In the medium term, the uncertainty is about the trajectory of US trade policy itself. The Supreme Court ruling and the subsequent use of Section 122 authority have created a legal and procedural framework that could be used for further escalation or de-escalation. The report does not take a view on which path the administration will choose, but it does note that the lack of clarity is already affecting freight planning. Shippers are reluctant to commit to long-term contracts or capacity investments when the rules of the game could change in 150 days.

The impact on freight pricing is complex. The report notes that ocean container rates were up 51% sequentially in the most recent week, though down 12% year-over-year. This volatility is not simply a function of demand; it is also a function of capacity dynamics and fuel costs. The report warns that ongoing geopolitical conflicts could drive rates higher, but it cautions against conflating capacity-driven price increases with demand-driven ones. The key insight is that the current rate environment is a mix of genuine demand, fuel surcharges, and strategic pricing by carriers. Investors who treat all rate increases as bullish signals risk misreading the cycle.

## The West Coast Trucking Market Is Sending a Mixed Signal That Demands Careful Interpretation

Truck spot rates on the West Coast were up 25% year-over-year, excluding fuel. That is a strong number, and it suggests that the import surge is translating into real demand for drayage and short-haul trucking. But load availability was down 1% sequentially, and rail intermodal volumes were down 1% year-over-year after a strong prior week. The pattern is one of choppiness, not trend.

The report's authors are careful to note that weekly data is noisy and that multi-week trends are more informative. But the mixed signals in the trucking market are worth examining because they may indicate a shift in how goods are moving inland. If import volumes are rising but truck load availability is flat to down, it could mean that more cargo is moving via rail or that inventory is being held at ports longer. Alternatively, it could simply be a timing issue—a week of data does not make a trend.

For investors in transport stocks, the trucking data is a reminder that the recovery is not uniform. The report upgrades the trucking sector on the view that the likelihood of recession has lessened, but it also notes that the earnings bottom has been elusive. The path to an earnings upgrade cycle depends on volume growth, particularly in higher-margin business-to-business and manufacturing flows. The current import surge is consumer-driven, and that may not be enough to sustain a broad-based trucking recovery.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The June Cliff and the Red Sea Reopening Risk

The most important unanswered question in this report is what happens in June. The data through early June is positive, but the report itself flags that the trajectory through June will be the true test. If the restocking cycle fades and import volumes revert to pre-Liberation Day levels, then the current optimism will look like a head fake. If volumes hold or accelerate, then the narrative shifts from a one-time pull-forward to a structural reset.

A second unanswered question is the potential impact of a Red Sea reopening. The report mentions this as a risk to ocean rates, noting that a return of Red Sea transit could add effective capacity and pressure rates lower. But it does not quantify this risk or model the timing. For investors in freight forwarders and ocean carriers, the Red Sea dynamic is a wild card that could either amplify or offset the tariff-driven demand surge.

A third open question is the behavior of Asia ex-China exports. The data shows that vessel growth from that region is matching China's, but TEU growth is lagging. This could mean that the shift to alternative sourcing is happening in volume but not in value, or it could mean that the data is too noisy to draw firm conclusions. Either way, the divergence between vessel counts and TEU volumes is worth monitoring closely.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Four Scenarios for the Rest of 2026

Based on the data and analysis in this report, investors should consider four scenarios for the remainder of 2026:

Scenario One: Sustained Restocking. In this scenario, import volumes remain elevated through the summer as shippers continue to build inventory buffers. The positive year-over-year comparisons persist, and the transport sector sees a genuine volume inflection. This scenario is most favorable for trucking, parcels, and freight forwarders with customs brokerage exposure.

Scenario Two: Demand Vacuum. In this scenario, the current restocking cycle runs its course by early summer, and import volumes fall sharply as shippers pause to assess the post-150-day tariff regime. The transport sector experiences a second-half slowdown, and the earnings recovery is delayed into 2027.

Scenario Three: Structural Shift. In this scenario, the tariff uncertainty accelerates the China Plus One strategy, and Asia ex-China volumes grow structurally while China volumes stabilize at a lower level. This scenario benefits logistics providers with diversified networks and penalizes those overly dependent on China-US lanes.

Scenario Four: Geopolitical Shock. In this scenario, ongoing conflicts escalate, driving fuel costs and ocean rates higher, while simultaneously depressing global demand. This scenario is the most negative for transport stocks, as it combines cost inflation with volume contraction.

Each scenario has different implications for stock selection, and the data from the next four to six weeks will be critical in determining which path the market is on.

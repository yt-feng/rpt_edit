# Supply Chains Are Not Yet Normal: The Congestion Index Is Quietly Diverging From the Narrative

The dominant narrative in markets today is that supply chain chaos is behind us. Inflation has moderated. Port backlogs have cleared. The memory of 30,000 containers floating off California has faded into a cautionary tale told in boardrooms. Yet the data tells a more nuanced story. A global investment bank report tracking U.S. supply chain congestion reveals that the bottleneck scale remains at a score of 2 on a 10-point scale. That is not zero. It is not one. It is two. And the direction of travel matters more than the absolute level.

For the week ending June 8, the weekly composite index increased by 7% week over week, reversing the prior week's 5% decline. The index is now moving in the wrong direction. This is not a new crisis. But it is a signal that the system has not yet achieved the frictionless state that many investors assume is the baseline. The congestion scale has been stuck at 2 for months, and the underlying metrics are sending mixed signals that deserve careful parsing.

The implications for transport equities, inflation dynamics, and corporate inventory strategy are significant. A persistent congestion score of 2, with upward weekly volatility, suggests that the supply chain is operating in a new equilibrium -- one that is more expensive, less predictable, and structurally different from the pre-COVID baseline. The question is not whether we have recovered from the pandemic-era bottlenecks. The question is whether we have permanently shifted to a higher-friction operating environment.

## The Composite Index Is Rising Again, and the Composition of That Rise Contains Critical Signals

The headline number is straightforward: the weekly bottleneck index increased by 7% week over week. But a 7% move in a composite index is not noise. It reflects real changes in the physical movement of goods across multiple nodes of the supply chain. To understand what this means, one must look beneath the aggregate to the components that are driving the move.

East Coast container ship backlogs increased from 4 to 5 ships in the most recent week. This is a small absolute number, but the direction is notable. The East Coast has become a pressure relief valve for the system as shippers diversify away from West Coast ports. When East Coast backlogs rise, it signals that diversion strategies are reaching capacity limits. The West Coast backlog remained flat at 1, which is essentially negligible. But the East Coast increase suggests that the system is finding new pinch points as it reconfigures.

Ocean container shipping rates from China to the U.S. West Coast accelerated to +16% year over year. This is a meaningful increase, particularly given that rates had been deeply negative on a year-over-year basis as recently as March. The sequential acceleration from +14% to +16% may seem modest, but it confirms that the downward trend in rates has bottomed and is now reversing. For companies that have been enjoying lower freight costs as a tailwind to margins, this trend bears close monitoring.

Chassis dwell times increased slightly on average at U.S. ports. This is a classic indicator of friction in the last mile of port operations. When chassis dwell times rise, it means containers are sitting longer on equipment that needs to be recirculated. This creates a cascading effect: slower chassis turns mean fewer containers moved per unit time, which puts upward pressure on both rates and transit times. The increase is small, but it is consistent with a system that is operating near its effective capacity rather than with significant slack.

## Rail Intermodal Growth Is Decelerating Sharply, and This Is a Leading Indicator of Weaker Demand

The most striking data point in the report is the deceleration in West Coast rail intermodal traffic growth. The average growth rate for Union Pacific and BNSF fell from +14% year over year in the prior week to -2% year over year in the most recent week. This is not a gradual softening. It is a sharp reversal.

BNSF intermodal traffic dropped from +18.7% year over year to +3.3% year over year. Union Pacific went from +8.4% year over year to -7% year over year. The magnitude of these swings suggests that something structural is happening in the movement of goods from West Coast ports to inland destinations. Rail intermodal is the primary mode for moving containerized imports from the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach to the rest of the country. When rail volumes decelerate this quickly, it implies that either import volumes are declining or that the mix of transportation modes is shifting.

The most likely explanation is a combination of both. Import volumes have been softening as consumer spending patterns normalize and as retailers work through excess inventory. The monthly data on inbound loaded containers at the three major West Coast ports shows year-over-year declines that are becoming more pronounced. After growing at +9.5% year over year in April, the data for May and June shows a sharp deceleration. This is consistent with the broader narrative of a normalization in goods demand after the pandemic-era surge.

But there is a second-order effect that is more concerning. If rail intermodal volumes are declining while ocean shipping rates are rising, it suggests that the cost of moving goods inland is becoming a binding constraint. Shippers may be choosing to hold inventory at port-adjacent warehouses rather than paying the full cost of inland distribution. This would show up in warehousing utilization data, which indeed remains elevated.

## The Monthly Metrics Tell a Story of Gradual Tightening That the Weekly Data Confirms

The report includes monthly metrics that provide a lower-frequency but more comprehensive view of the system. The April data shows mixed results compared to March, but the pattern is consistent with a system that is not loosening as quickly as many assume.

Warehousing capacity indices have been declining. The Logistics Managers' Index for warehousing capacity fell from 55.4 in April to 50.0 in May, and then to 47.8 in June. A reading below 50 indicates contraction. This means that warehouse space is becoming less available, not more. For companies that have been relying on just-in-time inventory strategies, this creates a strategic problem. If warehouse space is tightening, the ability to hold buffer inventory is constrained, which increases the risk of stockouts when demand spikes.

Warehousing utilization, meanwhile, has been rising. The utilization index moved from 60.1 in April to 62.5 in May to 62.2 in June. This is not a dramatic increase, but it confirms that existing warehouse space is being used more intensively. The combination of falling capacity and rising utilization is a classic recipe for higher storage costs and tighter logistics markets.

Door-to-door shipping days from China to the U.S. have stabilized around 47 to 50 days, down from the peak of 54 days in March. This is a positive development, but it is important to note that the pre-COVID baseline was significantly lower. The system has improved, but it has not returned to the frictionless state that existed before the pandemic. A 47-day transit time is not a crisis, but it is a constraint that affects inventory planning and working capital requirements.

Class 8 truck driver counts continue to decline gradually. The driver count fell from 1,491,400 in March to 1,490,600 in April to 1,487,700 in May. This is a slow bleed, but it is persistent. The trucking industry has been struggling with driver shortages for years, and the data suggests that the situation is not improving. For the supply chain, this means that the capacity to move goods from ports and warehouses to final destinations is structurally constrained.

## The Report Raises More Questions Than It Answers About the New Normal

The most valuable contribution of this report is not the data it provides, but the questions it forces readers to confront. The bottleneck scale has been at 2 for months, but the underlying metrics are sending contradictory signals. Ocean rates are rising, rail volumes are decelerating, chassis dwell times are increasing, and warehousing capacity is tightening. This is not the profile of a system that has fully normalized.

One critical question is whether the current congestion level represents a floor or a ceiling. If the index has settled at 2 because the system has found a new equilibrium, then investors need to recalibrate their expectations for transport costs and inventory carrying costs. A persistent score of 2 implies that the supply chain is operating with less slack than the pre-COVID baseline, which means that any demand shock will be amplified. A small increase in consumer spending could translate into significant congestion and cost increases.

Another unanswered question is the impact of tariffs and geopolitical conflicts. The report explicitly acknowledges that the key question is what impact these factors will have on demand and the timing of freight flows. The report notes that if supply chain pressures continue to mitigate, the index could move into consistent territory of 1 in 2026. But the path to that outcome is uncertain. Tariffs could accelerate reshoring and regionalization of supply chains, which would change the geography of congestion. Geopolitical conflicts could disrupt key shipping lanes, as we have already seen in the Red Sea.

A third question is about the relationship between congestion and inflation. The conventional wisdom is that supply chain congestion was a temporary driver of inflation that has now receded. But if the congestion index is stuck at 2 and showing signs of upward movement, then the disinflationary tailwind from supply chain normalization may be weaker than expected. This has implications for central bank policy and for corporate pricing power.

## A Decision Framework for Supply Chain Risk in a Persistent Congestion Regime

For executives and investors who need to translate this analysis into actionable decisions, the following framework can help structure thinking about supply chain risk.

The first dimension is time horizon. For the next 6 to 12 months, the data suggests that the system will remain in a state of moderate congestion. The index is unlikely to spike to 8 or 9, but it is also unlikely to fall to 1. Companies should plan for freight costs that are above the pre-COVID baseline and for transit times that are longer than historical averages. This means that inventory buffers should be maintained at levels higher than the just-in-time ideal.

The second dimension is geography. The divergence between West Coast and East Coast congestion patterns matters. The West Coast is relatively fluid, with minimal ship backlogs. The East Coast is showing signs of tightening. Companies that have diversified their port exposure to the East Coast may need to reassess whether that diversification is still providing the intended benefits. If East Coast ports are becoming congested, the diversification strategy may be losing its effectiveness.

The third dimension is mode. The deceleration in rail intermodal volumes, combined with rising ocean rates, suggests that the cost trade-offs between different transportation modes are shifting. Shippers should be modeling scenarios in which rail becomes relatively more expensive or less reliable, and in which trucking becomes the marginal mode for time-sensitive shipments. This has implications for logistics budgets and for service level commitments to customers.

The fourth dimension is inventory. The tightening of warehousing capacity and the rise in warehousing utilization suggest that holding inventory is becoming more expensive and more difficult. Companies need to optimize their inventory positioning to balance the cost of storage against the risk of stockouts. This is a classic risk management problem, but the parameters have shifted. The optimal inventory level is higher than it was in 2019, but the cost of holding that inventory is also higher.

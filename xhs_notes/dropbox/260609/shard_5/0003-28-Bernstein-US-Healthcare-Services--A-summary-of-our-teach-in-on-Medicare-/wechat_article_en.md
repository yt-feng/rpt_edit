# Medicare Advantage Is Not a Growth Story Anymore — It Is a Margin Recovery Story with Utility-Like Endgame

The dominant narrative around Medicare Advantage has long been one of relentless expansion: more seniors, more enrollment, more market share. That story is now incomplete. MA enrollment will continue to grow, but at a moderating 4–5% pace, and the program is already capturing over half of all Medicare beneficiaries. The real investment thesis has shifted from volume growth to margin recovery. After a brutal cycle of rate compression, elevated medical cost trends, and excessive competition, MA margins are troughing. The industry is now entering a period of disciplined repricing, competitor exit, and structural consolidation that should lift margins by roughly 1% in 2026 and 0.5% annually through 2030. This is not a return to the high-margin days of the past. It is the beginning of a utility-like equilibrium where a handful of scaled players operate with modest but stable returns.

This transition matters now because 2024 and 2025 represented the low point of the insurance cycle in MA. Rate increases from CMS were insufficient to cover accelerating medical cost trends, particularly in Part B outpatient services and hospital utilization post-COVID. Several managed care organizations reported margin compression, and the market punished the sector accordingly. But the forces that drove that pain are now reversing. The 2026 rate notice from CMS showed a sharp effective growth rate of over 9%, a dramatic improvement from the roughly 2.3% rates of the prior two years. Combined with a withdrawal of marginal competitors and a renewed focus on pricing for profitability, the conditions for a margin inflection are in place.

The strategic question for investors is no longer whether MA will grow, but who will capture the recovery, how durable the improved margins will be, and what structural risks could upend the utility-like endgame. This article unpacks the cycle, the competitive dynamics, the policy backdrop, and the disruptive forces that will define the next phase of MA.

## The Recent Margin Compression in MA Was a Classic Insurance Cycle, Not a Structural Breakdown

The margin crisis in Medicare Advantage from 2023 through 2025 is best understood as the predictable downswing of an insurance underwriting cycle, not evidence that the program is fundamentally broken. During the 2005–2015 period, MA revenue grew at 15–20% annually, and industry margins reached approximately 5%. Those returns attracted a wave of new entrants, including regional plans, startup Medicare Advantage organizations, and large commercial insurers expanding their footprint. As competition intensified, plans competed on benefits, premiums, and network design, compressing margins even before medical cost trends accelerated.

When CMS rate increases slowed sharply in 2024 and 2025, the combination of elevated competition and inadequate rate growth created a perfect storm. Medical cost trends, particularly in Part B outpatient services, ran well above the effective rate growth that CMS provided. The gap between rate growth and cost trend, which historically averaged roughly 200 basis points in favor of cost growth, widened further during this period. Plans that had underpriced to gain market share found themselves unable to absorb the shock. The result was a sector-wide margin trough.

This pattern is consistent with how insurance cycles have historically played out in Medicare Advantage. The current recovery is being driven by three factors that are already visible. First, competition is withdrawing. Several smaller and mid-sized players have scaled back their MA footprints, and the rate of new entry has slowed significantly. Second, the 2026 rate notice represents a meaningful improvement, with CMS providing a substantial catch-up after two years of below-trend increases. Third, the industry has collectively shifted its focus from market share growth to margin protection. Plans are repricing benefits, tightening networks, and reducing supplemental benefit generosity. The combination of these forces should allow industry margins to expand by roughly 100 basis points in 2026 and continue improving at a more moderate pace through the end of the decade.

The implication for investors is that the worst of the margin compression is behind the sector, but the recovery will be gradual and uneven. Plans with scale, underwriting discipline, and strong star ratings are best positioned to capture the upside. Those that relied on aggressive benefit design to attract price-sensitive members may continue to struggle as the competitive environment normalizes.

## The Long-Term Endgame for MA Is a Utility-Like Market with Four to Five Scaled Players

The most important structural insight from the current cycle is that Medicare Advantage is evolving toward a utility-like market structure. This is not a prediction of low returns, but a recognition that the industry is consolidating around a small number of large, efficient players who will operate with modest but stable margins. The report projects that MA margins will settle in the 2–3% range over the long term, while Medicaid margins will stabilize around 1.5%. This is consistent with the economics of regulated utility industries, where returns are constrained by policy but predictable enough to support steady capital allocation.

The top three players — UnitedHealth Group, Humana, and CVS Health — already control approximately 55% of MA membership. The report identifies a long tail of smaller competitors, but the trend is toward further consolidation. Scale advantages in Medicare Advantage are significant. Larger plans have better bargaining power with providers, more sophisticated analytics for risk adjustment and utilization management, and the ability to spread fixed costs across a larger membership base. They also have the resources to invest in value-based care arrangements, which are becoming increasingly central to margin performance.

The utility-like endgame has important implications for valuation. Historically, MA-focused managed care organizations have traded at premiums to the broader market, reflecting their growth profile and margin potential. As the market matures and margins converge toward a stable but lower range, those premiums are likely to compress. Investors will need to evaluate MA-focused companies less as growth stories and more as steady-state cash flow generators. The differentiation will come from operational efficiency, star rating performance, and the ability to manage medical cost trends better than peers.

However, the consolidation thesis is not without risk. The report notes that the market remains relatively fragmented, with a long tail of regional and specialty plans. Some of these players may be acquired, but others may persist as niche competitors targeting specific geographies or member segments. The speed and completeness of consolidation will depend on regulatory attitudes toward market concentration, as well as the ability of smaller plans to remain competitive through partnerships or technology investments.

## Policy Risk Is Shifting from Existential Threats to Affordability Debates

One of the most important strategic developments in the Medicare Advantage landscape is the evolution of policy risk. During the 2010s, the primary policy threat to MA was the possibility of Medicare for All or other single-payer proposals that would eliminate the program entirely. That threat has receded significantly. MA now represents approximately 55% of Medicare enrollment, compared to roughly one-third during earlier reform debates. The program has become too large and too popular to dismantle. Even progressive policymakers have shifted their focus from eliminating MA to reforming it.

The new policy battleground is affordability and access. The report identifies a growing concern that the uninsured rate, which fell to historic lows during the pandemic, could drift back toward 9% by 2028 if current policy trends continue. Democratic policymakers are likely to push for measures that restore the uninsured rate to around 5%, which could include expanded subsidies for marketplace coverage, Medicaid expansion in remaining non-expansion states, or new public option proposals. These efforts would not directly target MA, but they would affect the broader managed care landscape and could create regulatory pressure on MA plan designs and premiums.

The more immediate policy risk for MA is the ongoing debate about star ratings, risk adjustment, and payment adequacy. CMS has made several changes to the star rating system in recent years, including the addition of health equity measures and adjustments to how quality bonuses are calculated. These changes have created volatility in plan revenues, particularly for plans that rely heavily on bonus payments. The report does not provide a definitive view on how star rating reform will play out, but it is clear that the regulatory environment is becoming more complex and less predictable.

For investors, the policy message is nuanced. The existential risk to MA has declined, but the program is not immune to regulatory headwinds. The most important variable to monitor is the annual rate notice from CMS, which sets the effective growth rate for MA payments. The 2026 notice was favorable, but future notices could revert to tighter increases if medical cost trends moderate or if fiscal pressures on the federal budget intensify. Investors should also watch for changes to risk adjustment models, which could shift payments among plans and create winners and losers.

## Three Disruptors Could Reshape the MA Margin Trajectory: GLP-1s, AI, and Value-Based Care

The margin recovery thesis described above assumes a relatively stable operating environment. But three disruptors — GLP-1 receptor agonists, artificial intelligence, and the continued expansion of value-based care — could meaningfully alter the trajectory of MA margins, either positively or negatively.

GLP-1 drugs, originally developed for diabetes management and now widely used for weight loss, represent both a cost challenge and a potential opportunity for MA plans. The report notes that GLP-1s will contribute to rising pharmacy costs, which is a near-term headwind for margins. However, the longer-term effect could be more positive. If GLP-1s meaningfully reduce obesity-related complications, cardiovascular events, and other chronic conditions, they could extend life expectancy and reduce overall medical spending for the senior population. The report estimates that GLP-1s could increase the total addressable Medicare market by 5–10% through longer lifespans. For MA plans that can manage pharmacy costs effectively, the net effect could be favorable.

Artificial intelligence is the second disruptor, and its impact is harder to quantify. The report identifies AI as a tool that could improve the efficiency of hospital operations and patient care. For MA plans, the most immediate applications of AI are in prior authorization, claims processing, and utilization management. These are areas where MA plans have historically faced criticism for administrative burden and member dissatisfaction. If AI can streamline these processes while maintaining cost control, it could improve both margins and member satisfaction. However, the adoption of AI in healthcare is still early, and the report does not provide specific estimates of its financial impact.

Value-based care is the third and most established disruptor. The report argues that VBC will become increasingly central as margin pressure persists, driven by the view that reimbursement rates will lag medical cost trends by 100–150 basis points over the long term. MA plans that can successfully transition their provider networks to value-based arrangements will be better positioned to manage cost trends and protect margins. The challenge is that VBC requires significant upfront investment in care coordination infrastructure, data analytics, and provider partnerships. Plans that have already made these investments, particularly UnitedHealth Group through its Optum platform, have a competitive advantage.

The interaction among these three disruptors is uncertain. GLP-1s could increase costs in the short term but reduce them in the long term. AI could improve efficiency but also create new regulatory risks around data privacy and algorithmic fairness. Value-based care could improve margins for some plans while creating financial strain for others that lack the scale or expertise to implement it effectively. Investors should view these disruptors not as binary outcomes but as variables that will differentiate winners from losers in the utility-like endgame.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Shape of the Margin Recovery

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several important questions unresolved. The most critical is the precise timing and shape of the margin recovery. The report projects a 1% margin improvement in 2026 followed by 0.5% annual growth through 2030, but this trajectory depends on assumptions about medical cost trends, competitive behavior, and regulatory policy that are inherently uncertain.

If medical cost trends moderate faster than expected, margins could recover more quickly. If they remain elevated due to persistent inflation in labor costs or hospital pricing, the recovery could be delayed or attenuated. Similarly, if competition withdraws more aggressively than anticipated, the remaining players could capture disproportionate margin gains. But if new entrants or existing competitors double down on market share growth, the margin recovery could be slower and shallower.

The report also does not fully address the distribution of margin recovery across the industry. Some plans are better positioned than others based on their geographic mix, star ratings, and value-based care capabilities. The report provides individual stock ratings, but it does not offer a systematic framework for identifying which plans will capture the most upside from the margin cycle. Investors will need to conduct their own analysis of plan-level exposures to rate changes, medical cost trends, and competitive dynamics.

Finally, the report leaves open the question of how the utility-like endgame will affect valuation multiples. If MA margins stabilize at 2–3%, what is the appropriate earnings multiple for a mature, regulated business? The answer depends on growth expectations, capital return policies, and the perceived stability of the regulatory environment. These are questions that the report raises but does not fully resolve.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the MA Margin Cycle

For investors who want to act on the insights in this report, a structured decision framework is essential. The first step is to assess each plan's exposure to the margin cycle. Plans with high MA concentration, such as Humana, are more sensitive to margin improvement than diversified insurers like Cigna or UnitedHealth Group. However, high concentration also means higher risk if the recovery is slower than expected.

The second step is to evaluate each plan's competitive positioning. Scale matters, but so does star rating performance, provider network strength, and value-based care penetration. Plans that have invested in care coordination and risk adjustment capabilities are better positioned to manage medical cost trends and capture the full benefit of rate increases.

The third step is to assess policy and regulatory risk. Plans with heavy exposure to dual-eligible populations or markets with aggressive state Medicaid policies face different risks than plans focused on the individual MA market. The 2026 rate notice was favorable, but future notices could be less so, particularly if fiscal pressures on the federal budget intensify.

The fourth step is to consider the disruptors. Plans that are early adopters of AI and value-based care may have a competitive advantage, but the benefits of these investments may take years to materialize. GLP-1 exposure is a double-edged sword: plans that manage pharmacy costs effectively could benefit from longer member tenure, but those that fail to control drug spending could see margin erosion.

This framework is not a substitute for detailed financial analysis, but it provides a structured way to think about the key variables that will determine which plans outperform in the margin recovery.

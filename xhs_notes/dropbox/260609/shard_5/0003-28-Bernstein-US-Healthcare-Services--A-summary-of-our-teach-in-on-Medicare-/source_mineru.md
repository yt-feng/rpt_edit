Insurance cycle - We see the recent negative margins in MA as having been the result of a traditional insurance cycle driven by excessive competition. Revenue growth rates in the 15-20% range during 2005-2015 and margins of around 5% attracted a significant amount of additional competition. This competition lead to reduced margins and set the stage for a margin crisis as rates shocks occurred.

We see MA margins inflecting after a trough period. Following a period of margin compression, we expect industry MA margins to expand \~1% in 2026, followed by \~0.5% annual growth through 2030. This recovery is driven by a withdrawal of competition, rate improvement off 2024–25 lows, and an industry focus on pricing for profitability.

MA growth remains durable, but is moderating. Moving forward, we expect MA enrollment to grow at a \~4–5% pace. The value proposition remains compelling with MA offering enhanced supplemental benefits for beneficiaries and structurally lower costs versus FFS for the government. We ultimately see MA capturing virtually all the Medicare market.

The long-term Government MCO market will trend “utility-like.” Over time, we expect both MA and Medicaid to resemble utility-like markets, consolidating around \~4–5 scaled players and operating with relatively modest margins (\~1.5% for Medicaid; \~2–3% for MA).

Key disruptors include GLP-1s, AI, and VBC. GLP-1s will contribute to rising pharmacy costs, but offer the potential of increasing life span, potentially expanding Medicare TAM by 5-10%. AI offers the opportunity to improve the efficiency of hospital operations and patient care. VBC will become increasingly central as margin pressure persists, driven by our view that reimbursement rates will lag medical cost trend by \~100–150 bps long-term.

Affordability and access will dominate the policy debate. Policy focus is increasingly centered on affordability and coverage expansion. Medicare for All appears less viable than in prior cycles, particularly with MA now representing \~55% of Medicare enrollment vs. \~1/3 during earlier reform debates. We expect a Democratic push to restore the uninsured rate to around the 5% level, from what we see could drift towards a potential 9% by 2028.

## WHAT IS MEDICARE ADVANTAGE?

Medicare covers about 20% of the population. Medicaid and safety net programs cover \~30% and employer \~50%. However, the amount of spend on Medicare is larger than its proportion of members due to the increased medical spend by the senior population.

## THE MA PRODUCT, DISTRIBUTION, AND VALUE PROPOSITION

Value proposition of MA. In general, traditional medicare pays for 80% of healthcare costs and the patient pays for 20%. Members have to purchase stand-alone drug coverage and supplemental insurance to pay for the remainder. MA plans cover these extra expenses, usually have out of packet maximums, and provide supplemental benefits like vision, dental, and hearing aids.

Financial elements are generally the highest priority buying criteria for consumers. 65-70% of the MA market has zero premiums, and the remainder generally has a small premium. A second concern for consumers is whether their preferred provider is in network, which can be a reason to avoid MA for some seniors. Supplemental benefits such as cash cards, vision, and chiropractic are also important determinants of consumer satisfaction. Brand and ratings is of lower importance, but Star ratings can be a major determinant impacting the deductible or supplemental benefits a plan provides. In general, consumers are happier with MA than they are with commercial insurance. This could be due to the lower monthly premiums and deductible associated with MA. Consumer satisfaction surveys between MA and FFS Medicare reveal comparable satisfaction ratings, with prior authorizations and network restrictions being the main consumer complaints surrounding MA.

The reason patients switch to MA is typically the extra benefits it provides. Extra benefits include Silver Sneakers, hearing aids, and other benefits which appeal to seniors. Every year, members have to reaffirm their choice between MA and FFS Medicare, providing an opportunity for additional members to enter MA. On average, MA members receive \$194 per month in extra benefits, equating to \~15% extra value. The largest proportion of extra benefit is cost sharing, which includes lower copay and plan maximums. The second-largest component is the included Part D coverage, and the third largest is supplemental benefits. Around 2017-2019, MA plans began offering benefits to cover part or all of Part B, and this has become the fastest growing component of extra benefits. The extra benefits that are offered can be another differentiating feature in product design.

## MEDICAL COSTS AND MA RATES

Cost trend has been growing faster than MA rate increases, which was a major reason for MA underperformance in 2023, 2024, and 2025. CMS declares each year what the growth rate has been for medical costs, which has been around 4-5%. Medicare Part A (hospital) costs trended around 2.5-3% pre-COVID and have been higher post-COVID due to wage inflation and increased utilization. Part B (outpatient) has run higher than Part A. When combining inpatient and outpatient expenses, cost trend has averaged around 4-5%, as can be seen in Exhibit 8.

Rate notices and cost trend evaluation are done in the year before plans take effect. In February, the initial rate increases are released, and the final rate is released in April. The rates can vary based on geography. MCOs observe trend throughout the year, and MA plan bids are submitted in early June. When an MCO is developing their plan, they consider the rate increase, cost trend, and desired product features before submitting their bid. Plans are finalized in October and open enrollment begins October 15 $^{th}$ for members to choose their plan. The main factors to monitor during the plan year is whether negative selection bias has been introduced by changes to plans and whether there have been changes in utilization. Q2 results are often a good barometer of whether MCOs will observe impacts from benefit design.

MCO bid construction. The formula for constructing a bid includes estimating medical costs, determining extra benefits and premium levels, and determining operating costs to define a target profit level. It's generally perceived that MA saves about 30% compared to Medicare FFS. In MA, the extra savings are used for supplemental benefits and operating expenses. Coming into 2026, the advance notice rate increase was \~0%. A potential side effect of this margin compression could have been "shrinkflation." This is seen in consumer goods where manufacturers shrink package size. A similar mechanism can occur in the MA industry by decreasing benefits and increasing the deductible. MCOs can also improve margins through things like VBC to improve costs. This mechanism is core to our thesis that VBC's value proposition will increase in the face of prolonged margin compression. Exiting unprofitable markets is another option when faced with margin pressure, which can be a quick way to achieve margin expansion.

There are several levers to improve MA revenue. In traditional businesses like Employer, pricing discipline involves increasing rates, but in MA the government largely sets the rate increases. However, MA plans still have several mechanisms to impact revenue. The first is Star ratings, which provide bonuses for quality, and the second is risk adjustment, which provides increased payments for more complex patients. MCOs receive 5% extra payment for members who are in 4-star plans or above. These typically flow out as extra benefits but can also be a lever to improve margins. Additionally, MA plans can influence the cost of their product by exiting certain markets or products, cutting benefits, increasing deductibles, steering patients to lower cost providers through network management, and reducing operating costs.

## THE RECENT CRISIS

In 2023, we saw the remnants of a supply shock associated with COVID. Over time, growth in hospital employment

closely mirrors growth in same facility admissions. The $3^{rd}$ and $4^{th}$ quarters of 2020 experienced a supply shock recession, and employment in the healthcare sector was slower to bounce back than other industries. Hospital employment began to rebound in 2022, with hospital employment growth rate increasing 1-1.5 points faster than historical norms leading to a strong increase in hospital volumes. The significant increase in utilization impacted MCO margins in 2023. In recent years, hospital employment has overshot its historical trendline leading to a decline in hiring and a subsequent normalization in utilization, creating a tailwind for MCOs in 2025 and seemingly in 2026.

## How does MA Risk Adjustment works and v28 changes?

- Basic premise is MA plans should be paid more to manage members that have costlier conditions.  
CMS introduced v28 in 2023, to be phased in over a three-year period.  
- Constraining process - CMS also decided not include HCCs in the new model where conditions did not accurately predict costs  
- The net impact of these changes has been a 2-3% annual headwind to premiums (reflected in lower MA rates) since 2023 (CY26 is the last year of transition).

This concern over excess coding led to low MA rates during 2024/2025. The low effective rates were an executive agency effort to cut rates, and were coupled with the risk adjustment formula changes in v28.

## FUTURE ENVIRONMENT AND KEY ISSUES

There remains approximately 2.5% of risk adjustment due to excess coding intensity that we believe will ultimately need to be removed. CMS has suggested this could be closer to \~50 bps based on its February analysis. We expect any adjustment to be phased in over several years, likely through \~100 bps annual reductions to effective growth rates. CMS is also evaluating potential changes to base years and home risk assessments. In addition, there is retrospective exposure from risk adjustment audits and potential settlements. ELV has set aside \~\$1B related to its ongoing suit around excess coding, while our prior estimates suggest UnitedHealth's exposure is in the \~\$2–4B range.

MedPAC has argued that, beyond coding intensity, favorable selection may represent an additional \~10 percentage points of excess margin. We do not agree with this view, a position that has recently been supported by both the WSJ and Paragon. Historically, MA may have benefited from favorable selection via avoidance of higher-acuity beneficiaries, resulting in \~10% selection bias. However, with MA penetration now \~55%, risk adjustment mechanisms that compensate for higher-acuity patients, and enrollment trends skewing toward lower-income populations, we view persistence of meaningful selection bias as unlikely.

Fiscal pressure remains an important backdrop. Medicare spending currently represents \~13% of the federal budget and is projected to rise to \~18% by 2032. With the Medicare trust fund expected to be depleted by 2033, funding gaps will need to be addressed either through increased government contributions or higher beneficiary premiums. As a result, we expect MA rate growth to lag underlying cost trend by \~100–150 bps on a sustained basis.

Over time, we expect CMS to recognize structurally lower costs in MA relative to FFS, which could support policies aimed at increasing MA penetration. The current administration has already begun evaluating mechanisms such as auto-enrollment, though no formal policy has been implemented to date.

We are proponents that MA will move to a VBC model. Currently, about 1/3 of MA treatment is provided under a value-based care model. An alternative to pure VBC models are VBC-centric models, including Clover Health, that are disaggregating the principles of VBC and retaining them within an MA plan. These models are trying to engage physicians and enable physicians to accomplish the same goals as VBC but not through risk transfer, through bonus payments or paying a fee-for-service payment to perform additional services. The MA plan assists in coordinating the tasks that the physician would have to coordinate under a traditional VBC model. In our view, this makes the durability of MA higher, as it less likely to be purely disintermediated by VBC.

We believe vertical integration will remain an area of increasing policy scrutiny, although we view forced break-ups as a relatively low-probability outcome. A more realistic risk would be legislative efforts to effectively “stack” MLRs or cap margins across affiliated entities, which would reduce the economic rationale for maintaining integrated structures. In that scenario, traditional MCO and VBC businesses would likely be the first areas of focus, potentially leading to selective separation of these segments.

Importantly, we do not view this as inherently negative. In some cases, separation could unlock value or allow businesses to operate with greater focus. For example, we could see a scenario where UNH's VBC platform is able to grow more rapidly as a standalone entity.

Looking ahead, the 2028 election represents a key policy overhang. From a Democratic perspective, we expect the primary focus to center on expanding coverage back toward peak insured levels. The uninsured rate could drift toward \~9% by 2028, and reducing that back toward a more normalized \~5% level is likely to be a central policy priority, particularly against a backdrop of potential employment disruption from AI.

While “Medicare for All” continues to serve as a conceptual framework for broader system reform, political traction in recent cycles has been more closely tied to affordability and access. We expect these themes, rather than wholesale system overhaul, to dominate the policy debate. Although a Medicare for All-oriented candidate is likely to emerge, we believe coverage expansion will ultimately be the defining healthcare issue in the 2028 election cycle.

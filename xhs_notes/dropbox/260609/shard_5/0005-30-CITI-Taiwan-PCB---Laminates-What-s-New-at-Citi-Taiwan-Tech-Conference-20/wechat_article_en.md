# The Coming Bottleneck in AI Hardware Is Not in Chips but in Laminates

The most critical constraint on AI infrastructure expansion over the next 18 months will not be GPU availability, foundry capacity, or even power. It will be copper-clad laminate—the specialized material that sits between every silicon die and the circuit board that connects it to the world. A recent Taiwan technology conference revealed a consensus among three of the most important players in the printed circuit board and CCL supply chain: capacity additions in CCL are structurally lagging behind PCB expansion, and the resulting shortage is already driving price increases that customers are accepting without resistance. This is not a cyclical tightening. It is a structural imbalance that will reshape pricing power, supply relationships, and competitive positioning across the AI server supply chain.

The implications extend beyond the obvious beneficiaries. If CCL remains undersupplied relative to PCB capacity for the next several years, then the companies that control CCL production—particularly for the high-specification materials required by AI servers—will capture an outsized share of value in the hardware stack. Meanwhile, PCB manufacturers face a dual challenge: they must secure enough CCL to feed their own aggressive capacity expansions, and they must decide whether to absorb rising laminate costs or pass them through to hyperscaler customers who, at least for now, are willing to pay.

What makes this moment different from prior supply crunches is the nature of the demand. AI servers do not use commodity CCL. They require advanced grades—M7 and M8 materials—with precisely controlled dielectric properties, thermal stability, and dimensional consistency. These are not products that can be quickly ramped by any existing CCL factory. The qualification cycles are long, the raw material inputs are specialized, and the production yields are lower than for standard grades. The supply-demand imbalance is therefore not merely a matter of total tonnage. It is a matter of spec-grade availability, and that creates a bottleneck that cannot be solved by simply adding more capacity of any kind.

## CCL Capacity Additions Are Structurally Trailing PCB Expansion, Ensuring Sustained Pricing Power for Laminate Suppliers

The core insight from the conference is straightforward and consequential. Both major CCL suppliers, EMC and TUC, explicitly stated that their capacity expansion pace cannot keep up with the pace of PCB capacity additions from customers and other PCB manufacturers. This is not a temporary mismatch. It reflects a fundamental difference in the economics and execution timelines of the two industries.

PCB capacity can be added relatively quickly. A new PCB plant, particularly one focused on high-layer-count motherboards for servers, can be built and qualified in 18 to 24 months. The equipment is standardized, the process technology is mature, and the workforce requirements, while significant, are manageable in regions with established electronics manufacturing ecosystems. Gold Circuit Electronics, for example, plans to add one greenfield plant each year from 2027 through 2029. That is an aggressive cadence, but it is achievable because PCB manufacturing is a scale business with proven production models.

CCL capacity is a different proposition. Building a new CCL plant requires securing supply agreements for specialized raw materials—glass fiber, copper foil, and proprietary resin systems. The production equipment, particularly for high-end laminates, is less standardized and has longer lead times. The qualification process with customers, especially for AI-grade materials, can take 12 months or more. And the capital intensity per unit of output is higher than for PCB, which means that capacity decisions carry greater financial risk. As a result, CCL suppliers are more cautious about adding capacity, and when they do, the new volume comes online more slowly.

The consequence is that for every dollar of new PCB capacity being built, less than a dollar of new CCL capacity is being planned. That arithmetic leads directly to shortage, and shortage leads to pricing power. EMC and TUC both indicated that they would continue to raise prices, either to reflect inflation or to capture a premium based on customer demand. The language is important. They are not merely passing through cost increases. They are explicitly pricing for scarcity, and the market is accepting it.

## The Pricing Dynamic Has Shifted from Negotiation to Allocation, and Customers Are Accepting Higher Costs to Secure Supply

Perhaps the most striking signal from the conference was the attitude of PCB manufacturers toward rising CCL prices. Gold Circuit Electronics noted that its customers have all agreed to PCB price hikes. This is not a grudging acceptance. It is a strategic choice. Hyperscalers and server OEMs understand that the AI infrastructure buildout is constrained by supply chain bottlenecks, and they are willing to pay a premium to secure allocation.

This represents a fundamental shift in the power balance within the supply chain. For most of the past decade, PCB and CCL manufacturers operated in a buyer's market. Customers could play suppliers against each other, demand annual price reductions, and switch vendors with relative ease. That era is over. The combination of surging AI demand, limited CCL capacity, and the technical complexity of high-spec materials has created a seller's market, and the sellers know it.

The pricing behavior described at the conference is instructive. EMC intends to continue passing through higher raw material costs while maintaining long-term strategic relationships with key AI customers. TUC plans to adopt a more aggressive pricing strategy for M7 and below CCL grades, while keeping M8 pricing aligned with EMC. This suggests a tiered approach to pricing power: for the highest-spec materials, the two major suppliers are coordinating implicitly to avoid destructive competition; for lower grades, they are competing more aggressively. The strategic insight is that the real value capture will occur at the top of the spec ladder, where supply is tightest and switching costs are highest.

For PCB manufacturers, the implication is clear. They must secure CCL supply through long-term procurement forecasts and committed volumes. Gold Circuit Electronics stated that it can still obtain CCL through previously negotiated agreements, but rush orders outside of committed volumes are unlikely to be fulfilled. This means that PCB companies with strong supplier relationships and accurate demand forecasting will have a competitive advantage. Those that rely on spot purchases or last-minute orders will face production delays or higher costs.

## The Thailand Diversification Strategy Is Proceeding, but Execution Risks and Timeline Delays Create Uncertainty for PCB Manufacturers

A major theme of the conference was the geographic diversification of PCB and CCL production, particularly into Thailand. This is driven by customer demands for supply chain resilience and the desire to reduce dependence on China. However, the execution challenges are proving more significant than many investors expected.

Gold Circuit Electronics provided concrete numbers on its Thailand ramp. Monthly revenue contribution from the Thailand plant is expected to grow from approximately NT$600 million in the second quarter of 2026 to NT$1.3 billion in the third quarter. The facility is currently focused on general-purpose server products, with AI server production scheduled to begin in the second half of 2026. Profitability is expected to improve gradually as utilization ramps. These are achievable targets, but they imply a slower trajectory than the most optimistic scenarios.

TUC's Thailand experience is more cautionary. The company's Thailand facility is now expected to begin contributing revenue by the end of the third quarter of 2026, later than previously anticipated. The primary cause is delays in equipment deliveries. This is a reminder that building new production capacity in Southeast Asia is not simply a matter of replicating existing plants in China. The supply chains for specialized manufacturing equipment are global, and lead times are long. Labor availability, infrastructure quality, and regulatory approvals all add uncertainty.

The strategic question for investors is whether Thailand can become a meaningful production hub for AI-grade PCBs and CCLs within the next three years. The answer appears to be yes, but only for certain products and with significant execution risk. General-purpose server boards can be produced in Thailand relatively quickly. High-layer-count AI server boards and advanced CCL grades will take longer, and the qualification processes may be more stringent for facilities outside of China.

## The Report Leaves Critical Questions Unanswered About the Timing and Magnitude of the CCL Shortage

For all its valuable detail, the conference coverage leaves several important questions unresolved. These are not weaknesses in the report. They are the natural boundaries of what can be known at this point, and they represent the most important analytical challenges for investors.

First, how large is the CCL supply gap relative to demand? The report states that CCL capacity additions are trailing PCB additions, but it does not quantify the gap. Is it 10 percent, 20 percent, or more? The answer matters enormously for pricing forecasts and earnings models. A small gap can be managed through price increases and efficiency gains. A large gap implies production constraints that could cap the growth of AI server shipments.

Second, when will the shortage peak? The report suggests that CCL tightness will persist, but it does not specify a timeline. If the shortage is most acute in 2027 and begins to ease in 2028 as new capacity comes online, then the pricing power of CCL suppliers may be temporary. If the shortage extends into 2029 or beyond, then the structural thesis is much stronger.

Third, what is the risk of substitution? EMC noted that customer adoption of PTFE material remains at a very early evaluation stage and is not a done deal. But if PTFE or other alternative materials become viable for AI server applications, the competitive dynamics could shift. The report does not provide enough information to assess the likelihood or timeline of such substitution.

Fourth, how will the supply chain respond if the shortage becomes acute enough to constrain AI server production? At some point, hyperscalers may decide that the economics of paying higher CCL prices are preferable to delaying data center builds. But there is a limit to how much pricing power the supply chain can exercise before customers begin to push back or invest in alternative solutions.

These questions are not answerable from the conference alone. They require deeper analysis of capacity plans, demand forecasts, and technology roadmaps. But they are precisely the questions that serious investors should be asking.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the PCB-CCL Supply Chain

For investors and strategists evaluating opportunities in this space, the conference insights suggest a structured approach to decision-making. The framework has three dimensions: position in the value chain, spec-grade exposure, and geographic diversification.

The first dimension is value chain position. CCL suppliers have stronger pricing power than PCB manufacturers in the current environment, and that advantage is likely to persist as long as capacity additions remain unbalanced. Within PCB, companies with strong supplier relationships and long-term procurement agreements have a significant advantage over those that rely on spot markets. The key question for any PCB company is whether its CCL supply agreements are sufficient to support its planned capacity expansion.

The second dimension is spec-grade exposure. The tightest part of the CCL market is high-spec materials for AI servers. Companies that produce or consume large volumes of M7 and M8 CCL will experience the most pricing pressure and the most supply risk. Companies focused on lower-grade materials may face less severe constraints but also less pricing upside. The strategic choice is whether to move up the spec ladder and accept the supply risk in exchange for higher margins, or to remain in lower-grade segments where competition is more intense but supply is more reliable.

The third dimension is geographic diversification. Thailand is emerging as a key production hub, but the timeline and scale of the ramp are uncertain. Companies that can successfully establish AI-grade production in Thailand will gain a strategic advantage, particularly if customers continue to demand China-plus-one supply chains. Companies that are slow to diversify may face customer pressure or lose share to more agile competitors.

The most important insight from this framework is that the winners in the current cycle will be those that can simultaneously manage supply risk, capture pricing power, and execute geographic diversification. These are not independent challenges. They are interconnected, and success in one area can create advantages in others.

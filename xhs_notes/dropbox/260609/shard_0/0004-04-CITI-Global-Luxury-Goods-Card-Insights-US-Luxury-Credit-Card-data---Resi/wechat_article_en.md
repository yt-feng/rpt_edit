# US Luxury Demand Is Not Recovering — It Is Re-Segmenting, and Only One Income Tier Is Keeping the Market Afloat

The headline number from May's US luxury credit card data appears reassuring: total luxury brand spend rose 3% year-on-year, marking the fifth consecutive month of growth. But that single figure conceals a far more consequential structural shift. For the first time in over a year, two-year stacked spend turned positive, reaching +2% from -3% in April. This is not a broad-based recovery. It is a narrow, income-concentrated reprieve driven entirely by the top of the consumer pyramid. The data from a major credit card panel covering more than 10 million US cardholders reveals a market bifurcating along income lines with increasing clarity. Transaction values — a proxy for average selling prices — remain robust, up mid-teens year-on-year. Transaction volumes, however, continue to decline by roughly 10%. The arithmetic is unambiguous: fewer people are buying, but those who remain are spending significantly more per transaction. This is not demand recovery. This is demand concentration.

The macro context makes this pattern more worrying, not less. Consumer sentiment softened for the third consecutive month in May, with the University of Michigan survey showing sharper declines among lower-income and non-college consumers. The Conference Board index edged lower as weaker current business and labour conditions offset improved income expectations. Core PCE inflation remains sticky above 3%, and headline inflation is being pressured by elevated energy prices tied to the ongoing Middle East conflict and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Real income growth is negative for large segments of the population, and the personal savings rate has eroded to 2.6%. Against this backdrop, the notion that luxury demand is "resilient" is true only if one defines resilience as the spending behaviour of the top quintile of households whose equity and property wealth effects remain intact.

The implications for luxury equity investors are stark. The sector has underperformed the European market by 26 percentage points year-to-date, and valuations have derated roughly 20% in both absolute and relative terms to approximately 20x forward P/E — a 15-20% discount to historical averages. Consensus expectations for FY26 and FY27 sales and EBIT growth have been cut by low single-digits and mid-single-digits respectively. The question is not whether luxury demand is weak. It is whether the current price-to-earnings discount adequately captures the risk that the aspirational consumer — the engine of luxury volume growth for the past decade — may not return at all.

## The Two-Year Stack Turning Positive Is a Statistical Artifact, Not a Demand Inflection

The most closely watched metric in the report is the two-year stacked spend, which turned positive for the first time since early 2024. This is important, but it must be interpreted correctly. The positive reading of +2% in May, up from -3% in April, is driven primarily by the base effect of an exceptionally weak May 2024, when luxury card spend fell 1.7% year-on-year. The two-year stack simply measures the average annual growth rate over the past 24 months. When the year-ago period was deeply negative, the current year does not need to be strong to generate a positive stack. It needs merely to be less bad.

This is not a pedantic technical point. It has direct implications for how investors should assess the trajectory of luxury demand. The sequential improvement in the two-year stack from -3% to +2% is welcome, but it does not indicate accelerating momentum. The underlying year-on-year growth rate actually decelerated from +4% in April to +3% in May, achieved against a roughly 5-percentage-point tougher comparable. In other words, the May result was weaker than April in absolute terms, but it looked better on a two-year basis only because May 2025 was particularly poor.

The more instructive lens is to examine the composition of spend growth. Transaction values rose mid-teens year-on-year. Transacting customer counts fell roughly 10%. This divergence has persisted for over 18 months and shows no sign of narrowing. It tells a consistent story: luxury brands have trained their existing high-value customers to accept higher prices, but they have failed to attract or retain the broader customer base that drove volume growth in the pre-pandemic era. The aspirational buyer — the consumer who purchased entry-level handbags, small leather goods, canvas accessories, and designer sneakers — has been systematically priced out of the market. Steep price increases implemented during the pandemic, which in some cases exceeded 10% annually, have permanently altered the demand curve for these categories.

The strategic risk for luxury houses is that this volume decline is not cyclical but structural. If the aspirational consumer does not return when macro conditions improve — because the price-value equation has shifted irreparably — then the industry's growth model must be fundamentally rethought. Volume expansion was the primary driver of revenue growth for most soft luxury brands in the 2010s. If that engine is permanently impaired, brands must either rely on continued price increases (which face diminishing returns as elasticities rise) or accept lower long-term growth rates. The current valuation discount may be rational, but it may also be insufficient if the market has not yet fully priced in a lower structural growth trajectory.

## Category Divergence Reveals Which Brands Are Winning the Income-Concentration Game

The May data by product category offers a granular view of where demand is holding up and where it is collapsing. The pattern is not random. It maps directly onto the income sensitivity of each category's customer base.

Watch brands and retailers posted the strongest performance, with year-on-year spend accelerating to +7% in May from -4% in April. The two-year stack improved dramatically to +23% from +6%. This is the most income-concentrated category in luxury. Watch buyers tend to be older, wealthier, and less sensitive to macro volatility. The category also benefits from a powerful wealth effect: rising equity markets directly boost demand for high-ticket discretionary items like mechanical watches. The improvement in watches is consistent with the thesis that the top tier of luxury consumers is not merely resilient but actively spending more.

Jewellery also strengthened, with year-on-year growth accelerating to +6% in May from +1% in April, and the two-year stack improving to +16% from +3%. Precious-metal prices have been rising, which supports ASP growth, but the volume story here is similar to watches: jewellery is a high-consideration, high-price-point category that skews toward affluent buyers. The improvement in jewellery spend suggests that the wealth effect is broad enough to lift multiple categories within the hard-luxury segment.

Leather goods, by contrast, slowed to +2% year-on-year in May from +6% in both March and April. The two-year stack was broadly unchanged at -7%. This is the core category for most European luxury conglomerates, and it is the category most exposed to aspirational demand. The deceleration is concerning because leather goods had been showing signs of stabilization in the first quarter. The May data suggests that stabilization may have been temporary. The two-year stack remaining negative at -7% indicates that even after adjusting for base effects, leather goods spend is still well below pre-2024 levels.

Ready-to-wear softened slightly to +2% from +3%, with the two-year stack improving to -2% from -7%. RTW is a mixed category: it includes both high-price-point couture pieces (income-insensitive) and more accessible contemporary lines (income-sensitive). The modest improvement in the two-year stack suggests that the category is finding a floor, but the year-on-year deceleration indicates that momentum is not building.

The pattern across categories is consistent with a market that is being propped up by the top 10-20% of consumers by income. Categories with higher average transaction values and lower exposure to aspirational buyers are outperforming. Categories that rely on volume and accessible price points are underperforming. This is not a temporary divergence that will correct when macro conditions improve. It is a structural re-segmentation of luxury demand along income lines.

## The Open Question the Report Cannot Yet Answer: Is the Aspirational Consumer Lost for Good?

The report provides rich data on what is happening but stops short of answering the most consequential question for long-term investors: will the aspirational luxury consumer return, or has the industry permanently lost that cohort?

The data suggests several reasons for concern. First, the volume decline has persisted for over two years. Transacting customer counts have been down roughly 10% year-on-year for most of that period, with no meaningful improvement even as macro conditions have stabilized. This is not a cyclical dip. It is a sustained contraction in the customer base.

Second, the price increases that drove the volume decline have not been reversed. Most luxury brands implemented low- to mid-single-digit price increases in 2025 and very low single-digit increases in 2026 to date. Hermès remains the outlier with 5-6% increases in January 2026. Hard-luxury brands have raised prices by low- to mid-single-digit percentages to reflect higher precious-metal costs and Swiss franc strength. There is no evidence that any major brand is considering price reductions to win back volume. The industry's pricing strategy remains firmly anchored in the assumption that demand is inelastic and that the aspirational consumer will eventually adjust.

Third, the macro environment is not supportive of a volume recovery. The US personal savings rate has fallen to 2.6%, the lowest level since the Global Financial Crisis. Negative real income growth is squeezing middle-income households. The unemployment rate has gradually increased to the 4.3-4.4% range and is expected to rise further as labour demand remains softer than labour supply. The Conference Board index shows weaker current business and labour conditions. These are not conditions under which aspirational luxury spending typically rebounds.

Fourth, there is a structural shift in consumer behaviour that predates the current macro cycle. The pandemic accelerated a trend toward experiential spending and away from goods consumption. Younger consumers, who are the future aspirational buyer base, are allocating a smaller share of their discretionary budgets to luxury goods and a larger share to travel, dining, and experiences. This shift may be permanent, and it directly undermines the volume growth model that luxury brands have relied on for decades.

The report does not answer whether this is a permanent loss or a temporary withdrawal. The data cannot answer that question because it only captures spending, not intentions. But the pattern is sufficiently persistent and the macro backdrop sufficiently challenging that investors should treat the aspirational consumer's return as a risk, not a base case.

## A Decision Framework for Luxury Equity Investors in a Two-Tier Market

The May credit card data, combined with the broader macro and valuation context, suggests a decision framework for investors that moves beyond simple sector-level positioning. The luxury market is no longer a single asset class. It is two distinct markets operating under the same label, and investment decisions should reflect that bifurcation.

**Step one: assess each brand's exposure to the aspirational consumer.** The report provides a useful ranking of US-exposed companies. Tapestry and Capri are the most exposed, followed by Watches of Switzerland, Birkenstock, and EssilorLuxottica. At the other end, Moncler, Prada Group, Hermes, and Swatch Group are the least exposed. US exposure is a proxy for aspirational exposure because the US market has a larger middle-income luxury consumer base than Europe or Japan. But US exposure is not the only factor. Brands with a high share of entry-price-point products — small leather goods, canvas bags, sneakers, slides — are more exposed to aspirational demand regardless of geography. Brands with a high share of ultra-high-price-point products — high jewellery, complicated watches, couture — are less exposed.

**Step two: assess pricing power sustainability.** The report notes that pricing has become more homogeneous across brands, with most implementing low- to mid-single-digit increases. But there are outliers. Hermes and Richemont's jewellery maisons (Cartier, Van Cleef & Arpels, Buccellati) have multi-year catch-up opportunities. These brands have pricing power that is not fully reflected in current price points. Brands that can continue to raise prices without losing volume will outperform in a two-tier market. Brands that have exhausted their pricing headroom will face margin pressure and volume declines.

**Step three: assess wealth effect exposure.** The primary driver of luxury demand in the current cycle is the wealth effect from equity markets and property gains. Brands with a customer base that is heavily weighted toward equity-owning, property-owning households will benefit disproportionately from this dynamic. Brands with a customer base that is more dependent on wage income and savings will underperform. The data on transaction values versus volumes is a direct proxy for this: brands with strong ASP growth but weak volume growth are benefiting from wealth effects. Brands with weak ASP growth and weak volume growth are not.

**Step four: assess valuation in the context of structural risk.** The sector trades at approximately 20x forward P/E, a 15-20% discount to historical averages. This discount partially reflects the risks outlined above. But it may not fully capture the possibility that volume growth never returns to pre-pandemic levels. If the aspirational consumer is permanently lost, the long-term growth rate for the industry could be 2-3% rather than 5-7%. That would imply a structurally lower valuation multiple. Investors should stress-test their valuation assumptions against a scenario in which volume remains flat for the next five years and all growth comes from pricing and mix.

**Step five: monitor the data that matters.** The two-year stack is useful, but it is backward-looking and base-effect-dependent. The more forward-looking indicators are transaction volumes, savings rates, and consumer sentiment among lower- and middle-income households. If volumes stabilize or improve while ASP growth moderates, that would signal a return of the aspirational consumer. If volumes continue to decline while ASP growth remains strong, the current bifurcation will persist. If volumes decline and ASP growth falters, the industry faces a more serious demand problem than currently priced.

## What the Full Report Reveals That This Analysis Cannot

This article has focused on the strategic implications of the May credit card data, but the full report contains additional layers of analysis that are essential for a complete investment thesis. The report includes detailed monthly data on high-end department stores, online luxury platforms, and off-price resale channels, which together form the Total Luxury Market index. That index showed spend broadly stable at +1% year-on-year in May, with online luxury off-price and resale channels outperforming traditional retail. This suggests that the inventory glut that plagued the industry in 2023-2024 is still working its way through the system, and that discount channels are capturing a disproportionate share of demand.

The report also includes data on sport brands and beauty categories, which serve as a useful control group. Sport brands and retailers remained stable at -4% year-on-year, while beauty improved to -3% from -7%. The fact that even lower-price-point categories are showing negative growth reinforces the thesis that the consumer weakness is broad-based and not limited to luxury.

Most importantly, the full report includes the underlying data tables that allow investors to construct their own two-year stacks, category-level trends, and channel-level analyses. The charts in the original report — which this article cannot reproduce — provide a visual representation of the divergence between transaction values and volumes that is the central analytical insight of the entire dataset. Seeing that divergence plotted over time makes the structural nature of the shift unmistakable.

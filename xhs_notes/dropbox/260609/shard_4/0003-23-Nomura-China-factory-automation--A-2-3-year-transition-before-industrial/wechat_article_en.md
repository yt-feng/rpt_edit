# Industrial AI in China Will Not Scale Until OT Vendors Solve the Installed-Base Trap

The narrative that artificial intelligence will rapidly transform China's factory floors is compelling, but it masks a harder truth: the monetization of industrial AI remains fundamentally anchored to legacy distributed control system (DCS) installed bases, and the inflection point for meaningful revenue scaling is still two to three years away. A global investment bank report published in June 2026, based on an industry survey and analysis of China's leading operational technology vendors, argues that domestic OT incumbents are positioned to outrank IT platforms in AI deployment, but the path is gated by structural transition pains that no vendor has yet fully resolved. The near-term opportunity is not about AI as a standalone growth driver, but about AI as a revenue amplifier that drags along legacy hardware upgrades. For investors, the key question is not which vendor has the best AI model, but which vendor can navigate the installed-base trap—the tension between protecting existing DCS revenue streams and deploying new AI services that require cross-vendor compatibility, state-owned enterprise security clearance, and subscription-based pricing models that Chinese industrial clients have historically resisted.

The report defines industrial AI through four software pillars—TPT (Time-series Pre-trained Transformer), UCS (Universal Control System), AOP (Autonomous Operating Plant), and OMC (Operation Management & Control System)—layered on open-source foundation models. These pillars are not interchangeable; they represent distinct layers of the industrial AI stack that different vendors are trying to own. TPT is purpose-built for the unique characteristics of industrial time-series data, which differs fundamentally from the text and image data that dominate consumer AI. UCS rebrands legacy DCS by integrating telco and cloud compute, reducing cabinet space by approximately 90% and cabling by more than 80%. AOP represents the autonomous operating layer, while OMC handles management and control. The critical insight is that domestic vendors compete less on model capability than on vertical depth. Inovance Technology anchors its position in new energy; SUPCON Technology anchors in petrochemicals and chemicals; Hollysys anchors in rail transit and power. Each has entrenched its position through accumulated process know-how, not horizontal scale. This vertical specialization means that industrial AI deployment will look very different across sectors, and the winners will be those that can translate domain expertise into AI services that solve real operational problems, not those that build the most sophisticated foundation model.

## Inovance's Physical-Fusion Strategy Creates a Different AI Tier from Software-Led Incumbents

The most striking divergence in the report is the contrast between Inovance's approach and that of software-led process incumbents like SUPCON. Inovance monetizes AI not through subscription software but through hardware pull-in. Its AI deployment is physical-fusion: embedding AI and compute into servo drives, motors, and special-purpose programmable logic controllers at new-energy and battery plants. This is not a minor tactical difference; it represents a fundamentally different business model and competitive position. Inovance pairs integrated controller-servo solutions with AI plus real-time force control, feeding into humanoid-robot supply chains. The implication is that Inovance is building a hardware-anchored ecosystem where AI is a feature that enhances the value of physical equipment, rather than a separate software product that must be sold and renewed independently. This hardware DNA places Inovance in a different AI tier from software-led incumbents, and it may prove more resilient in an environment where Chinese industrial clients are skeptical of software subscriptions and wary of vendor lock-in. The question the report raises but does not fully answer is whether Inovance's hardware-fusion approach can scale beyond new energy into other verticals where its process know-how is less established, and whether the margins on hardware-bundled AI are structurally lower than the software margins that SUPCON is pursuing.

## SUPCON's AI Revenue Is Real but Heavily Tied to Bundled DCS Hardware, Not Pure Software

SUPCON Technology's disclosed CNY184 million of industrial-AI revenue in the first quarter of 2026, representing 12% of total revenue, is a notable data point, but the report warns that at least 20% of that figure is bundled DCS hardware. This is not a criticism of SUPCON's strategy; it is a reality of how industrial AI is being sold in China today. The flagship AOP product, paired with TPT and subscription Models-as-a-Service and Results-as-a-Service offerings, is sold into SUPCON's existing petrochemical client base. The bundling is not accidental; it is a deliberate strategy to use AI as a lever to pull through legacy DCS upgrades. The report estimates that industrial-AI software margins exceed 20%, far above the sub-10% margins typical of DCS hardware, but those software margins will not materialize at scale until the installed base is upgraded and subscription models gain traction. The tension is clear: SUPCON needs to sell DCS hardware to maintain its relationship with clients and fund the AI transition, but every hardware sale reinforces the legacy architecture that AI is supposed to replace. The report suggests that SUPCON's 2026E revenue recovery is likely to be supported by delayed revenue recognition, not AI, which implies that the AI contribution to revenue growth is still marginal and that the company is using accounting timing to smooth the transition.

## The 2-3 Year Transition Period Is Gated by Structural Constraints That No Vendor Can Solve Alone

The report identifies three binding constraints that collectively create a 2-3 year transition-pain period before industrial AI scales meaningfully. First, MaaS and RaaS subscription models face domestic-substitution and security resistance from state-owned enterprise clients. SOEs are the dominant customers in process industries like petrochemicals and power, and they are inherently cautious about adopting subscription-based AI services that run on cloud infrastructure, particularly when those services may involve data sovereignty concerns or dependence on foreign technology stacks. The report notes that this resistance has already cost some legacy orders, which is a concrete signal that the transition is not frictionless. Second, cross-vendor incompatibility is acute. The report cites a specific case: a Siemens PCS7 biopharma project stalled for over half a year because its Profinet protocol did not bridge to SUPCON's Modbus, with full replacement being the only fix. This is not an edge case; it is a systemic feature of an installed base built over decades with proprietary protocols and vendor-specific architectures. The cost of switching is high, and the risk of being locked into an incompatible AI stack is a real deterrent for industrial clients. Third, the installed base itself is a constraint. DCS systems have long replacement cycles, and the installed base of legacy systems represents both an opportunity and a liability. Every legacy system that is not upgraded is a potential revenue loss for AI vendors, but upgrading requires convincing clients to make capital expenditures that may not have immediate payback.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Subscription Stickiness Question and the Role of IT Platforms

The report leaves several meaningful open questions that any serious investor or strategist should want to explore further. The most important is the question of subscription stickiness. The report asserts that subscription stickiness, not one-off projects, is the durable inflection point for industrial AI, but it provides limited evidence that Chinese industrial clients are ready to commit to subscription models. The resistance from SOEs is documented, but the report does not fully explore whether the subscription model is structurally suited to the capital budgeting cycles and procurement processes of Chinese industrial enterprises, which are accustomed to paying for hardware and software as capital expenditures, not operating expenses. A second open question is the role of IT platforms. The report argues that domestic OT incumbents will outrank IT platforms in AI landing, but it does not examine the possibility that major cloud providers or enterprise software companies could disrupt the OT vendors by offering AI services that are platform-agnostic and easier to deploy. The report's framing assumes that OT depth is the decisive advantage, but that assumption deserves scrutiny in a world where foundation models are becoming commoditized and the marginal value of vertical-specific fine-tuning may decline over time. A third open question is the geographic scope. The report focuses exclusively on China, but the dynamics of industrial AI deployment may be very different in other markets, and it is not clear whether the lessons from China's state-dominated, vertically fragmented industrial landscape apply globally.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Evaluate OT Vendors' AI Prospects

For readers who want to translate this analysis into actionable decisions, the report implicitly offers a framework built on three questions. First, how deep is the vendor's installed base in a vertical where AI can deliver measurable operational improvements? The answer determines whether the vendor has a captive market for AI services and whether it can cross-subsidize AI development with legacy hardware revenue. SUPCON's petrochemical installed base is deep, but the report suggests that the near-term AI revenue from that base is heavily bundled with hardware, which limits margin expansion. Inovance's new-energy installed base is growing rapidly, but it is less mature and may not yet have the scale to support a standalone AI business. Second, what is the vendor's strategy for overcoming cross-vendor incompatibility? The report's example of the Siemens-SUPCON protocol mismatch is a cautionary tale. Vendors that can offer multi-vendor compatibility or invest in open standards may have a structural advantage, but the report does not identify any vendor that has solved this problem. Third, how does the vendor's business model align with client incentives? Inovance's hardware-fusion model aligns with clients who want to avoid software subscriptions and vendor lock-in, but it may limit the vendor's ability to capture recurring revenue. SUPCON's subscription model aligns with the goal of high-margin recurring revenue, but it faces resistance from clients who are not ready to commit. The optimal strategy may depend on the specific vertical and client segment, and the report suggests that no single model will dominate.

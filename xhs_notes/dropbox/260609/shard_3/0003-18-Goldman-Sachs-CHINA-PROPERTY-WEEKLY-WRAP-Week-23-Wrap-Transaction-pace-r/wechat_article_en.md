# The Housing Provident Fund Reform Is More Than a Welfare Adjustment — It Is a Strategic Recalibration of China’s Housing Demand Engine

China’s property market is sending a mixed signal that many investors are misreading as mere volatility. In Week 23, primary transaction volumes declined 13% week-on-week, and secondary volumes fell 7%. On the surface, this looks like a pause in momentum. But the year-on-year data tells a different story: primary sales rose 15% and secondary sales surged 31% compared to the same period last year. The market is not retreating — it is rotating. Beneath the week-to-week noise, a structural shift is underway, and the most important catalyst is not a stimulus package or a rate cut. It is the long-awaited reform of the Housing Provident Fund (HPF), which the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development (MOHURD) has now put forward for public consultation.

This reform is far broader than initial expectations. It expands permissible withdrawal cases to include home renovations and property management fees, formalizes nationwide eligibility for over 200 million flexible workers, and mandates cross-regional recognition of HPF accounts. These changes are not administrative tweaks. They represent a deliberate expansion of the HPF from a narrow home-purchase subsidy into a broader housing-consumption financing vehicle. For investors, the question is not whether the market will recover, but which segments of the housing ecosystem will benefit first, and which policy levers remain unturned.

The timing of this reform is critical. The market is emerging from a period of extreme valuation compression. Covered developers now trade at an average 28% discount to end-2026E NAV for offshore names and 27% for onshore names, with P/B multiples of 0.5x and 0.4x respectively — levels that are at or below historical troughs seen in 2008, 2011, and 2014. Valuations this depressed typically reflect an assumption that the demand base has permanently shrunk. The HPF reform directly challenges that assumption by unlocking demand from two previously constrained pools: the self-employed and flexible workforce, and existing homeowners seeking renovations. If the reform is implemented effectively, it could re-rate the entire sector by re-anchoring demand expectations.

But the reform is not a silver bullet. The report leaves several critical questions unanswered — most notably, whether further HPF loan rate cuts and proactive lending initiatives, such as deploying unutilized deposits or offering loan interest subsidies, will follow. These are the real swing factors. Without them, the reform expands eligibility but does not necessarily increase purchasing power. The market is watching, and the next policy moves will determine whether this is a genuine demand reset or just a regulatory expansion with limited economic impact.

## The HPF Reform Is Broader Than Expected, But Its Demand Impact Depends on Complementary Credit Policies

The revised HPF regulation targets three areas: expanding withdrawal cases, broadening participation, and enabling cross-regional portability. The inclusion of home renovations and property management fees as permissible withdrawal cases is a meaningful expansion beyond the initial scope. This directly addresses the aging housing stock in major cities, where renovation demand is substantial but has historically been funded through personal savings or informal channels. By channelling HPF funds into this segment, the reform creates a new demand stream for construction materials, renovation services, and home improvement platforms.

The formalization of nationwide eligibility for individual business owners, part-time workers, and flexible employees is equally significant. The report notes that this segment is estimated to exceed 200 million workers by end-2025. Previously, participation was limited to a 36-city pilot. Now, the entire national flexible workforce can potentially access HPF benefits. This is not just a welfare expansion — it is a demand expansion. These workers have been largely excluded from formal housing finance channels. Bringing them into the HPF system gives them a savings vehicle and, eventually, a borrowing capacity that can be deployed into the housing market.

However, the critical missing piece is whether the reform will be accompanied by further HPF loan rate cuts or proactive lending initiatives. The report explicitly flags this as a key area to watch. Without lower borrowing costs, the expansion of eligibility may not translate into incremental demand. The unutilized HPF deposit pool is substantial, and deploying it through interest subsidies or lower lending rates would be the most effective way to stimulate housing consumption. Investors should treat the current reform as a necessary but insufficient condition for a demand recovery. The real catalyst will come if the next policy iteration includes rate reductions or creative deployment of idle funds.

## Transaction Data Shows a Market That Is Stabilizing, Not Weakening — And the Divergence Between Primary and Secondary Tells a Strategic Story

The Week 23 data reveals a pattern that is easy to misinterpret. Primary sales volumes fell 13% week-on-week, and secondary volumes fell 7%. Subscription and visitation data also declined roughly 5% week-on-week. But year-on-year comparisons tell a different story: primary sales were up 15%, and secondary sales were up 31%. Sentiment indicators remained stable, with new home search activity flat week-on-week and price expectations steady for both agents and home-buyers.

This divergence between sequential weakness and year-on-year strength is characteristic of a market that is finding a new equilibrium. The sequential decline reflects normal post-peak seasonality and perhaps some cautiousness ahead of policy announcements. The year-on-year strength, however, suggests that the underlying demand base is not deteriorating. In fact, secondary market volumes have been particularly resilient, with YTD volumes now 1% higher year-on-year and 22% above the 2024 average. This is not a market in freefall. It is a market that has stabilized at a lower base and is now showing signs of organic demand recovery.

The implications for investors are clear. The primary market remains constrained by developer balance sheet issues and inventory overhang, but the secondary market is functioning more normally. This means that the recovery will likely be led by existing home transactions first, with new home sales following only after inventory clears and developer confidence returns. The HPF reform, by enabling renovations and expanding eligibility, directly supports the secondary market by giving homeowners and potential buyers more financial flexibility. The strategic bet is not on a V-shaped recovery in new home sales, but on a gradual normalization of transaction volumes across both markets, with the secondary market acting as the leading indicator.

## Valuations Are at Historical Troughs, But Trough Valuations Alone Do Not Trigger Rerating — Policy Catalysts Must Be Credible

The report provides detailed valuation context. Offshore developers trade at 0.5x 2026E P/B, while onshore developers trade at 0.4x. These multiples are below or at the troughs seen in 2H2008, 2H2011, and 1H2014. The discounts to NAV are also severe — 28% for offshore and 27% for onshore. On a standalone basis, these valuations suggest that the market is pricing in a permanent impairment of earning power and asset values.

But valuation alone is never a sufficient reason to buy. What matters is whether the policy environment is credible enough to reverse the impairment narrative. The HPF reform is a step in that direction, but it is not yet a complete solution. The market is waiting for evidence that the reform will be implemented with sufficient force to actually change demand dynamics. This is why the unanswered questions about further rate cuts and lending initiatives are so important. If the next policy iteration includes aggressive deployment of unutilized deposits or meaningful loan interest subsidies, the valuation discount could compress rapidly. If not, the current valuations may persist or even widen.

The report also notes that stronger SOE developers outperformed in Week 23, with China Resources Land and China Overseas Land & Investment declining only 3% week-on-week compared to 7-8% declines for POE and other SOE developers. This dispersion reflects a flight to quality within the sector. Investors are rewarding developers with stronger balance sheets, lower leverage, and better access to funding. The HPF reform may benefit all developers eventually, but the initial rerating will be concentrated among those with the financial capacity to absorb new demand and the asset quality to attract buyers.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Transmission Mechanism from Policy to Demand Remains Opaque

The report is excellent at documenting what is happening, but it leaves several critical questions open. First, the exact mechanism by which HPF reform will translate into incremental housing demand is not fully specified. Expanding eligibility to 200 million flexible workers is one thing; ensuring that these workers actually have sufficient savings to make meaningful withdrawals or obtain loans is another. The report does not provide data on the average HPF balance of this cohort, nor does it model the potential demand uplift.

Second, the report does not address the supply side. Inventory months remain elevated at 27.8, compared to an average of 28.5 in May 2026. While this is a slight improvement, it still indicates a significant overhang. Even if HPF reform unlocks new demand, it may take several quarters for that demand to absorb existing inventory. The report does not model the timeline for inventory normalization under different policy scenarios.

Third, the report does not fully explore the implications of cross-regional HPF portability. This provision could accelerate migration flows from lower-tier to higher-tier cities, as workers can now bring their HPF balances with them. This would benefit Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities at the expense of Tier 3 and Tier 4 cities. The report does not quantify this potential redistribution effect, which could have significant implications for regional developer exposure and land market dynamics.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the report. They are analytical frontiers that investors need to explore themselves. The report provides the data and the framework; the next step is to build scenarios around these unknowns and stress-test portfolio positions accordingly.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position Across the HPF Reform Cycle

The HPF reform creates a multi-stage investment opportunity. Investors should think of it as a three-phase cycle, each with distinct winners and risks.

Phase one is the policy announcement and implementation phase. During this phase, the market will reprice developer valuations based on expectations of future demand. The winners here are developers with strong balance sheets, high-quality land banks in Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities, and exposure to the renovation and secondary market segments. The risk is that the reform disappoints on implementation or that complementary credit policies do not materialize.

Phase two is the demand transmission phase. This is when the actual impact of expanded HPF eligibility and withdrawal cases begins to show up in transaction data. The winners here are companies with direct exposure to housing consumption — real estate agencies, home improvement platforms, and building materials suppliers. The report notes that BEKE (new and existing) likely saw GTV rise 9% year-on-year in April-May, with existing transactions up 16%. This is an early signal that the demand transmission is already starting. The risk is that the demand uplift is concentrated in the secondary market and does not translate into new home sales.

Phase three is the supply response phase. Once demand stabilizes, developers will begin to replenish land banks and restart new projects. The winners here are developers with access to cheap financing and the ability to execute quickly. The risk is that the supply response overshoots and recreates an inventory problem.

Investors should position for Phase one now, with a focus on quality developers and housing service platforms. Phase two and Phase three will require monitoring transaction data and land sales trends. The report provides the weekly data infrastructure to do this — the key is to use it to identify inflection points rather than react to them.

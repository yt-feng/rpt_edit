EN_A: And L&F? It seems to be taking a completely different route.

EN_B: Exactly. And they already have a long-term supply deal with Samsung SDI for their US energy storage business. So L&F is betting on the US ESS market, which loves low-cost LFP batteries.
EN_A: And what about LG Chem? They are the giant here, so their strategy seems more complex.
EN_B: LG Chem is playing a multi-pronged game. They are seeing a recovery in cathode shipments thanks to the ramp-up of North American battery production. They are also developing their own LFP cathode, but that is coming later, around 2027 or 2028. At the same time, they are restructuring their massive chemical business, consolidating naphtha cracker capacity.
EN_A: Why does the chemical restructuring matter for battery materials?
EN_B: Because LG Chem's chemical division is suffering from oversupply and weak margins. By restructuring, they can cut costs and free up resources to invest in battery materials, which management sees as the key growth driver. The report mentions that the new CEO is very focused on this.
EN_A: I also noticed something about a new technology called precursor-free LFP. What is that?
EN_B: It is a very advanced technique. Normally, making cathode material takes three steps: make a precursor, mix it with lithium, and then calcine it. Precursor-free LFP skips the first two steps and does everything in the calcination process.
EN_A: That sounds incredibly difficult.
EN_B: It is. The challenge is that you have to uniformly mix the metals, add lithium, and form the crystal structure all at once. But if it works, it could significantly lower costs and reduce dependence on imported Chinese precursors. The report says L&F and others are working on it.

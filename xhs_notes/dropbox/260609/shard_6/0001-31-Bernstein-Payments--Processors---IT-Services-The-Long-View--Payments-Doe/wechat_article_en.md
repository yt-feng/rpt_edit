# The Cash-to-Card Runway Is Longer Than Markets Assume, But the Growth Engine Has Fundamentally Changed

For over a decade, the dominant narrative in payments has been a simple one: cash is dying, cards are winning, and the secular tailwind will continue to deliver steady double-digit volume growth for network giants. That story is no longer complete. The 12th annual global payments forecast from a leading institutional research team makes clear that the cash digitization thesis remains intact, but its mechanics, geography, and revenue implications have shifted in ways most investors have not yet fully priced in. The headline number is straightforward: 8% constant-currency card volume growth from 2025 to 2030, down from 9% in the 2022-2025 period and 11% during the pandemic acceleration. But beneath that deceleration lies a more nuanced and strategically important story.

The core argument of this analysis is that the payments industry is transitioning from a volume-driven growth model to a value-driven one. While card volume growth is slowing, revenue growth for the leading networks can remain in the double digits. This is not a contradiction. It is a structural shift driven by three forces: the rising importance of transaction count over transaction value, the expanding penetration of value-added services, and the untapped opportunity in domestic scheme displacement. Investors who fixate on volume deceleration risk missing the more powerful revenue story unfolding beneath the surface.

The timing matters because the market is currently wrestling with a paradox. On one hand, the US is now 72% penetrated by cards, up from 58% a decade ago. On the other hand, cash and checks still represent roughly $11 trillion in global consumer-to-business payments. The runway is real, but it is no longer uniform. It is concentrated in specific regions, specific use cases, and specific payment formats. The question is not whether cash digitization continues, but where, how fast, and who captures the value.

## Card Volume Growth Is Decelerating, But That Is Only Half the Revenue Equation

The global card volume forecast of 8% constant-currency growth over the next five years represents a clear step down from the 9% recorded between 2022 and 2025 and the 11% seen during the 2019-2022 pandemic period. This deceleration is not a surprise to close followers of the sector, but the composition of the slowdown matters more than the aggregate number.

The decline is driven by two primary forces. First, the US market, which accounts for a disproportionate share of global card volumes, is approaching saturation. Card penetration in the US has plateaued at roughly 72%, and the forecast for US card volume growth is now 5-6% on a constant-currency basis, down from 6% in the prior period. Second, Asia presents a more complex picture. While the region remains under-penetrated at 41% card penetration, volume growth has been held back by local market dynamics, including the rise of alternative payment methods and the unique structural situation in mainland China, which the analysis explicitly excludes from its core forecasts.

The temptation is to read this deceleration as a negative signal for the networks. That would be a mistake. The reason is that card volume is only one driver of network revenue. Transaction count, which the analysis forecasts growing at 9% globally between 2025 and 2030, is actually a more important revenue driver for Visa and Mastercard, accounting for more than one-third of their gross revenue. Transaction counts tend to grow faster than dollar volumes because of the ongoing fragmentation of payments into smaller, more frequent transactions, a trend accelerated by contactless payments, e-commerce, and the potential emergence of agentic commerce.

The implication is clear. Even if dollar volume growth slows to 8%, a 9% growth in transaction count, combined with expanding value-added service penetration, can support double-digit revenue growth. This is not theoretical. The analysis notes that even in highly penetrated card markets, the networks have historically demonstrated an ability to grow revenues at double-digit rates. The deceleration in volume is a headwind, but it is one that the networks can more than offset through other levers.

## Europe and Emerging Markets Are the New Frontier for Card Penetration Growth

The conventional wisdom has long held that the US is the most attractive market for card networks, and that emerging markets, while promising, are structurally challenged by low income levels and strong local payment alternatives. The forecast turns this conventional wisdom on its head. Europe, not the US or Asia, emerges as the most compelling growth story for card volumes over the next five years.

The analysis forecasts 11% constant-currency card volume growth for Europe between 2025 and 2030, driven primarily by continental European markets such as Germany, Italy, and Spain. This is only a modest deceleration from the 16% growth seen in the 2022-2025 period. The reason is straightforward: Europe still has significant room for cash digitization. Card penetration in Europe stands at roughly 77%, but this masks enormous variation across countries. Germany still has 32% cash usage at point of sale. Italy is at 24%. Spain is at 34%. These are not fringe markets. They are among the largest economies in the world, and they are only now undergoing the kind of digital payment transformation that the US experienced a decade ago.

The catalyst for this transformation is contactless payments. Global tap-to-pay penetration for Mastercard reached 77% in 2025, up from 45% in 2021. Contactless payments are particularly effective at driving cash-to-card conversion in everyday, high-frequency use cases such as transit, groceries, and vending machines. These are the sticky use cases that build long-term payment habits, and they are precisely the areas where continental Europe has been slow to adopt cards historically.

Latin America and the Middle East and Africa are also forecast to deliver strong growth, with card volume CAGRs of 13% and 14% respectively. These regions remain significantly under-penetrated. Latin America is at 48% card penetration, up from just 29% in 2019. The Middle East and Africa are at 61%, up from 26% in 2019. The pace of digitization in these markets has been remarkable, and the runway remains substantial.

The strategic implication for investors is that the geographic center of gravity for card volume growth is shifting. The US, while still the largest market in absolute terms, is becoming a lower-growth, higher-maturity region. The incremental growth will come from markets where the networks have historically been less dominant, and where success requires navigating local payment preferences, regulatory environments, and competitive dynamics.

## Domestic Scheme Displacement Represents an Underappreciated Revenue Opportunity

One of the most striking findings in the analysis is the scale of the opportunity in domestic scheme displacement. Globally, domestic card schemes process roughly $4 trillion in volumes. These are networks like Girocard in Germany, Cartes Bancaires in France, and a host of other national schemes that have historically been protected by local preferences, regulatory barriers, and merchant relationships.

The analysis highlights that Visa gained 5-6 percentage points of share in continental Europe against Girocard and Cartes Bancaires in 2024 alone. This is not a marginal development. It represents a direct attack on the last bastion of card network competition in developed markets. Domestic schemes have survived largely because they were considered good enough for local consumers and merchants, and because the global networks had limited incentive to invest in displacing them. That calculation has changed.

The reason is that domestic schemes are typically lower-revenue per transaction for the networks, but they represent high-margin incremental volume. The cost of processing a transaction on an existing global network is largely fixed, so displacing a domestic scheme transaction with a Visa or Mastercard transaction generates disproportionately high incremental revenue and profit. The $4 trillion in domestic scheme volume is not merely a volume opportunity. It is a high-quality revenue opportunity.

The question is whether this displacement trend is sustainable and scalable. Domestic schemes in countries like South Korea, where they represent 97% of network volume, will be far harder to displace than those in Germany or France. The regulatory environment also matters. European regulators have historically been sympathetic to domestic schemes as a way to maintain competition and reduce dependency on US-based networks. Any acceleration in domestic scheme displacement will likely attract regulatory scrutiny, which could slow the trend or impose conditions that reduce its profitability.

## The Unanswered Question: How Will Agentic Commerce and Stablecoins Reshape the Growth Trajectory?

Every long-term forecast in payments faces the challenge of predicting the impact of technologies that are still in their infancy. The analysis is admirably transparent about this limitation. It explicitly states that its forecasts do not include meaningful tailwinds from agentic commerce or stablecoins, and that it is simply too early to bake these factors into the numbers.

This is the right analytical approach, but it leaves investors with an important open question. Agentic commerce, where autonomous software agents conduct transactions on behalf of users, has the potential to dramatically increase the number of payment transactions, particularly in categories that have historically been tough to address, such as rent and healthcare. The analysis notes that it is monitoring this development closely, and that the commercial models for cards in these categories are still being developed.

Stablecoins present an even more complex question. The analysis takes a skeptical view, describing stablecoins as a solution looking for a problem in retail consumer payments. This is a defensible position, given the current lack of widespread consumer adoption and the regulatory uncertainty surrounding digital currencies. But the analysis also acknowledges that stablecoins are finding utility in markets where dollars are desirable, suggesting that their role may be more significant in cross-border and institutional use cases than in everyday consumer payments.

The strategic implication is that the forecast of 8% card volume growth may prove conservative if agentic commerce takes off, or may prove optimistic if stablecoins or other alternative payment methods disrupt card networks in unexpected ways. Investors should view the forecast not as a precise prediction, but as a baseline scenario that embeds conservative assumptions about technological disruption. The real value of the analysis lies not in the exact growth number, but in the framework it provides for thinking about the drivers of that growth.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguishing Between Volume Growth, Revenue Growth, and Value Creation

The most important contribution of this analysis is that it forces investors to distinguish between three distinct concepts that are often conflated in payments investing: volume growth, revenue growth, and value creation.

Volume growth is the headline number that gets the most attention. It is important, but it is increasingly misleading as a standalone metric. The analysis shows that volume growth is decelerating, but that this deceleration is concentrated in lower-value, higher-maturity markets. The volume that is growing is increasingly coming from higher-value segments: cross-border transactions, e-commerce, and domestic scheme displacement.

Revenue growth is a more meaningful metric, and the analysis makes a compelling case that it can remain in the double digits even as volume growth slows. The drivers are transaction count growth, value-added service penetration, and the expansion into new payment flows. For investors, the key question is whether the networks can sustain the pace of value-added service innovation and adoption that has driven revenue growth in recent years.

Value creation is the ultimate test. It depends not only on revenue growth, but on the cost of achieving that growth and the competitive dynamics of the industry. The analysis rates Visa, Mastercard, Adyen, and several other payment companies as outperform, suggesting that the current market pricing does not fully reflect the revenue resilience embedded in the forecast. But the analysis also identifies risks, including regulatory pressure and the potential for alternative payment methods to disrupt debit card volumes in specific markets.

The decision framework for investors should be built around three questions. First, is the volume deceleration structural or cyclical? The analysis suggests it is structural, driven by market maturation in the US and Asia, but that this is manageable because the networks have multiple levers to sustain revenue growth. Second, can the networks maintain their competitive position against domestic schemes and alternative payment methods? The evidence from Europe is encouraging, but the situation in Asia and Latin America is more complex. Third, how should investors weigh the potential upside from agentic commerce and stablecoins against the downside risks from regulation and competition? The analysis does not provide a definitive answer, but it provides the framework for making that judgment.

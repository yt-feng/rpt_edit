# The LCD Panel Price Cycle Has Peaked: Why the Next Six Months Favor Discipline Over Ambition

The window for aggressive positioning in TFT-LCD panel stocks is closing. After a period of price recovery driven by disciplined supply management and pre-emptive inventory building, the fundamental tailwinds are now shifting direction. TV panel prices are expected to begin a gradual decline starting in the third quarter of 2026, with IT panels following in the fourth quarter. This is not a crash scenario, but it is a clear signal that the cyclical upswing has run its course.

The implications for investors are more nuanced than a simple sell call. The structural changes in the display industry, including supply consolidation and a shift toward production-to-order models, mean the downside may be shallower than in previous cycles. Yet valuations for many panel makers, particularly in Taiwan, have already priced in the favorable dynamics. The risk-reward calculus has tilted decisively toward caution.

This analysis examines the specific price outlook, the structural supply-side changes that matter, the emerging glass-substrate opportunity that remains a long-term story, and the critical gap in the current research that investors must fill for themselves. It then translates these insights into a decision framework for navigating the next two to three quarters.

## The Price Trajectory Is Clear: TV Panels Roll Over in Q3, IT Panels Follow in Q4

The momentum that drove TV panel prices up by 1 to 2 percent month-on-month in the first quarter of 2026 has dissipated. April and May were flat, and June is expected to be flat across all mainstream sizes from 32-inch to 75-inch. The cause is straightforward: the earlier restocking cycle, which began at the start of the year and was amplified by promotional events such as the Winter Olympics and the FIFA World Cup, is tapering off. Brands have largely completed their inventory builds, and order momentum is softening.

The supply side, however, is not the problem. Panel makers have demonstrated remarkable discipline in controlling output, with fab utilization expected to remain in the 80 to 85 percent range in the second quarter of 2026. This discipline is the key reason the expected decline is moderate rather than severe. The base case is for TV panel prices to trend down from the third quarter, but the decline should not be significant. Supply-side discipline provides a floor.

The pattern for IT panels is slightly different but ultimately in the same direction. Monitor panel prices edged up 0.2 percent month-on-month in June, while notebook panels were flat. The relative stability of IT panels compared to TV panels reflects a different demand profile, but the overall trajectory is the same. The research expects IT panel prices to remain relatively stable in the near term before rolling over in the fourth quarter. The key question for investors is whether this two-quarter lag between TV and IT panel declines creates a tactical opportunity or simply a delayed risk.

The pattern is clear: the cyclical peak is behind us. The question is no longer whether prices will decline, but how far and how fast.

## Supply-Side Discipline Is Real, But It Does Not Prevent a Downcycle

The most important structural change in the display industry over the past three years has been the shift in behavior among Chinese panel makers. They have strategically moved from pursuing top-line growth at all costs to prioritizing profitability. Government subsidies, once a powerful force driving overcapacity, have become less available. This has reinforced supply discipline in a way that was absent in previous cycles.

The evidence is compelling. The industry has shifted to a production-to-order model that has shortened price cycles from multi-year swings to quarterly fluctuations. Consolidation has accelerated, with LG Display selling its Gen 8.5 Guangzhou fab to TCL and Sharp shutting down its Gen 10 Sakai fab. These moves reduce total available capacity and remove some of the most aggressive price competitors from the market.

This structural change matters for the magnitude of the coming downcycle. In previous eras, a peak in panel prices would be followed by a brutal correction as overcapacity drove prices below cash costs. The current environment is different. With supply discipline intact, the decline should be shallower and shorter. The research explicitly states that on a quarterly average basis, TV panel prices are expected to increase by low single-digit percentages quarter-on-quarter in the second quarter, before the decline begins.

However, it would be a mistake to conflate a shallower decline with no decline. The discipline that prevents a crash also prevents prices from rising further. The fundamental dynamic of weakening demand relative to supply is now in place. The question is not whether the floor will hold, but whether current stock prices already reflect a floor that is higher than reality. For Taiwanese names trading at 1.4 to 1.9 times price-to-book, well above mid-cycle averages of 0.8 times since 2022, the answer appears to be no.

## The Glass Substrate Opportunity Is Real, But Not for 2026 or 2027

One of the most exciting long-term narratives in the display space is the potential for glass substrates in advanced semiconductor packaging. The logic is compelling. Glass offers larger dimensions than traditional silicon interposers, which could improve cost efficiency. It provides better electrical performance with lower signal loss. It reduces the mismatch in coefficients of thermal expansion among different materials, mitigating warpage. And it offers greater mechanical strength to resist distortion during manufacturing and operation.

The research has been a believer in this technology and has published deep-dive analysis on the topic. The potential is genuine. If glass substrates become a standard component in advanced packaging architectures, the addressable market for panel makers expands far beyond displays.

Yet the current report makes a crucial and sobering point: the supply chain is still optimizing processes. This remains less of a story for 2026 and 2027. Meaningful contribution from emerging businesses will take time. Moreover, panel makers are not the only players seeking to penetrate this market. They face competition from established semiconductor materials companies, glass specialists, and other entrants.

For investors, this creates a dilemma. The glass narrative provides a reason to assign a higher valuation to panel stocks, particularly those with exposure to advanced packaging. But the timing is uncertain, and the competitive dynamics are unresolved. The research views the investment case for Taiwanese names on this angle as less compelling, given that valuations already exceed peak levels of the past few years. The glass story is a real option, but options have time value, and time is not on the side of investors paying peak multiples today.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Demand Elasticity Question

The report provides a clear supply-side analysis and a well-reasoned price trajectory. But it leaves one critical question insufficiently addressed: what happens if global TV demand surprises to the downside?

The analysis notes that TV panel shipments could be below seasonal levels in the second half of 2026, given the strong first half. The logic is that brands have already pulled forward demand through early restocking. But the report does not fully explore the scenario in which end-consumer demand is weaker than expected. The major promotional events cited, including the Winter Olympics and the FIFA World Cup, are assumed to shift shipment timing rather than boost global demand. The report explicitly states there is no clear evidence these events will significantly increase global TV demand.

This is a reasonable assessment, but it leaves open a more bearish possibility. If consumer spending softens in key markets, the inventory that brands have built could take longer to clear. The discipline on the supply side would still provide a floor, but that floor could be lower than current expectations. The research assumes the decline will not be significant, but it does not quantify what "not significant" means in a downside scenario.

Investors need to form their own view on end-market demand. The report provides the framework for supply and pricing, but the demand variable remains the most uncertain input. For those who believe the global consumer is resilient, the shallow decline thesis holds. For those who see risks to spending, particularly in discretionary categories like televisions, the downside could be more material than the base case suggests.

## A Decision Framework for the Next Two Quarters

The report's analysis translates into a clear decision framework for investors evaluating panel stocks over the next six months. The framework has three dimensions: time horizon, valuation context, and competitive positioning.

On time horizon, the key distinction is between the next one to two months and the next two to three quarters. In the very near term, prices are flat, and there is no immediate catalyst for a sharp move. The risk is not imminent, but it is building. For investors with a three-to-six-month horizon, the direction of travel is clear: prices will be lower, and valuations will come under pressure. The tactical question is whether to exit now or wait for a bounce that may not come.

On valuation context, the report makes a stark distinction between Taiwanese and Chinese names. Taiwanese panel makers AUO and Innolux trade at 1.4 and 1.9 times price-to-book respectively, well above their mid-cycle averages since 2022. The research views these valuations as having priced in all favorable dynamics. In contrast, Chinese leader BOE trades at 1.7 times book, which is above its mid-cycle average of 1.2 times, but the research believes the premium is justified by BOE's scale advantage, more diversified revenue mix, and relatively cheaper valuation compared to its Chinese peer TCL.

On competitive positioning, the preference for BOE over TCL is driven by two factors: BOE's scale advantage and its higher concentration in the display business. For investors who want exposure to the display cycle but are concerned about the downside, BOE offers a relative safe haven. For those who want to avoid the cycle entirely, the report implies that Taiwanese names are the most vulnerable.

The framework suggests three distinct strategies. First, for investors who are already positioned in Taiwanese panel stocks, the case for reducing exposure is strong. The risk-reward is less attractive, and the glass narrative does not justify current multiples. Second, for investors considering new positions, the window for entry has likely passed. Waiting for the decline to materialize and valuations to reset is the more prudent approach. Third, for investors who must maintain exposure to the display sector, rotating toward BOE offers the best relative risk-reward.

## The Unresolved Analytical Questions That Demand Further Research

No single report can answer every question, and this analysis leaves several meaningful gaps that investors should explore further. The first is the specific trajectory of IT panel prices. The report expects them to remain stable relative to TV panels in the coming quarters, but the timing of the rollover in the fourth quarter is based on a general assumption rather than specific demand data. What would cause IT panel demand to weaken earlier or later than expected?

The second gap is the competitive dynamics in the glass substrate market. The report notes that panel makers are not the only players seeking to penetrate this market, but it does not identify who the competitors are or what their relative advantages might be. For investors trying to value the glass option, understanding the competitive landscape is essential.

The third gap is the impact of trade policy and tariffs. The report does not address how potential changes in trade flows, particularly between China and major consuming regions, could affect panel pricing and shipment volumes. This is a meaningful omission, given the geopolitical uncertainties that persist.

These are not criticisms of the report, which is focused and disciplined in its scope. Rather, they are invitations for investors to do their own work. The best research raises as many questions as it answers.

EN_A: A recent report from a global investment bank on the auto industry has a pretty striking title: “Preparing for the World to Change.” What’s that really about?

EN_A: So the big question the report is trying to answer is not “should we worry,” but “how long can this resilience last?”

EN_B: Exactly. The report finds that the financial hit in early 2026 is largely mitigated by hedging and pass-through strategies. But the real pressure starts to build in 2027 as those hedges roll off.

EN_A: What kind of pressure? Energy and logistics?

EN_B: Yes. The conflict’s first impact is on energy and freight costs. Companies have decent hedging for now, but by 2027, those costs will hit the income statement directly.

EN_A: So what are companies doing? Just waiting?

EN_B: Not at all. One of the most interesting insights is that many auto suppliers are actively looking for non-auto growth. They’re leveraging their existing technology to enter data centers, humanoid robots, and even defense systems.

EN_A: That’s a big shift. What does it mean?

EN_B: It means the traditional auto supply chain is trying to decouple from the car cycle. But the report is cautious: this business is still too small to have a meaningful impact. Real contributions are probably 2 to 5 years away.

EN_A: What about China? The report mentions strong exports.

EN_B: Yes. Chinese OEMs are aggressively expanding globally, especially in ASEAN, South America, and Europe. Suppliers that align with Chinese exporters get a revenue boost. But it forces legacy automakers to either localize their supply chains or rethink pricing.

EN_B: The report says EVs have entered a “normalization” phase. Consumer demand is shifting to hybrids and ICE. Suppliers are carefully managing their EV investments and even asking OEMs to share costs on new projects.

EN_B: Right. And here’s a key悬念: despite the optimism from executives, IHS just cut its production forecasts for 2026 through 2028. That directly contradicts the “resilient” narrative from the companies.

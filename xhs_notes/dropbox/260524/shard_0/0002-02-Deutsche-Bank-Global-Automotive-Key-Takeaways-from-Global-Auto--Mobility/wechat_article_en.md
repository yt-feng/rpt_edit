# The Auto Industry's Resilience Is Real but Borrowed: Why the Next 18 Months Demand a New Strategic Playbook

The global automotive industry is sending a contradictory signal that investors and executives must interpret correctly. On one hand, European and US auto markets remain surprisingly resilient through mid-2026, with OEMs describing order momentum as good across powertrains and suppliers reporting improved call-off stability year-over-year. On the other hand, IHS has just slashed its production forecasts by 500,000 units for 2026, 1.2 million for 2027, and 800,000 for 2028, explicitly baking in higher-for-longer oil prices stemming from the Middle East conflict. This tension between present strength and deteriorating forward expectations is the defining strategic challenge of the current cycle.

The critical insight is that the industry's current stability is largely a function of hedging, pass-through mechanisms, and timing lags that are already scheduled to roll off. Energy and raw material hedges that protected 2026 earnings will begin to expire heading into 2027. Logistics and freight cost inflation is only now starting to appear in supplier P&Ls. Raw material cost pass-throughs to OEMs operate on a three-to-six-month lag, meaning the true cost impact of the current geopolitical environment has not yet fully manifested. The second half of 2026 and 2027 will reveal whether the industry's resilience was structural or merely a function of financial engineering and delayed recognition.

What makes this moment particularly consequential is that the industry is simultaneously navigating a multi-front transformation: the normalization of EV expectations, the aggressive global expansion of Chinese OEMs, the emergence of non-automotive growth opportunities for suppliers, and ongoing restructuring and capacity rationalization. Each of these trends would be strategically significant in isolation. Together, they create a decision environment where the margin for error is exceptionally thin and the penalty for misreading the cycle is severe.

## Near-Term Volume Resilience Masks a Production Outlook That Has Already Deteriorated for 2027 and Beyond

The disconnect between what companies are experiencing today and what the production forecasts are projecting for tomorrow is the most important tension in the current data. Suppliers and OEMs across both Europe and North America describe current conditions as surprisingly resilient. Order books are solid. Call-off stability has improved sequentially. There is no evidence yet that higher oil prices are translating into softer consumer demand. Near-term visibility stands at roughly ten to twelve weeks, and the second quarter of 2026 is shaping up to be solid.

Yet IHS has already moved. Its May revision cut production forecasts not just for 2026 but more aggressively for 2027 and 2028, embedding the assumption that the current geopolitical and inflationary environment will persist and deepen. This is not a forecasting error on IHS's part; it is a structural judgment that the industry's own hedging and pass-through protections are temporary. The companies themselves acknowledge this implicitly. Every supplier and OEM in the report states that they are preparing for adverse impacts later in the year or in 2027. No one is arguing that the current resilience is permanent.

The implication for decision-makers is straightforward: the second quarter of 2026 is likely to be the high-water mark for near-term confidence. Companies that treat the current environment as a new normal rather than a temporary shelter will find themselves strategically exposed when the hedges roll off and the cost inflation that has been building in lower-tier supply chains finally reaches their P&Ls. The strategic imperative is to use this window of stability not for complacency but for repositioning.

## Pricing Power Is Limited Across All Major Markets, Forcing OEMs to Rely on Mix and Model Year Changeovers Rather Than Direct Price Increases

The pricing environment reveals a fundamental constraint on the industry's ability to defend margins through the coming cost headwinds. In the United States, it is difficult to raise prices to fully offset tariffs. Europe remains challenging from a pricing standpoint. China is showing signs of stabilization, but stabilization is not recovery. The implication is that the traditional lever of passing through cost increases to consumers is largely unavailable in the current environment.

OEMs have responded by focusing on what they can control: model year changeovers and mix strength. These serve as natural boosts to average selling prices without requiring explicit price increases that would risk demand destruction. Incentives and inventory remain carefully managed. Profitability is increasingly supported by contribution from software and services, which carry higher margins and are less sensitive to commodity and tariff dynamics.

The strategic question this raises is whether mix improvement and software revenue can offset the scale of cost headwinds that are building. The answer depends on the magnitude of those headwinds, which remains uncertain. What is clear is that the industry cannot rely on pricing as a primary margin defense mechanism. This places a premium on cost structure flexibility and on the ability to reconfigure product portfolios toward higher-value segments without alienating the volume-oriented customer base that remains essential for capacity utilization.

## The Middle East Conflict Is a Cost Shock That Has Been Deferred, Not Avoided, and the 2027 Exposure Is Materially Higher Than 2026

The most underappreciated risk in the current environment is the timing structure of the Middle East conflict's financial impact. For most companies, the direct exposure of operating businesses to the region itself remains small and negligible in a group context. The cost impact comes through inflation in raw materials, logistics, freight, and energy, and that impact is only now starting to show.

The critical detail is the hedging profile. Companies entered 2026 with decent hedging levels on energy and raw materials, which has kept the headwinds comparably small for this year. But those hedges roll off heading into 2027, at which point the exposure to spot prices and unhedged procurement becomes materially larger. Logistics and freight cost inflation is already appearing, and lower tiers of the supply chain are beginning to pass through cost increases. Suppliers are largely passing higher raw material costs to OEMs, but with a three-to-six-month lag, meaning the cost recognition cycle has not yet peaked.

For a supplier like Autoliv, raw material cost increases of approximately $90 million are expected, mostly in the second half of 2026, with only 50% recovery already secured. The remaining recovery is subject to negotiation. For Aumovio, the inflation hit from freight and logistics alone could be in the double-digit millions of euros, fully considered in guidance for now but representing real cash exposure. For Aptiv, commodities impact of 110 basis points in the first quarter is expected to subside in the second half, but that expectation assumes no further escalation.

The strategic implication is that companies need to stress-test their 2027 plans under a scenario where hedging protection is absent and where logistics and lower-tier supply chain inflation continues to compound. The window to negotiate pass-through agreements with OEMs, to secure volume commitments, and to implement efficiency measures is now, not when the cost impact is already hitting the income statement.

## Non-Automotive Growth Is the Most Promising Long-Term Narrative, but It Remains Too Small to Offset Near-Term Headwinds in the Current Cycle

The most structurally interesting development in the supplier landscape is the increasingly explicit ambition to grow non-automotive revenue by leveraging existing intellectual property and manufacturing capabilities into adjacent end markets. The range of opportunities being pursued is broad: humanoid components, data center infrastructure, defense systems, energy storage, and aerospace. For an industry that has been structurally challenged on growth for years, this represents a genuine strategic pivot.

The numbers, however, tell a sobering near-term story. Aptiv's non-automotive revenue today is approximately $3 billion, which the company itself describes as not flowing through in a meaningful way compared to its light vehicle business. BorgWarner's data center opportunity through its Endeavor turbine generator business is targeting $300 million in revenue by 2027, but capacity decisions beyond 2 GWh have not yet been made, and product certification is still in process. For most suppliers, the meaningful contribution from non-automotive revenue is two to five years down the road.

This creates a strategic tension. The non-automotive pivot is essential for the long-term narrative and for breaking the industry's correlation with light vehicle production cycles. But it does not solve the margin challenge of 2026 and 2027. Companies must simultaneously invest in these new growth platforms while continuing to restructure and rationalize their core automotive businesses. The risk is that non-automotive investments become a distraction or a cash drain if the core business comes under more pressure than anticipated.

The unanswered question is which end markets will prove most scalable and which suppliers have the technological and manufacturing DNA to execute effectively. Data center infrastructure requires different engineering capabilities and customer relationships than automotive. Defense has different procurement cycles and qualification requirements. Humanoid robotics is still a nascent market with uncertain volume trajectories. The suppliers that succeed will be those that choose selectively rather than trying to pursue all opportunities simultaneously.

## The China Export Dynamic Is Reshaping Global Competition, but the Speed and Magnitude of Localization Requirements Remain Uncertain

Chinese OEMs are aggressively expanding their global footprint, capturing market share in regions including ASEAN, South America, and Europe. For suppliers, aligning with Chinese exporters acts as a significant revenue tailwind. Autoliv estimates that 44% of its China revenue comes from local OEMs, and it projects that Chinese OEMs will reach 60% domestic market share. The company has already won its first Chinese OEM request for quotation in Europe.

The export patterns out of China so far this year have been very strong, and conversations suggest this may be accelerated by OEMs shipping ahead of anticipated Brazilian tariffs. But the strategic question is whether this export momentum is sustainable or whether it will trigger defensive localization requirements and trade barriers that fundamentally alter the competitive landscape.

For legacy automakers, the China export dynamic forces a difficult choice: heavily localize supply chains in markets where Chinese OEMs are gaining share, or compete on price and accept margin compression. For suppliers, the opportunity to serve Chinese OEMs globally is real, but it comes with risks around pricing pressure, intellectual property protection, and the potential for over-dependence on a single customer group.

The report does not fully answer how quickly Chinese OEMs will move from export to local production in key markets, nor does it resolve the question of whether European and US policymakers will respond with tariffs or other barriers that could disrupt the current trajectory. These are the open analytical questions that will determine whether the China export dynamic is a multi-year growth tailwind or a source of volatility and competitive disruption.

## A Decision Framework for the Next 18 Months: Three Strategic Questions Every Auto Executive and Investor Must Answer

The volume of information in this report can obscure the core strategic challenge. To cut through the complexity, decision-makers should organize their thinking around three questions that capture the most important uncertainties and the most consequential trade-offs.

First, how much of your 2027 cost exposure is currently unhedged and unpassed? The hedging roll-off is the single most predictable cost shock in the system. Companies that have already secured volume commitments and pass-through agreements for 2027 are in a fundamentally different position than those that are still negotiating. The time to lock in these protections is now, while OEMs still have visibility into their own production plans and while the current resilience creates negotiating leverage.

Second, what is your capacity to fund non-automotive investment without compromising the core business? The non-automotive pivot is strategically necessary but financially demanding. Companies that over-invest in new platforms while their core automotive margins are under pressure risk creating a funding gap that forces distressed asset sales or equity issuance. The discipline of staging investments, setting clear milestones for capital commitment, and maintaining the flexibility to pause or redirect resources is essential.

Third, how exposed are you to the China export dynamic on both the upside and the downside? The upside is revenue growth from serving Chinese OEMs globally. The downside is competitive pressure from Chinese OEMs in your home markets and the potential for trade disruption that affects your own export-dependent operations. Companies need a dual strategy: capturing the export opportunity while building the operational flexibility to respond to competitive and regulatory shocks.

These three questions do not have easy answers, and the correct response will vary by company depending on its product portfolio, geographic exposure, customer concentration, and balance sheet strength. But they provide a framework for thinking systematically about the next 18 months rather than reacting to events as they unfold.

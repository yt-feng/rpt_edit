# The Three Disconnects Reshaping Global Markets: Rates, AI Economics, and the Oil Capex Inflection

The most dangerous assumption in capital markets today is that the patterns of the last decade remain intact. They do not. A global investment bank report synthesizing research across asset classes, sectors, and geographies reveals three structural disconnects that demand a fundamental rethinking of portfolio construction. First, the correlation between equities and bond yields has turned decisively negative, leaving portfolios exposed to a speed bump if oil disruption persists. Second, the economics of artificial intelligence spending face a reckoning, as most enterprises currently lose money on AI implementation while the infrastructure buildout accelerates. Third, oil exploration capex is inflecting sharply, with seismic spending as the leading indicator of a pricing upcycle that few have fully priced. These three forces are not independent. They interact. And the strategic question for serious investors is not whether to react, but how to position before the market reprices them.

The risk of relying on historical correlations has rarely been higher. For much of the post-financial crisis era, equities and bonds moved together, providing limited diversification. That relationship has broken. As yields on the 30-year U.S. Treasury exceed 5% and the equity market makes new highs, the correlation has turned negative to an extent not seen since the 1990s. This means that rising bond yields no longer signal economic strength that supports equities. Instead, they reflect a tightening financial condition that now weighs directly on equity valuations. The key insight is that this is not a temporary technical phenomenon. It is a structural shift driven by the interaction of AI-related capital spending, fiscal expansion, and energy price dynamics. Investors who treat the current negative correlation as a short-term anomaly risk being caught flat-footed when the next leg of rate volatility hits.

The second disconnect is perhaps more profound. The market has priced AI as a transformative force without adequately distinguishing between the investment required and the returns generated. The report highlights a critical tension: Europe urgently needs to invest in data centers for security of supply, and the power grid remains the key bottleneck. This is supportive for electrification-exposed names and behind-the-meter solutions. Yet at the same time, the economics of AI for most enterprises remain unsustainable. Most companies lose money implementing AI today. The pace of token growth and whether we see indiscriminate use or a more disciplined approach remains an open debate. This creates a strategic paradox. The infrastructure buildout is real and necessary, but the demand that justifies it may not materialize as expected. The winners will be those who can distinguish between the inevitable spending on enabling infrastructure and the uncertain returns from AI applications themselves.

The third disconnect is the most cyclical but potentially the most tradable. Oil exploration-related mentions on earnings calls have already inflected sharply. The first place spending comes through is seismic. After a decade of underinvestment, active seismic vessels have declined by approximately 70%, from around 60 vessels in 2013 to between 17 and 30 today. There have been no newbuilds since 2017, and the fleet is aging. This sets the stage for a structural pricing upcycle in seismic services, particularly for companies that have consolidated capacity. The report identifies one such name as a Buy, noting that sales are expected to pick up in the second half. The broader implication is that the oil capex cycle is turning, and the companies that provide the picks and shovels for exploration will benefit before the producers themselves see the returns.

## The Negative Equity-Bond Correlation Is Not a Trading Opportunity; It Is a Risk Management Signal

The shift in the equity-bond correlation from positive to negative represents a regime change that portfolio construction must reflect. For years, a balanced portfolio of 60% equities and 40% bonds provided reliable diversification because both assets tended to rise together during periods of economic growth and fall together during crises. That pattern has inverted. When bond yields rise, equities now fall. This means that the traditional hedge of holding bonds against equity risk no longer works in the way it once did. The report notes that the two-month correlation between U.S. equities and the 10-year Treasury has turned the most negative since the 1990s. This is not a cyclical blip. It reflects the fact that the primary driver of both markets has shifted from growth expectations to inflation and rate expectations.

The strategic implication is that investors need to think about hedging differently. The traditional bond hedge is losing its effectiveness. The report highlights that defensive sectors are no longer behaving defensively, and that a big part of this is AI and its associated spend. This suggests that sector positioning must be more dynamic and that traditional factor exposures may not provide the protection they once did. The key question is whether this regime persists. If it does, the risk of a speed bump increases materially, particularly if oil disruption continues into the second half. The report does not predict a crash, but it flags that the combination of negative correlation and elevated oil prices creates a vulnerability that disciplined investors should acknowledge, not ignore.

## AI Infrastructure Spending Is Certain; AI Application Returns Are Not

The most important analytical distinction in the AI theme is between the infrastructure buildout and the application economics. The report makes clear that investment in data centers, power, and grid infrastructure is necessary and accelerating. Europe's need for security of supply in data centers is a genuine policy priority, and the bottlenecks in power and grid create clear beneficiaries. Utilities exposed to electrification, companies providing behind-the-meter solutions, and semiconductor firms enabling data center capacity all have identifiable demand drivers. The report identifies specific names in each of these categories, and the logic is straightforward: when governments and corporations must build, the suppliers of the building blocks benefit.

The problem is on the demand side. The report revisits a critical piece of analysis questioning the sustainability of most enterprises losing money implementing AI. This is not a fringe view. It is a central analytical challenge to the prevailing narrative. If the majority of AI applications do not generate positive returns, then the infrastructure buildout is essentially speculative. The market is betting that the applications will eventually justify the spending, but that bet is far from certain. The report flags that the pace of token growth and whether we see indiscriminate use or a more disciplined approach is still being debated. This is not an academic question. It determines whether the current level of AI capital spending is rational or excessive. The strategic answer is to own the infrastructure providers where demand is visible and to avoid the application layer where returns are unproven.

## The Oil Capex Cycle Has Turned, and Seismic Is the Leading Indicator

After years of underinvestment, the oil exploration cycle is inflecting. The report provides a clear signal: exploration-related mentions on earnings calls have already started to rise sharply. The first place this spending shows up is seismic, which is the earliest stage of the exploration value chain. The structural setup is compelling. Active seismic vessels have declined by approximately 70% over the past decade. The remaining fleet is aging, and there have been no newbuilds since 2017. This combination of declining supply and rising demand creates a pricing upcycle. The report identifies a seismic company that has consolidated capacity through acquisition and is now uniquely positioned to benefit.

The broader implication is that the oil services sector is entering a period of pricing power that has been absent for years. The report does not argue that oil prices themselves will rise dramatically, but rather that the service companies providing exploration and production support will see margin expansion as utilization increases. This is a classic late-cycle trade, but it is one that has been delayed by a decade of underinvestment. The key risk is that the cycle is shorter than expected, but the structural constraints on vessel supply suggest that pricing power could persist longer than typical cycles. For investors willing to look beyond the upstream producers, the service providers offer a more direct exposure to the capex cycle without the commodity price risk.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between AI Infrastructure and Energy Demand

The report provides detailed analysis of AI infrastructure needs and oil capex dynamics, but it does not fully explore the interaction between these two themes. The data center buildout is a significant driver of electricity demand, and that demand will need to be met by a grid that is already under pressure. The report notes that power and grid remain key bottlenecks for data center investment, but it does not quantify the incremental energy demand from AI or the implications for oil and gas demand. If the AI infrastructure buildout accelerates, it could create a structural increase in electricity demand that competes with other uses and puts upward pressure on natural gas prices. This would be positive for energy producers but negative for the broader economy, as higher energy costs feed into inflation.

The report also does not resolve the tension between the need for discipline in AI spending and the urgency of the infrastructure buildout. If enterprises continue to lose money on AI applications, the pace of data center construction may slow, reducing the demand for power and grid investment. Conversely, if AI applications become profitable, the infrastructure buildout will accelerate, creating even more demand for energy. The outcome of this feedback loop is uncertain, and the report does not provide a definitive view. This is not a weakness; it is an honest acknowledgment of the complexity. The implication for investors is that they must monitor both sides of the equation and be prepared to adjust positions as the evidence evolves.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Three Disconnects

The most useful way to synthesize the report's insights is to construct a simple decision framework that maps the key uncertainties to portfolio actions. The first dimension is the trajectory of bond yields. If yields continue to rise, the negative correlation with equities will persist, and traditional balanced portfolios will underperform. The response is to reduce duration exposure, increase cash allocations, and favor sectors with pricing power that can pass through higher costs. The second dimension is the return on AI investment. If AI applications become profitable, the infrastructure buildout is validated, and the beneficiaries are the enablers of that buildout. If AI applications remain unprofitable, the buildout is speculative, and the winners are the few companies that can monetize the infrastructure regardless of application returns. The third dimension is the oil capex cycle. If exploration spending accelerates, the service providers benefit directly, and the pricing upcycle in seismic is confirmed. If the cycle falters, the service providers face a margin squeeze.

The framework suggests that the highest-conviction positions are those that benefit under multiple scenarios. Infrastructure enablers that serve both AI and traditional energy demand are attractive because they have multiple demand drivers. Companies with pricing power that can pass through cost increases are attractive because they are insulated from inflation. Companies with consolidated capacity in a structurally constrained market are attractive because they have pricing leverage regardless of the broader demand environment. The report identifies several names that fit these criteria, and the decision framework provides a systematic way to evaluate them.

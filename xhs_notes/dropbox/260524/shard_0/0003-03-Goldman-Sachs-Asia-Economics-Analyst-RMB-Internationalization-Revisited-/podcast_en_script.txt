EN_A: A new report from a global investment bank is making the case that RMB internationalization has entered a completely different phase. B, what do you think is the single most important shift?
EN_B: The biggest change is the driver. People used to think of RMB internationalization as all about trade settlement. But now, capital and financial account transactions account for 75% of all cross-border RMB flows. Bond investment alone makes up 46%. So the next phase is not just about settlement channels. It’s about something much bigger.
EN_A: 46% from bond investment? That really changes the picture. So what do foreign investors actually need to feel comfortable holding and trading RMB?
EN_B: The report boils it down to three things. First, offshore RMB liquidity needs to be cheaper and more stable. Second, risk management tools need to be better. And third, the pool of RMB assets needs to be deeper. All three have made progress, but each also has clear bottlenecks.
EN_A: Let’s start with liquidity. Offshore RMB funding used to be very volatile. Is that problem really going away?
EN_B: It is improving. In the past, offshore funding costs could be almost 200 basis points higher than onshore. Now that gap has narrowed to just 15 to 25 basis points. Several things helped. The RMB has appreciated about 3% against the dollar this year. And the PBOC-HKMA swap line has acted as a stabilizing force.
EN_A: So the appreciation directly lowers the cost of holding RMB. What about risk management? How does the report assess that?
EN_B: It’s uneven. FX hedging is relatively developed. The RMB’s share of global OTC FX derivatives has jumped from 1% in 2010 to over 8% now. But rates hedging is far behind. The RMB’s share of global interest rate derivatives is only 0.8%. And liquidity is concentrated in the short end and five-year tenor. That means foreign investors cannot easily hedge longer-duration bond risks.
EN_A: So if I want to buy a ten-year Chinese government bond, I don’t have a good tool to manage the interest rate risk?
EN_B: Exactly. That’s the core bottleneck. The report points out that offshore access to CGB futures is still very limited. Until that changes, foreign allocation to long-duration bonds will stay constrained.
EN_A: And what about the asset pool? Beyond government bonds, what else could attract foreign capital?
EN_B: The report suggests two directions. On the onshore side, more connect programs could open up, like those for REITs and commodities. On the offshore side, we need a deeper offshore RMB bond yield curve, a larger dim sum bond market, and potentially a Hong Kong commodity ecosystem with more RMB-denominated products.
EN_A: The dim sum bond market has been very active. The data in the report shows issuance in the first four months of this year nearly doubled compared to last year.
EN_B: Yes, that’s a very positive signal. But the report also warns about the bigger picture. The next phase of RMB internationalization is unlikely to rely on full capital account liberalization. Instead, it will be offshore-led and centered on Hong Kong.
EN_A: So Hong Kong’s role is critical. But the report also leaves some questions open, right?
EN_B: Two big ones. First, if the RMB comes under depreciation pressure again, will offshore liquidity stay stable? The report mentions the experience of the past two years, but it doesn’t give a definitive answer. Second, how exactly will the interest rate swap market deepen? Will it require more derivative products, or does the onshore bond market itself need to become more active first? The report points in a direction, but the exact path still needs to be verified.

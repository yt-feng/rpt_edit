# RMB Internationalization Has Reached an Inflection Point: The Next Phase Will Be Driven by Offshore Markets, Not Trade Settlement

The narrative around RMB internationalization has shifted. For years, progress was measured by the share of China’s trade settled in renminbi. That metric has improved meaningfully—from 13% in 2019 to 30% in 2025. But trade settlement alone will not carry the currency to the next stage. The data now show a structural transformation: cross-border RMB transactions reached RMB 64 trillion in 2024, up from RMB 9 trillion in 2017, and capital- and financial-account flows now account for roughly 75% of that total. Bond investment alone represents 46% of all cross-border RMB payments, dwarfing goods trade at 19%.

This is not merely a compositional shift. It is a fundamental change in what drives demand for the currency. The first phase of RMB internationalization was built on trade invoicing and settlement channels. The next phase will require something far more demanding: foreign participants must find the RMB easy to fund, convenient to hedge, and attractive to hold as an asset. Those conditions are not yet fully in place.

The timing matters. Policy support has become more explicit, with the 15th Five-Year Plan calling for further RMB internationalization and deeper capital account opening. Market conditions have turned more favorable: the RMB has appreciated roughly 3% year-to-date, more than offsetting the annualized cost of holding RMB assets implied by US-China interest rate differentials. Offshore RMB-denominated bond issuance nearly doubled in the first four months of 2026 compared with the same period in 2025. These developments have re-energized client interest, but the question is whether they represent a durable trend or a cyclical tailwind.

The answer depends on three market foundations: offshore RMB liquidity, risk management infrastructure, and the depth of RMB-denominated assets. None of these can be built overnight. But the report’s framework suggests that the path forward is likely to be offshore-led and centered on Hong Kong, rather than reliant on broad capital-account liberalization. That distinction carries strategic implications for investors, corporates, and policymakers alike.

## The Progress Is Real but Narrowly Concentrated in China-Linked Settlement

When measured against the standard functions of an international currency—unit of account, medium of exchange, and store of value—the RMB’s progress is uneven. On the positive side, the RMB’s share in global payments has risen to 3-4% over the past two years, and its share of global official reserves stands at roughly 2%. Both represent meaningful improvement from a decade ago.

Yet these numbers remain strikingly low relative to China’s economic weight. China accounts for 19% of global GDP and 12% of global trade. The RMB’s share in global import invoicing among sample countries was only about 1.5% in 2023. Its share of international debt securities by currency of denomination was 0.9% in 2025. The gap between China’s global footprint and the RMB’s international usage is not closing quickly.

The clearest progress is in China-linked trade settlement. The share of RMB settlement in China’s goods trade has more than doubled since 2019. But even here, RMB invoicing in goods trade with China remains below 10%. The pattern is consistent: the RMB is gaining ground in transactions where one counterparty is Chinese, but its use in third-party trade or as a pure vehicle currency remains negligible.

This concentration matters because it limits the self-reinforcing network effects that make a currency truly international. A currency used primarily for bilateral trade with China does not generate the same liquidity, hedging demand, or asset-holding incentives as one used broadly in global commerce. The next phase of internationalization requires moving beyond this narrow base.

## Financial Flows Now Dominate, Shifting the Requirements for Progress

The composition of cross-border RMB transactions tells a more forward-looking story. Since 2017, total cross-border RMB flows have grown sevenfold, but the mix has changed dramatically. Current-account transactions—primarily trade settlement—now account for only about 25% of the total. Capital- and financial-account flows account for the remainder.

Within that category, bond investment is the dominant use case, representing 46% of all cross-border RMB transactions in 2024. Direct investment accounts for another 13%, Stock Connect for 4%, and the Qualified Foreign Investor program for 7%. The implication is clear: foreign participation in RMB markets is increasingly driven by portfolio allocation decisions, not trade settlement needs.

This shift changes the nature of the challenge. Trade settlement requires channels for invoicing and payment. Portfolio investment requires liquidity, hedging instruments, and a credible store of value. Foreign investors need to be able to fund RMB positions efficiently, manage duration and currency risk, and exit positions without excessive cost. These requirements are not yet fully met.

The report’s framework identifies three bottlenecks: offshore RMB liquidity that is stable and cheap enough to support investment activity; risk management tools that allow investors to hedge interest rate and currency exposure; and a sufficiently broad pool of RMB-denominated assets to attract and retain foreign capital. Each of these represents a necessary condition for the next phase, and none can be addressed through trade policy alone.

## Offshore Liquidity Has Improved but Remains Conditionally Stable

Offshore RMB liquidity in Hong Kong has become cheaper and more stable in recent months, but the improvement is partly cyclical. The spread between 3-month CNH HIBOR and 3-month SHIBOR has narrowed to 15-25 basis points, compared with 100-200 basis points and much higher volatility during 2022-2024. This narrowing reflects RMB appreciation, which has reduced incentives for speculative short positions and eased the pressure on offshore funding rates.

The structural question is whether this stability can persist through different exchange rate regimes. During periods of RMB depreciation pressure, CNH has historically weakened faster than CNY, creating a discount that pushes offshore liquidity back onshore. The PBOC has also used tighter offshore funding conditions as a tool to discourage CNH shorts. These dynamics mean that offshore RMB liquidity is not yet independently stable; it remains sensitive to exchange rate expectations and policy intervention.

The official infrastructure is in place to support liquidity. The PBOC-HKMA standing swap line provides RMB 800 billion in backstop capacity, and the HKMA offers a range of liquidity facilities from intraday to 12-month tenors. But these are backstops, not primary sources of liquidity. For the offshore market to function efficiently in normal times, it needs a deeper pool of RMB deposits, a more active interbank market, and a yield curve that supports pricing across maturities.

Hong Kong’s RMB deposit base, including certificates of deposit, stands at roughly RMB 1.1 trillion. That is substantial but still small relative to the scale of potential demand. The report’s framework shows how this liquidity circulates through FX swaps, interbank lending, and CNH HIBOR pricing, but the system remains dependent on sustained inflows from trade settlement, direct investment, and connect programs. Any disruption to these channels could quickly tighten conditions.

## Risk Management Tools Are the Most Binding Constraint for Deeper Foreign Participation

If offshore liquidity is a question of cost and stability, risk management is a question of capability. Foreign investors need two primary hedging tools: FX derivatives to manage currency risk, and interest rate derivatives to manage duration risk in bond portfolios.

On FX hedging, the progress is measurable. The RMB’s share of global OTC FX derivatives turnover rose from 1.0% in 2010 to 8.1% in 2025. This is a significant increase and suggests that currency hedging is becoming more accessible. The offshore deliverable FX market in Hong Kong provides reasonable liquidity for spot and forward transactions.

The bottleneck is on the rates side. The RMB’s share of global OTC interest rate derivatives turnover was only 0.8% in 2025. Interest rate swap liquidity is concentrated in the front end and at the 5-year tenor, leaving longer-dated maturities relatively illiquid. Offshore investors have only limited access to China government bond futures. This means that a foreign investor holding a 10-year Chinese government bond faces significant challenges in hedging the duration risk.

This constraint is not merely technical. It affects the willingness of long-term institutional investors—pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds—to allocate to RMB bonds. These investors typically require the ability to match assets to liabilities and manage interest rate exposure across the curve. Without adequate hedging tools, the perceived risk of holding RMB bonds increases, even if the underlying credit quality is strong.

The report’s analysis suggests that addressing this gap will require both onshore market opening and offshore market deepening. Expanding access to CGB futures for offshore investors, developing a more liquid offshore RMB interest rate swap market, and building a complete yield curve in the dim sum bond market are all necessary steps. None of these are quick fixes.

## A Broader RMB Asset Pool Requires Both Onshore Access and Offshore Innovation

The third foundation is asset supply. Foreign investors can only allocate to RMB assets if there are sufficient assets to buy and hold. Currently, the RMB-denominated asset pool is dominated by Chinese government bonds and policy bank bonds, accessed through Bond Connect or direct CIBM access. These are high-quality assets, but the range is narrow.

The report identifies several areas for expansion. On the onshore side, connect programs could be extended to cover onshore REITs and commodity products, giving foreign investors access to real estate and commodity exposure in RMB. On the offshore side, the dim sum bond market needs to become larger and more liquid, with a complete yield curve that spans short to long maturities. A potential Hong Kong commodity ecosystem with more RMB-denominated products could also broaden the asset base.

The strategic logic is clear: a currency that is used only for government bonds and trade settlement will remain a niche currency. For the RMB to become a meaningful component of global portfolios, it needs to offer the same diversity of assets as the dollar or the euro—equities, corporate bonds, real estate, commodities, and structured products. That requires both policy decisions and market development.

The report does not fully answer how quickly this asset expansion can occur, or which segments are most likely to develop first. It also leaves open the question of whether foreign demand will materialize at the scale needed to justify the investment in market infrastructure. These are the unresolved analytical questions that make the full report worth reading.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Demand-Side Uncertainty

The report provides a thorough supply-side analysis of what is needed for the RMB to move beyond trade settlement. It identifies the bottlenecks in liquidity, hedging, and asset supply. It offers a clear framework for how offshore market development can proceed through Hong Kong. But it leaves several demand-side questions open.

First, it is not clear how much foreign demand for RMB assets exists at current yields and risk-adjusted returns. The recent appreciation and narrowing of rate differentials have improved the carry, but these conditions could reverse. Foreign portfolio flows into Chinese bonds have been volatile, and structural demand from reserve managers has been slow to build. The report documents the RMB’s 2% share of global reserves, but does not project how quickly that share could rise under different scenarios.

Second, the geopolitical dimension is under-explored. RMB internationalization is not just an economic process; it is affected by trade tensions, sanctions risk, and the broader fragmentation of the global financial system. Some countries may increase RMB holdings for geopolitical reasons, while others may reduce them. The report acknowledges the resurfacing of “petroyuan” discussions amid Middle East tensions, but does not model how geopolitical factors interact with the market foundations it identifies.

Third, the role of technology and digital infrastructure is mentioned but not fully developed. The Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) has seen record transaction volumes, but its relationship to SWIFT and the broader payments ecosystem is complex. The potential for digital RMB to change cross-border payment dynamics is not addressed in detail.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the report. They are the natural boundaries of any analytical framework. For readers who want to form their own view on the pace and trajectory of RMB internationalization, these are precisely the questions that require deeper analysis.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Dimensions to Monitor

For investors trying to position for the next phase of RMB internationalization, the report’s analysis can be translated into a practical monitoring framework. Three dimensions matter most.

First, track the stability of offshore RMB funding spreads. The gap between CNH HIBOR and SHIBOR is a real-time indicator of whether offshore liquidity conditions are supportive or constraining. A sustained narrowing below 50 basis points suggests that funding is not a barrier. A widening above 100 basis points, especially if accompanied by volatility, signals that the offshore market is under stress.

Second, monitor the development of RMB interest rate derivatives. The launch of new products, expansion of CGB futures access, or growth in offshore IRS volumes would be leading indicators that the hedging bottleneck is being addressed. Conversely, stagnation in these markets would suggest that the constraint remains binding.

Third, watch the dim sum bond market and offshore RMB loan growth. These are the building blocks of an offshore RMB asset pool that is independent of onshore access. Strong issuance volumes, a lengthening of maturities, and diversification of issuers would all indicate that the offshore ecosystem is deepening.

These three dimensions are not exhaustive, but they capture the most critical supply-side conditions. If all three are improving, the conditions for broader RMB internationalization are falling into place. If any one of them stalls, the process will remain concentrated in China-linked trade settlement.

# GSIB Surcharges Are Rising Faster Than the Market Expects, and the New Capital Rules Will Not Provide the Relief Many Assume

The latest Y-15 filings reveal a sharp increase in Global Systemically Important Bank scores for the first quarter of 2026, with the median surcharge under current rules jumping by 50 basis points quarter-over-quarter. This is not a marginal fluctuation. It is a structural signal that the largest U.S. banks are becoming more systemically interconnected at precisely the moment when regulators are finalizing a new capital framework. The market has focused on the March 2026 capital proposal as a source of relief for the banking sector. That interpretation is incomplete. While the proposed rules would dampen the quarter-to-quarter volatility in GSIB scores, they do not eliminate the upward trend in systemic risk scores. The implication for investors is clear: capital surcharges are likely to rise over the next 12 to 18 months regardless of which rule set prevails, and the distribution of that increase across banks will be highly uneven.

JPM Chase stands out as the extreme case. Its GSIB score under current rules surged by 159 points to 1,248, pushing its implied surcharge from 5.5 percent to 7.0 percent. This is not a one-off event driven by a single quarter of activity. The underlying drivers are broad-based: trading and available-for-sale securities increased by 46 points, cross-jurisdictional claims rose by 37 points, and OTC derivatives notional added another 20 points. These are not temporary balance sheet blips. They reflect the structural expansion of JPM's global markets footprint and its role as a central counterparty in cross-border financial flows. The question that investors must now confront is whether this trajectory is sustainable, and whether the bank can manage its score back down within the one-year buffer period before the higher surcharge becomes binding.

The conventional wisdom holds that the new capital proposal, released in March 2026, will solve this problem. It will not. The proposal introduces two key changes: a one-time coefficient adjustment of 1.2 times, and a shift from end-of-period balances to average balances across most systemic indicators. The coefficient adjustment reduces the raw sensitivity of scores to balance sheet growth, but it does not reverse the direction of travel. The averaging mechanism smooths out quarterly spikes, which is why the median surcharge increase under the new rules is only 10 basis points compared to 50 basis points under current rules. But smoothing is not the same as reduction. If balance sheet growth continues, the averaged scores will simply ratchet higher over time. The proposal does not cap scores. It delays their recognition.

## The Quarter-Over-Quarter Surge in GSIB Scores Is Concentrated in the Money Center Banks and Reflects Structural Growth in Trading and Cross-Border Activity

The data from the Y-15 filings is unambiguous. Every major bank in the coverage group saw its GSIB score increase in the first quarter. The median score rose by 32 points under current rules, but the distribution is heavily skewed toward the top. JPM's 159-point increase is an outlier, but GS and Citi also posted significant gains of 74 and 53 points respectively. The pattern is not random. The three banks with the largest increases are precisely those with the largest trading and investment banking operations. BofA, Wells Fargo, and the custody banks saw much smaller increases, reflecting their more deposit-centric business models.

The decomposition of the score changes reveals a consistent story. The largest single driver across the group was the increase in total adjusted trading and AFS securities, which added 46 points at JPM, 14 at GS, and smaller amounts elsewhere. This is a direct consequence of the elevated market activity in the first quarter. Higher client trading volumes, increased market-making inventories, and larger securities portfolios all feed directly into the complexity component of the GSIB score. The second largest driver was the increase in cross-jurisdictional claims and liabilities, which reflects the global nature of these banks' wholesale funding and lending operations. OTC derivatives notional also contributed meaningfully, particularly at JPM and BofA.

The strategic implication is that these score increases are not easily reversible. A bank can reduce its GSIB score by shrinking its balance sheet, reducing derivatives activity, or cutting cross-border exposures. But each of those actions would also reduce revenue and market share. In a competitive environment where global markets revenue is growing and client demand for derivatives and cross-border services is rising, banks face a structural tension between profitability and regulatory efficiency. The first quarter data suggests that, for now, the market is winning.

## The New Capital Proposal Dampens Volatility but Does Not Change the Upward Trajectory of Systemic Scores

The March 2026 proposal has been widely described as a net positive for the banking sector, and in many respects it is. The one-time coefficient adjustment of 1.2 times is less punitive than the 1.7 times that some analysts had expected. The shift to average balances reduces the impact of quarter-end balance sheet optimization. The migration from a ratio-based to a balance-based metric for short-term wholesale funding simplifies the calculation and removes some of the gaming that occurred under the old rules.

But these changes do not address the fundamental driver of rising GSIB scores, which is balance sheet growth. Under the new proposal, JPM's implied surcharge would be 5.4 percent in the first quarter, up from 5.2 percent in the fourth quarter. That is a much smaller increase than the 150 basis point jump under current rules, but it is still an increase. And because the new rules use average balances, the score will not revert quickly even if the bank reduces its quarter-end exposure. The smoothing effect works in both directions. It reduces the speed of ascent, but it also reduces the speed of descent.

The more important question is whether the new rules will be finalized as soon as the third quarter of 2026, as the report suggests. If they are, the current rules will only apply for two more reporting cycles. The banks that saw large score increases in the first quarter have an incentive to begin managing their average balances down immediately, because those averages will determine their scores under the new regime. The banks that saw smaller increases have less urgency, but they cannot afford to be complacent. The new rules will lock in the elevated scores from the first quarter for the next several quarters, creating a higher base from which future increases will be measured.

## The One-Year Buffer Under Current Rules Creates a Critical Decision Point for JPM and BofA

Under the current rules, GSIB surcharges are based on year-end data, and banks have a one-year buffer to bring their scores back down before a higher surcharge takes effect. JPM's score in the fourth quarter of 2025 pushed it into a higher bucket, and its score in the first quarter of 2026 pushed it even higher. Under the current rules, the surcharge that applies from the fourth quarter of 2025 will take effect in January 2027 unless the bank can bring its score back down by the end of 2026. The first quarter data makes that task significantly harder.

JPM's score of 1,248 in the first quarter is well above the threshold for the 5.5 percent surcharge bucket. Even if the bank manages to reduce its score in the second, third, and fourth quarters of 2026, it will need to achieve a substantial reduction to avoid the higher surcharge. The same dynamic applies to BofA, though its score increase was smaller. The buffer year is a grace period, not a guarantee. Banks that cannot reduce their scores will face higher capital requirements that will directly impact their return on equity and their ability to return capital to shareholders.

The market has not fully priced this risk. The consensus view is that the new capital rules will render the current rules irrelevant, and that the buffer year will provide ample time for banks to adjust. But the new rules have not been finalized, and the current rules are still in effect. A delay in finalization, or a change in the political environment, could leave the current rules in place for longer than expected. Investors who assume that the buffer year will automatically solve the problem are underestimating the difficulty of reducing systemic scores in a rising rate environment with active markets.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: How Will Banks Trade Off Revenue Growth Against Regulatory Efficiency?

The report provides a detailed decomposition of the score changes and a clear comparison between the current and proposed rules. But it leaves open a critical strategic question: how will the largest banks manage the trade-off between revenue growth and regulatory efficiency in the coming quarters? The first quarter data suggests that the money center banks prioritized market share and client activity over score management. That is a rational choice in a period of strong market conditions. But it may not be sustainable if scores continue to rise.

The report does not model the revenue impact of reducing GSIB scores. It does not estimate how much trading revenue JPM would have to give up to bring its score back to the fourth quarter level. It does not analyze whether the custody banks, which saw smaller increases, have a structural advantage under the new rules. These are the questions that matter for portfolio construction. Investors need to know not just what the scores are, but what banks are likely to do about them.

The report also does not address the second-order effects of higher surcharges on capital allocation. If JPM's surcharge rises to 7.0 percent under the current rules, or to 5.4 percent under the new rules, how will that affect its ability to compete with non-bank financial intermediaries that are not subject to GSIB surcharges? Will the higher capital requirements push the bank to exit certain businesses or geographies? These are open questions that the report implicitly raises but does not answer.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Assess Each Bank's GSIB Risk

The GSIB score data is complex, but it can be simplified into a decision framework that applies to each bank in the coverage group. Investors should ask three questions.

First, what is the bank's score trajectory relative to its bucket threshold? The absolute score matters less than the distance to the next bucket. JPM's score of 1,248 is already in the highest bucket, so the risk is that it moves even higher. GS and Citi are close to the next bucket threshold, which means a single quarter of elevated activity could push them into a higher surcharge. BofA and Wells Fargo have more headroom, but their scores are rising.

Second, how much of the score increase is driven by volatile versus structural factors? The decomposition shows that trading and AFS securities are the largest drivers at most banks. Trading balances are inherently volatile and can be reduced. But cross-jurisdictional claims and OTC derivatives are more structural and harder to adjust. Banks with a higher proportion of structural drivers will find it harder to reverse their score increases.

Third, what is the bank's capacity to absorb a higher surcharge without cutting dividends or buybacks? A 50 basis point increase in the surcharge reduces return on equity by roughly the same amount, assuming no offsetting actions. Banks with higher pre-provision net revenue and lower payout ratios have more flexibility. Banks that are already operating at the lower end of their target capital range will face more pressure.

These three questions provide a systematic way to evaluate the GSIB risk for each bank. They do not replace a full fundamental analysis, but they focus attention on the most important variables.

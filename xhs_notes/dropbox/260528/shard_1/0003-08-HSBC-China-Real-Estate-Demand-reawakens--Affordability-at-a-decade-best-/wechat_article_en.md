# Housing Affordability Has Reset to a Decade-Best Level — and the Market Has Not Yet Priced In the Demand Consequences

The most important shift in China's real estate market is not the supply-side contraction, which has been well documented. It is that housing affordability has improved to levels not seen in ten years, and this improvement is broad-based, structurally significant, and still underappreciated by investors focused on developer balance sheets. A global investment bank report analyzing 106 cities across China demonstrates that both home price affordability and monthly mortgage payment affordability have reset materially. In Shanghai, the monthly payment for a 70-square-meter home has fallen from 82 percent of household disposable income in 2021 to 50 percent today. This is not a marginal improvement. It is a threshold shift that brings home ownership back within reach for average-income families for the first time in a decade.

Why now matters. The market has rallied on supply-side optimism — fewer starts, less inventory, policy support. But the demand side has been treated as a lagging indicator, something that will recover only after confidence returns. The report's data suggests the causality may run the other way. Affordability-driven demand is already visible in secondary transactions, particularly in units priced below RMB 3 million. In Shanghai, such units account for 64 percent of secondary market volumes. As purchase restrictions ease and mortgage rates decline further, the pool of eligible buyers expands not incrementally, but exponentially. The rally's next leg will be driven by demand, not supply.

The argument is straightforward but its implications are not yet priced in. Most investor frameworks for China real estate remain anchored to developer solvency, inventory levels, and policy timing. The affordability reset introduces a new variable: the structural expansion of addressable demand. This changes the risk-reward calculation for developers with exposure to mass-market and upgrade segments, and it changes the timeline for when a self-sustaining recovery might take hold.

## The Double Declines — Home Prices and Mortgage Rates — Have Created a Demand Catalyst That Is Broader Than Most Investors Assume

The improvement in affordability is not a single-variable story. It is the product of two simultaneous declines: home prices have corrected 11 to 25 percent from their peaks across city tiers, and mortgage rates have fallen by approximately 2.6 percentage points from their last peak. Together, these forces produce a compound effect that is larger than either factor alone.

The report's analysis of Shanghai is instructive. For a typical 70-square-meter unit, the monthly mortgage payment under a pure commercial loan has dropped from RMB 13,000 to RMB 9,400. Under a blended commercial and Housing Provident Fund loan, the payment falls to RMB 9,000, representing 48 percent of household disposable income. This is below the 50 percent threshold that banks typically use for loan approval. In 2021, the same metric stood at 76 percent. The improvement is not theoretical — it directly expands the set of households that can qualify for a mortgage.

What is often underestimated is the mortgage rate channel. In tier-1 cities, where absolute home prices are higher, the sensitivity of monthly payments to interest rate changes is amplified. A 100-basis-point reduction in mortgage rates saves a Shanghai homebuyer approximately RMB 1,200 per month on a typical unit. Over the life of a 20-year loan, this amounts to nearly RMB 300,000 in interest savings. The report calculates that monthly interest savings now total 42 percent relative to a scenario where home prices had corrected by 15 percent without rate cuts. This is not a minor tailwind; it is a structural improvement in the economics of home ownership.

The broader pattern across 106 cities confirms the trend. Home price affordability ratios — defined as home price per unit divided by household disposable income — have fallen to 16.3 for tier-1 cities, down from 21.5 at the 2021 peak. Tier-2 cities have declined to 7.2 from 9.3, and tier-3 cities to 6.1 from 7.3. These levels are comparable to where they stood in 2015, before the last major upcycle began. Mortgage payment affordability — monthly payment as a share of income — has similarly declined, with tier-1 cities falling from 1.24 to 0.76, tier-2 from 0.54 to 0.34, and tier-3 from 0.38 to 0.26.

The implication is clear. The affordability reset is not confined to a single city or price segment. It is occurring across all tiers and is most pronounced in the mass-market segment where transaction volumes are highest. This is precisely the demand base that can stabilize prices and restore liquidity to the secondary market.

## Secondary Market Liquidity Is the Bridge Between Affordability and Developer Recovery — and It Is Already Improving

The report makes a critical distinction that many market participants overlook. Improved affordability does not automatically translate into higher primary market sales. The transmission mechanism runs through the secondary market first. Homeowners need to sell existing units before they can upgrade to new ones. If the secondary market is illiquid, the upgrade chain breaks, and demand for new homes remains constrained.

The data shows that secondary market liquidity is already improving. In Shanghai, daily secondary transactions in April 2026 reached 958, up from 580 in the January-February period and significantly above the 768 level in November 2025. This is not an isolated phenomenon. Across 13 key cities, secondary transactions as a share of total transactions have risen from 39 percent in 2021 to 68 percent in the first four months of 2026. The secondary market is not just a substitute for primary sales; it is becoming the primary channel for price discovery and demand realization.

Why this matters for developers. A liquid secondary market enables the "exit to upgrade" dynamic that the report identifies as the next critical catalyst. Homeowners who can sell their existing units at reasonable prices are more willing to purchase new homes, particularly in the premium and upgrade segments where developers' margins are higher. The report notes that units priced below RMB 3 million account for 64 percent of Shanghai's secondary transactions. These are not luxury homes; they are the entry-level and trade-up units that form the base of the housing pyramid. When this segment becomes liquid, it unlocks demand further up the chain.

The evidence from April 2026 is encouraging. The number of cities seeing positive month-on-month trends in secondary asking prices surged to 13 in February 2026, up from just 2 in November 2025. This is early but meaningful. It suggests that the affordability-driven increase in transaction volumes is beginning to stabilize prices at the margin. If this trend continues, it will create the conditions for a secondary-to-primary transmission that the market has been waiting for.

## The Transmission from Secondary to Primary Markets Will Be Faster in Cities Experiencing a Supply Squeeze — and This Creates a Concentrated Opportunity

Not all cities will benefit equally from the affordability reset. The report identifies a specific subset of markets where the secondary-to-primary transmission is likely to play out more quickly: cities experiencing a supply squeeze. In these markets, the combination of reduced new construction and improving secondary liquidity creates a tighter supply-demand balance that can support faster price recovery and quicker sell-through for developers.

The logic is straightforward. When new supply is constrained, buyers who would otherwise purchase new homes are forced into the secondary market. This increases secondary transaction volumes and supports secondary prices. As secondary prices stabilize or rise, the gap between secondary and primary prices narrows, making new homes more attractive relative to existing units. Developers with premium projects in these cities can then achieve faster sales velocity and stronger pricing power.

The report's analysis of construction decline is relevant here. Fewer new starts mean less future supply, which should support pricing power for developers who have already completed or are nearing completion on their projects. The May holiday pulse check cited in the report — "busy sites, better sales" — suggests that this dynamic is already beginning to play out in certain markets.

For investors, the implication is that developer selection should be driven not just by balance sheet quality but by geographic exposure. Developers with concentrated pipelines in supply-constrained, high-affordability cities are better positioned to benefit from the secondary-to-primary transmission. The report's preferred picks — CR Land and C&D International — are Buy-rated precisely because of their rich pipeline of premium projects in such markets. C&D, in particular, is highlighted as having room to catch up in year-to-date share price performance, suggesting that the market has not yet fully discounted this advantage.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer — and Why Investors Should Stay Engaged

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several critical questions open. These are not weaknesses but invitations to deeper analysis, and they define the agenda for the next phase of the investment debate.

First, the affordability framework relies on city-average disposable income data, which likely understates the income of actual homebuyers relative to renters. It also excludes Housing Provident Fund contributions, which can offset mortgage payments. The report acknowledges these limitations but does not quantify their impact. How much larger is the addressable demand pool when these factors are included? The answer could be significantly larger than current estimates suggest.

Second, the report uses a uniform per-capita living space assumption across all cities and all years, based on the 2020 National Population Census. This ignores variations over time and across city tiers — typically, living space per capita is smaller in tier-1 cities and larger in lower tiers. If actual living space in tier-1 cities is smaller than assumed, the affordability improvement is even greater than reported. If it is larger, the improvement is somewhat less. The direction of the bias is uncertain.

Third, the transmission from secondary to primary markets is asserted but not yet proven in the data. While secondary transaction volumes are rising and prices are stabilizing in some cities, it remains to be seen whether this will translate into sustained primary market demand. The May holiday pulse check is encouraging but anecdotal. Investors need to monitor the ongoing sales of developers' signature projects to confirm that the transmission is real and durable.

Fourth, the report does not address the potential for policy reversal. If affordability improves too quickly and prices begin to rise, regulators may reintroduce tightening measures. The current policy stance is supportive, but the report's own analysis shows that affordability is now at decade-best levels. This creates a tension: the very conditions that support demand recovery could also trigger policy caution if they lead to renewed price speculation.

Finally, the report does not model the impact of income growth on affordability over time. If household incomes continue to rise at trend rates, the affordability improvement becomes self-reinforcing. If income growth slows, the improvement erodes. The assumption of stable or growing income is reasonable but not guaranteed.

These open questions are not reasons to dismiss the report's thesis. They are reasons to stay engaged, monitor the data, and refine the investment framework as new information emerges.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position for the Affordability-Driven Recovery

The report's analysis can be distilled into a three-step decision framework for investors evaluating China real estate exposure.

Step one: Assess affordability improvement at the city level. Not all cities have experienced the same degree of improvement. Investors should rank cities by the magnitude of the decline in price-to-income and mortgage payment-to-income ratios. Cities where affordability has improved most from peak levels, particularly those where the monthly payment ratio has fallen below 50 percent, represent the highest potential for demand recovery.

Step two: Evaluate secondary market liquidity trends. Affordability is necessary but not sufficient. Investors need to confirm that improved affordability is translating into higher transaction volumes. Key indicators include weekly secondary transaction volumes, the share of secondary transactions in total market activity, and the number of cities seeing positive month-on-month price trends. The report's data suggests that Shanghai, Chengdu, and Beijing are leading in this regard.

Step three: Identify developers with exposure to supply-constrained, high-affordability markets. The transmission from secondary to primary markets is not automatic. It depends on developers having the right product in the right locations. Developers with premium project pipelines in cities where new supply is constrained are best positioned to benefit. The report identifies CR Land and C&D International as preferred picks, but the framework can be applied more broadly.

For each developer, investors should assess: the geographic concentration of their land bank, the share of premium projects in their pipeline, the pace of sell-through in recent project launches, and the level of net gearing. The report's valuation summary shows that CR Land trades at a 37 percent discount to net asset value with a 2026 price-to-earnings ratio of 9.1 times, while C&D trades at a 63 percent discount with a 2026 P/E of 6.9 times. Both offer dividend yields above 4 percent. These valuations are not demanding if the affordability-driven recovery gains traction.

## The Next Phase of the Rally Will Be Demanded, Not Supplied — and the Data Is Already Telling the Story

The China real estate market has spent the last two years adjusting to a new supply-side reality. Construction starts have fallen, inventory has declined, and developers have consolidated. This was necessary but not sufficient. A market cannot recover on supply alone. It needs demand, and demand needs affordability.

The affordability reset documented in this report changes the calculus. For the first time in a decade, home ownership is within reach for average-income families in China's largest cities. The monthly payment burden has fallen below the 50 percent threshold that defines mortgage eligibility. Secondary transaction volumes are rising, prices are stabilizing, and the conditions for a secondary-to-primary transmission are falling into place.

The market has not yet fully priced this in. Developer valuations remain at deep discounts to net asset value, reflecting skepticism that a durable demand recovery is possible. But the data is moving in the right direction, and the affordability improvement is structural, not cyclical. It will not reverse unless home prices rise sharply or mortgage rates increase significantly — neither of which appears likely in the near term.

Investors who wait for confirmation risk missing the inflection. The secondary market data from April 2026 is already providing that confirmation. The next step is to monitor whether it translates into primary market sales in the months ahead.

The US-Iran conflict has reinvigorated focus on foreign official demand for US Treasuries, as recent data suggest that foreign official investors have been net sellers of USTs since the start of the conflict. Given the context of Dollar strength and weaker external balances in Asia, we think the outflows are primarily the result of managing currency depreciation and mitigating capital outflows, rather than a structural shift.

We find that the Dollar is one of the most meaningful drivers of valuation-adjusted foreign official Treasury demand. Foreign official institutions primarily manage their reserve portfolios based on policy objectives, with FX intervention and buffering capital flows being two major use cases. There are numerous reasons for large foreign official holdings of US Treasuries. $^{1}$ Among those proposed by Bernanke (2005) are the Asian financial crisis and other EM debt crises of the late-1990s, which forced many countries to shift from being net importers of financial capital to next exporters. # A return towards more normal demand patterns rather than a structural shift

Empirically, the Dollar has become a less significant explanatory factor for foreign official demand in recent years, especially over the last year. One recent driver could be the volatility in the Dollar and yields since April 2025—our FX strategists have found that excess volatility can explain lower reserve shares—and over the longer run, reserve diversification could be contributing. However, year-to-date patterns suggest a return towards a more historically normal relationship between the Dollar and foreign official demand, as early year Dollar weakness corresponded to foreign official buying, before March’s Dollar strength gave way to net selling.

Since March, Fed custody holdings have continued to decline. While USD performance has become more differentiated, we think the pattern remains consistent with pockets of more stringent currency management. Despite the observed outflows, the move in swap spreads along the curve was relatively uniform along much of the curve beyond the very front-end (which outperformed) through March (Exhibit 7). Amid the March swap spread moves, there was relatively limited dispersion along the curve and spreads have since more than unwound the move despite some evidence that flows may have continued. And while our convenience yield framework showed a decline in Treasury convenience during the initial phase of the shock in March, it has since stabilized. We view that combination as consistent with relatively digestible selling pressure that has left limited lasting impact on the broader market.

More broadly, we are skeptical that the response to the oil shock reflects a structural shift in foreign official Treasury demand for a few reasons. First, the overall picture of foreign official flows has been largely in the realm of what we would have historically expected based on market moves alone. Second, FX intervention in a managed currency is typically a sign that policymakers intend to keep it tied to the Dollar. The worse signal for structural UST demand would be evidence that some economies with managed exchange rates were abandoning the Dollar as a base currency, as this would more structurally reduce their need for USD reserves. And third, Treasury ownership as a reserve asset remains supported by substantial capital market depth, while alternatives are still somewhat constrained by the lack of available portfolio assets. For example, our FX strategists have previously shown that denominating even a small portion of GCC oil exports in RMB would lead to a significant increase in RMB reserves, to the extent that it would be both difficult to invest and quickly erode the reserve coverage necessary to protect their current FX management system.

USD strength following the conflict interrupted a key Dollar weakening trend that had supported stronger foreign investor participation in Treasury auctions through 2025 and broader demand (Exhibit 8). Assuming an end to the conflict, we would expect a return to the Dollar weakening trend and lower volatility to be a modest tailwind to foreign official UST demand.

# Global Markets Daily: The Energy Shock Impact on Foreign Official Treasury Demand

The US-Iran conflict has reinvigorated focus on foreign official demand for US Treasuries, as recent data suggest that foreign official investors have been net sellers of USTs since the start of the conflict. Given the context of Dollar strength and weaker external balances in Asia, we think the outflows are primarily the result of managing currency depreciation and mitigating capital outflows, rather than a structural shift.

We find that the Dollar is one of the most meaningful drivers of valuation-adjusted foreign official Treasury demand. While the Dollar appears to have become a less significant factor in explaining foreign official demand over time, recent foreign official flows look like a return to more normal patterns.

Despite net selling by foreign official accounts, Treasury swap spreads do not show clear evidence of sustained pressure in aggregate. Were a more persistent negative demand shift driving broader UST price action, we think it would have had a more durable impact on spreads and other measures of Treasury convenience.

We do not think that the patterns in recent months point to a structural shift in foreign official Treasury demand. First, the overall picture of foreign official flows has been largely consistent with what we would expect based on market moves alone. Second, central banks' defense of currency arrangements typically signals a strong intention to remain tied to the Dollar and maintain ownership of USTs in the long run. And third, Treasury ownership remains supported by substantial capital market depth.

Assuming the conflict ends, we would expect a return to the Dollar weakening trend to provide a modest tailwind to foreign official institutions' UST demand, as it had started to do once again pre-conflict.

Foreign official institutions primarily manage their reserve portfolios based on policy objectives, with FX intervention and buffering capital flows being two major use cases. There are numerous reasons for large foreign official holdings of US Treasuries. $^{1}$ Among those proposed by Bernanke (2005) are the Asian financial crisis and other EM debt crises of the late-1990s, which forced many countries to shift from being net importers of financial capital to next exporters. Another contributing factor was the shift toward current account surpluses across many of the large oil exporting economies following the oil rallies of the late-1990s and early-to-mid-2000s. Foreign investor participation has evolved since the GFC, with foreign private investors playing a larger role as foreign official holdings have stagnated while the size of the overall market has steadily grown (Exhibit 1). This observed shift may overstate the reality due to custodial bias, however, as reserve managers that use private custodians will see their holdings classified as private rather than official investment in the Treasury's TIC report.

Large Gulf energy exporters have historically been a smaller, but reliable source of foreign official Treasury demand because the oil trade is overwhelmingly denominated in US Dollars (Exhibit 2), resulting in recycling of Dollar revenues from current account surpluses into Treasuries. Consequently, the halt to oil flows has increased concerns that lower USD revenues across the Gulf Cooperation Council, or GCC countries (Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates) will disrupt a key source of Treasury demand. The majority of foreign Treasury ownership is allocated across G10 and Asia. However, we would highlight that some of the accumulation in G10 Treasury holdings reflects reserve assets held at large European custodians, though the ultimate owner may be based outside of Europe.

While the GCC countries have seen a hit to growth from lower production, the immediate aftermath of the oil price shock may have been slightly net positive for their aggregate current account balance, as the higher price may have been able to outweigh lower export volumes (Exhibit 3). Consistent with initially more insulated external balances, the latest TIC report shows the GCC economies net purchased Treasuries in March (Exhibit 4). However, as our economists have noted, the more prolonged the disruption to oil flows, the more important volume effects become relative to price

effects, which could weigh on this initial pattern over time. Some GCC economies' external balances will likely be more negatively affected than others as well, given that some countries have found alternative routes for continued oil exports. In direct contrast, for much of Asia and some of G10, paying up for oil has corresponded to a current account deterioration that has incurred net Treasury selling across these regions (Exhibit 4).

# Early evidence on the Treasury demand response to the shock

Recent data from the US Treasury and Fed custody holdings suggest that foreign official investors have net sold Treasuries since the start of the conflict as the Dollar has strengthened (Exhibit 5). Consistent with policy motivations—such as managing currency moves, mitigating capital outflows, or simply a desired mix of reserve currencies—driving valuation-adjusted foreign official flows, we find that broad USD performance is a key explanatory factor for foreign official demand (Exhibit 6). We suspect the estimated relationship between currency moves and Treasury purchases is likely biased smaller than reality, given the circular relationship between intervention and Dollar performance.

# A return towards more normal demand patterns rather than a structural shift

Empirically, the Dollar has become a less significant explanatory factor for foreign official demand in recent years, especially over the last year. One recent driver could be the volatility in the Dollar and yields since April 2025—our FX strategists have found that excess volatility can explain lower reserve shares—and over the longer run, reserve diversification could be contributing. However, year-to-date patterns suggest a return towards a more historically normal relationship between the Dollar and foreign official demand, as early year Dollar weakness corresponded to foreign official buying, before March’s Dollar strength gave way to net selling.

Since March, Fed custody holdings have continued to decline. While USD performance has become more differentiated, we think the pattern remains consistent with pockets of more stringent currency management. Despite the observed outflows, the move in swap spreads along the curve was relatively uniform along much of the curve beyond the very front-end (which outperformed) through March (Exhibit 7). Amid the March swap spread moves, there was relatively limited dispersion along the curve and spreads have since more than unwound the move despite some evidence that flows may have continued. And while our convenience yield framework showed a decline in Treasury convenience during the initial phase of the shock in March, it has since stabilized. We view that combination as consistent with relatively digestible selling pressure that has left limited lasting impact on the broader market.

More broadly, we are skeptical that the response to the oil shock reflects a structural shift in foreign official Treasury demand for a few reasons. First, the overall picture of foreign official flows has been largely in the realm of what we would have historically expected based on market moves alone. Second, FX intervention in a managed currency is typically a sign that policymakers intend to keep it tied to the Dollar. The worse signal for structural UST demand would be evidence that some economies with managed exchange rates were abandoning the Dollar as a base currency, as this would more structurally reduce their need for USD reserves. And third, Treasury ownership as a reserve asset remains supported by substantial capital market depth, while alternatives are still somewhat constrained by the lack of available portfolio assets. For example, our FX strategists have previously shown that denominating even a small portion of GCC oil exports in RMB would lead to a significant increase in RMB reserves, to the extent that it would be both difficult to invest and quickly erode the reserve coverage necessary to protect their current FX management system.

USD strength following the conflict interrupted a key Dollar weakening trend that had supported stronger foreign investor participation in Treasury auctions through 2025 and broader demand (Exhibit 8). Assuming an end to the conflict, we would expect a return to the Dollar weakening trend and lower volatility to be a modest tailwind to foreign official UST demand.

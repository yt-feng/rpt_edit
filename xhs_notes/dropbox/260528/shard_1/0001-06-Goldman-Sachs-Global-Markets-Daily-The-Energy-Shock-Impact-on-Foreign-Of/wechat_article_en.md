# Foreign Official Treasury Selling Is a Cyclical Defense of Currency Pegs, Not a Structural Break

The US-Iran conflict has triggered a sharp reassessment of foreign official demand for US Treasuries, with many market participants interpreting recent net selling as the beginning of a secular decline in dollar reserve holdings. This interpretation is almost certainly wrong. The evidence from flows, swap spreads, and currency management patterns points to a far more conventional explanation: foreign official institutions are selling Treasuries to defend managed exchange rates and mitigate capital outflows, not to exit the dollar system. The distinction matters enormously for portfolio strategy. If this were a structural shift, the implications for Treasury yields, the dollar, and global reserve architecture would be profound and lasting. If it is cyclical—as the data suggest—then the selling pressure will reverse once the geopolitical shock subsides and the dollar weakening trend resumes.

Why now? Because the oil shock has created a natural experiment. The conflict simultaneously strengthened the dollar, deteriorated current accounts in Asia, and temporarily improved external balances in GCC economies. This divergence allows us to isolate which countries are selling and why. The regional pattern of Treasury flows maps almost perfectly onto current account pressures, not onto any coordinated de-dollarization campaign. Asia, bearing the cost of higher oil imports, has been the primary seller. GCC economies, benefiting from higher prices even as volumes fall, have been net buyers. This is exactly what you would expect if central banks were managing liquidity needs, not if they were permanently reducing their dollar exposure.

The analytical challenge is to distinguish between a temporary liquidity adjustment and a permanent portfolio reallocation. The report provides three powerful arguments for the former. First, the magnitude of selling is consistent with what historical models would predict given the dollar's move. Second, intervention to defend a currency peg is itself evidence of commitment to that peg—and by extension, to the dollar reserves that underpin it. Third, the depth and liquidity of the Treasury market remain unmatched by any alternative, making a rapid structural shift implausible. These arguments are compelling, but they also leave open critical questions that every serious investor should explore.

## The Dollar Has Been the Dominant Driver of Foreign Official Flows, and Recent Patterns Confirm the Historical Relationship

The empirical relationship between dollar performance and foreign official Treasury demand is one of the most robust findings in this analysis. When the dollar weakens, foreign officials tend to buy Treasuries. When it strengthens, they sell. This is not a coincidence. It reflects the policy logic of reserve managers: a weaker dollar increases the purchasing power of dollar-denominated reserves, reducing the need for active intervention, while a stronger dollar often signals capital outflows or currency pressure that forces central banks to liquidate reserves to support their own currencies.

What makes the current episode notable is that the relationship appeared to break down in recent years. The dollar became a less significant explanatory factor, leading some to conclude that reserve diversification was permanently weakening the link. The report argues, convincingly, that this breakdown was temporary and driven by exceptional volatility. Excess volatility in both the dollar and yields created a regime where normal hedging and intervention patterns were disrupted. Now, with the conflict-induced dollar spike, the relationship has reasserted itself with remarkable clarity. Foreign official selling in March and April tracks almost perfectly with what the historical model would predict given the dollar's move.

The strategic implication is that investors should not extrapolate the current selling into a permanent trend. If the dollar stabilizes or weakens as the conflict de-escalates, foreign official demand should return. The risk is not that central banks have permanently lost interest in Treasuries. It is that they are temporarily forced to sell at a time when private demand may also be under pressure. This creates a tactical vulnerability, not a strategic crisis.

## The Regional Pattern of Treasury Flows Reveals a Liquidity Management Story, Not a De-Dollarization Campaign

The most illuminating data in the report is the breakdown of Treasury purchases by region. Asia ex-Japan has been the dominant seller, and the timing aligns perfectly with current account deterioration caused by higher oil import costs. These economies are running down their dollar reserves to pay for more expensive energy and to support currencies under pressure. This is textbook reserve management. It is not a political statement about the dollar's role.

GCC economies tell the opposite story. Despite the disruption to oil flows, their aggregate current account balance has likely improved in the near term because the price effect has outweighed the volume effect. Higher oil prices mean more dollar revenues, even if fewer barrels are flowing. Consequently, GCC countries have been net buyers of Treasuries. This is exactly what you would expect from economies that recycle petrodollars into dollar assets. The fact that they are buying during a conflict that originated in their region is powerful evidence that de-dollarization is not a priority.

The report also notes that some GCC countries have found alternative export routes, further insulating their external balances. But it wisely flags a critical caveat: the longer the disruption persists, the more volume effects will dominate price effects. If the conflict drags on, even GCC economies could become net sellers. This is a timeline risk that investors must monitor. For now, however, the regional pattern supports the cyclical interpretation.

## Treasury Swap Spreads Show No Evidence of Structural Selling Pressure

If foreign official selling were truly structural and sustained, it would show up in market prices beyond just flow data. The report examines swap spreads as a key diagnostic. Swap spreads reflect the relative value of Treasuries versus interest rate swaps, and they are sensitive to changes in the convenience yield that investors attach to Treasuries as a liquid, safe asset.

The evidence is clear: swap spreads moved during the initial shock but have since stabilized and partially reversed. There was no persistent widening that would indicate a durable shift in Treasury convenience. The move was relatively uniform across the curve, suggesting a broad-based but temporary adjustment rather than a targeted selloff by a specific investor class.

This is a crucial piece of the puzzle. If foreign officials were permanently reducing their Treasury holdings, the convenience yield would decline permanently, and swap spreads would reflect that. They do not. The stabilization of spreads, even as selling has continued, suggests that the market has absorbed the flows without lasting damage. Private investors have stepped in to fill the gap, and the depth of the Treasury market has proven sufficient.

The report's framework for measuring convenience yield is worth understanding in detail, as it provides a real-time check on whether selling pressure is becoming structural. Investors should monitor this metric closely. A sustained decline in convenience yield would be the first genuine warning sign. So far, it has not materialized.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: What Happens If the Conflict Persists?

For all its analytical rigor, the report does not fully address the scenario where the conflict becomes protracted. Its central argument rests on the assumption that the shock is temporary and that a return to the pre-conflict dollar weakening trend will restore foreign official demand. But what if the conflict lasts for years? What if it escalates into a broader regional war that permanently disrupts oil flows and reshapes trade patterns?

In that scenario, the cyclical factors the report identifies could become structural. Prolonged current account deterioration in Asia could force permanent reductions in reserve holdings. GCC economies, after an initial boost from higher prices, could see their external balances deteriorate as volume effects dominate. The dollar could remain strong for geopolitical reasons, suppressing demand for Treasuries on a sustained basis. And the cumulative effect of years of selling could eventually erode the convenience premium that makes Treasuries so attractive.

The report acknowledges this risk in passing, noting that the longer the disruption, the more volume effects matter. But it does not model the second-order implications. If Asian central banks are forced to sell Treasuries for an extended period, they may eventually decide to reduce their target reserve levels, creating a structural reduction in demand. If GCC economies see their dollar revenues permanently impaired, they may accelerate diversification into other currencies and assets.

These are not the report's central forecast, but they are the questions that investors should be asking. The current data supports the cyclical view. But the range of possible outcomes widens significantly with each passing month of conflict. The report's framework is robust for the near term. For the medium term, it requires active monitoring and scenario analysis.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguishing Cyclical from Structural Selling

The report provides the analytical building blocks for a practical decision framework. Investors should evaluate foreign official Treasury selling along three dimensions to determine whether it is cyclical or structural.

First, examine the dollar. Cyclical selling is correlated with dollar strength. Structural selling would occur even when the dollar is stable or weakening. If the dollar retraces and foreign official demand does not recover, that is a red flag. Second, analyze regional patterns. Cyclical selling is concentrated in economies with current account pressure. Structural selling would be broad-based and include economies with strong external balances. The fact that GCC countries are buying is a strong signal that this is cyclical. Third, monitor market price indicators. Cyclical selling is absorbed by the market without lasting damage to swap spreads or convenience yields. Structural selling would leave a permanent footprint.

Using this framework, the current episode clearly falls into the cyclical bucket. But the framework also provides clear triggers for reassessment. If the dollar weakens and foreign official demand remains negative, shift to a more cautious view. If GCC countries begin selling, that would signal that the volume effect is overwhelming the price effect. If swap spreads widen persistently, the convenience premium is eroding.

Investors should also consider the asymmetry of outcomes. If the cyclical view is correct, the current selling creates a buying opportunity. Foreign official demand will return, providing a tailwind for Treasuries. If the structural view proves correct, the selling is just beginning, and the implications for yields, the dollar, and global asset allocation are far more negative. The report's analysis suggests the former is far more likely, but the latter cannot be dismissed entirely.

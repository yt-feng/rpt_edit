# India’s Manufacturing Imperative Is No Longer Optional; It Is the Only Path to Sustained External Stability

India’s balance of payments is under pressure, and the recent policy measures to attract capital inflows are a necessary but insufficient response. For the near term, steps such as removing capital gains taxes on government securities for foreign investors and incentivizing external commercial borrowings will help. But these are stopgap solutions. The deeper structural challenge is that India cannot rely on capital account inflows to indefinitely mask a widening current account deficit. The only durable solution is to boost manufacturing competitiveness, lift goods exports, and fundamentally rebalance the economy away from its reliance on services-led growth and volatile portfolio flows.

The report from a global investment bank makes this argument with clarity: India’s medium-term funding challenge is not about liquidity; it is about competitiveness. Without a manufacturing renaissance, the country will struggle to generate the savings required to fund its investment needs, and its external position will remain vulnerable to shifts in global sentiment, energy prices, and the relative performance of other Asian economies. This is not a new insight, but the urgency has escalated. The data show that India’s real effective exchange rate has fallen more than 3.7 standard deviations below its 10-year mean, a signal of sustained external pressure that no amount of short-term capital account engineering can fully resolve.

The argument is straightforward but its implications are far-reaching. Manufacturing matters because it directly addresses both sides of the balance of payments equation. On the current account side, a more competitive export sector can narrow the trade deficit. On the capital account side, manufacturing attracts foreign direct investment that is more stable than portfolio flows. This double boost is exactly what India needs. But achieving it requires a coherent policy framework, not piecemeal incentives. The report outlines what that framework must include, and the stakes could not be higher.

## The Balance of Payments Pressure Is Structural, Not Cyclical, and Short-Term Fixes Will Not Hold

The numbers tell a stark story. Between December 2024 and March 2026, India’s balance of payments registered a cumulative deficit of USD 52 billion. That is more than double the USD 25 billion deficit recorded during the 2011-2013 taper tantrum period. The composition of this deficit reveals a deeper problem: both the current account and the capital account are under strain simultaneously.

On the current account, the trade deficit has widened due to higher oil prices and a contraction in exports to the Middle East. India’s oil and gas trade deficit stands at 3.4 percent of GDP, one of the widest in the region. That exposes the economy to terms-of-trade shocks that are largely outside its control. On the capital account, net foreign investor flows have recorded outflows in five of the last six quarters since the fourth quarter of 2024. Foreign portfolio investors have been able to exit at relatively high valuations because domestic equity demand remains strong, but that is a double-edged sword: it masks the fact that foreign investors are voting with their feet.

Foreign direct investment has also been weak. Gross FDI inflows have been weighed down by outward FDI, driven by repatriation amid private equity and venture capital exits. The result is that net FDI has been depressed, and the overall capital account has not been able to offset the current account deficit in the way it did during previous episodes of stress. The Reserve Bank of India has responded by ramping up interventions, but even adjusted for forward positions, the import cover ratio has declined. This is not a cyclical blip; it is a structural deterioration.

The implication is clear. If India cannot attract sufficient capital inflows to finance its current account deficit, the adjustment will have to come through a weaker currency, lower growth, or both. Short-term measures to encourage external commercial borrowings or remove withholding taxes on debt investments can provide temporary relief, but they do not address the underlying competitiveness problem. The capital account can be managed, but it cannot be permanently engineered to compensate for a structurally weak current account.

## India’s Relative Earnings Story Has Lost Its Edge, and the Energy Shock Has Exacerbated the Problem

Why have foreign investors become less enthusiastic about India? The report identifies two interrelated factors: a slowdown in relative earnings growth and the energy shock. Both are symptoms of a deeper issue, which is that India’s growth model is not keeping pace with the structural shifts reshaping global capital flows.

India’s corporate earnings growth had slowed to a 19-quarter low of 8.2 percent year-on-year for the September 2025 quarter, excluding energy and financials. Since then, policy easing has driven a recovery, with revenue growth rebounding to 16 percent year-on-year by March 2026. But the relative story has become less compelling. Markets more directly levered to artificial intelligence and industrial supply chains, notably North Asian economies, have a far more powerful earnings impulse. In the first quarter of 2026, India’s BSE 500 earnings grew at 13 percent year-on-year. That looks modest compared to Korea at 171 percent, Taiwan at 49 percent, Japan at 33 percent, and the United States at 29 percent.

This is not simply a cyclical divergence. It reflects a structural reallocation of global capital toward economies that are integrated into the technology and supply chain ecosystems that investors believe will drive the next phase of productivity growth. India’s services-led growth model, while impressive in its own right, does not offer the same exposure to these themes. The country has not yet built the manufacturing base that would allow it to participate in global supply chains at scale.

The energy shock has compounded the problem. India’s relatively wide oil and gas trade deficit means that rising energy prices impose a larger drag on the trade balance and growth than in many of its peers. Investors are pricing in this headwind, and it further weakens the case for allocating capital to India relative to other markets in the region. The combination of a less compelling earnings story and a negative terms-of-trade shock has eroded the premium that India once commanded in the eyes of global investors.

## Manufacturing Can Provide a Double Boost to the Balance of Payments, but Only If Policy Is Coherent and Sustained

The core argument of the report is that manufacturing can address both the current account and the capital account simultaneously. This is the double boost that India needs. On the current account side, boosting manufacturing competitiveness would lift goods exports, narrow the trade deficit, and reduce the economy’s vulnerability to energy price shocks. On the capital account side, a more competitive manufacturing sector would attract foreign direct investment, which is more stable and longer-term than portfolio flows. It would also create jobs, lift incomes, and generate the domestic savings needed to fund higher investment without relying on external capital.

But achieving this double boost requires more than rhetorical commitment. The report identifies five policy pillars that must be in place. First, policy must target high-growth sectors where India has a comparative advantage or can build one. This means identifying specific industries such as electronics, pharmaceuticals, automotive components, and chemicals, and designing incentives that are sector-specific rather than generic. Second, the supply chain must be integrated, both domestically and regionally. Fragmented logistics, inconsistent power supply, and regulatory bottlenecks undermine competitiveness. Third, the financial system must be aligned with manufacturing goals. This means ensuring that credit flows to manufacturing firms, especially small and medium enterprises, and that the banking system is capable of supporting long-term investment. Fourth, research and development spending must be supercharged. India spends less on R&D as a share of GDP than most of its competitors in Asia, and this gap must be closed if the country is to move up the value chain. Fifth, the workforce must be skilled. Manufacturing requires a different set of skills than services, and the education and training system must adapt accordingly.

The report does not claim that these policies are easy to implement. But it makes a compelling case that without them, India will remain trapped in a low-competitiveness equilibrium. The country will continue to rely on services for growth, portfolio flows for external financing, and ad hoc policy measures to manage periodic balance of payments crises. That is not a sustainable strategy.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Political Economy of Manufacturing Reform

The report is strong on diagnosis and prescription, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most significant is the political economy of manufacturing reform. The policies required to boost manufacturing competitiveness are well understood, but they have been difficult to implement in practice. Land acquisition, labor market flexibility, and regulatory simplification are politically sensitive issues that have resisted reform for decades. The report acknowledges that these are challenges, but it does not provide a framework for understanding how they might be overcome.

A second unanswered question is about the role of the state versus the private sector. The report calls for policy to target high-growth sectors, but it does not specify how the government should choose which sectors to prioritize. Industrial policy has a mixed record in India and elsewhere. The risk of picking winners is that the government may be captured by incumbent firms or may misjudge the trajectory of global demand. The report would benefit from a more detailed discussion of how to design sectoral policies that are transparent, time-bound, and subject to competitive pressure.

A third question is about the external environment. The report focuses on what India can do domestically, but the global context matters enormously. The rise of protectionism, the fragmentation of global supply chains, and the rapid technological change driven by artificial intelligence all create both opportunities and risks for India’s manufacturing ambitions. The report touches on these factors but does not fully explore how they might alter the calculus.

These are not criticisms of the report; they are natural extensions of its analysis. Any serious reader will come away from the report convinced that manufacturing is the key to India’s external stability, but also aware that the path to achieving it is fraught with uncertainty. The report invites further inquiry, and that is a sign of its quality.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Policymakers: Three Questions to Ask

For investors and policymakers trying to assess India’s manufacturing trajectory, the report implies a simple but powerful decision framework. Three questions should guide the analysis.

First, is India making progress on the five policy pillars? The report identifies targeting high-growth sectors, integrating supply chains, aligning the financial system, increasing R&D spending, and skilling the workforce as the critical success factors. Investors should track progress on each of these dimensions. If the government is taking concrete steps on multiple fronts, the outlook for manufacturing is positive. If progress is limited to one or two areas, the risk of disappointment is higher.

Second, is the external environment becoming more or less favorable? The double boost from manufacturing depends on global demand for the goods that India produces. If global trade growth is strong and supply chains are diversifying away from China, India has a tailwind. If protectionism rises and global demand weakens, the tailwind becomes a headwind. Investors should monitor trade policy, technology trends, and the growth outlook for India’s key export markets.

Third, is the financial system capable of supporting the transition? Manufacturing requires long-term investment, and that investment must be financed. If the banking system is healthy and credit is flowing to productive sectors, the transition is more likely to succeed. If the banking system is constrained or if credit is being allocated to less productive uses, the transition will be slower and more costly.

These three questions provide a framework for making sense of the data and policy announcements that will emerge in the coming quarters. They are not a substitute for detailed analysis, but they offer a starting point for understanding whether India’s manufacturing story is real or rhetorical.

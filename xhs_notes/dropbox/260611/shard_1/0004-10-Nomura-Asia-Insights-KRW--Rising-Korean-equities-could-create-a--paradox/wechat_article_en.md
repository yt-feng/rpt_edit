# The Korean Won Is Being Crushed by Its Own Success: Why a Booming Stock Market and Surging Exports May Not Save the Currency

The Korean won is defying economic gravity. In 2026, South Korea is running a current account surplus of extraordinary proportions—USD 37.3 billion in March alone, annualized to roughly 23% of GDP. The KOSPI index has surged 87% year-to-date, making it the best-performing major equity benchmark globally. By any conventional logic, these should be the ingredients for a resurgent won. Instead, the currency has weakened approximately 5.5% against both the US dollar and its trade-weighted basket, ranking among the worst performers in Asia ex-Japan. This is not a puzzle; it is a warning. The structural forces driving Korea's capital account—pension fund rebalancing, retail investor behavior, corporate USD hoarding, and the mechanics of a concentrated equity market—are now powerful enough to overwhelm even the most impressive trade surplus. For investors, the critical question is not whether the won is cheap, but whether the forces that have made it cheap are about to intensify or reverse.

The paradox has not gone unnoticed by Korean policymakers. The Chief Presidential Secretary for Policy recently described the won's weakness as "a paradoxical phenomenon created by success, rather than a vulnerability of the Korean economy." That framing is politically convenient but analytically incomplete. Success in equity markets and export earnings does create its own FX dynamics, but those dynamics are not self-correcting. They are structural, embedded in the behavior of the National Pension Service, the allocation rules of global funds, and the investment habits of Korean retail investors. Understanding the won's trajectory requires a framework that moves beyond the trade balance and into the mechanics of portfolio flows.

A global investment bank report provides a rigorous scenario analysis that maps the possible paths for Korea's balance of payments over the next twelve months. The range of outcomes is staggering. Under the most adverse assumptions, net outflows could reach USD 204.7 billion. Under the most favorable, the country could see net inflows of USD 149.6 billion. The difference between these scenarios is not a matter of export demand or geopolitical luck. It is determined by three variables that are, to varying degrees, within the sphere of policy influence and market behavior: the pace of corporate repatriation of export earnings, the enthusiasm of Korean retail investors for foreign assets, and the extent of foreign equity selling triggered by concentration limits on Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. The won's fate will be decided by how these three forces interact.

## The National Pension Service Revision Is a Positive Step, But Rising Domestic Equities Create a Powerful Countervailing Force

The National Pension Service's decision to raise its end-2026 target allocation for domestic equities from 14.9% to 20.8% was widely interpreted as a pro-won measure. By reducing the planned outflow to overseas assets, the revision appeared to remove a significant source of structural USD demand. Based on March 2026 asset allocation data, the new targets imply that NPS overseas outflows for the remainder of 2026 could be as low as USD 1.2 billion, down from an estimated USD 43.9 billion under the previous targets. That is a material shift, and it has given some market participants reason to turn less negative on the won.

However, this calculation is static. It assumes that the value of NPS's domestic equity holdings remains at March levels. Since then, the KOSPI has rallied by approximately 60%. If NPS's domestic equity allocation has risen proportionally, the fund's actual domestic equity weight may now be in the range of 30%, far above the new 20.8% target. To return to that target, NPS would need to sell domestic equities and reallocate into overseas assets, generating USD demand in the process. The bank's analysis estimates that this rebalancing need could be as large as USD 70.5 billion. The new target, in other words, does not eliminate the outflow risk. It merely shifts the trigger from a fixed schedule to a contingent one, dependent on the continued rally of Korean equities.

The key variable is whether the KOSPI rally persists. If it does, NPS will be forced to sell domestic stocks into strength, acquiring foreign assets and putting downward pressure on the won. If the market corrects, the rebalancing need diminishes. This creates a counterintuitive dynamic: a strong equity market, normally a source of currency support, becomes a source of currency weakness through the pension fund channel. The NPS has also temporarily expanded its allowable range for strategic asset allocation, reportedly from 3% to 6%, giving it more room to delay rebalancing. But this is a deferral, not a cancellation. The eventual adjustment will come, and the direction of the flow is clear.

## The 10% Single-Stock Cap Rule on Global Funds Creates a Hidden and Potentially Massive Source of Won Weakness

The most underappreciated risk to the won lies in the concentration of Korea's equity market. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together account for a dominant share of the KOSPI's market capitalization. Many global equity funds operate under a 10% single-stock cap rule, meaning they cannot hold more than 10% of their portfolio in any one issuer. As these two stocks have rallied, their weight in fund portfolios has increased, pushing funds closer to or beyond this limit. The result is forced selling: funds must reduce their holdings of Samsung and SK Hynix to comply with their mandates, regardless of their fundamental view on the companies or on Korea.

The scale of this potential selling is enormous. The report models a scenario in which Samsung and SK Hynix rally by 60% while the broader market gains 10%. Under those assumptions, foreign outflows from these two stocks alone could reach USD 48 billion. If the rally is even stronger, the outflows grow proportionally. This is not a speculative tail risk; it is a mechanical consequence of the cap rule, and it is already in motion. The KOSPI's 87% year-to-date gain has been driven disproportionately by the tech sector, and the concentration problem has become acute.

The implications for the won are direct and negative. Foreign equity selling generates USD demand as investors repatriate proceeds. The larger the forced selling, the stronger the downward pressure on the won. This dynamic operates independently of Korea's current account surplus or its export performance. It is a capital account phenomenon, and it can overwhelm trade flows. The bank's scenario analysis shows that in the most negative case, foreign equity outflows alone could reach USD 95.3 billion, a number that would dwarf any reasonable expectation of current account inflows.

## The Alignment of Three Forces Is Required for a Sustained Won Recovery, and That Alignment Is Not Yet in Place

The bank's analysis makes clear that a sustained won recovery requires the simultaneous alignment of three factors. First, a de-escalation in US-Iran tensions that drives a weaker US dollar and allows for RMB appreciation, which in turn would encourage Korean exporters to increase their remittance of USD earnings back to Korea. Second, a moderation in the exuberance surrounding the global AI cycle, which would slow Korean retail portfolio outflows to the US, reduce the risk of forced foreign equity selling, and slow the growth of NPS's assets under management, thereby lowering the need for overseas diversification. Third, a reduction in the corporate USD hoarding that has been documented by local media and Korean authorities, which would increase the actual net trade settlement inflows available to support the currency.

None of these conditions are currently in place. The US dollar remains broadly strong. The AI theme shows no sign of losing momentum, with upcoming IPOs from SpaceX, Anthropic, and OpenAI likely to attract significant Korean retail interest. Corporate USD hoarding appears entrenched. The bank's analysis suggests that without alignment of these factors, Korea's balance of payments dynamics will remain unfavorable, and the won will continue to face headwinds.

This is not a forecast of perpetual weakness. It is a conditional statement about the forces that must change for the outlook to improve. Investors who are betting on a won recovery need to track these three variables closely. A ceasefire in the Middle East, a rotation out of US tech equities, or a policy intervention to encourage corporate repatriation could each shift the calculus. But none of these are imminent, and the structural forces pushing against the won are powerful.

## The Report's Scenario Analysis Provides a Decision Framework, But It Leaves Two Critical Questions Unanswered

The bank's scenario analysis is a valuable tool for investors. It lays out three paths for Korea's balance of payments over the next twelve months, each defined by specific assumptions about corporate repatriation, retail outflows, and foreign equity selling. The moderate scenario, which assumes a 60% repatriation ratio, retail outflows at half the recent pace, and a 60% rally in Samsung and SK Hynix, produces net outflows of USD 16.7 billion. The positive scenario, with a 79.8% repatriation ratio, zero retail outflows, and a more modest 30% rally in the two tech giants, generates net inflows of USD 149.6 billion. The negative scenario, with weak repatriation, strong retail outflows, and a 100% rally in Samsung and SK Hynix, produces net outflows of USD 204.7 billion.

The framework is useful, but it leaves two critical questions unanswered. First, what is the probability distribution across these scenarios? The report does not assign probabilities, and the range of outcomes is so wide that it is difficult to form a base case with confidence. Second, what is the role of policy intervention? Korean authorities have shown a willingness to use moral suasion and regulatory tools to influence capital flows. They could tighten rules on retail foreign investment, impose capital controls, or use the NPS's expanded SAA range to delay rebalancing. The effectiveness of these tools is uncertain, and the report does not model them explicitly.

These open questions are not weaknesses of the analysis. They are the natural frontier of the debate. For investors, the key insight is that the won's trajectory is not determined by the trade surplus. It is determined by the interplay of portfolio flows, corporate behavior, and equity market dynamics. Those factors are complex, but they are not opaque. Tracking the behavior of Korean retail investors, the performance of Samsung and SK Hynix, and the repatriation decisions of exporters will provide a clearer read on the won's path than any single macroeconomic indicator.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Leading Indicators to Monitor

For investors seeking to navigate the won's uncertainty, the report's analysis suggests three leading indicators that should be monitored on a weekly or monthly basis. First, the pace of Korean retail portfolio outflows to US equities. If these outflows resume their previous trend, the won will face sustained pressure. If they remain subdued, the balance of payments outlook improves. The upcoming US tech IPOs are a natural test case. Second, the performance of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix relative to the broader KOSPI. If the divergence continues, the forced selling dynamic intensifies. If the market broadens, the concentration risk diminishes. Third, the level of corporate USD deposits at Korean banks. If these deposits decline, it signals that exporters are repatriating earnings and supporting the won. If they continue to rise, the hoarding problem is unresolved.

These indicators do not provide a mechanical trading rule. They provide a framework for interpreting the flow of information. The won is not a simple function of the trade balance or interest rate differentials. It is a complex system driven by the behavior of institutional investors, retail speculators, and corporate treasurers. The report's contribution is to map that system and identify the key variables. The rest is judgment.

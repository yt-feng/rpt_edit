# The Regulatory Noise Around Hong Kong Deposits Is Real, But the Earnings Signal Is Overblown

The market has punished HSBC and Standard Chartered shares since late May, with both underperforming the European bank index by 5% and 7% respectively. The trigger was clear: a sequence of Chinese regulatory announcements targeting cross-border investment flows, online broker compliance, and Hong Kong deposit account procedures. Investors, already wary of geopolitical friction, read these signals as a potential clampdown on the capital flows that have fueled wealth management growth in Hong Kong. The logic seems intuitive, but it is incomplete. A careful examination of the actual financial mechanics suggests that even in an adverse scenario, the earnings impact on these two banks is marginal. The real story is not about lost deposits. It is about the market's inability to price regulatory ambiguity, and the strategic gap between what regulators have said and what they may yet do.

The core argument of this analysis is that the recent underperformance of HSBC and Standard Chartered reflects a mispricing of regulatory risk. The measures announced so far, while symbolically important, have a quantifiably small effect on net new money flows from mainland China sources. The earnings sensitivity is in the low single digits as a percentage of profit before tax. Yet the market has treated these announcements as if they threaten a structural change to the Hong Kong wealth franchise. This disconnect creates an analytical opportunity. The question is not whether regulators are tightening. The question is which parts of the cross-border investment ecosystem are truly at risk, and whether the market has correctly priced those risks into the stocks.

## The Announced Measures Are Procedural, Not Existential, for HSBC and Standard Chartered

Two regulatory actions have dominated investor attention. On 22 May, the China Securities Regulatory Commission announced new controls on online brokers and imposed fines, while the Hong Kong Monetary Authority issued a circular reinforcing account opening procedures and compliance requirements around investment accounts and source of funds. On 1 June, China's State Council released a decree regulating cross-border investment, with a focus on technology transfers and outbound capital monitoring. The financial press immediately connected these dots into a narrative of capital control tightening.

The reality is more nuanced. The HKMA circular largely restates existing rules. Standard Chartered's CFO noted at a recent conference that the bank's existing policies were already directionally aligned with the circular. The incremental actions required, such as closing legacy zero-balance accounts and obtaining client attestations on source of funds, add operational friction but do not change the fundamental economics of the business. By 9 June, press reports indicated that Hong Kong-based banks had completed the necessary system upgrades and that account opening services continued smoothly. This is not the profile of a regulatory shock.

The State Council decree is broader in scope but its direct application to banking remains unclear. The decree's primary motivation, according to economists who have studied it, relates to monitoring cross-border technology transfers and managing geopolitical risks associated with Chinese businesses abroad. It is not a capital control measure. The one article that could affect banks, article 33, states that investment in overseas financial markets shall be governed by regulations with detailed rules to be formulated separately. That is an open question, not a done deal. The market is pricing worst-case outcomes for a rule set that has not yet been written.

## The Earnings Sensitivity to a Full Stop of Onshore Mainland China Flows Is Below 2% of PBT

The most important analytical step is to isolate the portion of net new money that actually originates from onshore mainland China. Based on management commentary from both HSBC and Standard Chartered, the typical mainland Chinese resident holding a Hong Kong account sources approximately 90% of their deposit flow from offshore locations such as Singapore or elsewhere in Hong Kong. Only about 10% comes directly from a mainland China bank account. Standard Chartered's CFO further clarified that roughly 30% of the bank's net new money comes from Global Chinese clients, and the vast majority of that money is already sitting offshore.

These figures matter because they define the upper bound of potential disruption. In 2025, HSBC generated approximately $86 billion in net new money and Standard Chartered approximately $52 billion. If we assume that 10% of group net new money originates from onshore mainland China, and we model an extreme scenario where that flow stops entirely, the reduction amounts to roughly $9 billion for HSBC and $5 billion for Standard Chartered. That sounds large. But the earnings impact depends on the margin earned on those flows.

At a 100 basis point margin, the impact is 0.3% of 2025 PBT for HSBC and 0.7% for Standard Chartered. Even at a generous 300 basis point margin, which is above current HIBOR of 2.7%, the impact rises to only approximately 1% of PBT for HSBC and 2% for Standard Chartered. These are not numbers that justify a 5% to 7% relative underperformance. The market is reacting to a tail risk that, even in its most extreme form, is financially contained.

## The Real Risk Is Not in Deposits, but in the Unwritten Rules for Wealth Products and Insurance

The deposit analysis is reassuring, but it is not the full picture. Wealth income accounts for roughly one third of operating income for both banks. For HSBC, wealth fees from its disclosed Hong Kong segment contribute approximately one fifth of total wealth fees and roughly 2% to 3% of group operating income. For Standard Chartered, Hong Kong wealth contributes approximately 40% of total wealth income. These are material numbers, and they are exposed to a different regulatory vector.

The current regulatory measures do not directly target insurance or investment products. The report notes that these products are either out of scope or already in line with pre-existing rules. But the logic of the State Council decree, which focuses on monitoring cross-border technology and capital flows, could easily be extended. If the detailed rules under article 33 impose restrictions on the types of financial products that mainland Chinese residents can purchase through Hong Kong accounts, the wealth fee stream would be affected in ways that the deposit analysis does not capture.

This is the open analytical question that the market is struggling with. The deposit impact is small. The wealth product impact is unknown. And because the detailed rules have not been published, investors are forced to assign probabilities to scenarios that range from benign to disruptive. The current underperformance suggests the market is weighting the disruptive scenarios more heavily than the data on deposits would justify. That may create an opportunity for investors who can distinguish between regulatory noise and structural change.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating Hong Kong Bank Exposure to Cross-Border Regulatory Risk

For investors trying to position around this uncertainty, a structured framework is more useful than a binary view. The first dimension is the source of funds. Onshore mainland China flows are small relative to total net new money, and the earnings sensitivity is low. Offshore flows from global Chinese clients, which are the majority, are not directly affected by mainland regulatory measures. The second dimension is the product type. Deposits are relatively safe. Insurance and investment products face greater uncertainty because they are more likely to be caught in a broader regulatory net aimed at monitoring cross-border capital flows.

The third dimension is the regulatory timeline. The HKMA circular is already being implemented, and the impact is procedural. The State Council decree is still awaiting detailed rules. The timing and content of those rules will determine whether the current risk premium is justified or excessive. The fourth dimension is the bank-specific exposure. Standard Chartered has a higher proportion of Hong Kong wealth income relative to group earnings, and a higher proportion of net new money from Global Chinese clients. That makes it more sensitive to regulatory changes that affect the wealth product suite, even if the deposit impact is similar to HSBC.

The framework suggests that the current underperformance may be overdone for both banks, but that Standard Chartered carries more idiosyncratic risk if the regulatory focus shifts to wealth products. The deposit analysis provides a floor. The wealth product analysis provides a range of outcomes that depend on regulatory decisions that have not yet been made.

# China's Restaurant Recovery Is Splitting into Two Distinct Trajectories: The Resilient and the Stalling

The narrative that China's restaurant sector is undergoing a uniform post-pandemic recovery no longer holds. The May 2026 data reveals a market that is fragmenting along lines of brand strength, operational model, and category resilience. While the broad trend softened sequentially from April, the dispersion in same-store sales growth (SSSG) across brands widened meaningfully, creating a landscape where aggregate metrics obscure more than they reveal. This is not a cyclical pause; it is a structural sorting process that will define winners and losers for the next several quarters.

The key insight from the latest monthly tracker is that the "rising tide" phase ended in late 2025. What remains is a market where consumer spending is more selective, more value-conscious, and less forgiving of weak concepts. The high base effect for freshly made drinks (FMD) has arrived as anticipated, compressing year-over-year comparisons for the entire sub-sector. Yet even within this headwind, some brands are holding ground while others are sliding sharply. The divergence is not random—it follows identifiable patterns related to pricing strategy, menu innovation, and store-level economics.

For serious investors, the question is no longer whether the sector will recover. It is which business models can sustain positive momentum when the macro tailwinds fade and the easy comparisons disappear. The May data provides the first clear evidence that the answer is not uniform.

## The Hot Pot and Casual Dining Segments Reveal Opposite Trajectories: Haidilao's Stalling Versus Tai Er's Acceleration

The contrast between Haidilao and Tai Er in May is instructive and should command the attention of anyone tracking the Chinese casual dining space. Haidilao's average table turn turned slightly negative year-over-year in May, a notable deceleration from the mid-single-digit increase recorded in April. While management attributes part of this to the calendar shift of the Dragon Boat Festival, the post-holiday data tells a more concerning story: table turns settled at approximately 3.5x to 3.6x after Labor Day, essentially flat with April levels. This implies a recovery rate of roughly 80-82% versus 2019, slightly above April's 80% but still materially below the first-quarter level.

The issue is not that Haidilao is losing customers to competitors. The issue is that its recovery trajectory has flattened at a level that remains well below pre-pandemic benchmarks. The brand's YTD average table turn increase of low-single-digit percentage is tracking slightly behind the sell-side consensus expectation of 4% per-store sales growth. This gap, while small in absolute terms, raises a critical question: has Haidilao reached a natural ceiling on its recovery, or is this a temporary soft patch? The data suggests the former, given that the brand has now spent multiple months in the low-80% recovery range without meaningful upward progression.

Tai Er tells a fundamentally different story. Despite weather headwinds during Labor Day that dragged its SSSG to mid-to-high single digits during the holiday period, the brand posted low-double-digit SSSG for the full month of May in mainland China. More importantly, post-holiday SSSG accelerated, indicating that the underlying demand momentum is intact. Tai Er's performance remains above its first-quarter level of 10.9% SSSG, and the new store model continues to drive growth while even older models recorded low-to-mid-single-digit positive SSSG.

The strategic implication is clear: Tai Er's menu refresh and new store format are generating genuine incremental demand, while Haidilao's reliance on its established brand equity appears to be yielding diminishing returns. The divergence is not about macro conditions—both brands operate in similar economic environments. It is about the effectiveness of operational strategy in a market where consumers are more willing to trade down or trade across brands than they were two years ago.

## The Freshly Made Drinks Sector Faces a High-Base Squeeze, But Brand-Level Divergence Is the Real Story

The FMD segment entered May with the most anticipated headwind of the year: the high base effect from the prior year's strong performance. The data confirms that this effect is real and material. Most FMD brands saw year-over-year SSSG pressure in May, and the compression was broad-based. However, the magnitude of the pressure varied enormously, and that variance carries significant investment implications.

Nayuki's per-store sales decline expanded to 24% year-over-year in May, a sharp deterioration from the 11% decline in April. This is not a base effect story; it is a brand-specific demand problem. Nayuki is losing share in a segment where consumers are increasingly price-sensitive and where competitors are offering more compelling value propositions. The sequential acceleration of the decline suggests that the brand's positioning in the premium segment is becoming increasingly difficult to sustain as consumer sentiment softens.

In contrast, leading FMD brands demonstrated greater resilience, supported by category expansion and new product launches. The report notes that multiple brands commented on a sequential or year-over-year decline in delivery mix, which is a positive signal for dine-in economics and suggests that the channel shift away from delivery is benefiting margins even if top-line growth is moderating.

The pricing dynamics within FMD are particularly revealing. The report's tracker indicates that more pricing activities were introduced in coffee from freshly made tea brands. Guming launched a weekly 9.9 RMB coupon, Mixue introduced a buy-one-get-one-free offer for stores with coffee machines, and Luckin launched a daily 9.9 RMB offering for select SKUs across categories. These moves are designed to educate customers and drive dine-in traffic, but they also signal that the competitive intensity in the coffee-adjacent space is escalating.

The critical question for FMD investors is whether these pricing promotions are demand-creating or margin-eroding. The report suggests that promotion magnitude and scale remain disciplined, which is a cautiously positive assessment. But the risk is that as more brands adopt aggressive pricing to defend market share, the sector could enter a race to the bottom that compresses margins across the board. The divergence between Nayuki's sharp decline and the relative resilience of leading brands suggests that scale, brand equity, and product differentiation still matter—but the margin of safety is narrowing.

## The Secondary Brands and Legacy Concepts Are Falling Further Behind, Creating a Two-Tier Market

Beyond the headline names, the May data reveals a widening gap between the top-tier brands and the rest of the market. Jiumaojiu and Song continue to experience pressure, with SSSG trends remaining similar to their first-quarter performance of negative 20% and negative 11%, respectively. Gourmet Master's mainland China sales decline remained severe at negative 46% year-over-year, essentially unchanged from April's negative 48%. The store count has stabilized at 290-300 units, but this masks a year-over-year decline of over 30% as the brand transitions to a franchised model.

The pattern is consistent: brands that lack a clear competitive moat, whether through concept differentiation, cost leadership, or operational excellence, are seeing their sales trajectories diverge negatively from the market leaders. This is not a temporary phenomenon. The base effects are becoming easier for these brands as they lap prior-year declines, yet their performance is not improving. This suggests structural demand erosion rather than cyclical weakness.

The international operations of some brands provide a partial offset. Gourmet Master's US market sales growth sequentially accelerated in April and May, with local-currency sales up 4% and 8% year-over-year, respectively. The company expects US store count to reach 95 by mid-2026 and over 100 by year-end. This geographic diversification is a positive, but it remains a small part of the overall business and does not change the fundamental challenge in the core China market.

For investors, the implication is that the Chinese restaurant market is becoming a two-tier system. The top tier—brands like Tai Er and the leading FMD players—are demonstrating the ability to generate positive SSSG even in a soft demand environment. The bottom tier—legacy brands, undifferentiated concepts, and those reliant on past brand equity—are sliding into what appears to be a structural decline. The middle ground is shrinking, and the May data suggests that brands that cannot demonstrate clear momentum will face increasing pressure on store-level economics and franchisee confidence.

## The Data Does Not Yet Answer Whether the Recent Softness Is Cyclical or Structural, and This Uncertainty Is the Central Investment Risk

For all the insight the May tracker provides, it leaves one critical question unanswered: is the sequential softening from April a temporary demand dip driven by weather, calendar shifts, and cautious consumer sentiment, or is it the beginning of a more sustained deceleration?

The report's language is carefully balanced. It notes "softness in consumption sentiment and weather headwinds" but also points to post-holiday acceleration for Tai Er and disciplined promotion activity. The high base effect for FMD is well understood and largely discounted by the market. But the data does not yet provide a clear signal on whether the underlying demand trend is stabilizing or deteriorating.

Several indicators could provide clarity in the coming months. Air traffic run rates and commodity inflation data, which the report tracks as high-frequency indicators, will be important to monitor. A sustained decline in air traffic would suggest that consumer mobility and spending are weakening further. Conversely, stabilization or improvement would support the view that May was a soft patch rather than a trend change.

The Dragon Boat Festival calendar shift is a genuine one-off factor, but it does not explain the entirety of the softness. Haidilao's post-holiday table turn of 3.5x-3.6x is essentially the same as April's level, suggesting that the underlying run rate has not improved. This is the data point that most concerns us: if the recovery has plateaued at 80-82% of 2019 levels for a brand of Haidilao's scale and brand recognition, what does that imply for the rest of the market?

The answer to this question will determine whether the current valuation multiples for Chinese restaurant stocks are justified. If the softness is cyclical and the sector re-accelerates in the second half of the year, current prices may offer attractive entry points. If it is structural and the two-tier divergence widens further, the premium for top-tier brands will expand while the discount for laggards will deepen. The May data provides the evidence to frame the question but not yet the answer.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Filters to Distinguish Resilient Brands from Vulnerable Ones

Based on the patterns evident in the May data, we propose a three-filter framework for evaluating Chinese restaurant investments in the current environment.

First, assess the brand's SSSG trajectory relative to its own base and its peer group. A brand that is generating positive SSSG in a soft month like May, especially on a post-holiday basis, has demonstrated genuine demand momentum. Tai Er passes this test. Haidilao, with its flattish post-holiday table turn, does not. Nayuki, with its expanding decline, clearly fails.

Second, evaluate the brand's pricing strategy and its impact on margins. The report notes that promotion activity is disciplined overall, but the range is wide. Brands that are using targeted, time-limited promotions to drive traffic without eroding overall pricing power are in a stronger position than those resorting to broad-based discounting. The coffee-adjacent pricing moves from Guming, Mixue, and Luckin are worth monitoring closely, as they could signal the early stages of a price war in the FMD segment.

Third, examine the brand's unit economics and store-level payback periods. The report's data on store openings and closures is revealing. Haidilao opened four company stores and one franchise store while closing four company stores in May—a net addition of one store. Gourmet Master's store count is stable but at a level that implies significant year-over-year contraction. These are not signs of aggressive expansion; they are signs of capital discipline in an uncertain environment. Brands that are opening stores at a healthy clip while maintaining positive SSSG are demonstrating that their unit economics support growth. Brands that are treading water on store count while SSSG declines are likely facing deteriorating payback periods.

Applying this framework, the market appears to be rewarding brands that combine positive SSSG with disciplined expansion and rational pricing. The risk is concentrated in brands that show negative SSSG, declining store counts, or aggressive discounting. The May data reinforces the view that stock selection in this sector is more important than sector allocation.

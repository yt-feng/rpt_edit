# High Jewelry Is No Longer Just a Brand-Building Cost Center — It Is Becoming the Most Strategic Weapon in Luxury’s Polarized Market

Richemont’s Jewellery Maisons posted 24% organic growth in the first quarter of its fiscal year, a result that surprised both market-side and market-side expectations. An estimated 20% of those sales came from high jewelry — pieces that often exceed €1 million and require more than a year of inventory turnover. This is not a cyclical blip. It is the structural consequence of a market where wealth creation is concentrating in narrow pockets — driven by artificial intelligence, entrepreneurial fortunes, and asset appreciation — while the broad aspirational consumer base shrinks. The top 0.14% of luxury clients, numbering roughly 600,000 people, now account for €236 billion in spending, up 48% in the most recent measurement period. The rest of the 424 million luxury consumers, by contrast, cut their spending by 20%, surrendering €113 billion in aggregate. In this environment, the brands that win will be those that capture the top-tier client. And the product category most suited to that task is high jewelry.

The logic is straightforward but underexplored by observers. High jewelry functions as couture for the wrist and neckline: extremely high ticket, extremely low volume, and extremely powerful as a brand halo. For decades, most luxury conglomerates treated it as a loss leader — a creative exercise that generated prestige but not profit. Gross margins on high jewelry run 50-60%, compared to 70-80% for design jewelry. EBIT margins are even more compressed, at 10-20% versus 40-50%. Inventory sits for over a year, not six months. Johann Rupert, Richemont’s chairman, stated plainly on his company’s most recent earnings call: “The higher the jewelry, the lower the margin. High high, high jewelry does not enjoy the same margins.” Yet the strategic calculus is shifting. When a single very important client can spend more in one private viewing than a boutique generates in a quarter, the economics of client acquisition change. High jewelry becomes not a margin drag but a relationship anchor.

This article examines why high jewelry is becoming the critical category in luxury’s new demand structure, how brands are reorganizing around it, and what observers should watch as the category scales beyond its heritage roots.

## The Polarization of Luxury Spending Makes High Jewelry the Only Category That Simultaneously Serves Brand Building and VIC Retention

The data on consumer polarization is stark. A global research report segments the luxury market into five cohorts based on spending per capita. The top tier — 0.6 million people spending an average of €393,300 each — grew their total outlay by 48%, adding €113 billion in incremental spending. The largest cohort, 383 million “other aspirational” consumers spending an average of €1,500 each, cut their collective spending by exactly the same amount: minus €113 billion. The market is not growing; it is redistributing. The top 0.14% of clients now control 23% of total luxury spend, up from a lower share in prior periods.

This redistribution has a direct product implication. The top-tier client does not seek logo-driven canvas bags or entry-level leather goods. That consumer seeks rarity, provenance, craftsmanship, and the ability to own something no one else can acquire. High jewelry delivers all four. Each piece is often one-of-a-kind. Materials are dominated by precious stones, not mass-producible metals. Distribution is not through stores or e-commerce but through bespoke clienteling and private viewings in exclusive locations — Lake Como for Dior, St. Tropez for Cartier, Venice for Chaumet. The experience itself becomes part of the value proposition.

For luxury groups, the strategic imperative is clear. If the top 0.14% of clients drive nearly a quarter of revenue and are growing while the mass market shrinks, the brands that invest in the products and services that attract that client will compound their advantage. High jewelry is the most direct channel to that client. A €2 million necklace sold to one VIC generates more revenue than 200 entry-level handbags, with far lower marketing cost per euro of sale and far deeper client relationship. The halo effect also lifts the rest of the brand. A client who buys high jewelry is far more likely to purchase ready-to-wear, watches, and accessories from the same maison. The acquisition cost of that client, amortized over a lifetime of purchases, makes the low margin on the initial jewelry sale irrelevant.

## The Category Is Attracting New Entrants Because the Economics of Client Acquisition Have Inverted

The most telling signal of high jewelry’s strategic importance is who is entering the category. Gucci unveiled its first-ever high jewelry collection in 2019, a move that would have been unthinkable a decade earlier for a brand built on logo-driven leather goods. Hermès, whose core identity is silk and saddlery, is doubling down on high jewelry by presenting its largest collection since the category’s 2010 launch, ahead of its Haute Couture debut in January 2027. Chanel hired a Cartier veteran as the director of its jewelry studio, effective October 2026. Tiffany & Co. continues to use high jewelry exhibitions — in London, Tokyo, Seoul — as a pillar of its turnaround strategy.

These moves share a common logic. The traditional luxury pyramid — accessible entry products at the base, aspirational mid-tier, and ultra-luxury at the peak — is flattening. The mid-tier consumer is retrenching. The top is expanding. Brands that lack a credible high jewelry offering cannot credibly claim a position at the top of the pyramid. They become confined to the shrinking middle.

The entry barriers, however, are high. High jewelry requires decades of gemstone sourcing relationships, master artisans who can spend 1,900 hours on a single ring, and the patience to carry inventory for more than a year. A new entrant cannot simply hire a designer and launch a collection. The necessary trust with VICs — who will not purchase a €1 million piece from a brand without heritage in the category — takes years to build. This is why the incumbent Place Vendôme houses — Cartier, Van Cleef & Arpels, Boucheron, Chaumet — retain structural advantages. But the brands that are investing now are betting that the window of opportunity is open because the VIC base is growing faster than the heritage houses can serve it alone.

## High Jewelry Functions as a Decision Framework for Allocating Capital Across the Luxury Portfolio

For observers and strategists, high jewelry offers a useful lens for evaluating luxury groups. The category condenses three critical strategic questions into a single observable decision: Is the brand investing in the top client, or is it trying to serve everyone?

The first question is capital allocation. High jewelry ties up significant working capital. Inventory turns slower than one year. Gross margins are 50-60%, not 70-80%. EBIT margins are 10-20%. A group that is serious about high jewelry must be willing to accept lower short-term returns for longer-term client value. This is the opposite of the private-equity or margin-maximization mindset. Richemont, with its heritage in jewelry and its chairman’s explicit acknowledgment of the margin trade-off, has made this bet consistently. LVMH, through Bulgari, Tiffany, Chaumet, and Fred, is placing a multi-brand bet on the category. Kering, through Gucci and Boucheron, is still building credibility.

The second question is distribution capability. High jewelry cannot be sold through standard retail. It requires private clienteling, invitation-only events, and the ability to fly VICs to exclusive locations. Brands that lack this infrastructure — or that try to sell high jewelry through e-commerce or store counters — will face challenges. The distribution model is closer to a private bank’s wealth management desk than to a fashion boutique.

The third question is creative authenticity. High jewelry collections are built around narratives — Hermès uses the horse motif, Chanel uses camellia and star symbols, Van Cleef & Arpels spent four years developing an Ancient Egypt-inspired collection without falling into orientalism. The creative director must have the freedom to spend 85,000 hours on 125 pieces, as Cartier did for its “Le Chœur des Pierres” collection, without being pressured to produce commercial volume. Brands that treat high jewelry as a marketing stunt rather than a genuine creative endeavor will be exposed when VICs compare pieces across houses.

## The Geography of High Jewelry Growth Is Shifting Toward Markets with Concentrated Wealth and Cultural Appetite for Rarity

The report identifies Greater China, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East as the regions that have driven recent growth in high jewelry. North America is emerging as a market with serious potential, similar to other luxury categories. This geographic pattern is not accidental. These are markets where wealth is newly created, often concentrated in entrepreneurial or family-owned businesses, and where the cultural signaling of rare gemstones carries particular weight.

Greater China remains the largest single market for high jewelry, but the composition of demand is changing. The report notes that Chaumet’s CEO described exceptional high jewelry demand as resilient, while pieces around the €150,000 mark require more innovation to tempt clients. This suggests that the top of the market — pieces above €1 million — is less price-sensitive and more driven by scarcity and storytelling. The middle tier of high jewelry, where prices are high but not extraordinary, faces more competition and requires stronger differentiation.

The Middle East has long been a stronghold for high jewelry, driven by family wealth, cultural traditions of gift-giving, and a preference for colored gemstones. Southeast Asia, particularly Singapore and Thailand, has benefited from wealth migration and the expansion of family offices. North America, historically more focused on watches and design jewelry, is showing signs of catching up as new wealth from technology and other sectors seeks tangible, portable assets.

For luxury groups, the implication is that high jewelry investment should be calibrated to regional wealth concentration, not to overall GDP growth. A brand that allocates high jewelry inventory and clienteling resources to markets where the top 0.14% is expanding will generate higher returns than one that spreads resources evenly across all regions.

## The Structural Tension Between Creative Ambition and Financial Discipline Will Define Which Brands Succeed at Scale

The most honest observation in the report comes from Richemont’s chairman: high high jewelry does not enjoy the same margins. This creates a tension that every luxury group must manage. The creative teams want unlimited budgets for gemstones, craftsmanship hours, and exclusive events. The finance teams want margin expansion and inventory turns. The VICs want uniqueness at any cost. These three forces pull in different directions.

The brands that succeed will be those that treat high jewelry as a long-term investment in client equity, not as a profit center in the current year. They will accept 10-20% EBIT margins on the jewelry itself, knowing that the VIC acquired through that piece will generate 40-50% margins on design jewelry, accessories, and ready-to-wear for decades. They will measure success not by the margin on the necklace but by the lifetime value of the client who bought it.

The risk is that some groups, under pressure from observers to show margin improvement, will underinvest in high jewelry at exactly the moment when the VIC base is growing. They will save money in the short term and lose the client relationship in the long term. The polarization of luxury spending makes this trade-off more significant than ever. The top 0.14% is not just growing; it is becoming the only growth engine in the market. Brands that fail to serve that client will be left serving a shrinking middle.

The evidence from the report is clear: high jewelry is no longer a niche category for connoisseurs and collectors. It is the strategic center of gravity for luxury in a polarized world. The brands that understand this — and that have the patience to invest through the margin compression — will compound their advantage. The brands that treat it as a side project will find themselves locked out of the fastest-growing segment of the market.

EN_A: A recent report from a global investment bank took a deep dive into the software ecosystem, focusing on the Databricks DAIS conference. What was the single most interesting takeaway for you?
EN_B: The idea of redefining what a durable software moat looks like. The report introduces a very practical concept: good sticky versus bad sticky.
EN_A: Good sticky and bad sticky? That sounds intuitive, but what is the real difference?
EN_B: A good sticky company is one that innovates fast and has products customers love. A bad sticky company might have innovated less, but customers are stuck because of high migration costs, and there are lots of complaints. The report argues that AI is making migration much easier, so those bad sticky moats are going to erode.
EN_A: So Databricks falls into the good sticky category? What was the evidence?
EN_B: Exactly. At the conference, Databricks announced several key products, like LTAP, which lets analytical and transactional engines run on the same open-format data source. And Open Sharing, an open protocol for sharing data across clouds. This dramatically lowers the barrier to switching from legacy platforms.
EN_A: But lowering the barrier to switch also lowers the barrier to switch away. Isn't that a risk for Databricks itself?
EN_B: That is the fascinating insight from the report. Databricks's strategy is to lower switching costs to force itself to innovate faster. It even said that if a better solution comes along, customers should be able to leave. This actually raises the competitive bar for the entire industry and limits new entrants.
EN_A: So it is like using openness to drive growth. The report also talked about custom versus packaged apps. What was the key point there?
EN_B: That is the second big theme. The report argues the most valuable AI applications won't be inside existing SaaS systems. They will exist in the white space between those systems. That means custom agentic apps, built for specific enterprise use cases.
EN_A: So what does that mean? Are traditional SaaS vendors in trouble?
EN_B: It means value is shifting from packaged software to the infrastructure and operating system that enables these custom apps. To build this new agentic layer, companies need an intelligent operating system. The report outlines four ways to build it, including Databricks's Ontology, Palantir, the hyperscalers, and some AI point products.
EN_A: Which approach will win? Did the report have a conclusion?
EN_B: Not a definitive one, but it offered a framework. More complex organizations will likely go custom. Smaller businesses will probably still use off-the-shelf SaaS. Overall, the report is incrementally more positive on custom apps as a driver of consumption for platforms like Databricks. But there is a big question the report doesn't fully answer.
EN_A: What question is that?
EN_B: The total cost of ownership for custom apps. ServiceNow once quantified it as 5 to 10 times higher than SaaS. Databricks claims its new tools, like Unity Catalog, lower that cost. But whether that is true in practice is something that still needs to be validated. The report leaves that as an open question.
EN_A: And the third big takeaway? Beyond moats and custom apps, what else stood out?
EN_B: Databricks is moving into new application markets, like SIEM, security information and event management, and CDP, customer data platforms. The report notes that many customers were already using Databricks for these things. Now Databricks is just commercializing them.
EN_A: So that is a direct threat to incumbents like Cloudflare or Datadog?
EN_B: Yes, but the report says it will take time. In security, Databricks needs to solve the cost of data ingestion and build out more complex correlation and go-to-market capabilities. It points out that Cloudflare itself took years to succeed in security. But in CDP, progress might be faster because switching costs are lower and the ROI is more direct.
EN_A: So the core narrative of the report is that Databricks is expanding from a data platform into applications, while using an open ecosystem to build its own good sticky moat.
EN_B: That is exactly right. But the report leaves one bigger cliffhanger: Databricks's ambition in database technology. It launched LTAP to solve a 40-year-old problem of unifying transactional and analytical databases. This could disrupt the entire database market.

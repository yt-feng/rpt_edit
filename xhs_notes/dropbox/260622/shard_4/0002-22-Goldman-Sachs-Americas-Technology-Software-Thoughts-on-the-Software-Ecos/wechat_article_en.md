# The Software Moat Is No Longer What You Think: Why "Bad Sticky" Is Becoming a Liability and Custom Agentic Apps Are Reshaping the Entire TAM

The most dangerous assumption in enterprise software investing today is that customer inertia equals durable competitive advantage. For years, the industry has rewarded vendors that built deep integrations, proprietary data formats, and complex migration paths, effectively trapping customers through switching costs. That era is ending. A global investment bank report emerging from the Databricks Data & AI Summit in San Francisco makes a compelling case that the software industry is undergoing a fundamental reordering of value. The central insight is this: the definition of a software moat is being rewritten in real time, and the companies that will win are those that embrace open ecosystems, relentless innovation, and a willingness to cannibalize short-term pricing power for long-term market share.

The report introduces a critical distinction between "good sticky" and "bad sticky" companies. "Good sticky" firms innovate at an accelerating pace on core differentiators and build products their customers genuinely love. "Bad sticky" companies have innovated less over the last several years but remain sticky because of the prohibitive costs of migration, often accompanied by low customer engagement or high complaint levels. The thesis is stark: bad sticky moats are being eroded, and artificial intelligence is the primary instrument of that erosion. AI now makes it dramatically easier to streamline migrations, meaning the cost of leaving a mediocre vendor is collapsing. This is not a slow-moving trend; it is a structural shift that will separate the software industry into two distinct trajectories over the next three to five years.

The timing of this shift is not accidental. The same technology that is enabling the next wave of enterprise value creation, custom agentic applications that sit in the white space between traditional SaaS silos, is also the technology that is dismantling legacy lock-in. The report suggests that the software total addressable market could see a 20% uplift by 2030, translating into a two-point tailwind to industry growth, from 7% to 9% annually. But that uplift is not evenly distributed. It will accrue disproportionately to vendors that can enable two specific architectures: open standards and easy data ingestion for a single source of truth, and a unified operating system or ontology layer that supports custom agentic apps with governance and model controls. Today, that list includes Snowflake, Palantir, and Databricks. Over the medium term, it may extend to Microsoft, ServiceNow, and application vendors that figure out how to monetize headless architectures.

## The "Bad Sticky" Moat Is Crumbling, and AI Is the Wrecking Ball

The concept of "bad sticky" is more than a clever label; it is a diagnostic framework for assessing vulnerability across the software landscape. The report makes clear that the pace of innovation, measured not by product release counts but by the significance of the pain points addressed, is the true differentiator. Databricks, for example, announced several product releases that are structurally significant: LTAP, which allows analytical and transactional engines to operate from a single open-format source of truth, and Open Sharing, an open protocol for sharing data, models, agents, and skills across any cloud or vendor. These are not incremental features. They are architectural statements about how value should flow in a modern enterprise.

The strategic implication is profound. Databricks is explicitly lowering barriers to switching *to* its platform from legacy data management systems, but it is also acknowledging customer freedom. The company has stated that if a more innovative non-Databricks solution arrives, it will be easy to run a multi-vendor ecosystem or switch off entirely. This is not altruism; it is a calculated strategy that creates an upward bias to R&D and M&A intensity. Databricks plans to manage to free cash flow breakeven, meaning it is forgoing short-term profitability to fund aggressive disruption of adjacent total addressable markets, such as security information and event management (SIEM) and customer data platforms (CDP). The company has even noted that it could choose to price two times higher, but that short-term pricing optimization often comes at the expense of long-term competitiveness and lifetime value.

This logic extends beyond Databricks. The report points to publicly traded companies like Shopify and Cloudflare as exhibiting similar "good sticky" characteristics: high pace of innovation, disruptive pricing aimed at long-term market share momentum, and products that customers actively want to use, not just tolerate. The question every software investor should be asking is not whether a company has high gross retention rates, but whether those rates reflect genuine product love or the high cost of leaving. The answer will determine which companies compound value over the next decade and which ones get disrupted.

## Custom Agentic Applications Will Capture the Largest Share of the Software TAM Uplift

The second major thesis of the report concerns the blurring lines between infrastructure and application software, and the emergence of custom agentic apps as the primary driver of new value creation. Historically, enterprise software has been organized around packaged SaaS applications that exist in silos: CRM, ERP, marketing automation, and so on. The most interesting agentic applications, however, are likely to exist in the white space between these silos. They are inherently custom, tailored to business-specific use cases that no off-the-shelf product can fully address.

To enable this new agentic application layer, customers need to build an intelligent operating system layer that pulls data from systems of record, often in a headless architecture, and from other enterprise data stores. The report identifies four key approaches to building this OS layer. First, Databricks' new Ontology product, which includes functionality like OntoRank to prioritize data sources based on context. Second, Palantir, which leverages Databricks and Snowflake as building blocks and moves the burden of engineering entirely from the customer to Palantir's Foundry deployment team. Third, the hyperscalers, with Microsoft's Satya Nadella recently emphasizing that every enterprise should have its own version of frontier intelligence that marries frontier models with specific enterprise data. Fourth, select AI point products such as ServiceNow's agent control tower or privately-held Dataiku.

The critical insight here is that the line of demarcation between custom and packaged will likely hinge on organizational complexity. More complex organizations and applications will be custom built. Less complex organizations, including small and medium businesses, will likely leverage SaaS off-the-shelf offerings like Salesforce Agentforce or ServiceNow Control Tower. Given how early the agentic software TAM is, the report expects a rising tide to lift many boats in the near term. However, the conversations at both the Databricks and Snowflake conferences make the analysts incrementally positive on custom apps as a driver of underlying consumption for Databricks, Snowflake, and Palantir.

This is not just about building apps; it is about maintaining them. The report notes that the cost of building an application has already collapsed with coding tools. But the cost of maintaining a custom app in production has historically been five to ten times the total cost of ownership of SaaS. Many of Databricks' innovations directly lower this maintenance TCO. Unity Catalog acts as a universal governance layer embedded in ontology. Ontology leverages a single source of truth. LTAP collapses the need for brittle pipelines and extract-transform-load processes between different data sources. The implication is clear: the economic case for custom applications is strengthening, and the vendors that enable this shift will capture disproportionate value.

## The Database Wars Are Being Fought on a New Front: Transactional and Analytical Convergence

One of the most strategically significant arguments in the report is that while the rest of the industry is focused on agentic AI, Databricks sees an opportunity to further advance the database technologies that will support agentic workloads. This is a classic "pick and shovel" strategy, and it is grounded in a powerful observation: all of the new software being written today needs a database, and Databricks can provide database technology that takes outsized share in this greenfield TAM.

The centerpiece of this argument is LTAP, which the report describes as solving a 40-year problem in database engineering. Historically, enterprises have used separate tools for transactional databases, ETL, warehouses, business intelligence, governance, and AI. This fragmentation creates complexity, latency, and cost. LTAP, powered by a new serverless Postgres layer called Lakebase, allows analytical and transactional engines to operate from a single open-format source of truth. This is not a marginal improvement; it is a structural reduction in the need for replicas, change data capture pipelines, and the brittle infrastructure that has long separated application databases from analytics systems.

The emphasis on Postgres is particularly telling. In an agent-driven world, workloads operate at machine speed and require cost-efficient, deterministic, and inherently branchable environments. Lakebase introduces primitives such as near-instant database branching, enabling agents to safely fork datasets, experiment, write changes, and revert to prior states as needed. This is a fundamentally different set of requirements than traditional human-driven analytics. Agents need not just read access to enterprise context, but governed, low-latency transactional systems where they can act safely, at scale, and with repeatability.

The broader implication is that Databricks is positioning itself to capture operational workloads that have historically sat outside its core competencies. The company's product expansion aims to collapse a fragmented database and data infrastructure market into an integrated platform built on open formats, shared governance, and a common data layer. The early results are visible: Databricks' core revenue growth, excluding token pass-through, has accelerated for the last five quarters. Snowflake is also starting to show acceleration, albeit to a lesser extent. The database wars are no longer just about warehousing or analytics; they are about becoming the foundational layer for the entire enterprise data stack in an AI-native world.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Organizational and Go-to-Market Challenges of Domain-Specific Applications

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several meaningful open questions that merit careful consideration. The most significant concerns Databricks' expansion into domain-specific applications like SIEM and CDP. The report notes that customers were already using Databricks as the cornerstone of their custom SIEM and CDP strategies, and that these announcements essentially commercialize what more sophisticated customers were already doing. The product offerings are likely to be compelling because they directly solve problems around data ingestion and aggregation.

However, the report also acknowledges that it will likely take time for Databricks to build out its domain-specific experience. In security, for example, in addition to solving the key cost problem around data ingestion, Databricks may need to introduce more sophisticated event streaming and correlation functionality, and build out its go-to-market. The report draws a useful comparison to Cloudflare, which took several years to evolve its security go-to-market before seeing more success in the 2025 timeframe. Similarly, Datadog is still in the process of crystallizing its security strategy after mixed traction in cloud security.

The question that remains unanswered is whether Databricks can execute on these domain-specific expansions with the same velocity it has shown in its core data platform. The company's strategy of disrupting adjacent TAMs is aggressive and intellectually coherent, but it requires building capabilities that are fundamentally different from its historical strengths. In security, domain expertise matters deeply for credibility with CISOs and security operations centers. In marketing, the switching costs may be lower, and customers can map good CDP functionality directly to more effective revenue generation, which could accelerate adoption. But the organizational complexity of building multiple domain-specific go-to-market motions simultaneously should not be underestimated.

Another open question concerns the competitive dynamics between Databricks and Palantir. The report describes a generally complementary relationship, in part because of Palantir's strong deployment motion and the fact that Palantir pulls through usage of Databricks. But the biggest question for customers is whether they are willing to trade off outsourcing the heavy lift on building the ontology for the risk of longer-term lock-in with Palantir. Palantir's pricing model is recurring, but the value provided is weighted up front to its original ontology build. Databricks has noted that it will sometimes engage with Palantir customers upon their renewals who look to rebuild agentic workflows with Databricks. This suggests that the relationship is more competitive than it appears on the surface, and the outcome may depend on whether customers prioritize flexibility over speed of deployment.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the New Software Landscape

For readers trying to translate these insights into investment or strategic decisions, the report suggests a clear framework based on three diagnostic questions.

First, assess the nature of the moat. Is the company "good sticky" or "bad sticky"? Look for evidence of accelerating innovation on core differentiators, pricing strategies that prioritize long-term market share over short-term margin, and genuine customer enthusiasm rather than mere tolerance. Companies that rely on proprietary data formats, complex migration paths, or contractual lock-in without corresponding product excellence are increasingly vulnerable.

Second, evaluate the company's position in the custom agentic application stack. Is it enabling the operating system layer that will support custom apps? Is it building the ontology and governance infrastructure? Or is it primarily a packaged application vendor that could be bypassed by custom solutions? The report suggests that the value in the software stack will increasingly accrue to vendors that enable custom app development, not just those that sell pre-built applications. This has implications for traditional SaaS vendors that have built their businesses on siloed, monolithic architectures.

Third, assess the company's ability to converge transactional and analytical workloads. The convergence of OLTP and OLAP, enabled by technologies like LTAP, represents a structural shift in database economics. Companies that can offer a unified platform for both operational and analytical workloads, on open formats with shared governance, are likely to capture a disproportionate share of new software spending. Companies that remain specialized in one domain or the other may find themselves squeezed.

This framework is not static. The report makes clear that the competitive landscape is evolving rapidly, and the answers to these questions will change over time. But the direction of travel is clear: open ecosystems, relentless innovation, and the ability to enable custom agentic applications are the new criteria for software value creation. The companies that understand this and act on it will compound value for years to come. The companies that do not will find their "bad sticky" moats eroding faster than they expect.

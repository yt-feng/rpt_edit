# The European Chemical Industry Is Not Just Cyclically Weak — It Is Structurally Reorganising, and the Winners Will Be Those Who Control Reformulation and Specialty Innovation

The second half of 2025 is shaping up to be a reckoning for European chemicals — not merely a seasonal downturn, but a genuine phase of value destruction that will expose long-hidden overcapacity once geopolitical disruptions subside. A recent virtual roundtable of senior chemical executives, convened by ICIS and the European Association of Chemical Distributors, delivered a strikingly bleak consensus: even if the Middle East conflict ends soon, normalisation will take far longer than markets expect, and the return of normal supply chains will only reveal how much structural damage has already been done.

The core argument emerging from this discussion is that the European chemical industry is facing a three-part structural crisis that no single company can solve alone. First, China has used the disruption to cement its role as the dominant exporter of intermediates and solvents, extinguishing any short-lived hopes of a European competitiveness revival. Second, reformulation — initially a temporary response to high feedstock prices — is accelerating and becoming permanent in certain segments, permanently altering demand patterns. Third, European production capacity is being shut down in ways that are likely irreversible, forcing customers to look elsewhere for supply while simultaneously asking European producers to restart lines that may never return.

What this means for investors is not simply a call to avoid European chemicals. It is a call to identify which companies have built the structural advantages — multi-source global supply networks, deep specialty innovation capabilities, and customer-specific formulation expertise — that will allow them to emerge stronger from this reorganisation. The roundtable pointed directly to three such advantages: well-integrated global suppliers like BASF that can reshuffle production across continents; specialty distributors that have become indispensable innovation partners; and companies with high R&D spend and customer-specific formulation sales, such as Givaudan and Novonesis.

But the report also leaves critical questions unanswered. How permanent is the reformulation shift? Can European producers realistically restart shut-down capacity? And what happens when Chinese producers, now moving into semi-specialties, begin to compete directly with the very specialty players that have so far been protected? These are not minor uncertainties — they are the central strategic questions that will determine the next decade of returns in this sector.

## The End of the Conflict Will Not Bring Relief — It Will Reveal the Full Extent of Overcapacity

The most powerful insight from the roundtable was not about the conflict itself, but about what happens after it ends. The consensus among the CEOs was clear: a resolution to the Middle East conflict will not be a catalyst for recovery. Instead, it will expose the underlying overcapacity that has been masked by supply disruptions.

The logic is straightforward. During the conflict, supply chains were disrupted, shipping routes were extended, and buyers hoarded inventory or scrambled for alternatives. This created an artificial floor under prices and margins. Once peace returns, the backlog of ships — estimated to take six to eight weeks just to clear — will release a wave of supply into the market. At the same time, feedstock that has been trapped in the Middle East will flow freely again, further depressing input costs and squeezing margins for producers who have been able to pass through higher costs.

The third quarter, one CEO warned, will be "messy." Buyers are already reluctant to make decisions because they cannot determine fair pricing in a market that is about to change dramatically. This is not a normal inventory correction. It is a structural readjustment where the return of normal supply will reveal that there is simply too much capacity chasing too little demand.

The implication for investors is that any company relying on a cyclical recovery in European chemicals is likely to be disappointed. The overcapacity is not going away. It has been building for years, driven by Chinese investment, weak domestic demand in China, and the inability of European producers to compete on cost. The conflict only delayed the reckoning.

## China Has Used the Crisis to Cement Its Export Dominance — and Is Now Moving into Specialties

The roundtable painted a stark picture of China's strategic positioning during the conflict. While other Asian countries suffered feedstock supply issues, China maintained better-than-expected feedstock availability and used its weak domestic demand to push product into export markets. The result has been a dramatic surge in Chinese chemical exports.

The data is striking. Chinese chemical exports to the rest of Asia rose 70% in March and April alone. Methanol exports were up more than 200% in April. These are not marginal increases — they represent a fundamental shift in trade flows that is unlikely to reverse when the conflict ends.

Equally important, the roundtable highlighted that China is now moving beyond commodities and into semi-specialties. This is a direct challenge to the strategy that many European specialty chemical companies have relied on: compete on innovation and customer relationships, not on cost. As Chinese producers advance into specialty segments, they increasingly recognise the need for local presence in Europe — people on the ground who understand the market and customers. This means they are not just exporting product; they are building the commercial infrastructure to compete directly with European incumbents.

The strategic question for European companies is whether they can maintain their innovation advantage as Chinese producers invest in R&D and local market knowledge. The answer is not obvious. The roundtable noted that specialty players are already prioritising long-term innovation partners, which suggests that the market is segmenting. Companies that can demonstrate deep customer-specific formulation expertise and high R&D spend will be rewarded. Those that cannot will face increasing price competition from Chinese imports.

## Reformulation Is Accelerating — and May Be More Permanent Than Markets Assume

One of the most underappreciated developments in the current environment is the acceleration of reformulation. High feedstock prices have driven operators and consumers to reformulate their products, replacing expensive chemical inputs with cheaper alternatives. The roundtable characterised this as largely a temporary, reversible measure, but there was an important caveat: some operators may choose to reformulate permanently.

This is a structural threat to demand. Once a product has been reformulated, the old formulation may never return, even if feedstock prices normalise. The new formulation may be cheaper, more efficient, or simply good enough for the application. The CEO of IMCD confirmed a significant uptick in reformulation demand, viewing it as a positive opportunity for specialty distribution — but that is a distributor's perspective. For producers of the chemicals being replaced, reformulation is a direct demand destruction.

The implication for investors is that demand forecasts based on historical patterns may be too optimistic. The reformulation wave is not just a temporary response to high prices; it is a structural shift in how customers think about their input costs. Companies that are exposed to commoditised chemicals that can be easily replaced will face sustained demand erosion. Companies that produce hard-to-replace specialty chemicals, or that can help customers reformulate while maintaining performance, will be better positioned.

## European Production Shutdowns Are Likely Irreversible — and That Changes the Competitive Landscape

The roundtable delivered a sobering warning about European production capacity. One CEO stated bluntly that European plants now shutting down are likely not coming back. The reasons are multiple: the massive expenditure required as facilities approach carbon limits, refinery closures that leave chemical plants exposed on feedstock, and the simple economics of competing with lower-cost producers elsewhere.

This is not just a supply-side issue. It has direct implications for customers. The roundtable noted that losing a principal supplier is highly disruptive, taking six to eight months to find an alternative and bringing margin erosion via a larger logistics component, alongside supply reliability risks. Customers are already experiencing this disruption. One CEO highlighted a marked decline in European sourcing, from roughly 80% three years ago to around 60% today.

The irony is that customers increasingly recognise the value of European production for European consumption. Many products are simply unavailable in Europe, prompting customers to actively ask European producers to restart production of certain chemicals. But those restarts may not happen. The capacity that is being shut down is not coming back, and the logistics and regulatory barriers to restarting are too high.

For investors, this creates a bifurcation. Companies that have maintained European production capacity — or that have invested in flexible, multi-source supply networks that can redirect production from other regions — will become more valuable as the supply base shrinks. Companies that are dependent on European production that is being shut down will face increasing supply risk and margin pressure.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Permanent the Reformulation Shift Will Be — and That Is the Most Important Strategic Uncertainty

For all its insights, the roundtable left one critical question unresolved: how much of the reformulation activity is temporary, and how much is permanent? The CEOs themselves were divided on this point. Some characterised reformulation as largely reversible, while others acknowledged that some operators may choose to reformulate permanently.

This uncertainty has profound implications for investment strategy. If reformulation is mostly temporary, then demand will recover once feedstock prices normalise, and the current disruption is a buying opportunity for exposed names. If reformulation is largely permanent, then the demand base for many chemicals has been permanently reduced, and the structural outlook is much worse.

The data to resolve this question simply does not exist yet. Reformulation takes time to implement, and the full effects may not be visible for another two to three quarters. Moreover, the permanence of reformulation will depend on the relative prices of the old and new inputs, which are themselves uncertain. If feedstock prices remain elevated, reformulation becomes more likely to stick. If they fall sharply, the incentive to revert to the old formulation increases.

Investors should watch for signs of permanence: are customers investing in new equipment or processes that lock in the new formulation? Are they signing long-term supply agreements for the new inputs? Are the new formulations being patented or protected as trade secrets? These are the signals that will distinguish temporary from structural reformulation.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Positioning

Given the complexity of the current environment, investors need a simple framework to evaluate their chemical holdings. Based on the roundtable insights, three questions can help determine whether a company is positioned to benefit or suffer from the structural reorganisation.

First, does the company have a multi-source global supply network that can redirect production across regions? The roundtable highlighted that well-integrated global suppliers like BASF have served as valuable partners to distributors throughout the conflict, helping them avoid production shortages by leveraging their multi-source networks. Companies with this capability can maintain supply reliability even as European capacity shuts down, and they can arbitrage cost differences across regions. Companies with single-source or regionally concentrated supply will face increasing disruption risk.

Second, does the company have a high share of sales from customer-specific formulations and high R&D spend? The roundtable identified a clear preference for long-term innovation partners, particularly as one moves downstream towards specialty chemicals and ingredients. Companies like Givaudan, with 96% of sales from customer-specific formulations, and Novonesis, with R&D spend of 11% of sales, are structurally advantaged. They are not easily replaced by Chinese imports, and their customers are willing to pay a premium for innovation and reliability.

Third, is the company exposed to commoditised chemicals that are subject to reformulation risk? If a company's products can be easily replaced by cheaper alternatives, it faces structural demand erosion. The reformulation wave is accelerating, and some of it will be permanent. Companies that produce hard-to-replace specialty chemicals, or that can help customers reformulate while maintaining performance, will be better positioned. Companies that produce easily substitutable intermediates or solvents face the greatest risk.

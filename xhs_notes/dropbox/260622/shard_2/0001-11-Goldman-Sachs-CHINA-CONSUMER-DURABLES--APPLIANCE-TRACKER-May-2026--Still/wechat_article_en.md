# China's Appliance Market Is Splitting in Two: Domestic Weakness Is Structural, But Export Recovery Signals a New Competitive Order

The narrative that China's appliance sector is experiencing a broad-based slowdown is misleading. The data from May 2026 tells a more nuanced and strategically important story: the market is bifurcating sharply between a domestically pressured environment that reflects structural demand exhaustion and an export-led recovery that is gaining genuine momentum. Investors who treat the 16% year-on-year decline in National Bureau of Statistics retail sales as a simple cyclical trough risk missing the deeper strategic shifts underway. The real question is not whether the domestic market will rebound—it is whether the export growth trajectory can sustain itself given the geopolitical and logistical headwinds that remain unresolved.

This is not a market waiting for a uniform recovery. It is a market where competitive advantage is being redefined along geographic and operational lines. The May data from a global investment bank's appliance tracker reveals that domestic ex-factory shipments for air conditioners missed expectations at -15% year-on-year, while export shipments beat expectations at -4% year-on-year. This divergence is not a one-month anomaly. It reflects a structural realignment where Chinese manufacturers are increasingly dependent on overseas demand to offset a domestic market that has entered a period of low-growth equilibrium.

The stakes are high. The appliance sector is a bellwether for China's consumer durables ecosystem, and its current trajectory has implications for supply chains, raw material demand, and capital allocation decisions across Asia. For serious investors, the May data is not a data point to be acknowledged—it is a signal that demands a strategic reassessment of which companies are positioned to win in a two-speed market.

## The Domestic Demand Weakness Is Not Cyclical; It Reflects a Structural Plateau in Replacement-Driven Consumption

The instinct to attribute the 16% year-on-year decline in retail sales to base effects or temporary consumer caution is understandable but incomplete. The data shows that domestic weakness has persisted across multiple product categories for months. Air conditioner domestic shipments fell -15% year-on-year in May, following -7% in April. Washing machine domestic shipments remained at -6% year-on-year in April. Refrigerator domestic sales only narrowed slightly to -4% year-on-year in April from -5% in March.

The pattern is consistent: high base effects from the trade-in subsidy program that peaked in mid-2025 are partly responsible, but the underlying issue is more fundamental. The trade-in program front-loaded demand, pulling forward purchases that would otherwise have occurred over a longer period. Once that stimulus faded, the market reverted to a lower baseline. This is not a temporary dip that will reverse sharply in the second half of 2026. It is a recalibration of the domestic market's growth potential.

The implication is strategic. Companies that have relied on domestic volume growth to drive earnings will face persistent pressure. The data shows that even leading brands are not immune. Midea's domestic AC shipments declined -35% year-on-year in May. Gree's domestic AC shipments fell -6%. Only Haier managed positive domestic AC growth at +5%. The divergence among these three giants is not random—it reflects different exposure to the trade-in cycle and different channel strategies.

For investors, the "so what" is clear: domestic-focused strategies are becoming a liability. The companies that will outperform in this environment are those that have already diversified their revenue base toward exports and emerging businesses. The domestic market is not about to collapse, but it is entering a phase where growth will be harder to achieve and more expensive to capture through price promotion. The early 618 data already shows that brands are resorting to lower prices versus Singles' Day to stimulate demand, even as input costs rise. That is a recipe for margin compression, not recovery.

## Export Recovery Is Real, But It Is Concentrated Among Players with Operational Scale and Geographic Diversification

The export data in May is the most encouraging signal in the report. Overall appliance exports accelerated to high-single-digit percentage growth year-on-year in May, beating both market expectations and the IOL production plan. AC export shipments improved to -4% year-on-year from -12% in April. Washing machine exports grew +14% year-on-year in April. Refrigerator exports turned positive to +13% year-on-year in April.

The improvement is not solely a function of easier comparisons. Consumer sentiment in the United States and Europe has shown sequential improvement. US pending home sales, a leading indicator for existing home sales, improved in May. These macro signals suggest that demand in developed markets is stabilizing, which should support further export growth in the coming months.

However, the export recovery is not uniform across companies. The data reveals a clear hierarchy. Midea's export AC shipments grew +4% year-on-year in May, outperforming the industry average of -4%. Haier's export AC shipments grew +10%. In contrast, Gree's export AC shipments declined -33%. In washing machines, Midea's export shipments grew +8% year-on-year in April, while Haier's grew +2%. In refrigerators, Midea's export shipments surged +26% year-on-year in April, and Hisense's grew +21%, while Haier's exports grew +36% from a low base.

The strategic insight here is that export success is not automatic. It requires established distribution networks, localized production capabilities, and product portfolios that can compete on value rather than just price. Companies like Midea have invested heavily in overseas factories, which means their true export exposure is higher than what IOL data captures, since they also export from non-Chinese facilities. This gives them a structural advantage in navigating trade barriers and shipping disruptions.

The question that remains open is whether the export recovery can accelerate further in the second half of 2026. The report notes that visibility around demand could improve following the signing of the US-Iran Memorandum of Understanding, but this is a geopolitical variable that is difficult to model. Shipping rates have increased in the past month, which could pressure margins for exporters that lack pricing power. The production plan still indicates mid-single-digit percentage export declines for major white goods in the second quarter, suggesting that the recovery is still fragile.

## The Pricing and Margin Dynamics Are Creating a Two-Tier Market Where Scale and Brand Equity Determine Outcomes

One of the most revealing sections of the report is the pricing analysis. Average selling prices showed mixed trends in May. Select small appliances like pressure cookers and soymilk makers saw positive ASP growth in both online and offline channels. But for core white goods like air conditioners and washing machines, ASP growth was under pressure. The report specifically notes that Midea, Haier, and Xiaomi lowered prices for entry-level products versus 2025 Singles' Day, while Gree largely maintained pricing.

This pricing behavior is not random. It reflects different strategic responses to the same environment. Companies with strong brand equity and diversified revenue streams can afford to hold prices and accept lower volume. Companies that need to defend market share or clear inventory are forced to discount. The risk is that a price war in a low-growth domestic market could erode profitability across the sector, benefiting only the most efficient operators.

The input cost environment adds another layer of complexity. Key commodities costs remained largely stable in the past month, which is neutral for margins. But shipping rates increased, which directly impacts export profitability. For companies that export a significant portion of their production, this creates a squeeze: they face rising logistics costs at the same time that domestic pricing power is weakening.

The strategic implication is that investors need to differentiate between companies based on their cost structure and pricing strategy. Companies that can pass through cost increases through product innovation or brand strength will weather this period better than those that are forced to compete on price alone. The data suggests that Midea and Haier have more flexibility than Gree, which is more dependent on the domestic AC market and has less room to maneuver on pricing.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Export Growth in a Geopolitically Fragmented World

The report provides a detailed snapshot of May data but leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important is whether the export recovery can be sustained if geopolitical tensions escalate. The report mentions that the Middle East conflict has not derailed export growth, but it does not model what happens if shipping disruptions worsen or if trade restrictions expand.

The US-Iran Memorandum of Understanding is cited as a potential source of improved visibility, but the details remain unclear. Investors need to assess whether this agreement will meaningfully reduce shipping costs and supply chain uncertainty, or whether it is a temporary diplomatic gesture that does not address underlying structural risks.

Another open question is whether the export recovery is driven by genuine demand improvement or by inventory restocking. If developed-market retailers are rebuilding inventories after a period of destocking, the export boost could be temporary. The report does not provide inventory data for US or European channels, which makes it difficult to distinguish between structural demand and cyclical restocking.

Finally, the report does not fully address the implications of rising shipping rates for export margins. If shipping costs continue to increase, the net benefit of export growth could be significantly reduced. Investors need to model scenarios where export volume growth is offset by margin compression, resulting in minimal earnings improvement.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Which Appliance Stocks Will Outperform

Based on the data and strategic insights from the report, investors should evaluate appliance companies using three criteria.

First, what is the company's revenue mix between domestic and export markets? Companies with higher export exposure are better positioned to benefit from the recovery in developed-market demand. Companies that are overly dependent on the domestic market face persistent headwinds from weak consumer sentiment and high base effects.

Second, does the company have pricing power in its core categories? The data shows that pricing pressure is intensifying in white goods, particularly in air conditioners and washing machines. Companies that can maintain or increase ASPs through product differentiation or brand strength will protect margins. Companies that are forced to discount to defend market share will see earnings erosion.

Third, does the company have operational flexibility to manage rising input costs? This includes not only commodity costs but also shipping rates and potential trade barriers. Companies with overseas factories, diversified supply chains, and strong relationships with logistics providers will be more resilient than those that rely on a single production base.

Applying this framework to the companies highlighted in the report: Midea scores well on all three criteria, with strong export growth, diversified product lines, and a history of margin discipline. Haier shows solid export performance but faces challenges in its domestic AC business. Hisense benefits from stabilizing central AC demand and a recovering revenue trajectory. Gree appears the most vulnerable, with weak export performance and limited pricing flexibility in its core domestic market.

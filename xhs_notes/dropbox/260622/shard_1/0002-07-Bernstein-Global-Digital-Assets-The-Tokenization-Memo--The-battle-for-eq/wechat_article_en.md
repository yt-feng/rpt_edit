# The Battle for Equity Tokenization Is Not About Technology — It Is About Which Business Model Captures the Settlement Layer

Tokenized real-world assets have crossed $51 billion in market capitalization, up 40% year-to-date, while the broader crypto market has declined roughly 20% over the same period. This divergence is not noise. It signals that institutional capital is not rotating into speculative digital assets but into infrastructure that promises to rebuild how securities are issued, settled, and traded. The question is no longer whether tokenization will happen. The question is which model will dominate — and who will own the economic rights that come with it.

The report from a global investment bank makes clear that the industry is now entering a phase of strategic competition, not technological experimentation. Three distinct business models are emerging: neutral infrastructure providers that enable traditional financial institutions to tokenize assets, trading platforms that use tokenization to offer multi-asset exposure on a single venue, and issuer-sponsored models that treat the blockchain as the actual settlement layer for company-issued shares. Each model carries different implications for ownership rights, regulatory risk, and revenue capture.

What makes this moment critical is the convergence of regulatory tailwinds and market demand. The SEC has proposed rescinding rules that would allow tokenized stocks to trade more freely on decentralized venues, issued a no-action letter for a tokenized equity pilot, and approved exchange proposals to trade tokenized securities. These are not incremental changes. They represent a structural shift in how the U.S. financial system may accommodate blockchain-based securities. Yet the industry still awaits full regulatory clarity, including the possibility of an "innovation exemption" that could allow onshore trading of tokenized U.S. stocks.

This is not a story about crypto replacing finance. It is a story about finance using crypto infrastructure to reclaim efficiency, reduce settlement risk, and expand access — while fighting over who controls the ledger. The battle for equity tokenization is a battle for the settlement layer of the 21st-century capital markets.

## The Neutral Infrastructure Model Wins If Trusted Intermediaries Retain Control, But It Risks Becoming a Commodity Utility

The first business model positions the tokenization platform as neutral infrastructure — a technology layer that enables traditional financial market participants to issue and manage tokenized assets without disrupting existing institutional relationships. Platforms like Securitize, which has partnered with the NYSE for its tokenized securities platform, with Computershare to enable tokenized shares for U.S. issuers, and with Jump Trading to launch DEX-style onchain trading, exemplify this approach.

The strategic logic is clear: do not compete with existing exchanges, asset managers, or transfer agents. Instead, provide the rails on which they can operate more efficiently. This model reduces friction with regulators and incumbents, because it does not challenge the fundamental structure of market ownership or custody. The platform earns fees for technology services, token design, and liquidity provision, while the traditional institution retains its relationship with the end investor.

The risk, however, is that neutral infrastructure becomes a low-margin utility. If multiple platforms offer similar tokenization capabilities, and if regulatory frameworks standardize the process, the differentiating value of any single provider diminishes. The platform that wins will be the one that can embed itself into the settlement process itself — not just the issuance process. That requires moving from being a technology vendor to being a regulated part of the market infrastructure, such as an SEC-registered transfer agent or an Alternative Trading System.

The open question is whether neutral infrastructure providers can capture enough of the economic value to justify their valuations, or whether they will be squeezed between the pricing power of incumbent exchanges and the regulatory costs of compliance. The report notes that Figure has already launched its own tokenized shares on the OPEN network, with another listing in the pipeline. That suggests that even neutral platforms may eventually need to become issuers themselves to capture full value.

## The Trading Model Captures Immediate Revenue but Surrenders Ownership Rights, Creating a Structural Vulnerability

The second model is the trading infrastructure approach, where regulated broker-dealer platforms offer trading services in tokenized stocks, primarily to non-U.S. investors. Robinhood's offering of tokenized U.S. stocks to European investors is a prominent example. A third-party sponsor buys the underlying shares, custodies them in a secured account, and issues a blockchain token against them. These tokens trade 24x7 with real-time settlement, and the platform earns incremental trading commissions.

This model generates immediate, scalable revenue. It does not require the platform to become a registered issuer or to navigate the complexities of shareholder rights. The tokens operate purely on blockchain rails, with no reconciliation with the actual shareholder registry. The third-party sponsor — not the tokenizer — remains the registered owner of the underlying shares. Tokenholders are not entitled to dividends, voting rights, or any other rights associated with share ownership.

This structural vulnerability is the model's Achilles' heel. Investors who buy tokenized equities under this model are not buying equities. They are buying a promise that the sponsor will honor the economic equivalent of the underlying shares. If the sponsor fails, if regulatory action seizes the custody account, or if the legal framework for tokenized securities changes, tokenholders have no direct claim on the issuer. They have a claim on the sponsor, which is a counterparty risk that undermines the very premise of blockchain-based settlement — that ownership is direct, transparent, and immutable.

The report highlights that recent SEC actions have been encouraging for the industry, including the proposal to rescind rules that would allow tokenized stocks to trade more freely on decentralized venues. But these actions also create a risk: if regulators eventually require tokenized securities to confer full ownership rights, the trading model may need to be restructured entirely. Platforms that have built their business on the current interpretation may face significant transition costs or even obsolescence.

The strategic implication is clear: the trading model is a bridge to a more complete tokenization regime, not a destination. The platforms that succeed will be those that can migrate from a sponsored token model to an issuer-sponsored model without losing their customer base or regulatory standing.

## The Issuer-Sponsored Model Is the Most Defensible, But It Requires Regulatory Permission That Has Not Yet Been Granted

The third model — issuer-sponsored tokenization — uses blockchain as the actual settlement layer for company-issued shares. Shareholders receive full ownership and protection rights, identical to those of traditional exchange-listed shares, while benefiting from faster and more capital-efficient settlement cycles. Players like Figure, Bullish, and Securitize are building regulated infrastructure layers for tokenized equities, using SEC-registered transfer agents, regulated Alternative Trading Systems, broker-dealers, and custody solutions.

This model is the most structurally sound because it does not create a derivative or a promise. It creates the security itself on the blockchain. The token is the share. The shareholder registry is the blockchain. Settlement is instantaneous and final because it is not a separate process — it is the process.

Bullish's acquisition of Equiniti, a traditional share registry and transfer agent, is a telling strategic move. It allows Bullish to operate a unified ledger for traditional and tokenized securities, effectively bridging the old world and the new. This is not a technology play. It is a regulatory and operational play. By owning the registry, Bullish controls the canonical record of ownership. That is the most valuable piece of the settlement infrastructure.

The challenge is that full issuer-sponsored tokenization requires regulatory clarity that does not yet exist. The industry still awaits an "innovation exemption" that could allow trading of tokenized U.S. stocks onshore. Without that exemption, issuer-sponsored tokenization remains limited to private securities, non-U.S. listings, or pilot programs. The report notes that the SEC has approved NYSE's and NASDAQ's proposals to permit trading of tokenized securities on the exchanges, but that is a permission for the exchange to offer a product, not a blanket authorization for issuers to tokenize their shares.

The strategic question is whether the issuer-sponsored model can scale before the trading model captures the market. If trading platforms achieve sufficient liquidity and network effects, they may become the default venue for tokenized equities, even if the underlying legal structure is inferior. Investors may tolerate counterparty risk for the convenience of 24x7 trading and real-time settlement. The issuer-sponsored model, while more defensible in theory, may arrive too late to capture the market.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Governance of Tokenized Equities and the Role of the Issuer

The report provides a comprehensive taxonomy of business models and regulatory developments, but it leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is the question of governance. If tokenized equities confer full ownership rights, then tokenholders must be able to exercise those rights — voting, dividend collection, participation in corporate actions — through the blockchain. How will that work in practice?

Current blockchain infrastructure is not designed for the complexity of corporate governance. Voting requires identity verification, which conflicts with the pseudonymity of many blockchain networks. Dividend distribution requires the token contract to interact with the issuer's treasury, which introduces operational risk. Corporate actions such as stock splits, mergers, and spin-offs require modifications to the token contract or the issuance of new tokens. These are solvable problems, but they require a level of standardization and coordination that does not yet exist.

The report also does not fully address the role of the issuer. In the traditional model, the issuer — the company whose shares are being traded — has a direct relationship with its shareholders through the transfer agent. In the tokenized model, that relationship is mediated by the tokenization platform. Who is responsible for maintaining the shareholder registry? Who handles lost tokens, disputed ownership, or fraudulent transfers? The report notes that Figure and Securitize are building regulated infrastructure, but it does not specify how the issuer's rights and obligations are preserved.

These are not technical questions. They are legal and operational questions that will determine whether tokenized equities can achieve mainstream adoption. The report's framework is valuable, but it stops short of providing a roadmap for governance. That is the next frontier.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Evaluating Tokenization Opportunities

For investors trying to navigate this landscape, the report provides enough information to build a decision framework based on three criteria: regulatory positioning, ownership rights, and network effects.

First, evaluate regulatory positioning. The platforms that have invested in SEC-registered transfer agents, broker-dealers, and custody solutions are better positioned for the eventual shift to issuer-sponsored tokenization. Platforms that rely on the trading model without building regulated infrastructure may face disruption if the regulatory framework tightens. The report's analysis of Bullish's Equiniti acquisition and Figure's regulated ATS suggests that these companies are betting on regulatory compliance as a moat.

Second, assess the ownership rights model. Platforms that offer tokenized equities with full shareholder rights are building a more defensible product. Platforms that offer only economic exposure through sponsored tokens are building a product that can be replicated or regulated away. The report's distinction between the trading model and the issuer-sponsored model is the most important strategic insight for investors.

Third, measure network effects. Tokenization platforms benefit from liquidity and asset holder growth. The report notes that total RWA asset holders have crossed 900,000, up 60% year-to-date. The platform that attracts the most issuers and the most investors will have a significant advantage in capturing the settlement layer. Coinbase's strategy of offering tokenized equities, equity perpetuals, pre-IPO perpetuals, and regulated crypto derivatives under one roof is a bet on network effects across asset classes.

The framework is not exhaustive, but it provides a structured way to evaluate the early "chess moves" that the report describes. The platforms that score highest on all three criteria — regulatory positioning, ownership rights, and network effects — are the ones most likely to capture the economic value of tokenization.

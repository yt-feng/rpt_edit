EN_B: Exactly right. The report raises DRAM wafer fab equipment, or WFE, for 2026 and 2027 from 44.6 and 57.1 billion to 48.6 and 62.5 billion. Customers are pulling forward tool purchases as much as they can.
EN_A: So what does that mean? Are memory makers like Micron and Samsung rushing to spend money now to meet the AI boom?
EN_B: Yes. The report says the upward revision is mostly from brownfield, meaning upgrades to existing fabs, not new greenfield construction. And it believes DRAM WFE revisions are still skewed to the upside.
EN_A: What about NAND? Is the report just as bullish on that side?
EN_B: Not exactly. The NAND WFE forecast is barely changed. But bit supply growth is revised up from 21 and 32 percent to 24 and 31 percent.
EN_A: Wait, so they produce more chips without spending more on equipment? How is that possible?
EN_B: It comes from better yield assumptions. The report now assumes higher yields, so you get more bits out of the same equipment base. But it also says NAND WFE acceleration is inevitable, just not in the near term.
EN_A: So which equipment makers benefit the most from this?
EN_B: In the short term, Applied Materials benefits the most because of its strong position in DRAM. The report even admits its downgrade of AMAT to equal-weight in May may have been slightly early.
EN_A: But they still prefer another name for the longer term?
EN_B: Correct. The analysis shows NAND WFE growth will far outpace DRAM in 2027, 53 percent versus 29 percent. And Lam Research has a better footprint in NAND, so the report maintains a relative preference for Lam over Applied.
EN_A: Are there any open questions the report doesn’t fully answer?
EN_B: Two big ones. First, the report doesn’t revise its logic WFE estimates. Could there be upside there too? Second, the report says its constructive view on Intel capex is not yet reflected. If Intel starts spending more, the whole equipment market number goes even higher.

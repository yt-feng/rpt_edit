# India Needs Its Own Foundation Models, Not Just Data Centers and Application Layers

The debate over whether India needs its own large language models has been dismissed as irrelevant by many in the country's technology establishment. The argument has been simple: build applications on top of existing foreign models, invest in data center infrastructure, and capture value at the edges. This is a comfortable narrative, but it is also a dangerous one. Recent developments have made the risk impossible to ignore. The United States has begun restricting access to frontier AI models for non-citizens, with Anthropic's latest models becoming the clearest example yet. This is not an anomaly. It is the logical next step in a pattern that has been unfolding for years, moving from critical minerals to semiconductor equipment to GPUs, and now to the models themselves. India's current AI strategy, which effectively outsources the core intelligence layer to foreign entities, is built on an assumption of continued open access that is no longer tenable.

The pattern of technology access being rationed and controlled is not new, but its application to AI marks a fundamental shift. AI is moving from a commodity to a strategic asset, becoming what one global investment bank report calls the "fighter jets" of this era. Bleeding-edge models will increasingly be guardrailed, treated not as software-as-a-service products but as critical national resources. For India, which has been telling itself that renting compute to power foreign LLMs and earning rent on data centers constitutes a sound AI strategy, this shift carries profound implications. There is genuine value in participating in the ecosystem through infrastructure build-out and application layers. But the core assumption that access to frontier models will remain open and equal is now in question. India needs to understand the risks embedded in effectively outsourcing its core AI capabilities.

## India's Absence of a Native Foundation Model Is Structural, Not a Deliberate Strategic Choice

It may seem counterintuitive that a country generating vast amounts of data, the very fuel for training global models, has not produced a competitive large-scale GenAI system. India is the source of enormous data flows that power models built elsewhere, yet it has not harnessed this advantage. This absence of an Indian "DeepSeek moment" is not the result of a thoughtful decision to focus on applications. It is structural, rooted in the historical shape of India's technology ecosystem.

India's tech sector has long been services-led, built on low-cost talent that fine-tunes software created by global giants. The country lacks the consumer-facing platforms in search, social media, and messaging that generate the rich, organized datasets critical for training advanced AI. China built walls and created large-scale platforms that provided cash flows, talent pools, and data to build AI models. India stayed open, and global platforms became the rails on which its digital economy runs. The IT services model, where value is captured through labor arbitrage rather than intellectual property ownership, further reinforced the focus on the application layer. Leaders of many Indian institutions have argued that the country does not need its own LLMs and can focus on applications. These views are more reflective of the path India has taken to reach its current position than a deliberate strategic choice about the future of AI.

## The Cost of Dependence on Foreign Foundation Models Extends Beyond Geopolitical Risk to Competitive Disadvantage

The most obvious risk is geopolitical disruption. Imagine India's core intelligence layer, from enterprise software to defense and space applications, powered by foreign LLMs. A geopolitical event could curtail access overnight, bringing critical systems to a halt. But there is a more likely and equally concerning scenario: India operates one or two generations behind, perpetually catching up. Applications built on older models struggle to compete with global offerings. An Indian IT giant with deep talent pools could lose out to a young US SaaS firm bootstrapped by amateur coders with access to cutting-edge models.

The real vulnerability lies with large-scale, horizontal GenAI applications built on external models. These applications, which span everything from customer service to content generation to enterprise workflows, depend on continuous access to the latest model capabilities. If India is locked out of frontier models, these applications will gradually become uncompetitive. There is, however, a more resilient path. Building domain-specific LLMs on proprietary data and layering AI solutions on top creates defensibility. The datasets that power India's industrial and healthcare sectors, for example, are hard to replicate and deeply contextual. Models trained on these datasets do not need to be the largest or most advanced. They need to be the most relevant. This path does not eliminate dependence, but it shifts the balance of risk.

## India's Technology Dependence Runs Far Deeper Than AI, Making the Current Moment Both a Risk and an Opportunity

Step back and examine the full stack. From hardware and core IT components to operating systems and enterprise software, India sits downstream across almost every layer. The country depends on US companies for CPUs, GPUs, mobile SoCs, operating systems, office suites, creative tools, cloud infrastructure, social media, and e-commerce. Indian providers exist in many of these categories, but none operate at global scale. The pattern is consistent and deep. This is not just about apps or platforms. It is about systemic dependence, and overwhelmingly, that dependence points in one direction: the United States.

There have been attempts by Indian companies to break through in pockets, to build credible vertical platforms, but most have struggled to scale, out-competed by global incumbents with deeper moats, better products, and capital at scale. The internet era was the clearest example. India stayed open, and global platforms quietly became the infrastructure on which its digital economy runs. If history is any guide, the path forward for AI seems obvious: allow the same playbook to repeat, with global companies building, owning, and monetizing India's AI layer while domestic players participate on the margins.

But something feels different this time. AI is not just another application layer. It is emerging as the underlying rail for everything from enterprise workflows to consumer interactions. And importantly, it is still early. Unlike past technology cycles where India entered late, this is a moment where the contours are still being shaped. The cost of missing this wave could be far higher than missing the internet wave. Waiting 20 to 30 years, as India effectively did in earlier tech cycles, could entrench dependence in ways that are far harder to reverse, locking value capture outside the country for decades.

## The Policy Framework for India's AI Future Involves an Imperfect Trade-Off Between Access and Autonomy

Recognizing the importance of sovereignty is one thing. Executing on it is another. The policy levers are limited, the competitive gap is large, and the alternatives are far from straightforward. Two strategic levers stand out, both imperfect.

The first approach is to restrict or pace access to global AI models while channeling significant capital and talent into building India-native LLM capabilities. This is the path China has taken, creating walled ecosystems that have produced domestic champions. But India's technology landscape is far more integrated with global systems. Ringfencing AI for domestic firms may lack practicality in a globally integrated technology landscape. The second approach is to mandate or incentivize localization, requiring foreign firms to build and operate India-based AI stacks that are insulated from geopolitical controls. This preserves access while creating structural safeguards. But it also risks creating a two-tier system where India gets access to models that are one or two generations behind, or where compliance costs create friction that slows adoption.

Neither option is clean. Both involve trade-offs between speed of access and depth of autonomy. But they frame the core policy question: does India prioritize the immediate benefits of using the best available models, or does it accept short-term friction in exchange for long-term strategic positioning? The answer is not obvious, and it will depend on how policymakers weigh the risks of dependence against the costs of decoupling.

## India's Real Opportunity Lies in Owning Domain-Specific Datasets and Building Specialized Models, Not in Chasing Frontier LLMs

The report points to a third path that is neither full dependence nor full autonomy, but something more pragmatic. India's real opportunity lies in owning and leveraging its rich, domain-specific datasets, especially across sectors like industrials and healthcare. These datasets can become the foundation for building defensible, smaller, specialized LLMs by Indian technology companies. On top of these specialized models, a wide range of applications can be developed. Importantly, these applications need not be confined to the digital world. They can extend into the physical economy, powering use cases such as training industrial robots, including next-generation humanoid systems where industrial context-specific data is more critical than the use of the latest GenAI model.

The core idea is simple but powerful: retain and control access to high-quality, hard-to-replicate vertical data. By limiting unrestricted access to such datasets for global platforms, India can create pockets of defensibility where domestic players can build meaningful capabilities. Over time, these can evolve into scalable solutions within India and eventually be exported as global offerings. Realistically, this path may not produce trillion-dollar tech giants in the near term. But that is not the only metric of success. Even a shift toward building credible, globally relevant platforms anchored in proprietary data would expand India's share of value creation beyond IT services and gradually reposition it within the global tech ecosystem. More importantly, it sets the foundation for the future. As talent and capital begin to recognize the viability of an India-led tech stack, it could trigger a virtuous cycle where the next generation of entrepreneurs builds not just for India, but from India.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Execution Gap Between Strategy and Reality

The report provides a clear diagnostic of India's dependence and a compelling argument for why the status quo is risky. It also outlines a framework for policy choices and a potential path forward through domain-specific models. But it leaves several critical questions unanswered, and these are the questions that will determine whether the analysis translates into action.

First, how does India actually build the talent pipeline for foundational model development when its entire technology ecosystem has been optimized for services and application layers? The IT services model has produced world-class engineers for integration, testing, and maintenance, but it has not produced the research depth needed for frontier AI work. Building that pipeline requires changes in education, research funding, and industry incentives that go far beyond any single policy lever.

Second, can India realistically limit access to its domain-specific datasets without creating friction that slows its own AI adoption? The datasets that are most valuable, in healthcare, industrials, and government, are often fragmented, poorly digitized, and controlled by multiple stakeholders with different incentives. Creating a unified strategy for data access and control is a coordination problem that has no easy solution.

Third, what is the timeline? The report argues that AI is still early, but the pace of development is accelerating rapidly. If India takes five years to build its specialized model capabilities, will the global frontier have moved so far that the gap is insurmountable? The report implies that domain-specific models can be effective even with older GenAI technology, but this assumption needs to be tested against the rapid improvement in general-purpose models.

These questions do not invalidate the report's thesis. They highlight the gap between strategic insight and operational reality. The answers will determine whether India's AI strategy becomes a case study in successful navigation or a cautionary tale about missed opportunities.

## A Decision Framework for Readers: How to Assess Your Own Exposure to India's AI Dependence

For business leaders, investors, and policymakers reading this analysis, the question is not just whether India needs its own foundation models. The question is how the current dependence structure affects your own position. Here is a framework for thinking about your exposure.

First, assess your reliance on foreign foundation models for core operations. If your enterprise software, customer-facing applications, or internal workflows depend on APIs from frontier model providers, ask yourself what happens if access is restricted or if you are forced to operate on older model versions. The risk is not just geopolitical disruption. It is also competitive: if your competitors in the US or China have access to better models, your cost structure and product quality will suffer.

Second, evaluate your data moat. Do you own proprietary, hard-to-replicate datasets that could form the basis for specialized models? If yes, you have a defensible asset that can be leveraged regardless of what happens with frontier models. If no, your value proposition is more exposed to the commoditization of AI capabilities.

Third, consider your talent strategy. Are you investing in building AI research and development capabilities, or are you relying on the services model of fine-tuning and integrating foreign models? The services model has been profitable, but it does not create the intellectual property or competitive moat that will matter in the next decade.

Fourth, think about your policy engagement. The choices India makes over the next two to three years will shape the AI landscape for decades. Are you engaging with policymakers to shape those choices, or are you waiting to see what happens? The report makes clear that the default path is continued dependence. Changing that path requires active intervention.

Finally, calibrate your timeline. The report argues that AI is still early, but early is relative. If you are making investment decisions with a five-year horizon, the current dependence structure may be acceptable. If you are making decisions with a ten- to twenty-year horizon, the risks are much higher. The cost of waiting to see how things play out could be significant.

# The Next Bottleneck Has Not Yet Shifted: Why MLCC Stocks Still Have Momentum Room and What Will Signal Their Peak

The conventional narrative around Japan's MLCC stocks is that they are riding a wave of AI-driven demand, and that their elevated valuations are simply a reflection of fundamental earnings growth. This view is incomplete, and it is dangerously passive. The real story is not about demand at all; it is about the position of these companies within a supply bottleneck structure that has historically determined when excess profits appear, how long they last, and when they collapse. The current rally in Murata Manufacturing and Taiyo Yuden is not an anomaly. It is a repeat of a pattern that played out during the Emerging Markets boom of the mid-2000s, and understanding that pattern is the only way to know when to hold, when to add, and when to exit.

The critical insight from a recent global investment bank report is that the AI boom is structurally analogous to the EM boom, but with one crucial difference: we are not yet at the stage where the bottleneck has shifted to the next group of stocks. In the EM boom, the collapse of the first wave of bottleneck stocks—companies like Sumitomo Metal Mining, Mitsui Mining, and Shin-Etsu Chemical—was not caused by a loss of competitive advantage or a deterioration in their oligopolistic positions. It was caused by a cascade effect. As demand surged, the supply bottleneck moved upstream, from electronic components to bulk commodities and ocean freight rates. Speculative capital, which had been chasing the first set of bottleneck stocks, simultaneously rotated into the new bottleneck sectors, leaving the original group to collapse under its own momentum-driven fragility.

Today, MLCC-related stocks occupy the same structural position that those EM-era bottleneck stocks did. They possess oligopolistic market positions and a supply structure that makes rapid production increases difficult. When demand spikes, their profits grow faster than their sales volume. This is not a sign of superior management or technology; it is a function of their position in the supply chain. The question is not whether MLCC stocks are overvalued. The question is whether the bottleneck has already shifted. The evidence suggests it has not.

This article will argue that MLCC stocks still have room to run, but that the nature of their current support is fragile, driven by momentum and beta rather than quality. It will lay out a framework for understanding when the bottleneck is likely to shift, and it will identify the specific signals that investors must watch to avoid being caught in the next simultaneous collapse.

## The AI Boom Has Not Yet Entered the Stage Where Bottlenecks Cascade Upstream

The most important analytical contribution of the report is its use of a historical analogy to map the current AI boom onto the EM boom of the mid-2000s. The report's quantitative team has measured the similarity between the two periods using an embedding method that aligns stock price patterns via dynamic time warping. The results are striking: the current behavior of MLCC stocks closely mirrors the behavior of EM-era bottleneck stocks during the sixth stage of that boom. The EM boom ultimately had nine stages before it collapsed, with the seventh and eighth stages marked by the shift of bottlenecks to freight rates and bulk commodities. If the AI boom follows a similar trajectory, we are still several stages away from the peak.

This matters because it shifts the analytical question from "are MLCC stocks expensive?" to "where is the bottleneck in the supply chain?" The report makes a compelling case that the bottleneck remains within the current stock group, including MLCCs, and that upstream sectors such as semiconductors, manufacturing equipment, HBM, and CoWoS are still on an expansion trend. As long as the bottleneck remains concentrated in these areas, the excess profit generation mechanism for MLCC companies will persist. The risk is not that these companies will suddenly lose their competitive edge; the risk is that the bottleneck will migrate to a different part of the supply chain, and speculative capital will follow.

The implication is clear: investors who treat MLCC stocks as a simple AI demand play are missing the structural logic of the market. The real driver is not the volume of AI chips being produced; it is the position of the bottleneck within the network of production. Until that bottleneck shifts, the momentum behind MLCC stocks is likely to continue, albeit with volatility.

## The Current Support for MLCC Stocks Comes from Momentum and Beta, Not Quality, and That Is Both a Strength and a Weakness

The report's factor analysis of the electrical equipment sector and the two MLCC-related companies reveals a telling pattern. Momentum and Beta factors are notably high, while Quality is low. This means that stock prices are currently being supported not by the quality of earnings or by fundamental improvement in business models, but by price trends and market correlation. This is the classic signature of a momentum-driven rally.

For short-term investors, this combination is actually attractive. The report estimates that high Momentum and high Beta, when combined with improving profit margin momentum, produce positive expected returns over the short term. The core buyers are trend-following strategies that enter positions based on signs of earnings improvement. The report's data suggests that these trend-followers still have room to build up their positions, especially if the improvement in profit margin momentum is sustained.

But this is also the source of fragility. When the market cools, stocks with high Beta and low Quality tend to correct rapidly and severely. The same momentum that drives prices up in a rising market can drive them down just as quickly when the trend reverses. This is not a hypothetical risk; it is a structural feature of the current positioning.

The report's analysis of global active investment trusts provides a more nuanced picture. These long-term investors, which focus on developed markets, have maintained a neutral to slightly underweight position in the Technology, Hardware & Equipment sector relative to the benchmark. This allocation has been largely stable for the past six months. The implication is that the patient, long-term capital that could provide a floor during a correction has not yet entered the market. The report suggests that these investors are likely to begin buying once a price correction has cooled the overheated market. This creates a pattern of support that is delayed rather than immediate. The market will have to fall before it finds a stable base of long-term buyers.

## The Rotation of Global Active Capital Away from China and Toward Japan Is a Structural Tailwind That Has Not Yet Fully Materialized

One of the most underappreciated dynamics in the current market is the ongoing rotation of global active investment trusts away from Chinese equities and toward Japanese, South Korean, and Taiwanese markets. The report documents this trend in detail, showing that the regional allocation of these funds has been shifting steadily. This is not a tactical move; it is part of a broader strategic rebalancing that reflects geopolitical risk, supply chain diversification, and relative valuation.

For Japanese electrical equipment stocks, including MLCC-related companies, this rotation is a significant tailwind. The report notes that growth sectors in Japan are well-positioned to benefit from this shift, particularly from a relative perspective. As long-term investors reduce their China exposure, they are naturally looking for alternative growth markets, and Japan's technology sector is a logical destination.

However, the report also shows that this rotation has not yet fully played out. The sector allocation to Japanese technology stocks by global active funds remains neutral to slightly underweight. This suggests that there is still room for additional inflows from long-term investors, particularly if the momentum in MLCC stocks is maintained. The risk is that these investors will wait for a correction before committing capital, which means that the current rally is being driven primarily by short-term momentum traders rather than by patient, long-term capital.

This distinction is critical for understanding the risk profile of MLCC stocks. A rally supported by long-term investors is more durable and less prone to sudden reversals. A rally supported by momentum traders is more fragile and more likely to experience sharp corrections. The current rally falls into the latter category, which means that investors should be prepared for volatility.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: How Will the Bottleneck Shift, and What Will Trigger It?

The report's analysis is rigorous and insightful, but it deliberately leaves one question open: what will cause the bottleneck to shift? The EM boom analogy provides a template, but it does not provide a precise trigger. In the EM boom, the shift was driven by a combination of rising commodity prices and ocean freight rates, which created new bottlenecks in bulk commodities and shipping. The subprime mortgage crisis was the trigger, but the underlying cause was the migration of the bottleneck.

For the AI boom, the equivalent trigger is unclear. Will it be a surge in energy costs that creates a bottleneck in power supply for data centers? Will it be a shortage of specialized manufacturing equipment that shifts the bottleneck from components to capital goods? Or will it be a geopolitical event that disrupts supply chains in a way that creates new bottlenecks in different parts of the network?

The report does not attempt to answer this question, and that is appropriate. The purpose of the analysis is to provide a framework for understanding the current position, not to predict the future. But for investors, this is the most important question to monitor. The report provides three factors to watch: the position of the upstream semiconductor and manufacturing equipment cycles, the allocation of long-term overseas capital, and the rise of the next bottleneck-related sector. These are the signals that will indicate when the cascade is about to begin.

The absence of a clear answer is not a weakness of the report; it is a reflection of the inherent uncertainty in the market. But it does mean that investors who rely solely on this framework must remain vigilant and be prepared to act quickly when the signals change.

## A Decision Framework for MLCC Investors: Three Conditions to Monitor Before Taking Profits

Based on the report's analysis, a disciplined investor can construct a decision framework for managing positions in MLCC-related stocks. The framework has three conditions that should be monitored simultaneously.

First, watch the upstream semiconductor and manufacturing equipment cycles. As long as these sectors remain on an expansion trend, the bottleneck is likely to remain within the current stock group. If these cycles begin to peak or show signs of slowing, it could indicate that the bottleneck is about to shift.

Second, track the allocation of long-term overseas capital to Japanese technology stocks. The report shows that global active funds are still neutral to slightly underweight in this sector. If this allocation begins to move toward overweight, it would provide a more stable base of support for MLCC stocks. If it remains neutral or moves toward underweight, the current rally is more fragile.

Third, identify the next bottleneck-related sector. The report suggests that the next bottleneck could emerge in upstream sectors such as semiconductors, manufacturing equipment, HBM, or CoWoS. If these sectors begin to show signs of excess profit generation similar to what MLCC stocks are currently experiencing, it could signal that speculative capital is about to rotate.

The decision to take profits should be based on a combination of these three conditions, not on any single factor. If all three conditions point toward a shift, it is time to exit. If they remain stable, the rally is likely to continue.

This framework is not a guarantee of success, but it provides a structured approach to a market that is driven by structural forces rather than by fundamentals alone. The report's value lies not in its predictions but in its identification of the underlying logic that drives the market.

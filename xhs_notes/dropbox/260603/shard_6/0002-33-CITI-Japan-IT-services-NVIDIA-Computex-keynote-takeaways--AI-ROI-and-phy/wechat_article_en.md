# AI ROI Is the Real Bottleneck for Enterprise IT Services, Not Model Capability

The enterprise AI conversation has been dominated by model size, training compute, and benchmark scores. Yet the most pressing question for IT services firms and their clients is no longer whether AI can perform complex tasks, but whether the economics of deploying AI agents make sense. A recent Computex keynote from NVIDIA made this tension explicit, and the implications for Japan's IT services sector are far more strategic than most market commentary suggests. The core argument of this analysis is that the AI phase transition from training through inference to agents will fundamentally reshape the business model of system integrators, shifting their value creation from building systems to designing, managing, and securing enterprise AI agent ecosystems. The firms that understand this shift before their clients do will capture the next cycle of growth.

The timing of this transition matters because enterprise AI adoption is reaching an inflection point where cost scrutiny is intensifying. Major technology buyers are no longer asking whether AI works. They are asking whether it pays for itself. Uber's COO recently stated that it remains unclear whether productivity gains sufficiently justify AI token costs. Amazon is shifting from maximizing token usage to optimizing token usage. These are not marginal observations; they signal a fundamental reassessment of AI spending discipline across the enterprise landscape. For IT services firms, this creates both a threat and an opportunity. The threat is that enterprises may slow AI adoption if the ROI case remains ambiguous. The opportunity is that system integrators can become the architects of cost-efficient agent design, a role that did not exist three years ago.

What makes this moment particularly significant is that NVIDIA itself has entered the model provider space with a clear message. The announcement of the Nemotron 3 Ultra model as an open, cost-effective solution for enterprise agent development directly addresses the ROI concern. This is not a minor product release. It is a strategic signal that the company recognizes the enterprise adoption bottleneck is economic, not technical. The implication for IT services firms is that their traditional role of system development will increasingly give way to enterprise AI agent design, management, and security. The question is not whether this transformation will happen, but which firms are positioned to lead it.

## The Enterprise AI ROI Crisis Is the Catalyst That Will Redefine the IT Services Business Model

The most important takeaway from the Computex keynote is not about the Vera Rubin generation or physical AI, though both are significant. It is about the explicit acknowledgment that enterprise AI adoption faces a cost justification problem that model improvements alone cannot solve. For the past two years, the dominant narrative has been that larger models and more compute would unlock transformative productivity gains. That narrative is now being stress-tested by enterprise CFOs who see API costs rising as model performance improves. OpenAI API and other API costs are increasing, and while some of this reflects unavoidable compute price pass-through, a growing number of enterprises can no longer justify these expenditures based on measurable returns.

This cost tension creates a structural opportunity for IT services firms. When enterprises cannot justify generic API costs, they must find ways to design dedicated, efficient agents for specific tasks. This is precisely the domain where system integrators have comparative advantage. Unlike cloud providers or model companies, IT services firms understand the specific workflows, data structures, and compliance requirements of their clients. They can design agents that use the right model for the right task, balancing cost and capability. The Nemotron 3 Ultra model, as an open and cost-effective option, gives these firms a tool that can dramatically improve the economics of agent deployment.

The strategic implication is that the IT services business model is shifting from project-based system development to ongoing agent lifecycle management. Enterprises will not buy agents as one-time implementations. They will need continuous monitoring, optimization, and security management as agent ecosystems scale. This recurring revenue model is far more attractive than traditional system integration, but it requires capabilities most IT services firms do not currently possess. The firms that invest in agent design frameworks, cost optimization tools, and security protocols will create durable competitive advantages.

## The Transition from Training to Inference to Agents Creates a New Layer of Enterprise Architecture That IT Services Firms Must Own

The AI industry narrative has focused on three phases: training massive foundation models, running inference on those models, and deploying agents that act on behalf of users. The Computex keynote made clear that NVIDIA sees agent AI as the current frontier and physical AI as the next. For IT services firms, the agent phase is the most consequential because it introduces a new layer of enterprise architecture that sits between the model and the business process.

In the training phase, IT services firms had limited relevance. Training is a capital-intensive activity dominated by cloud providers and hyperscalers. In the inference phase, firms gained some relevance through managed inference services and model deployment. But the agent phase fundamentally changes the equation. Agents require orchestration, task decomposition, model selection, error handling, and security governance. These are not model problems. They are enterprise architecture problems. And enterprise architecture is the traditional domain of system integrators.

The key insight is that enterprises will need to differentiate model usage based on task difficulty and importance. High-stakes financial calculations may require a more expensive, more capable model. Routine customer interactions may be better served by a smaller, cheaper model. Determining which model to use for which task, and managing the routing logic, will become a core IT services function. This is not a trivial technical challenge. It requires deep understanding of business processes, data sensitivity, and cost trade-offs. The firms that develop expertise in this area will become indispensable partners to enterprise clients.

Furthermore, the agent phase introduces security and governance requirements that are fundamentally different from traditional IT security. Agents act autonomously. They make decisions based on context. They interact with external systems and data sources. This creates attack surfaces that do not exist in traditional software architectures. IT services firms that can provide agent security frameworks will capture significant value, as enterprises will be reluctant to deploy agents at scale without robust guardrails.

## Physical AI Will Extend the IT Services Opportunity Beyond the Data Center into Industrial and Operational Environments

The Computex keynote concluded with a strong emphasis on physical AI, including robotics and autonomous driving initiatives. NVIDIA's focus on physical AI as the next phase after agent AI has direct implications for IT services firms, particularly those with industrial and manufacturing clients. The connection between physical AI and IT services is less obvious than the agent opportunity, but it may prove equally transformative.

Physical AI requires the integration of rule-based software with AI models. For specific task processing, such as tax calculations or manufacturing quality checks, using rule-based software is often more efficient than having an AI model verify the methodology each time. This hybrid approach, combining deterministic rules with probabilistic AI, is precisely the kind of systems integration challenge that IT services firms are built to solve. The firms that understand both the software and the operational domain will have a significant advantage.

For Japan's IT services sector, the physical AI opportunity is particularly relevant given the country's industrial base. Fujitsu, which has a business partnership with NVIDIA, is directly positioned to benefit from this trend. The firm possesses business-specific software, data, and expertise that become more valuable as physical AI advances. The implication is that IT services firms with deep industry domain knowledge, particularly in manufacturing, logistics, and industrial automation, will see growing demand for physical AI integration services.

The second-order implication is that physical AI will drive demand for edge computing infrastructure, real-time data processing, and operational technology security. These are areas where traditional IT services firms have limited expertise, but where the opportunity is substantial. Firms that invest in building physical AI capabilities, including robotics integration, sensor data management, and industrial cybersecurity, will be well-positioned for the next cycle of enterprise technology spending.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Competitive Dynamics Between IT Services Firms and Software Platforms in the Agent Era

While the analysis from the Computex keynote provides valuable insights into the technology direction and the IT services opportunity, it leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important of these is how the competitive dynamics will evolve between IT services firms and enterprise software platforms that are also building agent capabilities.

The keynote mentioned that software companies building agent AI based on NVIDIA include ServiceNow, Palantir, SAP, and CrowdStrike. These are not small players. They are dominant enterprise platforms with existing customer relationships, extensive data assets, and significant AI investments. If these platforms can deliver agent capabilities that are tightly integrated with their existing products, they may capture much of the value that IT services firms are targeting. The question is whether enterprises will prefer platform-native agents or custom-designed agents from system integrators.

The answer likely depends on the complexity and specificity of the use case. For standardized processes within a platform's domain, such as IT service management with ServiceNow or enterprise resource planning with SAP, platform-native agents may be sufficient and more cost-effective. For complex, cross-platform workflows that require integration across multiple systems, custom agents designed by IT services firms may be necessary. The competitive boundary between these two scenarios is not yet clear, and it will evolve as both platforms and service firms develop their capabilities.

Another unanswered question is the pricing model for agent services. Traditional system integration is project-based, with fees tied to time and materials. Agent lifecycle management suggests a subscription or consumption-based model, but the industry has not yet converged on standard pricing. IT services firms that can articulate clear ROI metrics for agent deployments will have an advantage in pricing negotiations. Those that cannot may find themselves competing on cost, which is a losing strategy.

## A Decision Framework for IT Services Firms Evaluating the Agent Opportunity

For IT services firms considering how to position for the agent era, the following framework provides a structured approach to strategic decision-making. The framework is organized around three dimensions: capability investment, go-to-market strategy, and partnership architecture.

On capability investment, firms should assess their current strengths in four areas: model selection and cost optimization, agent orchestration and workflow design, security and governance frameworks, and domain-specific business process knowledge. Most firms will have gaps in at least two of these areas. The critical decision is whether to build capabilities organically, acquire them, or partner with specialized providers. Given the pace of change, partnerships with model providers like NVIDIA may offer faster time-to-market than organic development, but they also create dependency risks.

On go-to-market strategy, firms must decide whether to target specific verticals or offer horizontal agent services. The vertical approach aligns with the domain expertise advantage that many IT services firms already possess. A firm that understands healthcare compliance, for example, can design agents that navigate regulatory requirements more effectively than a horizontal competitor. The horizontal approach offers larger addressable markets but requires competing with platform providers on scale and efficiency. For most firms, a vertical focus with selective horizontal capabilities is the prudent path.

On partnership architecture, firms need to evaluate their relationships with model providers, cloud platforms, and enterprise software vendors. The NVIDIA partnership is clearly important, particularly for firms like Fujitsu with existing relationships. But firms should also consider partnerships with specialist agent frameworks, security providers, and industry-specific software vendors. The goal is to build an ecosystem that allows the firm to offer end-to-end agent solutions without owning every component.

The most important strategic question that each firm must answer is whether it will be an agent designer, an agent operator, or both. The design role involves building the agent architecture and workflow. The operator role involves managing agents in production, including monitoring, optimization, and security. These roles require different capabilities and have different margin profiles. Design work is higher margin but more project-based. Operations work is lower margin but provides recurring revenue. The optimal strategy may be to offer both, using design engagements as a pathway to long-term operations contracts.

# The Next Frontier in Frontline NSCLC Is Not a Single Drug—It Is a Strategic Reclassification of First-Line Therapy

The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) 2026 lung cancer data have done more than generate new efficacy signals. They have forced a fundamental reconsideration of how first-line non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) should be organized. For years, the frontline treatment algorithm has been binary: PD-L1 high patients receive Keytruda monotherapy, and PD-L1 low patients receive Keytruda plus chemotherapy. That framework is now under pressure from two distinct mechanistic advances—TROP2-directed antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and PD-1xVEGF bispecific antibodies—each claiming to redefine the standard of care in different histologic and biomarker segments. The strategic question for investors is not which molecule wins. It is whether the next standard of care will be a single agent or a segmented portfolio, and whether the clinical data from China will replicate globally.

Two key opinion leaders (KOLs) from Johns Hopkins and Stanford, interviewed by a global investment bank, provided granular insight into how practicing oncologists are interpreting the data. Their responses reveal a profession that is simultaneously impressed by the new data and cautious about its applicability. The KOLs did not declare a winner. Instead, they mapped out a decision tree that separates squamous from non-squamous, high from low PD-L1, and China from global. This article argues that the most important insight from the KOL discussions is not the hazard ratio of any single trial, but the emergence of a dual-pathway treatment paradigm that will require investors to re-evaluate their assumptions about market share, sequencing, and the value of global confirmatory trials.

## The KOLs See sac-TMT as a Potential Home Run, but Only If Global OS Data Confirms the PFS Signal

The KOLs were notably enthusiastic about Merck and Kelun's sac-TMT (sacituzumab tirumotecan), the TROP2-directed ADC evaluated in the OptiTROP-Lung05 trial. One KOL described the progression-free survival (PFS) hazard ratio as "one of the few drugs that he has seen show a PFS HR that significant." This is not faint praise from an academic oncologist who sees approximately 200 NSCLC patients annually and focuses on early-phase immunotherapy trials. The KOLs also highlighted the low discontinuation rate of approximately 5%, which they described as "amazing" in the context of ADC toxicity management.

Yet the praise came with a critical caveat. The KOLs acknowledged that the Keytruda monotherapy control arm in the trial may have underperformed relative to real-world expectations. This raises the question of whether the observed PFS benefit is truly a home run or a double that looks like a home run because of a weak pitcher. One KOL provided a baseball analogy for interpreting hazard ratios: better than 0.85 is a single, 0.7 to 0.85 is a double, 0.5 to 0.7 is a triple, and below 0.5 is a home run. For sac-TMT to displace the standard of care, he would look for a PFS HR of 0.7 to 0.8 with response rates of 65% to 70%. The current data are in that range, but the KOLs stressed that final overall survival (OS) data will be the deciding factor. In oncology, PFS can be swayed by control arm performance, but OS is the ultimate arbiter.

The strategic implication is clear. Sac-TMT has demonstrated that TROP2-directed ADCs can achieve meaningful efficacy with manageable toxicity in a Chinese population. But the global Phase 3 trials will be the proving ground. If sac-TMT replicates its China data in a global population, it could challenge AstraZeneca's Datroway, which currently holds the developmental lead and has the advantage of FDA approval and established clinical experience. The KOLs noted that Datroway's existing approval simplifies physician adoption, but they also acknowledged that sac-TMT may have superior efficacy and safety based on China data. This creates a high-stakes race where the winner is not yet determined, but the loser may be any company that assumes its lead is insurmountable.

## The HARMONi-6 OS Data Are Meaningful, but the Age>65 Subgroup Debate Underscores the Risk of China-Only Generalization

The KOLs were positive on the overall survival benefit seen with Summit Therapeutics and Akeso's ivonescimab (ivo) in the Phase 3 HARMONi-6 study. The OS hazard ratio of 0.66, with relatively little drop-off from the PFS HR of 0.60, suggests a durable benefit that is unusual in frontline NSCLC. The KOLs noted that the data support the PD-1xVEGF bispecific class and that ivonescimab could potentially be better than Keytruda.

However, the discussion took a sharp turn when the KOLs addressed the plenary debate regarding the perceived lack of benefit in patients over 65. This subgroup analysis has become a focal point for skeptics who question whether the China-only data can be extrapolated to a global population. The KOLs acknowledged the debate and cautioned that global confirmation is necessary. They also highlighted regional pathophysiological differences in NSCLC, particularly related to smoking and other environmental factors, which could affect treatment response.

The age>65 debate is not a minor statistical quibble. It goes to the heart of whether ivonescimab's mechanism of action—inhibiting both PD-1 and VEGF—is truly differentiated or whether it is benefiting from a favorable patient mix in China. Older patients typically have more comorbidities and may be less able to tolerate the combination of immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy. If the global Phase 3 HARMONi-3 study fails to show a benefit in older patients, the drug's label could be restricted, and its commercial potential would be significantly diminished.

The KOLs believe the final PFS results from the squamous HARMONi-3 cohort in the second half of 2026 are likely to be positive, citing the totality of positive ivonescimab data across indications, including colorectal cancer data shared at ASCO. But they also noted that the readthrough from squamous to non-squamous NSCLC is modest, as squamous disease is more homogeneous and more prevalent in smokers. This suggests that ivonescimab's path to becoming a frontline standard may be limited to squamous histology, at least initially. For investors, the key question is whether the global HARMONi-3 study will confirm the China data or reveal a narrower therapeutic window.

## The Emerging Treatment Paradigm Separates Squamous from Non-Squamous, with ADCs and Bispecifics Occupying Distinct Niches

One of the most insightful observations from the KOL discussions was the implicit segregation of treatment pathways by histology. The KOLs see ADCs as more appropriate for non-squamous disease and PD-1xVEGF bispecifics as more appropriate for squamous disease. This is not a minor detail. It suggests that the frontline NSCLC market may not be a winner-take-all contest but rather a segmented landscape where different mechanisms dominate different patient populations.

The logic is mechanistic. Squamous NSCLC is more homogeneous and more strongly associated with smoking, which may make it more responsive to VEGF inhibition. Non-squamous disease is more heterogeneous and includes patients with actionable genetic alterations, where ADCs may have a broader therapeutic window. The KOLs also noted that the toxicity profiles differ: ADCs carry risks of stomatitis and interstitial lung disease, while bispecifics carry bleeding risks, particularly in patients with blood vessel encasement. These safety considerations will influence physician prescribing behavior, especially in older or frailer patients.

This segmentation has profound implications for trial design and commercial strategy. Companies developing ADCs for NSCLC will need to demonstrate superiority in non-squamous populations, while bispecific developers will focus on squamous. The overlapping territory—PD-L1 high patients who are eligible for either approach—will be the battleground, but the KOLs suggested that even in that segment, histology will drive the decision. One KOL explicitly stated that he sees ADCs more for non-squamous disease and PD-1xVEGF for squamous. This is not a prediction of future outcomes but a description of current clinical thinking.

The strategic takeaway for investors is that the value of a drug candidate depends not only on its efficacy but also on its positioning within this emerging framework. A drug that tries to be everything to everyone may end up being nothing to anyone. The companies that will succeed are those that design their trials and commercial strategies around a specific histologic and biomarker niche.

## The Report Raises More Questions Than It Answers About the Role of Novel Combinations and the Threat from Biosimilars

The KOLs highlighted several emerging approaches that are not yet reflected in the current data but could reshape the treatment landscape in the next three to five years. These include trispecific antibodies (PD-1xCTLA-4xVEGF) and RAS inhibitors, which could be combined with immunotherapies or ADCs. The mention of CStone Pharmaceuticals' CS2009, a trispecific molecule, underscores the rapid pace of innovation in Chinese biotech and the potential for novel mechanisms to leapfrog current standards.

But the report leaves several critical questions unanswered. First, how will the Keytruda patent expiry in 2028 affect the treatment algorithm? The KOLs noted that insurers may pressure physicians to use Keytruda after patent expiry, which could slow adoption of newer agents even if they show superior efficacy. Second, what is the optimal sequencing of ADCs and bispecifics? If a patient progresses on an ADC, can they be effectively treated with a bispecific, and vice versa? The KOLs did not address this question, but it will be critical for real-world treatment decisions.

Third, and perhaps most importantly, the report does not fully address the risk of biosimilar competition for Keytruda. If biosimilars enter the market at a fraction of the price, the economic calculus for new agents changes dramatically. A drug that offers a modest efficacy improvement may not justify a premium price if a cheap biosimilar is available. The KOLs mentioned this risk in passing, but the report does not explore its implications for pricing and market access.

These unanswered questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are inherent in the early-stage nature of the data. The KOLs are practicing oncologists, not forecasters. They can tell you what they think of the current data, but they cannot predict how the market will evolve in response to future data, regulatory decisions, and pricing pressures. Investors who rely solely on the KOLs' clinical impressions will miss the broader competitive and economic dynamics.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Evaluate the Next Wave of NSCLC Data

The KOL discussions provide a useful framework for investors to evaluate upcoming data readouts. The framework has three dimensions: histology, biomarker, and geography.

First, histology determines the relevant comparator and the expected magnitude of benefit. For squamous NSCLC, the comparator is Keytruda plus chemotherapy, and the bar for a clinically meaningful OS HR is approximately 0.75. For non-squamous NSCLC, the comparator may include pemetrexed-based regimens, and the bar may be lower because the existing standard is less effective. Investors should ask whether a drug's data are derived from a squamous or non-squamous population and whether the comparator arm is appropriate.

Second, biomarker status determines the addressable patient population. PD-L1 high patients (TPS >= 50%) are the most attractive target because they have the highest unmet need for alternatives to Keytruda monotherapy. But they are also the most difficult to displace because Keytruda is well-tolerated and has a long track record. PD-L1 low patients are easier to displace but represent a smaller commercial opportunity. Investors should ask whether a drug's efficacy is consistent across PD-L1 subgroups or whether it is driven by a specific biomarker.

Third, geography determines the credibility of the data. China-only data are increasingly common, but the KOLs made clear that global confirmation is essential. The age>65 subgroup debate for ivonescimab is a case study in why China data may not generalize. Investors should ask whether a drug has global Phase 3 data or only China data, and whether the global trial design adequately addresses the limitations of the China data.

This framework is not a substitute for detailed clinical analysis, but it provides a structured way to think about the competitive landscape. The next major data readouts—the HARMONi-3 squamous PFS analysis and the sac-TMT global OS data—will be the first tests of this framework. Investors who apply it rigorously will be better positioned to distinguish between genuine breakthroughs and statistical artifacts.

# China’s Value Retailers Are Not in a Price War—They Are in a Scale Race, and the Market Has Not Yet Priced the Acceleration

The sharp sell-off in China’s leading value retailers in late May was not a signal of a broken business model. It was a moment of market overreaction to a predictable competitive dynamic—and the subsequent official statements from both Busy Ming and Wanchen, reaffirming their commitment to rational competition, should be read not as defensive posturing but as a strategic signal. These companies are not fighting over a shrinking pie; they are racing to capture a rapidly expanding one.

The data from the first five months of 2026 makes this clear. Store openings have accelerated meaningfully in the second quarter, with both players adding over 3,000 net new units in the five months through May. This is not the behavior of companies trapped in a zero-sum subsidy war. It is the behavior of companies executing against a long-term growth thesis that the market has consistently underestimated. The real question for investors is not whether competition will intensify, but whether the current valuation adequately reflects the compounding power of scale, network effects, and category expansion that is now visibly unfolding.

The May sell-off and subsequent recovery have created an entry point that deserves serious scrutiny. At 17x and 14x forward P/E respectively, Busy Ming and Wanchen are trading at a discount to global value-retail peers despite superior growth trajectories. The risk-reward is asymmetric, and the catalysts ahead—including Busy Ming’s HSCI inclusion, improving per-store metrics, and Wanchen’s potential dual listing—are not yet reflected in consensus expectations. The market is still pricing these stocks as if the subsidy cycle will destroy margins. The evidence suggests otherwise.

## The Official Reaffirmation of Rational Competition Is a Structural Positive, Not a Mere PR Move

When Busy Ming and Wanchen each issued formal statements on June 2 explicitly opposing irrational competition and committing to an orderly market environment, they were doing more than calming investor nerves. They were aligning their public positioning with the broader national policy direction against “involution”—the phenomenon of excessive, value-destructive competition. This is not a coincidence, and it is not costless signaling. It reflects a coordinated recognition among the leading players that the long-term opportunity is large enough that short-term market share grabs through aggressive subsidies are strategically counterproductive.

The channel checks confirm that the recent increase in franchisee subsidies has remained disciplined. This is the critical nuance that the market initially missed. The sell-off was triggered by fear of a repeat of the brutal price wars seen in other Chinese consumer sectors, but the value-retail model in snacks and beverages operates under different economics. The barriers to entry are not low, the unit economics are not fragile, and the addressable market is not saturated. The leaders have already built distribution networks, brand recognition, and supply chain advantages that latecomers cannot replicate overnight. A rational competitive environment benefits the incumbents disproportionately.

The statements also serve a practical purpose: they give franchisees confidence. In a franchise-heavy model, trust in the brand’s commitment to sustainable profitability is a direct determinant of store-level investment and store-opening velocity. By publicly committing to rational competition, Busy Ming and Wanchen are reinforcing the franchisee value proposition at a moment when store openings are accelerating. This is a virtuous cycle, not a defensive one.

## Store-Open Acceleration in Q2 2025 Confirms That the Growth Engine Is Operating at Higher Gear

The monthly store tracker reveals a clear inflection point. Through May 2026, Busy Ming added 907 net new stores in that month alone, the highest monthly addition since the data series began. Wanchen added 1,051 net new stores in May, also a standout figure. This acceleration is not an anomaly; it follows a steady ramp-up from the beginning of the year, with both companies showing net additions that consistently exceeded their own prior-year run rates.

The market has been conditioned to view rapid store expansion with skepticism, worrying about cannibalization and diminishing per-store economics. But the data on average daily GMV per store tells a more nuanced story. For Wanchen, average daily GMV in May—including newly opened stores—was positive. This is significant because new stores typically take time to ramp, and a positive reading at the aggregate level suggests that existing stores are holding up well even as the network expands rapidly. For Busy Ming, per-store GMV trends have been improving sequentially, with a trajectory from mid-single-digit declines in early 2025 to flattish and then high-single-digit growth by early 2026.

The implication is clear: the market is not saturating. Category expansion, better product mix, and improved store-level execution are offsetting any dilution from new openings. The scale effect is working in favor of the leaders. As store density increases, distribution costs per unit decline, brand visibility improves, and franchisee economics strengthen. The acceleration in store openings is not a sign of desperation; it is a sign of operational confidence.

## The Margin Story Is Underappreciated: Scale and Efficiency Gains Are Not Yet in the Price

The consensus narrative around Chinese value retailers tends to focus on top-line growth and competitive dynamics, but the margin story is equally important and less understood. Both Busy Ming and Wanchen are benefiting from structural improvements in their cost structures. Larger store networks mean better bargaining power with suppliers, lower logistics costs per unit, and the ability to spread fixed costs over a larger revenue base.

The report indicates that margin expansion from scale and efficiency is expected to drive solid first-half results. This is not speculative; it is a direct consequence of the operating leverage inherent in the franchise model. As store counts grow, the central organization’s costs—supply chain, IT, training, brand marketing—do not need to grow proportionally. The marginal cost of adding a store declines, and the incremental revenue flows through to profit at a higher rate.

Yet the current valuation multiples do not reflect this. Busy Ming at 17x and Wanchen at 14x forward earnings are pricing in a future where margins are compressed or stagnant. If the margin trajectory is actually improving—as the data suggests—then the stocks are undervalued. The market is still applying a discount for competitive risk that has not materialized and may not materialize in the way investors fear.

## The Report Leaves One Critical Question Unanswered: What Happens When Store Density Reaches a Threshold?

For all the optimism around store acceleration and rational competition, there is a question that the report does not fully address: what is the optimal store density for each brand, and at what point does the law of diminishing returns begin to apply? The data shows strong per-store GMV trends today, but the track record of rapid expansion in Chinese retail suggests that cannibalization risk is not zero. It is merely deferred.

The answer likely depends on the maturity of each city tier. In tier-one and tier-two cities, store density may already be approaching levels where incremental openings generate lower returns. In lower-tier cities and rural areas, the runway is much longer. The leaders are likely aware of this and are calibrating their expansion accordingly. But the report does not provide a framework for investors to assess where each brand stands in terms of market saturation by region.

This is the unresolved analytical question that should give investors pause. The bull case rests on the assumption that the market is spacious enough for continued expansion. That assumption is probably correct for the next two to three years, but it is not infinitely elastic. At some point, the marginal store will not generate the same unit economics as the early movers. The question is when, and at what scale. The report’s data points are encouraging, but they do not yet answer the threshold question.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Conditions That Must Hold for the Thesis to Play Out

Investors evaluating Busy Ming and Wanchen should apply a three-part decision framework to test whether the current opportunity is real or a value trap.

First, monitor the trajectory of per-store GMV. If per-store GMV continues to improve or stabilize, the store acceleration is additive. If it begins to decline meaningfully, the expansion is destroying value. The report’s data through May 2026 is positive, but this is a metric that needs to be tracked monthly.

Second, watch the franchisee economics. The health of the franchise network is the single most important leading indicator. If franchisee profitability remains attractive, store openings will continue to accelerate organically. If franchisee margins come under pressure, the growth engine will stall. The official statements about rational competition are helpful, but the real test is whether franchisees are making money.

Third, assess the margin trajectory. Margin expansion from scale is the primary source of upside to earnings estimates. If margins are stable or improving, the current valuation is cheap. If margins are under pressure despite revenue growth, the business model has less operating leverage than assumed.

These three conditions—per-store GMV, franchisee health, and margin expansion—form a coherent framework for decision-making. If all three are positive, the stocks are buys. If any one deteriorates, the thesis needs to be reassessed. The report’s evidence supports the positive view today, but the framework allows investors to update their view as new data arrives.

## The Catalysts Ahead Are Real, and the Market Has Not Yet Priced Them

Busy Ming’s inclusion in the HSCI index, announced on May 22, is a prerequisite for Southbound Stock Connect access starting June 8. This is not a minor event. It opens the door to a new class of mainland Chinese investors who have been unable to buy the stock directly. The liquidity and valuation impact of such inclusions is well documented, and the effect is typically positive. The market has not yet priced this catalyst because the stock was in the middle of the sell-off when the announcement was made.

Wanchen’s potential dual listing in Hong Kong is another catalyst that could unlock value. A dual listing would improve the company’s access to international capital, increase its investor base, and potentially lead to a re-rating. The timeline is uncertain, but the mere possibility creates an option value that is not reflected in the current price.

Finally, improving per-store GMV trends, supported by category expansion, provide a fundamental underpinning for earnings upgrades. The report shows that both companies are seeing positive momentum in this metric. If this continues, consensus estimates will need to move higher, and the stocks will follow.

These catalysts are not hypothetical. They are concrete, time-bound events that are likely to drive stock price performance in the coming months. The market’s focus on competitive noise has obscured them, creating an opportunity for investors who can look past the short-term volatility.

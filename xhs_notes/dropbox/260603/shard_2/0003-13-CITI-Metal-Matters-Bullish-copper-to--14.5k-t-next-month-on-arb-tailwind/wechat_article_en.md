# The Copper Market Is Entering a Regime of Structurally Tight Supply, but the Path to $15,000 Per Ton Runs Through Geopolitics, Not Fundamentals Alone

The case for copper at $14,500 per ton within the next month and $15,000 within a year rests on a convergence of three forces: a physical market that is tighter than most investors appreciate, a US tariff arbitrage that is distorting global flows in ways that support prices, and a near-term macro environment that appears more resilient than the consensus fears. But the real insight from the latest proprietary data is not simply that copper is going higher. It is that the market is transitioning from a cyclical narrative to a structural one, and the transition is happening faster than the price curve reflects.

The risk of a sustained Strait of Hormuz closure through July is the single most underappreciated variable in the copper outlook. Most investors treat it as a binary tail risk. The data suggests it is a source of asymmetric upside. If the Strait reopens, risk assets rally and copper benefits disproportionately. If it remains closed, the impact on copper demand is likely to be far more muted than on oil or broader equities, because the structural demand drivers from energy transition and AI are less sensitive to a temporary energy price shock. The market is pricing in a negative scenario that may not materialize, and that creates an opportunity.

This is not a call to chase momentum. It is a call to recognize that the copper market has reached an inflection point where supply constraints, policy ambiguity, and demand resilience are creating a price floor that is higher than any point in the past decade, and where the upside scenarios are becoming more probable than the downside ones.

## US Tariff Ambiguity Is Acting as a Price Floor Through June, but the Post-July Dynamics Could Flip the Script

The lingering fear of a US tariff on refined copper imports is one of the most powerful yet misunderstood forces in the market today. It is not that a tariff is likely. The base case remains that no tariff will be imposed. But the strategic ambiguity from policymakers is effectively functioning as a policy tool to incentivize excess inventory storage in the United States. This has created a persistent COMEX-LME arbitrage that has widened significantly ahead of the end-June review deadline.

The consequence is a market structure where funds are reluctant to hold short positions, particularly on COMEX. Gross short positions remain subdued, and this is unlikely to change until there is clarity. The irony is that the absence of a tariff announcement is itself supportive of prices, because it keeps the arbitrage alive and sustains the flow of metal into the US. Once the review passes without a definitive tariff, the risk is that the market fades this premium. But the report argues that any bearish impact from fading tariff risk could be offset by a Hormuz reopening and by the underlying physical tightness.

The strategic question for investors is whether to fade the tariff premium or to treat it as a structural feature of the market for the remainder of the year. The data suggests that the premium is likely to persist through July, and that any drawdown in US inventories would be gradual rather than sudden. The risk of a tail scenario where tariffs are announced and phased from 2027 would be incrementally bullish, particularly for COMEX prices and for LME spreads. The asymmetry is clear: the upside from a tariff announcement is larger than the downside from no tariff.

## The Global Supply Picture Is Weaker Than the Headline Numbers Suggest, and the Disruption Allowance Needs to Be Higher

The report revises mine supply growth assumptions downward, applying a 7 percent disruption allowance for 2027 and 2028, and expects zero growth in 2026. This is not a minor adjustment. It reflects a recognition that execution and ramp-up risks at key mines such as Grasberg, El Teniente, Kamoa, and Cobre Panama are higher than previously assumed. The DR Congo, which has been a source of supply growth, faces sulfur market constraints that could limit sustained production increases. The broader environment of geopolitical instability means that reliance on supply from less stable jurisdictions carries more risk than the market is pricing.

The more important insight is about scrap supply. The global copper market is increasingly dependent on scrap to meet structural demand growth, given flat mine output. But the data shows that scrap recovery is not rising proportionally to higher prices. China scrap import data for January through April shows only a modest year-on-year uptick despite copper prices being more than 30 percent higher. This underperformance can be explained by higher energy and fuel costs, softer goods trade-in support in China, and a generally depleted global scrap supply chain after years of raw material shortages for smelters.

The implication is that the market cannot rely on scrap to fill the gap. If scrap supply underperforms against a backdrop of resilient demand, the deficit in 2027 could be larger than the roughly 360 kiloton forecast. The report models a deficit of that magnitude based on current spot prices, and estimates that a price increase of roughly $1,500 per ton would be needed to drive additional scrap recovery and substitution. That is the mechanical logic behind the $15,000 target. But the logic depends on demand not weakening, and that is where the analysis becomes more nuanced.

## Demand Is More Resilient Than Headline Data Shows, but the Composition Is Shifting in Ways That Matter

The proprietary global copper end-use consumption tracker shows subdued year-on-year growth as of March. That sounds bearish, but the composition tells a different story. The weakness is concentrated in China's renewable installations, which faced a high base from 2025 due to policy-induced front-loading in solar and EVs. That slowdown was anticipated and is largely a function of policy timing, not structural demand deterioration.

What is more important is the improvement in global PMI prints, which suggests upside for cyclical consumption. The implied softness in China's domestic energy transition demand is being offset by strong export growth. This is a pattern that has been observed in other industrial metals, and it suggests that the global demand picture is more diversified than the China-centric narrative implies.

The structural demand drivers from AI and energy transition are becoming more resilient to cyclical shocks. The report makes an important point: if a demand shock is driven by higher energy prices, the impact on copper demand is likely to be more cushioned than in the past because the structural users of copper are less price-sensitive in the short term. This is not a theoretical point. It has direct implications for how investors should think about the Hormuz risk. A sustained closure that drives oil prices higher would hurt cyclical demand, but the structural demand from data centers and renewable energy infrastructure would continue.

## The Report Leaves an Unresolved Question: How Much of the Bull Case Depends on a Hormuz Reopening That Is Not Guaranteed?

The base case assumes a Hormuz reopening in the third quarter of 2026. That assumption is central to the near-term bullish outlook. If the reopening does not materialize, or is delayed beyond the summer, the risk to risk assets and to copper would be material. The report acknowledges this explicitly, warning that copper remains subject to bearish tail risks from a sustained and unstable Middle East situation.

But the analysis does not fully quantify the probability of a delayed reopening or explore the scenarios in which the Strait remains closed through the end of the year. The bull case assigns only a 20 percent probability to the most optimistic scenario, but that scenario includes a Hormuz reopening as a supporting factor. If the reopening is delayed, the bull case probability would need to be revised downward, and the base case would need to incorporate a more persistent risk premium.

The unresolved question is whether the market is correctly pricing the probability of a Hormuz resolution. The report argues that growth and risk assets can hold up for at least a few months even if the Strait remains closed. That is a reasonable assumption, but it depends on the closure not triggering a broader economic slowdown. If the closure persists and energy prices remain elevated, the impact on consumer spending and industrial activity could become more pronounced. The copper market would not be immune.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios and the Trigger Points That Matter

For investors trying to position in copper, the report provides enough data to construct a simple but robust decision framework. The framework has three scenarios, each with a clear trigger point.

The base case, with a 60 percent probability, sees copper reaching $14,500 in the near term and $15,000 within a year. The trigger to watch is the end-June tariff review. If no tariff is announced and the Hormuz reopening proceeds as expected, the path to $15,000 is clear. The key risk within this scenario is that fading tariff risk becomes a headwind in the third quarter, but the report argues that physical tightness and the Hormuz reopening would offset this.

The bull case, with a 20 percent probability, sees copper reaching $17,000 by early 2027. The trigger is a combination of stronger cyclical growth, a repricing of rate cut expectations, and further supply underperformance. This scenario also assumes that the structural demand from AI and energy transition accelerates. The trigger point to watch is a sustained improvement in global PMIs above 54, combined with evidence that scrap supply is not responding to higher prices.

The bear case, also with a 20 percent probability, sees copper falling to $11,000 by early 2027. The trigger is a sustained and unstable Middle East situation that leads to a broader risk unwind, combined with higher interest rate expectations that weigh on physical demand. The trigger point to watch is a break below $12,000 on the LME, combined with a widening of credit spreads and a deterioration in global PMIs below 48.

The most important insight from this framework is that the probabilities are not static. If the Hormuz reopening is delayed beyond the third quarter, the bear case probability should increase. If the end-June tariff review results in a definitive tariff announcement, the bull case probability should increase. The framework gives investors a way to update their views as new information arrives, rather than relying on a static price target.

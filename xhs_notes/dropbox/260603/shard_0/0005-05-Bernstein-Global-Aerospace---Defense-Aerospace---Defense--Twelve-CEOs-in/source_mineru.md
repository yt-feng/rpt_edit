Last week, we hosted twelve Aerospace & Defense CEOs at Bernstein's Strategic Decisions Conference in New York. CEOs participating were from Anduril (private), ATI Materials (not covered), BAE Systems, Boeing, GE Aerospace, General Dynamics, Howmet, L3Harris, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, and RTX. Here, we summarize overall themes, as well as the messages from each company.

We heard common themes from the commercial aerospace companies, all of whom continue to see a strong aftermarket outlook, despite concerns regarding high fuel prices impacting airline profitability. This is still a watch item if high fuel prices are extended, but is not likely to hit results in 2026. For OE demand, suppliers were positive now that Boeing is taking deliveries up on the 737 and 787. Suppliers still have some challenges, given that OE and aftermarket are growing strongly at the same time. For engine suppliers, IGT is becoming an increasingly strong new market, as data center demand grows. Howmet and ATI highlighted IGT, and GE is benefiting in its aeroderivatives business.

On the defense side, there remains uncertainty about the 2027 defense budget. Still, all of the defense companies expect the budget to be up, even if not to the \$1.5 trillion level proposed by the administration. Missiles and missile defense remain the hottest areas, with growth frameworks being put in place. Solid rocket motor suppliers (L3Harris, Northrop Grumman, Anduril) are all responding. Space also continues to be an area of high budget growth. Shipbuilding continues to see higher budgets, with progress appearing to come on throughput at the shipyards. There was substantial discussion about the changing nature of warfare, with more focus on drones and counter-UAS programs. Lastly, the impact of new entrants in defense tech was widely discussed, with Anduril making the case for why change is happening.

# INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS

Our Strategic Decisions Conference presented a positive outlook for commercial aerospace stocks, as OE ramp rates rise and aftermarket demand remains solid even with airlines facing rising fuel costs. For defense stocks, there continues to be uncertainty around the overall budget. Demand for missiles and space remain very high.

# DETAILS

# CONFERENCE THEMES

# COMMERCIAL AEROSPACE - MAJOR THEMES

- No signs yet of a slowdown due to high fuel prices. All commercial aero companies reiterated that they have not seen any signs of weakness in demand, given the concerns around airline profitability amidst high fuel prices. Should the high fuel prices place longer-term threats to airline profitability, we could see pressure, particularly from discretionary purchases (e.g., avionics upgrades, interiors).   
- Rising Boeing production rates. Boeing confirmed that it has passed its capstone review with the FAA to move to rate 47/month on the 737, which is expected to be fully achieved during the summer. CEO Ortberg expressed optimism about future rate breaks to 52/month and 57/month - and then added that the company would look on to a 63/month rate. For the 787, he reaffirmed the goal of being at 10/month by year-end. Suppliers were reacting positively to the Boeing outlook. Suppliers are now seeing inventories down related to engines as coming down, so that deliveries are becoming more aligned with production rates. There are still, however, some components where more inventory burndown is still to come.   
- Airbus widebody ramp slower than expected. We heard from suppliers that the pull for the A350 ramp at Airbus was moving more slowly than expected. This is consistent with recent reported commentary by Airbus. regarding delays on the A350 and A320.   
- Aftermarket demand remains high for engines and equipment. Shop visits are generally covered for 12-18 months, or more for new models, limiting risks of cancellations. Also, lessor ownership of airplanes at many of the weaker carriers provides a source of protection for the companies in MRO. One issue, however, is that the combination of rising OE demand and continued strong aftermarket demand adds to pressure on supply chains.   
- A next narrowbody remains a long way away. While GE continues to discuss its RISE program, other commercial aero companies were much more focused on near-term production challenges. In the case of RTX, it was also nearer-term for Pratt & Whitney, with the GTF Advantage ramp being the current next step. At Boeing, it was also much more about current production ramps than looking toward the late 2030s.   
- IGT becomes a growth area for some. Howmet and ATI each describe growth potential as suppliers for industrial gas turbines, that leverages their aero engine technology. GE is also seeing increased demand for its aeroderivative products.

# DEFENSE - MAJOR THEMES

- 2027 budget. All the defense companies expressed optimism about the budget, although without assuming anything about how President Trump's will fare through Congress. Each company made the point that their programs were primarily supported by appropriations, rather than in reconciliation. We heard little concern about a possible CR, which has become routine recently.   
- Rapid growth in missile demand and production plans. Lockheed Martin and RTX each described the very strong demand outlook for missiles, which ties into new frameworks for production growth. Demand is still higher after high usage of munitions during the Iran war. Those frameworks apply to a broad range of missiles and plan for 3X or 4X current production over five to seven years. The contracts are expected to have guarantees that will reimburse the companies should growth not come to fruition. Given the high demand, missiles is now becoming a growth market for suppliers (e.g., Howmet, ATI) even though missiles had not previously been a material market for them.   
- Missile supply change challenges, particularly solid rocket motors. Related to the missile growth discussions, we heard a lot about solid rocket motor growth, primarily form L3Harris and Northrop Grumman. The OEMs for tactical missiles (LMT, RTX) described their desire to have multiple sources for specific motors. Microelectronics (e.g., seekers) and structural ca

- Shipbuilding throughput improving. Shipbuilding has received a huge amount of funding and backlogs are high. That said, completing production of new Navy ships has been a challenge. We heard a more positive message at the SDC, with throughput now rising as labor and supplier issues are addressed.   
- Space seen universally as a high growth segment. Defense companies described high demand for space, particularly for the newer large LEO constellations, plus space-based interceptors. This was true even though we continue to see limited clarity on the Golden Dome.   
- Defense support demand rises. The war in Iran has resulted in a higher operational tempo for U.S. military assets. Beyond the high usage of tactical missiles and interceptors, maintenance requirements for aircraft and ships has risen. Much of that heightened demand is still to come.   
- New entrants and asymmetric warfare. There was substantial discussion about the changing nature of warfare, with the advent of low cost drones and broader use of autonomous systems. Anduril discussed its approaches, emphasizing its speed to market and approaches to lower costs. The traditional primes described their efforts in autonomy, systems work, and counter-UAS. Several of the large primes also stressed their collaborative work with new entrants, including the use of venture funds.

# THE COMPANIES

# ANDURIL (BRIAN SCHIMPF - CEO)

# Anduril's origin and position.

- Origin. Anduril was first in Silicon Valley to go directly after the defense tech problem, recognizing that warfare was changing. Schimpf was at Palantir for over a decade, where it took more than five years to gain traction with the US government. Anduril has been able to get programs of record within 2 to 3 years. It has won competitions against multiple large primes.   
- Warfighting changes. After a period focused on counter-terrorism, there was a renewed sense of urgency and a recognition that the U.S. was entering a generational shift in warfare. Historically, these shifts create windows where new programs emerge and new capabilities are needed. Conflicts like Ukraine—and more recently the war in Iran—highlight that the U.S. and its allies face significant shortfalls in production capacity and inventory.   
- Size. Currently, has roughly 10,000 employees and is doubling revenue annually. 2025 was a little over \$2bn and that is likely to double in 2026. The biggest shift has been around this rapid ramp in production. Within five years, revenues could be in the \$30-40bn range.   
- Key programs. The US Air Force CCA (Collaborative Combat Aircraft) has moved from a paper design to first flight in one and a half years. It is now flying multiple times per week. Early production is expected within the next year. Other product areas have been low cost cruise missiles, interceptors, and hypersonics. Looking for opportunities to provide lower cost solutions.

# Differentiation

- Cost and speed. Schimpf believes that the company benefits from not coming out of a long history of production programs that placed more emphasis on exquisite solutions, rather than manufacturability and scalability.   
- Supply chain is critical. Owning and integrating key components is a more effective model, as it reduces the risks associated with lower tier suppliers. When lower tier suppliers are involved, they need to be facilitated, which does not work when most of the value accrues to the prime. Anduril acquired a smaller solid rocket motor supplier and invested. The company also designs actuators, avionics, and seekers in-house. For volume, they have worked with industrial scale PCB manufacturers.

# Why are new entrants now becoming successful?

\- Growing dissatisfaction with the status quo. In the late 2000s and early 2010s (2007–2010), the prevailing view was that the system didn't need new entrants—that existing defense primes and processes were sufficient. The idea that something fundamentally wasn't working was often dismissed outright. When Anduril was founded in 2017—the shift was dramatic. There was a clear and growing recognition that the traditional model was too slow and struggled to deliver

- Post Ukraine. There is more of an opening for non-traditional players - greater willingness from government stakeholders to engage companies like Anduril. The mindset has shifted from “this won’t work” to “this might not work, but it will at least be different—and we need different”   
- Trump administration. Schimpf indicated that they're in a position today where the current administration has a strong appetite to bring in new players into the defense ecosystem. That has created real opportunity. A major inflection point was the CCA award: AF selected Anduril over traditional incumbents on one of its highest-priority programs. Anduril is now viewed as an established player. Shortly after the CCA win, Anduril was sole-sourced on on-orbit space programs.   
- Software as a platform. Anduril has taken a fundamentally different approach with Lattice. Anduril developed Lattice as a unifying software platform that cuts across everything it does. The core thesis is that the future battlefield will be: highly networked, composed of many more sensors and effectors - and increasingly complex and data-rich. Making sense of that environment—and acting on it in real time—requires a common software layer that can connect, coordinate, and orchestrate all those systems. Another key difference is how modern software platforms are built. It looks fundamentally different from the way defense has historically approached software (systems like Aegis were developed decades ago). Still, it is important to be able to integrate with legacy systems.   
- Use of IP. Historically, the government resisted paying for IP that was already developed. The government wanted to build programs from scratch, which meant they would maintain control. This also lead to lock-in of suppliers for generational programs, which slows the ability to evolve to meet changing needs. The ability to leverage one's own IP is now opening up.   
- Many new entrants will not be around in five to ten years. A common pattern among new entrants is that they start as single-product or single category companies. That model is extremely hard to scale in defense because you quickly cap out within a narrow segment. Anduril has 25 business lines, so in any given year, some products are flat vs others are up a lot. For many smaller companies, they may end up being absorbed into the large primes.

# Product versus service revenue.

- Traditional sustainment models. Historically, sustainment has been a large share of total program revenue and often the most profitable phase. Anduril's approach reflects a different philosophy from the past: align incentives with outcomes, get paid for performance, availability, and effectiveness. Anduril examples include counter-drone as a service (USMC) with clear performance targets, including measured uptime/system availability. If systems fail or underperform there are financial penalties. If systems are reliable and continuously improved, then profitability improves. This becomes even more important in a software-defined world (software requires continuous updates, improvements).   
- How will growth evolve at Anduril? Schimpf explained that exponential growth is hard to model. Think about sustainment as \~20% of initial product sales per year, recurring. When ramping, sustainment revenue is relatively small compared to product sales in a given year, as it has not flowed back through, but stacks up over time. It is hard to say the composition between product versus sales, as it is contingent on the growth rate- as growth slows, then proportional sustainment revenue goes up. Sustainment will be an increasingly important aspect. But, for the next few years revenue will be highly skewed towards production.

# Commercial-style contracting

\- Receptivity to commercial contracts. Even though many primes talk about embracing commercial models, they often resist fixed-price development. Anduril won a contract where they were the only compliant bidder willing to take on fixed-price development, while others declined. On the development side, Auduril's track record has been good. But, CCA development is being done on a cost-plus basis.

\- Choosing fixed-price. When Anduril is doing fixed-price development programs, it avoids large fixed-price development programs (e.g., \$1B all at once) but has a preference for smaller well-defined tranches. On the production and sustainment side, they are almost universally doing fixed price and fixed price incentive fee. If you are doing cost plus, why would the prime invest in making your weapon/aircraft cheaper as the government will take all of your benefit.

# Investment strategy

- Leveraging investments. A core part of Anduril's strategy is investing in large-scale production facilities that can be leveraged across multiple products. The goal is to amortize fixed costs across a broad portfolio and drive higher utilization of facilities and equipment. At the same time, modern defense systems require an unusually wide range of technical expertise: electronic warfare, sensors, propulsion and aerostructures etc. They require deep, specialized knowledge across multiple domains. Building and integrating all of this is inherently difficult. Very few companies have the technical breadth, capital, and ambition to operate at this level.   
- Capital intensity. Investing across multiple programs and scaling production is inherently capital-intensive. There is a natural lag between when costs are incurred and when those investments begin generating revenue: Facilities are typically built \~2 years ahead of demand, equipment and capex are deployed \~12 months in advance, and headcount is scaled \~6 months ahead of revenue generation. As a result, at high growth rates, the business requires significant upfront capital, which delays free cash flow (FCF) generation.   
- As a private company. Schimpf indicated there is a strong incentive for private investors to convince private companies to stay private, which means they can put more money and get more return. There is no shortage of capital available to support Anduril's expansion. The vast majority will go towards working capital and capex over the next few years. Anduril's investors keep telling them to keep investing in order to keep the growth rate up.

Key risk for Anduril: All about execution. The company believes it has built a strong track record of delivering on commitments, which has to continue.

# BOEING (KELLY ORTBERG - CEO)

# Boeing Commercial Airplanes

- 737: Production stable at \~42/month with positive key performance indicators. The company has passed the FAA capstone review for rate 47, allowing the new production increase. Boeing has begun running the line at 47/month, with stabilization expected over the next few months. No significant supply chain issues are expected before rate 52/month, given the current inventory buffer. Boeing is activating its north production line in Everett, which will support a rate of 52/month. While stabilizing at 47, Boeing will gradually ramp up the new Everett line at low rates.   
- 737 margins - Boeing expects 737 margins to improve as production rates increase, supported by better pricing and a higher mix of larger (higher-value) 737-10 aircraft. The headwinds associated with late penalties with current deliveries should burn off over the next couple years. However, the new Everett (“north”) line will initially weigh on margins as it ramps up at low production rates. Once fully scaled to match Renton’s efficiency, the north line should contribute positively to profitability.   
- Spirit: Boeing has announced a \$1bn investment over three years to upgrade facilities, workforce, and training in Wichita. The focus is on improving safety, quality, and operational standards, aligned with FAA-driven processes used in Renton   
- 787 production: Currently at \~8/month with target to reach 10/month later in the year; ramp is gated by engine supply recovery and seat certification bottlenecks. Aircraft are being produced, but deliveries are constrained by seat certification leading to lumpiness in deliveries.   
- Certification updates (737-7/-10, 777X): 737-7 and -10 are >80% through flight test with certification expected by year-end, enabling 2027 delivery support (737-7 to be certified before 737-10 as 737-10 is larger and more complex). 777X flight testing continues through year-end, with ETOPS extending into next year. For 777X, cash outflows will remain elevated in 2026 and 2027 but will turn in the 2028 timeframe.   
- China: China's initial commitment for 200 aircraft is expected to convert into firm orders later this year. Once the government decides on a batch of narrowbody aircraft, these are allocated to Chinese airlines, which then work with Boeing to formalize the orders. This initial commitment effectively reopens the Chinese market, from which Boeing had not received an order in nearly a decade.   
- High Fuel Prices: Boeing has not yet seen any customer requests to delay or move deliveries. If financial strain occurs, airlines may defer deliveries, but Boeing can work with customers to reallocate slots. There is often strong demand from

other airlines willing to take up available positions. With sufficient notice (around 12 months for the 737 and approximately 18 months for the 787), Boeing can reassign production slots to avoid delivery disruptions.

# Boeing Defense & Space Systems

- Margins: Medium-term target remains high single-digit margins.   
- F-47: Boeing is investing heavily in its St. Louis facility to support F-47 production and potential future opportunities. The program is strategically important as F-18 production winds down.   
- KC-46- KC-46 has one remaining lot under the existing fixed-price contract, expected to be awarded by year-end. Subsequent production quantities will be repriced, which should allow for margin improvement.   
• T-7A: A significant portion of production is already under fixed terms, meaning profitability recovery will take time.   
- Space: Boeing's space business is split into two segments: human space exploration (e.g., Starliner) and national security space (including strategic satcom satellites and classified work). The national security space segment has multiple applications related to the Golden Dome initiative. The space exploration business faces challenges, particularly from fixed-price contracts such as Starliner. NASA has scheduled one Starliner launch this year and another next year, while Boeing works through fixing deficiencies identified in the prior mission.

# Boeing Global Services

\- On the commercial side, Boeing has limited exposure to flight-hour–based services, so reduced flying in the region has minimal impact. For defense, increased operational tempo is driving higher demand for parts, repairs, and replenishment

# GE AEROSPACE (LARRY CULP - CEO)

- Oil price impact in Q2'26. GE is confident that aftermarket trends will remain strong in 2026. Flight cycles have been impacted by the Middle East disruptions and the high jet fuel prices. But, GE will continue to benefit from its rich backlog for shop visits. Q2 is again shaping up to be a strong quarter. GE indicated that their conversations with airlines are not indicating any weakness. They also said the number of parked planes (indicator on potential retirements) is coming down. Demand for aftermarket is accelerating.   
- Beyond 2026. The backlog is expected to protect financials for at least 12-18 months, even if the current fuel price situation extends. At the time of Q1'26 results, visibility was not great enough for GE to upgrade the targets for 2026, but the outlook continues to be strong. They expect aftermarket growth for 2027 to again be in the double-digits. They are also willing to support airlines in case of restructuring. But they have not seen airlines try to defer repairs. GE believe that, in case of financial distress for some airlines, then engines could be reallocated to other airlines (directly or through lessors), limiting financial risk.   
- CFM56 profile. The outlook for CFM56 aftermarket remains extended. Around 30% of the CFM56 fleet still has not had a first shop visit, with 2026 workscopes described as stable. GE expected the number of shop visits to have a “slight fade” after 2028, but be offset by pricing and scope. Usage of parts for aeroderivative applications also is a potential backstop   
- LEAP profitability. LEAP demand is “extremely high,” with LEAP deliveries up 63%. Boeing is also providing an eventual rate target of 63/month on the 737. The significant growth in the fleet’s size and the aging profile of the engine should continue to support profitability for LEAP. The engine became profitable in the aftermarket in 2024 and will continue to see margins expand as the share of external shop visits grows. LEAP installed base should more than double from 2025 to 2030. LEAP aftermarket profit in dollars is expected to match CFM56 by 2030.   
- Airbus bottlenecks. The issues from 2025 are now resolved and GE's CEO noted a more constructive relationship between CFM and Airbus regarding capacity increases.   
- RISE. The focus from airlines is increasingly shifting to durability, in addition to fuel burn efficiency. GE noted that the RISE project should be able to deliver significant durability improvement, driven by the open-rotor architecture, which should be

an important selling point for the program. The program will continue to require increasing collaboration between CFM and the airframers.

\- Defense is roughly two-thirds U.S., plus allies, with growth framed as MSD to HSD and a very strong book-to-bill. If the company can keep converting that backlog and getting product out the door, management believes it has a clear path to HSD, with an additional uplift expected once sustainment spending moves higher, likely in the 2027 budget. International defense is at least in the HSD growth range and has been a great opportunity particularly for F110.

# RTX (CHRIS CALIO - CEO)

# Raytheon

- Budget outlook. The FY2027 base budget of \$1.15 billion reflects strong and growing investment in defense. Independent of potential added reconciliation funding, there is broad bipartisan support for increasing munitions production. Demand is not limited to the domestic market—roughly 30% of Raytheon sales and 48% of backlog are international, adding resilience even if U.S. funding fluctuates. On a TTM basis, Raytheon book-to-bill is 1.5x. Raytheon backlog of \$74bn does not include new missile framework agreements or Golden Dome opportunities not yet under contract, meaning added demand on top of existing orders.   
- Munitions production. Raytheon had begun ramping capacity prior to recent framework agreements set earlier this year. Munitions demand has risen further, with the need to replenish inventories use in Iran. Widebody production: The recovery has been slower. Widebody aircraft use significantly more titanium—approximately 5x the content of narrowbody aircraft.

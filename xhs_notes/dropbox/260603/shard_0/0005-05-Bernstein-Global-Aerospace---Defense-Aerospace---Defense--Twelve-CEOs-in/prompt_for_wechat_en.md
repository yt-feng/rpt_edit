# COMMERCIAL AEROSPACE - MAJOR THEMES

- No signs yet of a slowdown due to high fuel prices. All commercial aero companies reiterated that they have not seen any signs of weakness in demand, given the concerns around airline profitability amidst high fuel prices. Should the high fuel prices place longer-term threats to airline profitability, we could see pressure, particularly from discretionary purchases (e.g., avionics upgrades, interiors).   
- Rising Boeing production rates. Boeing confirmed that it has passed its capstone review with the FAA to move to rate 47/month on the 737, which is expected to be fully achieved during the summer. CEO Ortberg expressed optimism about future rate breaks to 52/month and 57/month - and then added that the company would look on to a 63/month rate. For the 787, he reaffirmed the goal of being at 10/month by year-end. Suppliers were reacting positively to the Boeing outlook. Suppliers are now seeing inventories down related to engines as coming down, so that deliveries are becoming more aligned with production rates. There are still, however, some components where more inventory burndown is still to come.   
- Airbus widebody ramp slower than expected. We heard from suppliers that the pull for the A350 ramp at Airbus was moving more slowly than expected. This is consistent with recent reported commentary by Airbus. regarding delays on the A350 and A320.   
- Aftermarket demand remains high for engines and equipment. Shop visits are generally covered for 12-18 months, or more for new models, limiting risks of cancellations. Also, lessor ownership of airplanes at many of the weaker carriers provides a source of protection for the companies in MRO. One issue, however, is that the combination of rising OE demand and continued strong aftermarket demand adds to pressure on supply chains.   
- A next narrowbody remains a long way away. While GE continues to discuss its RISE program, other commercial aero companies were much more focused on near-term production challenges. In the case of RTX, it was also nearer-term for Pratt & Whitney, with the GTF Advantage ramp being the current next step. At Boeing, it was also much more about current production ramps than looking toward the late 2030s.   
- IGT becomes a growth area for some. Howmet and ATI each describe growth potential as suppliers for industrial gas turbines, that leverages their aero engine technology. GE is also seeing increased demand for its aeroderivative products.

# ANDURIL (BRIAN SCHIMPF - CEO)

# Anduril's origin and position.

- Origin. Anduril was first in Silicon Valley to go directly after the defense tech problem, recognizing that warfare was changing. Schimpf was at Palantir for over a decade, where it took more than five years to gain traction with the US government. Anduril has been able to get programs of record within 2 to 3 years. It has won competitions against multiple large primes.   
- Warfighting changes. After a period focused on counter-terrorism, there was a renewed sense of urgency and a recognition that the U.S. was entering a generational shift in warfare. Historically, these shifts create windows where new programs emerge and new capabilities are needed. Conflicts like Ukraine—and more recently the war in Iran—highlight that the U.S. and its allies face significant shortfalls in production capacity and inventory.   
- Size. Currently, has roughly 10,000 employees and is doubling revenue annually. 2025 was a little over \$2bn and that is likely to double in 2026. The biggest shift has been around this rapid ramp in production. Within five years, revenues could be in the \$30-40bn range.   
- Key programs. The US Air Force CCA (Collaborative Combat Aircraft) has moved from a paper design to first flight in one and a half years. It is now flying multiple times per week. Early production is expected within the next year. Other product areas have been low cost cruise missiles, interceptors, and hypersonics. Looking for opportunities to provide lower cost solutions.

# Differentiation

- Cost and speed. Schimpf believes that the company benefits from not coming out of a long history of production programs that placed more emphasis on exquisite solutions, rather than manufacturability and scalability.   
- Supply chain is critical. Owning and integrating key components is a more effective model, as it reduces the risks associated with lower tier suppliers. When lower tier suppliers are involved, they need to be facilitated, which does not work when most of the value accrues to the prime. Anduril acquired a smaller solid rocket motor supplier and invested. The company also designs actuators, avionics, and seekers in-house. For volume, they have worked with industrial scale PCB manufacturers.

# Why are new entrants now becoming successful?

\- Growing dissatisfaction with the status quo. In the late 2000s and early 2010s (2007–2010), the prevailing view was that the system didn't need new entrants—that existing defense primes and processes were sufficient. The idea that something fundamentally wasn't working was often dismissed outright. When Anduril was founded in 2017—the shift was dramatic. There was a clear and growing recognition that the traditional model was too slow and struggled to deliver

- Post Ukraine. There is more of an opening for non-traditional players - greater willingness from government stakeholders to engage companies like Anduril. The mindset has shifted from “this won’t work” to “this might not work, but it will at least be different—and we need different”   
- Trump administration. Schimpf indicated that they're in a position today where the current administration has a strong appetite to bring in new players into the defense ecosystem. That has created real opportunity. A major inflection point was the CCA award: AF selected Anduril over traditional incumbents on one of its highest-priority programs. Anduril is now viewed as an established player. Shortly after the CCA win, Anduril was sole-sourced on on-orbit space programs.   
- Software as a platform. Anduril has taken a fundamentally different approach with Lattice. Anduril developed Lattice as a unifying software platform that cuts across everything it does. The core thesis is that the future battlefield will be: highly networked, composed of many more sensors and effectors - and increasingly complex and data-rich. Making sense of that environment—and acting on it in real time—requires a common software layer that can connect, coordinate, and orchestrate all those systems. Another key difference is how modern software platforms are built. It looks fundamentally different from the way defense has historically approached software (systems like Aegis were developed decades ago). Still, it is important to be able to integrate with legacy systems.   
- Use of IP. Historically, the government resisted paying for IP that was already developed. The government wanted to build programs from scratch, which meant they would maintain control. This also lead to lock-in of suppliers for generational programs, which slows the ability to evolve to meet changing needs. The ability to leverage one's own IP is now opening up.   
- Many new entrants will not be around in five to ten years. A common pattern among new entrants is that they start as single-product or single category companies. That model is extremely hard to scale in defense because you quickly cap out within a narrow segment. Anduril has 25 business lines, so in any given year, some products are flat vs others are up a lot. For many smaller companies, they may end up being absorbed into the large primes.

# Product versus service revenue.

- Traditional sustainment models. Historically, sustainment has been a large share of total program revenue and often the most profitable phase. Anduril's approach reflects a different philosophy from the past: align incentives with outcomes, get paid for performance, availability, and effectiveness. Anduril examples include counter-drone as a service (USMC) with clear performance targets, including measured uptime/system availability. If systems fail or underperform there are financial penalties. If systems are reliable and continuously improved, then profitability improves. This becomes even more important in a software-defined world (software requires continuous updates, improvements).

When ramping, sustainment revenue is relatively small compared to product sales in a given year, as it has not flowed back through, but stacks up over time.

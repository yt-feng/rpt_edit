# The Aerospace and Defense Industry Is No Longer Cyclical: A Structural Shift in Demand Is Reshaping the Investment Landscape

The most important signal to emerge from twelve Aerospace and Defense CEO conversations at a major strategic conference is not about quarterly earnings or production rate targets. It is that the industry has crossed a threshold into a fundamentally different demand regime. Commercial aftermarket strength, once expected to moderate as airline profitability came under pressure from high fuel costs, has not only held but is being reinforced by a separate and accelerating growth vector: industrial gas turbines for data center power. On the defense side, the uncertainty around the 2027 budget masks a more important reality—missile demand is being structured around multi-year production frameworks that contemplate three to four times current output, with contractual guarantees to protect suppliers if growth stalls. This is not a cyclical upswing. It is a structural re-rating of the entire sector's earnings power.

The conference took place at a moment when market participants were weighing two competing narratives. One narrative held that high fuel prices would eventually weaken airline profitability, reduce aftermarket spending, and bring commercial aerospace back to earth. The other narrative held that defense budgets, despite political noise, would ultimately prove resilient. The CEO discussions decisively rejected the first narrative and significantly strengthened the second. More importantly, they introduced a third narrative that few investors had fully priced in: the convergence of commercial and defense supply chains around shared technologies, particularly in engine components and advanced materials, is creating a new kind of industrial logic that benefits suppliers across both domains.

This article synthesizes the key insights from those discussions, interprets the strategic implications for investors, and identifies the questions that remain unresolved. It is not a summary of what was said. It is an argument about what the pattern means.

## Commercial Aftermarket Demand Is Structurally Stronger Than the Market Assumes, and the Risk from Fuel Prices Is Overstated

The conventional worry is straightforward: high jet fuel prices compress airline margins, leading carriers to defer discretionary maintenance, delay cabin upgrades, and reduce spare parts purchasing. The CEOs of every major commercial aerospace company at the conference reported that they have seen no such weakness. This is not merely a matter of timing. The structure of the aftermarket has changed in ways that make it more resilient than in previous cycles.

Shop visits for engines and equipment are now booked twelve to eighteen months in advance, and for newer engine models, the backlog extends even further. This forward coverage insulates aftermarket revenue from near-term airline financial stress. Furthermore, the growing share of aircraft owned by lessors rather than by weaker carriers provides an additional buffer. Lessors have stronger balance sheets and longer investment horizons. They are less likely to cut maintenance spending in response to a temporary fuel price spike. The risk that high fuel prices will materially impact 2026 results is negligible. The more relevant question is whether sustained fuel prices above current levels could affect 2027 and beyond, and even there, the CEO consensus was that the impact would fall on discretionary items like avionics upgrades and interior refurbishments, not on core engine and airframe maintenance.

The deeper insight is that the aftermarket is being driven by fleet age and utilization patterns that are independent of fuel costs. The global commercial fleet is older than it has been in decades, because production delays during the pandemic and subsequent supply chain constraints have slowed the delivery of new aircraft. Older aircraft require more maintenance. This demographic reality will persist regardless of whether oil trades at USD 70 or USD 90 per barrel. The aftermarket cycle has decoupled from the airline profitability cycle.

## Boeing Production Ramp Is Real and Creates a Multi-Year Tailwind for Suppliers, but Airbus Widebody Slowness Introduces Asymmetry

Boeing's confirmation that it has passed its capstone review with the FAA to move to a production rate of 47 aircraft per month on the 737 is a watershed moment. The company expressed optimism about further rate breaks to 52, 57, and eventually 63 per month. For suppliers, this is the signal they have been waiting for. Inventories of certain components, particularly engines, have been declining, meaning that supplier deliveries are becoming more aligned with actual production rather than with inventory build. The positive supplier reaction to Boeing's outlook reflects genuine relief that the production logjam is breaking.

The asymmetry comes from Airbus. Suppliers reported that the A350 ramp is moving more slowly than expected. This is consistent with Airbus's own recent commentary about delays on both the A350 and A320 programs. For investors, this means that the commercial aerospace recovery is not uniform. Suppliers with greater exposure to Boeing narrowbody programs have a clearer and more immediate growth path. Those with heavier exposure to Airbus widebody programs face a more uncertain timeline.

The strategic implication is that supplier selection matters more than sector allocation. Companies like Howmet and ATI, which serve both Boeing and Airbus and also benefit from the industrial gas turbine growth story, are positioned to capture upside from multiple sources. Suppliers that are overly dependent on a single airframer or a single platform face greater execution risk.

## Industrial Gas Turbines Are Becoming a Material Growth Market for Aerospace Suppliers, Creating a New Demand Axis

The most underappreciated theme from the conference was the emergence of industrial gas turbines as a meaningful growth market for companies that have historically focused on aerospace engines. Howmet and ATI each described significant growth potential in supplying components for IGTs, leveraging the same casting and materials technologies developed for jet engines. GE Aerospace is benefiting in its aeroderivatives business, which uses aircraft engine technology to generate power for data centers.

This is not a small incremental opportunity. Data center electricity demand is growing at an unprecedented rate, driven by the buildout of artificial intelligence infrastructure. Natural gas-fired power generation is the most scalable near-term solution, and gas turbines are the most efficient conversion technology. The aerospace supply chain has the capacity, the metallurgical expertise, and the quality systems to serve this market. The result is a new demand axis that is independent of both commercial air travel cycles and defense budgets.

For investors, the implication is that the traditional categorization of aerospace and defense companies needs to be revisited. A company like Howmet is no longer just an aerospace supplier. It is an industrial technology company with exposure to power generation, and that exposure is growing. The valuation frameworks that have historically been applied to aerospace suppliers may understate the long-term earnings power of companies that are successfully diversifying into IGT.

## Defense Budget Uncertainty Masks a More Important Structural Shift in Missile Production Frameworks

The 2027 defense budget is genuinely uncertain. None of the defense CEOs expressed confidence about the trajectory of the administration's proposed USD 1.5 trillion budget through Congress. All of them, however, expressed confidence that the budget would be up, and none of them expressed concern about the possibility of a continuing resolution, which has become routine.

The more important development is in missile production. Lockheed Martin and RTX each described frameworks for production growth that contemplate three to four times current output over five to seven years. These frameworks include contractual guarantees that would reimburse the companies if the expected demand does not materialize. This is a structural change in how the Department of Defense is approaching procurement. Instead of placing annual orders with uncertain follow-ons, the DoD is signaling multi-year commitments that allow suppliers to invest in capacity.

The implications for the supply chain are profound. Solid rocket motor suppliers, including L3Harris and Northrop Grumman, are responding with capacity expansions. The OEMs for tactical missiles are seeking multiple sources for specific motors, which creates opportunities for new entrants like Anduril, which has acquired a solid rocket motor supplier and invested in in-house production. Microelectronics, particularly seekers, and structural components are also becoming bottleneck areas. Companies like Howmet and ATI, which had not previously considered missiles a material market, are now seeing growth opportunities.

The open question is whether the production frameworks will survive a change in administration or a shift in congressional priorities. The contractual guarantees provide some protection, but they are not ironclad. If the fiscal environment deteriorates, multi-year commitments could be renegotiated. The risk is real, but it is a risk to the timing and magnitude of growth, not to the direction.

## The New Entrant Thesis Is Real, but the Path to Scale Is Narrower Than the Narrative Suggests

Anduril's presentation was the most closely watched session of the conference, and for good reason. The company is doubling revenue annually, expects to reach USD 30-40 billion in revenue within five years, and has won competitions against traditional primes on programs like the Air Force's Collaborative Combat Aircraft. CEO Brian Schimpf made a compelling case that the defense industrial base is undergoing a generational shift, driven by the recognition that traditional procurement processes are too slow and too focused on exquisite solutions.

The strategic insight from the discussion, however, was more nuanced than the headline narrative. Schimpf acknowledged that many new entrants will not survive. The common pattern among startups is to begin as single-product or single-category companies. That model is extremely difficult to scale in defense because the addressable market within a narrow segment is quickly exhausted. Anduril has 25 business lines, which allows it to absorb variability across products. Smaller companies with one or two programs are likely to be acquired by the large primes.

The second critical insight was about the role of software. Anduril's Lattice platform is designed as a unifying software layer that connects, coordinates, and orchestrates multiple systems on the battlefield. This is fundamentally different from how defense has historically approached software, which has been platform-specific and siloed. The ability to integrate with legacy systems while building a modern software architecture is a genuine competitive advantage. The question is whether this advantage is sustainable or whether traditional primes will develop equivalent capabilities through acquisition or internal development.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How the Convergence of Commercial and Defense Supply Chains Will Reshape Industry Structure

The conference discussions highlighted a pattern that the report identifies but does not fully explore: the same companies that supply engine components for commercial aircraft are now supplying components for missiles and industrial gas turbines. This convergence has implications for industry structure that go beyond the near-term earnings outlook.

If commercial aerospace demand, defense missile demand, and industrial gas turbine demand are all growing simultaneously, the supply chain will face capacity constraints that are more severe than any single end-market would produce alone. The companies that own the critical bottlenecks—specialty materials, precision castings, solid rocket motors—will have pricing power that they have not enjoyed in decades. The companies that are dependent on those bottlenecks will face margin pressure.

The report does not model this scenario. It does not answer the question of how pricing dynamics will evolve when a single supplier serves three end-markets that are all in upcycles. It does not address whether the contractual guarantees in missile production frameworks will extend to supplier pricing, or whether suppliers will be able to capture the full value of their scarcity. These are the questions that the full report should address, and they are the questions that will determine whether the structural shift in demand translates into structural improvement in margins.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Distinguish Between Cyclical and Structural Exposure

The following framework is designed to help investors distinguish between companies that are benefiting from a cyclical upswing and companies that are experiencing a structural improvement in their earnings power.

First, is the company's revenue growth driven by capacity additions or by pricing? Capacity-driven growth is more likely to be cyclical, because capacity can be added by competitors. Pricing-driven growth is more likely to be structural, because it reflects scarcity that cannot be easily replicated. Companies that own bottleneck technologies, such as specialty castings or solid rocket motors, are more likely to enjoy structural pricing power.

Second, is the company's end-market diversification genuine or superficial? A company that serves commercial aerospace, defense, and industrial gas turbines with the same core technology has genuine diversification. A company that serves multiple end-markets but with different technologies and different production processes has superficial diversification. The former benefits from economies of scope. The latter faces diseconomies of complexity.

Third, is the company's competitive position improving or eroding relative to new entrants? The defense market is clearly opening to new players. Companies that can demonstrate speed, cost efficiency, and software integration will gain share. Companies that rely on incumbency and program lock-in will lose share over time. The question is not whether the threat from new entrants is real. It is whether the company in question has a credible response.

# The Q1 Earnings Season Reveals a Two-Speed Economy That Is Resilient on the Surface but Conceals a Fragile Consumer and a Divergent Corporate Landscape

The first quarter earnings season for the S&P 500 delivered a headline that would comfort any observer: revenues grew at their fastest pace since 2021, earnings surprised to the upside by a wide margin, and corporate guidance remained constructive. A global investment bank report parsing the results of nearly all index constituents confirms that economic activity accelerated through the first three months of the year, with real revenues excluding energy rising 6.3 percent year-over-year. The data suggest that the American corporate sector entered 2026 with genuine momentum, and the initial shock of the Iran conflict and the associated spike in oil prices did not derail first-quarter spending.

Yet the same report that documents this strength also carries a cautionary subtext that becomes more troubling the closer one examines it. The resilience is real, but it is not uniform. The revenue growth is concentrated among the largest public companies, especially in technology. The consumer appears healthy in the aggregate, but the underlying cash flow position of households is deteriorating. The war has not yet crushed margins, but input costs are rising and corporate guidance for the second quarter already reflects downward revisions in the sectors most exposed to higher prices. The earnings season tells a story of a two-speed economy: one powered by hyperscaler capital expenditure and artificial intelligence investment, and another weighed down by a consumer whose saving rate is depleted and whose real disposable income has fallen.

The critical question for investors and strategists is not whether the first quarter was strong. It was. The question is whether the second half of 2026 can sustain this trajectory when the temporary boost from tax refunds fades, when the drag from elevated gasoline prices compounds, and when the margin pressure from rising input costs begins to show up in reported earnings. The earnings season provides the raw material for answering that question. The answer it suggests is that the economy is resilient, but the resilience is fragile and increasingly dependent on a narrow set of drivers.

## The Broad-Based Revenue Strength Conceals a Concentration That Makes the Aggregate Number Misleading

The headline real revenue growth of 6.3 percent year-over-year is an impressive figure, and it is easy to read it as a clean signal that the economy is firing on all cylinders. But the report makes clear that this aggregate number overstates the breadth of the expansion. When one strips out the technology sector, real revenue growth for the rest of the S&P 500 drops to just 2 percent. For the median company, the figure is a more moderate 5 percent. The outperformance relative to GDP growth is real, but it is driven disproportionately by the largest public companies, and within that group, overwhelmingly by the large technology firms.

This concentration matters because it changes how one should interpret the resilience of corporate revenues as a macroeconomic signal. The historical relationship between S&P 500 revenue growth and GDP growth suggests that the current pace of revenue expansion is running roughly two percentage points above what the 2.6 percent real GDP growth would normally support. That gap is not a sign of hidden strength in the broader economy. It is a sign that the largest firms, particularly those in technology, are capturing an outsized share of economic activity. The median company is doing fine, but it is not doing as well as the index average suggests.

The implication for strategy is straightforward. An investor or executive who relies on the aggregate S&P 500 revenue number as a proxy for the health of the corporate sector is likely to overestimate the resilience of the average firm. The concentration of growth means that the tail risks in the economy are larger than the headline suggests. If the technology sector experiences a slowdown, or if hyperscaler capex begins to decelerate, the revenue growth of the index could deteriorate rapidly because the rest of the corporate sector is already growing much more slowly. The earnings season data do not predict such a slowdown, but they do reveal that the structure of the expansion is narrow, and narrow expansions are inherently more vulnerable to shocks.

## The Consumer Appears Healthy in the First Quarter, but the Cash Flow Fundamentals Point to a Significant Slowdown in the Second Half

The report documents that consumer-facing companies reported solid sales growth in the first quarter, with the median consumer discretionary firm posting 9 percent year-over-year revenue growth and the median consumer staples firm posting 5 percent. Alternative measures of nominal consumer spending, including those that exclude gasoline purchases, continued to grow at roughly the same rate in recent months. The management commentary on earnings calls, while more cautious than in previous quarters, remained around its historical average. On the surface, the consumer held up well through the first quarter despite the jump in oil prices.

The report does not leave this observation unchallenged. It presents a detailed argument that the consumer is heading into a period of much weaker spending growth, and the logic is based on fundamentals that are difficult to dismiss. The personal saving rate is low. Real disposable income fell 1.1 percent year-over-year in the most recent monthly data. The boost from strong tax refunds is fading. Elevated inflation and higher gasoline prices will continue to act as a headwind. The report forecasts real consumer spending growth of just 1.3 percent in the second half of 2026, which is below consensus expectations.

The gap between the first-quarter data and the second-half forecast is not a contradiction. It is a lag effect. The consumer entered the first quarter with some residual momentum from the previous year, and the war-related spike in oil prices occurred late enough in the quarter that its full impact had not yet been felt in spending data. The earnings calls reflect this timing: management teams were still reporting solid results, but their guidance and commentary already reflected growing concern about the outlook. The report's quantitative measure of sentiment around the consumer on earnings calls declined sequentially, even though it remained around its historical average.

The most striking finding in this section is the normalization of spending growth between low-income and high-income households. The gap in same-store sales growth between retailers serving lower-income zip codes and those serving higher-income zip codes narrowed from 3.1 percentage points in the first quarter of 2025 to 1.1 percentage points in the first quarter of 2026. This convergence could be interpreted as a positive sign of broadening consumer strength. The report argues that it is more likely the calm before the storm. The factors that will weigh on consumption in the second half—government spending cuts, lower immigration reducing job and income growth, and higher gasoline prices—will disproportionately hit lower-income households. The narrowing gap may reflect a temporary convergence that is about to reverse.

## The War Is Already Compressing Margins in the Most Exposed Sectors, and the Full Pass-Through to Earnings Has Not Yet Occurred

The report's analysis of the war's impact on corporate margins is one of its most actionable sections. The company price announcement tracker, which captures both prices paid and received, increased to its highest level since late 2023. Companies are discussing higher prices more frequently, with the most commonly cited factor being higher oil prices, followed by increases in shipping costs, resin-based materials, and computer memory. The pattern is clear: input costs are rising across a broad front.

The critical insight is that companies expect these higher costs to compress margins, and the market is already pricing that compression. The report shows that analysts revised their margin expectations downward the most in precisely the sectors where the price announcement tracker increased the most. This correlation is not a coincidence. It is a signal that companies in those sectors are communicating to investors that they cannot fully pass through the higher input costs to customers, at least not in the near term.

The magnitude of the margin revision varies by sector, but the direction is uniform across the most exposed industries. This suggests that the margin compression is not a sector-specific issue but a broader phenomenon that will affect any company with significant exposure to oil, shipping, or resin-based inputs. The report does not quantify the aggregate impact on S&P 500 earnings, but the implication is clear: if input costs remain elevated, second-quarter margins will be lower than what was expected before the war, and the downward pressure will be most severe in the sectors that are already seeing the largest cost increases.

One nuance that deserves attention is the report's assessment of labor cost pressures. Mentions of wages and labor costs on earnings calls have fallen to the bottom of the pre-pandemic range. The report's tracker suggests that wages have increased 3.6 percent over the last year and 3.1 percent annualized in the first quarter, which is below the 4 percent rate that the report estimates would be consistent with 2 percent price inflation. This is a meaningful counterpoint to the margin pressure story. If labor costs remain contained, the compression from input costs may be temporary and may reverse as the war-related price spikes fade. The margin story for the second quarter is negative, but the underlying inflation dynamics are not as bad as the headline input cost data suggest.

## Artificial Intelligence Investment Continues to Surge, but the Labor Market Impact Is Still Concentrated in a Narrow Set of Roles

The report confirms that AI remains the dominant theme in corporate strategy and capital allocation. Hyperscaler capex plans surprised to the upside once again, with analysts revising their 2026 expectations 12 percent higher during the earnings season to over 750 billion dollars, representing 83 percent growth versus 2025. The report expects real business fixed investment to grow nearly 8 percent this year, with AI-related spending contributing three percentage points of that growth and tax incentives from the fiscal package contributing another three percentage points. The AI investment cycle is real, large, and accelerating.

The report also addresses the labor market implications of AI, and the finding is more nuanced than either the optimistic or pessimistic narratives might suggest. The share of management teams mentioning AI in the context of layoffs or hiring freezes is still very modest. Business surveys and the report's own analysis indicate that the aggregate labor market impact has been limited so far. The headline conclusion is that AI is not yet driving significant job displacement across the economy.

The nuance comes from the narrow segment of the economy where AI-driven layoffs are increasing sharply: technology companies and startups. In this segment, the frequency of companies tying layoffs to AI increased significantly in the first quarter, with companies often attributing reductions to advances in agentic models. The roles most frequently affected are operations, data labeling, software development, and customer support. This is not a broad-based phenomenon, but it is a real one, and it is concentrated in the sector that is also driving the largest AI investment.

The report provides a critical counterpoint to the layoff data. The same companies that are cutting some roles are increasing their demand for other roles. Online job postings data from LinkUp show increased demand for positions focused on computer hardware, systems engineering, and research. The pattern is not one of net job destruction but of reallocation. Companies are replacing some functions with AI-driven automation while simultaneously hiring for the skills needed to build and maintain the AI infrastructure. The net effect on employment in the technology sector is unclear from the data presented, but the direction of change is toward a different skill composition.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Consumer Weakness and AI Investment Creates an Unresolved Macro Puzzle

The report is thorough in its analysis of the consumer, the war's impact on margins, and the AI investment cycle. What it does not fully resolve is how these forces interact. The consumer is expected to slow sharply in the second half of the year, with real spending growth of just 1.3 percent. AI investment is expected to continue accelerating, with hyperscaler capex growing 83 percent. These two trajectories are on a collision course, and the report does not provide a clear framework for determining which force will dominate the macroeconomic outcome.

If consumer spending slows as much as the report forecasts, the revenue growth of consumer-facing companies will deteriorate, and the broader economy will feel the drag. But the AI investment cycle is large enough to offset some of that drag in the national accounts. The report estimates that business fixed investment will grow nearly 8 percent this year, with AI contributing three percentage points. That is a meaningful boost, but it is concentrated in a narrow set of sectors and a narrow set of companies. The question is whether the AI investment boom can sustain itself if the broader economy weakens and corporate revenues outside of technology begin to slow.

The report also does not fully address the feedback loop between AI investment and the consumer. The investment itself creates jobs in hardware, systems engineering, and research, but those jobs are concentrated in high-skill, high-income roles. The jobs being displaced by AI are in operations, data labeling, and customer support, which are lower-skill, lower-income roles. The net effect on aggregate consumer income and spending power depends on the relative magnitude of job creation and job displacement, and the report does not provide enough data to make that calculation. The pattern of reallocation suggests that AI may be contributing to the same kind of income divergence that the report documents in the consumer spending analysis: high-income households benefit from the investment boom while lower-income households face headwinds from automation and higher gasoline prices.

## A Decision Framework for Interpreting the Second Half: Monitor Three Leading Indicators That Bridge the Micro and Macro Data

The earnings season data provide a rich set of inputs for decision-making, but the challenge is translating the micro-level insights into actionable macro judgments. The following framework is designed to help investors and executives identify the key leading indicators that will determine whether the resilience of the first quarter persists or gives way to a sharper slowdown.

The first indicator to monitor is the trajectory of real disposable income. The report identifies this as the fundamental driver of consumer spending, and the April data already showed a 1.1 percent year-over-year decline. If this trend continues or accelerates, the forecast of 1.3 percent real consumer spending growth in the second half will prove optimistic. The key question is whether wage growth, which remains below the level consistent with 2 percent inflation, can accelerate enough to offset the drag from higher gasoline prices and fading tax refunds. The earnings call data on labor costs suggest that wage pressures are subdued, which is good for inflation but bad for consumer income growth.

The second indicator is the pass-through of input costs to consumer prices. The report shows that companies are discussing higher prices more frequently, but it does not yet show a corresponding acceleration in the prices they charge. The margin compression in the second quarter will be determined by how much of the input cost increase companies can pass through. If they can pass through most of it, margins will hold up better but inflation will accelerate, putting further pressure on the consumer. If they cannot pass through the costs, margins will compress but inflation will remain more contained. The trade-off is real, and the outcome will shape both corporate earnings and consumer spending power.

The third indicator is the sustainability of hyperscaler capex. The report documents that capex plans surprised to the upside in the first quarter, but it does not provide a view on whether this pace can continue. The 83 percent growth rate is extraordinary, and it is reasonable to expect some deceleration as the base effect becomes more challenging and as companies digest the investments they have already made. The critical question is whether the AI investment cycle has reached a point of diminishing returns or whether it is still in the early stages of a multiyear expansion. The answer to this question will determine whether the boost to business fixed investment can offset the consumer slowdown or whether both forces will weaken simultaneously.

# Tariff Distortions Are Masking a Genuine but Fragile Recovery in Swiss Watch Exports

The headline numbers for Swiss watch exports in April 2026 look alarming. A 16.6% year-on-year decline, accelerating sharply from March's 6% drop, appears to signal a sector in retreat. But the real story is not about collapsing demand. It is about how tariff-driven base effects have created a statistical mirage that obscures an underlying, though fragile, recovery. The April 2025 comparison period was artificially inflated by a wave of pull-forward shipments into the United States ahead of the "Liberation Day" tariff implementation. When that distorted base fell out of the calculation, the year-on-year numbers inevitably cratered.

This is the critical analytical trap for investors today. Reading the monthly export data at face value leads to a profoundly misleading picture of the luxury watch market's health. The true test of demand lies not in volatile year-on-year comparisons but in the trajectory relative to pre-tariff baseline levels. By that measure, the picture is more nuanced: US demand remains resilient, Greater China is showing tentative stabilization, and the Middle East, once a rare bright spot, is now flashing warning signals.

The current environment demands a strategic lens that separates signal from noise. Tariff volatility will continue to distort year-on-year comparisons for at least the next several months, particularly around July and August when additional pull-forward effects from 2025 will drop out of the calculation. Investors who react to the headline data without understanding these mechanics risk making precisely the wrong portfolio decisions. The real question is not whether April was a bad month. It is whether the underlying demand trajectory supports a sustained recovery, and which luxury names are best positioned to navigate the choppy waters ahead.

## The US Market Is Resilient When Measured Against a Sensible Baseline, but the Middle East Is a Growing Risk

The headline 56.4% year-on-year collapse in Swiss watch exports to the United States is the single largest contributor to April's overall decline. Any investor looking at that number in isolation would reasonably conclude that American consumers have turned away from luxury timepieces. But this conclusion is wrong. When measured against April 2024 levels, exports to the US actually grew by 8.9%. This is a slight moderation from the 12.1% growth rate seen in the first quarter of 2026 relative to the same period in 2024, but it is still a positive number. The American consumer has absorbed tariff-related price increases, according to company commentary, and demand has not collapsed.

This resilience is not uniform across all export destinations, and the divergence is instructive. Exports to France surged 46.3% year on year, a trend that began in December 2025. But this is almost certainly a reflection of re-exports through French distribution hubs rather than genuine domestic French demand. The data from France is a reminder that Swiss watch export statistics are not a pure demand signal. They are also a function of logistics, inventory management, and trade routing decisions by brands and distributors.

The more concerning development is the across-the-board decline in exports to the Middle East. The UAE fell 9.5%, Saudi Arabia dropped 17.3%, and Qatar declined 12.0%. This region was a rare pocket of growth for the luxury watch sector throughout much of 2025, and its deterioration is not a base-effect story. It reflects the impact of ongoing regional conflict. The risk here is twofold. First, the Middle East has been an important growth driver for several luxury brands, and its weakening removes a key support for the sector's recovery. Second, the indirect consequences of a drawn-out conflict could be more damaging than the direct trade disruption. Reduced travel between regions, higher oil prices feeding through to inflation, and weaker consumer confidence could all spill over into luxury demand in Europe and Asia. The Middle East situation is a risk that deserves far more attention than it is currently receiving from the market.

## Price Segment and Material Data Confirm the Base-Effect Story, Not a Structural Shift in Consumer Preferences

The breakdown by price segment provides further confirmation that April's decline is primarily a statistical artifact. The CHF 200-500 segment was the only category to record export value growth, at 7.7% year on year. This segment benefited from a relatively easy comparable base. At the other end of the spectrum, watches priced above CHF 3,000 faced the most challenging comparison, having grown 19.8% in April 2025, and consequently declined the most at 19.0%. The mid-range segments of CHF 500-3,000 and below CHF 200 posted more moderate declines of 12.2% and 14.3% respectively.

This pattern is exactly what one would expect if the April 2025 base had been artificially inflated by pull-forward demand. The high-end segment, which is most exposed to US demand and to tariff-sensitive buyers, saw the largest pull-forward effect and therefore the largest subsequent correction. The low-end segment, which is less sensitive to tariff dynamics, saw a smaller distortion. The data does not suggest that consumers are trading down or that demand for high-end watches has structurally weakened. It simply reflects the arithmetic of comparing a normal month to an abnormal one.

The material data tells the same story. Precious metal watches declined 24.3% year on year, the largest drop across all material categories. This is because precious metal watches had surged 23.4% in April 2025. Watches made from other metals, by contrast, were the only category to record year-on-year value growth, at 10.0%, supported by a particularly weak comparison base of minus 17.0% in April 2025. Steel watches declined 18.1% against a base that had grown 13.8%. Bimetallic watches fell 9.8% against a base that had surged 44.5%. In every case, the magnitude of the decline is proportional to the magnitude of the prior year's surge. This is not a demand story. It is a comp story.

## Greater China Is Stabilizing, but the Recovery Is Not Yet Self-Sustaining

The data from Greater China offers a more mixed picture that deserves careful interpretation. Exports to Hong Kong grew 13.5% year on year in April, while exports to Mainland China grew 17.1%. On the surface, these are encouraging numbers that suggest Chinese demand is recovering. But again, the base effect is at work. In April 2025, shipments to China were likely redirected toward the United States as brands scrambled to fulfill orders ahead of tariff implementation. The comparison base is therefore artificially low.

When measured against April 2024 levels, exports to Greater China remained approximately 15% lower. This is broadly in line with the trend observed throughout the first quarter of 2026 when compared to the same period in 2024. The conclusion is that Chinese demand is not deteriorating further, but it is also not recovering strongly. It is stabilizing at a level that remains well below the peak. This is consistent with a consumer environment in China that is still constrained by weak property markets, subdued consumer confidence, and a cautious approach to discretionary spending.

The implication for luxury investors is that a Chinese recovery cannot be relied upon to drive the next leg of growth for the sector. The best case scenario is a slow and steady improvement that brings demand back toward pre-tariff levels over the course of the next 12 to 18 months. The worst case is that stabilization gives way to further weakness if the macro environment in China deteriorates. This uncertainty is one reason why a defensive posture in luxury exposure makes sense.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Durability of US Demand Under Continued Tariff Pressure

The report provides compelling evidence that US demand has absorbed the initial round of tariff-related price increases. Company commentary suggests that consumers have accepted higher prices, at least so far. But this raises a critical question that the data does not yet answer: how much pricing power do luxury watch brands actually have, and at what point does tariff accumulation begin to erode demand?

The current tariff environment is not static. The "Liberation Day" tariffs that drove the April 2025 pull-forward were only the beginning. Additional tariff measures have been implemented since, and the trajectory of US trade policy remains highly uncertain. Each new round of tariffs forces brands to make a choice: absorb the cost into margins, pass it through to consumers, or find workarounds through supply chain reconfiguration. The report suggests that brands have so far chosen to pass through costs, and consumers have accepted them. But there is a limit to how much price increases the market can absorb before demand begins to crack.

The data does not yet show that limit being reached, but it also does not prove that it cannot be reached. The resilience of US demand in April 2026, when measured against 2024 levels, is encouraging. But it is only one data point. If tariffs continue to escalate, and if price increases accumulate, the risk of demand destruction grows. Investors need to monitor not just the headline export numbers but also the pricing strategies of individual brands and the elasticity of demand at different price points. This is an open analytical question that the current data cannot resolve, and it is the single most important uncertainty facing the sector.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating Tariff-Driven Volatility in Luxury Exposure

For investors trying to make sense of this environment, the key is to separate the data into three categories: signal, noise, and watch items. The noise is the month-to-month year-on-year export data, which will remain distorted by tariff base effects for at least the next several months. This data should be ignored for investment decision-making purposes. The signal is the demand trajectory relative to pre-tariff baselines. By this measure, US demand is resilient, Chinese demand is stable but weak, and Middle Eastern demand is deteriorating. The watch items are the indirect consequences of the Middle East conflict and the cumulative impact of additional tariff rounds on US pricing power.

Within this framework, the defensive posture recommended in the report makes strategic sense. High-quality names at fair value, such as Richemont with its strong jewelry momentum and leadership position, offer a core exposure that can weather the volatility. Self-help stories with promising trajectories, such as Burberry's well-executed turnaround or Ferragamo's early-stage transformation, offer upside potential that is less dependent on the macro environment. LVMH occupies an interesting middle ground, balancing high-quality exposure with self-help potential through Dior's revival and cost efficiencies.

The critical insight is that this is not a time for aggressive positioning based on top-down macro calls. The data is too noisy, and the risks are too asymmetric. A defensive core with selective exposure to idiosyncratic turnaround stories is the appropriate strategy until the tariff environment stabilizes and the underlying demand trajectory becomes clearer.

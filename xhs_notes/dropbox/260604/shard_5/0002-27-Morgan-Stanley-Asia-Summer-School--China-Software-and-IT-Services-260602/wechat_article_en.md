# China's Software Market Is Not Catching Up to the US—It Is Building a Different Stack Entirely

The dominant narrative in global technology investing holds that China's software and IT services market is simply lagging behind the United States and will eventually converge. This framing is misleading. A careful examination of the structural data reveals a more nuanced reality: China's enterprise IT spending is not merely smaller—it is fundamentally different in composition, driven by distinct demand forces, and may follow a trajectory that diverges from the US model rather than replicating it.

According to a recent global investment bank report, China's software and IT services market represents only 4.9 percent of the global total, compared to the United States' 47.7 percent share. Software and IT services account for just 22 percent of China's total IT spending, versus 59 percent in the US. When measured as a percentage of nominal GDP, China spends 0.6 percent on software and IT services—one-sixth of the US level of 3.6 percent. These numbers are stark, but they should not be read as a simple gap to be closed.

The argument that China's software market will inevitably grow to resemble America's overlooks the structural differences in how enterprises in each economy operate. China's IT spending remains heavily weighted toward hardware and infrastructure—a tilt that reflects both the country's manufacturing-heavy economic base and the distinct path of its digital transformation. The question for investors is not whether China will catch up, but whether the software opportunity in China is being systematically underestimated because we are using the wrong benchmark.

This article examines the structural drivers of China's enterprise software market, the implications of the AI infrastructure buildout, and the strategic decisions facing investors who must navigate a market that looks increasingly different from its US counterpart.

## The Composition of China's IT Spending Reflects a Different Economic Logic, Not Just a Developmental Stage

The most revealing data point in the report is not the absolute size of China's software market but its composition relative to GDP. At 0.6 percent of nominal GDP, China's software and IT services spending is lower than India (0.9 percent), Brazil (1.5 percent), Japan (2.1 percent), Australia (2.4 percent), and dramatically lower than the US (3.6 percent). This pattern cannot be explained solely by per capita income levels, because India—a lower-income economy—spends proportionally more.

The explanation lies in the structure of China's economy and the nature of its enterprise demand. China's economic growth has been driven by manufacturing, infrastructure, and state-directed investment. These sectors tend to spend heavily on hardware—servers, networking equipment, industrial control systems—rather than on enterprise software licenses and professional services. A factory floor requires programmable logic controllers and sensors before it needs CRM software. A state-owned enterprise prioritizes IT localization and security compliance over marketing automation.

The report's survey data from China CIOs confirms this pattern. In the first half of 2026, CIOs expected external IT spending growth of only 5.3 percent for software and 7.0 percent for professional services, compared to 3.6 percent for hardware, communication, and networking equipment. While software spending growth expectations have moderated from the 13.1 percent recorded in the second half of 2025, the overall trajectory suggests that enterprises are not abandoning software investment but are becoming more selective.

The strategic implication is clear: China's software market will grow, but it will not mirror the US software stack. The opportunity is concentrated in specific sub-segments that align with China's economic priorities—industrial software, supply chain management, government digitization, and AI infrastructure—rather than in broad-based enterprise SaaS adoption.

## The AI Infrastructure Buildout Is Reshaping the Software Market from the Bottom Up

The report introduces a five-layer AI stack framework that is essential for understanding where value is being created in China's technology ecosystem. The layers—energy, chips and computing infrastructure, cloud infrastructure, AI models, and applications—are interdependent, and each represents a multi-billion-dollar opportunity. But the critical insight is that China's AI buildout is proceeding from the bottom up, not from the top down.

China's data center capacity has grown from 4.0 gigawatts in 2017 to an estimated 27.0 gigawatts in 2025, with projections reaching 31.0 gigawatts in 2026. This physical infrastructure buildout is the foundation for everything above it. The cloud infrastructure layer—data centers, hyperscalers, and orchestration software—is where the most visible spending is occurring today. But as the report's framework makes clear, the higher layers (AI models and applications) depend entirely on the strength of these lower layers.

This bottom-up dynamic has two important consequences for software investors. First, the near-term opportunity in China's software market is heavily weighted toward infrastructure software—system infrastructure software, application development and deployment platforms, and data management tools—rather than end-user applications. The report's IDC estimates show system infrastructure software growing from US$12.9 billion in 2025 to US$19.9 billion in 2030, while applications grow from US$24.1 billion to US$42.7 billion over the same period. The fastest-growing sub-segments within application development and deployment include artificial intelligence platforms (42 percent CAGR), app development (30 percent CAGR), and data management software (23 percent CAGR).

Second, the bottom-up buildout means that the applications layer—where most end-user value is created—will take longer to mature in China than in the US. The US market has already gone through multiple cycles of enterprise software adoption, and AI applications are being layered onto existing digital infrastructure. In China, the infrastructure itself is still being constructed. This does not mean the opportunity is smaller; it means the timing and sequencing are different.

## The CIO Survey Reveals a Market That Is Cautious but Structurally Optimistic

The report's CIO survey data provides a granular view of enterprise sentiment that complicates any simple bullish or bearish narrative. The percentage of CIOs expecting upward IT budget revisions fell from 48 percent in the second half of 2025 to 38 percent in the first half of 2026, while those expecting downward revisions rose from 15 percent to 27 percent. This suggests a near-term cautiousness that reflects macroeconomic uncertainty and the lag effects of China's economic slowdown.

However, the longer-term trends tell a different story. Overall external IT spending growth expectations for 2026 stand at 5.2 percent, down from 12.6 percent in the 2025 survey but still positive. More importantly, the structural drivers of IT demand—nominal GDP growth with late-cycle effects, efficiency improvement to hedge rising labor costs, government requirements for digitization and IT localization, and the industry cycle of hardware and infrastructure investment—remain intact.

The report's historical data shows that software and IT services revenue growth has been volatile, ranging from negative 5 percent to positive 50 percent on a quarterly basis over the past seven years. This volatility is not a sign of a weak market but of a market that is highly sensitive to policy cycles, macroeconomic conditions, and technology transitions. The current period of subdued growth—revenue growth has been near zero percent for the past two years—is likely a pause rather than a structural decline.

The key question is what will reaccelerate growth. The report points to two potential catalysts: the AI infrastructure buildout and the government's push for IT localization. Both are multi-year trends that will create sustained demand for software and services, but they will not produce a uniform uplift across all sub-segments. Investors need to identify which companies are positioned to benefit from these specific drivers rather than betting on a broad market recovery.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Profitability Paradox and the Localization Premium

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is the profitability paradox of China's software companies. While the US software market features numerous companies with operating margins above 30 percent and rule-of-40 metrics, China's software companies have historically struggled with profitability. The report does not provide a clear framework for understanding whether this is a temporary phenomenon driven by competitive dynamics and investment cycles, or a structural feature of a market where customers are less willing to pay for software.

The second unresolved question concerns the premium for IT localization. The report identifies government requirements for digitization and IT localization as a demand driver, but it does not quantify how much of a premium domestic software vendors can command over international alternatives. If localization is driven by compliance rather than value, the pricing power of domestic vendors may be limited. If it is driven by a genuine need for tailored solutions, the opportunity could be significantly larger.

Third, the report's global comparison data shows that China's software and IT services spending as a percentage of total IT spending is projected to remain flat at 22 to 23 percent through 2030, while the US figure declines from 59 percent to 54 percent over the same period. This convergence is driven by a decline in the US share, not an increase in China's share. The report does not fully explain why China's software share is not rising, or what would need to change for it to do so.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the analysis; they are the natural boundaries of a research report that must focus on what can be measured. For investors, they represent the most important areas for further investigation.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating China's Software Market

Based on the report's analysis, investors should evaluate China's software opportunities through a three-dimensional framework rather than relying on aggregate market growth projections.

The first dimension is the layer of the AI stack. Companies operating in the lower layers—infrastructure software, cloud platforms, and data management—will benefit from the current buildout phase but may face margin pressure as competition intensifies. Companies in the applications layer will have more pricing power but will need to wait for the infrastructure to mature before their addressable market expands meaningfully.

The second dimension is the end-market exposure. Companies serving manufacturing, government, and state-owned enterprises will benefit from localization mandates and industrial digitization. Companies serving consumer-facing and export-oriented industries will be more exposed to macroeconomic cycles and global competition.

The third dimension is the business model. The report's data suggests that China's software market is moving toward a mix of cloud-based and on-premise solutions, but the pace of cloud adoption varies significantly by sub-segment. Companies with recurring revenue models will command higher valuations but may face longer sales cycles. Companies with project-based revenue will have more immediate cash flow but less visibility.

The most attractive opportunities are likely to be found at the intersection of these three dimensions: companies that operate in the applications layer, serve government and industrial end-markets with localization-driven demand, and have successfully transitioned to recurring revenue models. These companies are rare, but the report's data provides the tools to identify them.

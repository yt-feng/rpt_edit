# The K-Shaped Recovery in China’s Housing Market Is Not a Recovery at All

The most dangerous assumption an investor can make about China’s property market in 2026 is that stabilization in Tier 1 cities signals a broader turnaround. The data from May tells a different story. Secondary home prices across 85 sample cities fell 0.5% month-on-month, accelerating from April’s 0.4% decline, with 93% of tracked cities recording monthly drops. Yet within this broad-based weakness, Tier 1 cities held steady at just 0.1% month-on-month declines. This divergence is not a precursor to a synchronized recovery. It is the defining structural feature of a market that has permanently bifurcated.

The narrative of a housing market bottom has circulated for over a year, fueled by policy easing and intermittent sales bursts. Each time, the data has disappointed. What is different now is that the pattern of divergence has become systematic rather than cyclical. Secondary listing volumes, visitation trends, and price momentum are no longer moving together across city tiers. They are moving in opposite directions, and the gap is widening. Investors who treat the property sector as a single tradeable theme are missing the most important analytical insight of this cycle: the K-shape is the trend, not a temporary deviation.

The implications extend beyond real estate. China’s property sector has historically been a bellwether for consumer confidence, credit appetite, and local government fiscal health. A market that remains soft in the vast majority of cities while showing selective strength in a handful of elite locations tells us something fundamental about the distribution of wealth, income expectations, and policy effectiveness. Understanding this requires abandoning the old framework of cyclical troughs and instead adopting a framework of structural divergence.

## Broad-Based Price Declines Are Accelerating, but the Headline Masks a Critical Tier-1 Exception

The headline numbers are unequivocal. Secondary listing home prices fell 0.5% month-on-month in May, compared with 0.4% in April. Year-on-year, the decline stands at 11.3%. The breadth of weakness is striking: 93% of sample cities recorded month-on-month declines, and 64% of those saw the pace of decline accelerate. In April, only 19% of cities experienced faster declines. This is not a market finding its footing. This is a market where downward pressure is intensifying across the vast majority of locations.

Yet the Tier 1 story is fundamentally different. These cities recorded only a 0.1% month-on-month decline, essentially flat. The report attributes this to stronger secondary home sales driven by policy easing in Shenzhen and Guangzhou in late April. That explanation is correct as far as it goes, but it understates the deeper dynamic. Tier 1 cities benefit from concentrated demand from high-income households, limited new supply, and a structural migration premium. Policy easing acts as a catalyst, but the underlying resilience comes from factors that lower-tier cities simply cannot replicate.

The so-what for investors is clear. A national average price trend is becoming a misleading statistic. It blends two entirely different markets. If you are invested in Tier 1 exposure, the data looks manageable. If your exposure is to Tier 2, Tier 3, or lower, the data is deteriorating. A strategy that relies on a broad-based recovery is not merely optimistic; it is analytically unsound.

## Secondary Listing Supply Is Shifting from a Flood to a Trickle, but That Does Not Mean Demand Is Returning

One of the more nuanced findings in the May data concerns secondary listing volumes. Total listings across roughly 50 sample cities edged up only 0.1% month-on-month, while new secondary listings continued to decline, falling 4% month-on-month and 10% year-on-year. On the surface, this could be interpreted as a positive supply-side signal. Fewer new listings mean less inventory overhang, which should eventually support prices.

But the interpretation requires caution. The decline in new listings is not necessarily a sign that homeowners are confident and holding firm. It may equally reflect that potential sellers are unwilling to list at current depressed prices. When prices are falling and transaction volumes are thin, the natural response for many homeowners is to withdraw from the market rather than accept a loss. This creates a statistical illusion of tightening supply when in reality, latent supply remains enormous.

The report notes that 67% of cities recorded month-on-month declines in new listings, down from 96% in April. This suggests that the supply contraction is becoming less broad-based, which could be an early sign that more sellers are testing the market. If that interpretation is correct, the modest decline in new listings may reverse in coming months, putting renewed pressure on prices. The critical unknown is whether the current supply dynamics represent a genuine reduction in overhang or merely a temporary pause in selling activity. Investors should watch the trajectory of new listings in June and July as a leading indicator.

## Visitation Data Confirms That the Spring Sales Bounce Has Faded

Perhaps the most forward-looking indicator in the report is the visitation data. Visits to agent shops across 45 sample cities declined 2% month-on-month in May, breaking the pattern of month-on-month increases seen in prior years. This decline comes after a strong March-April sales period, suggesting that the policy-driven boost has already been absorbed by the market.

The year-on-year comparison remains positive at 12%, but that is a lagging artifact of how weak May 2025 was. The sequential decline is what matters for the near-term outlook. If visitations are already cooling in May, the June and July data will likely be weaker. The report expects secondary sales volume to decelerate further in June and potentially turn year-on-year negative in the third quarter as policy effects and pent-up demand fade.

This is a critical judgment. If correct, it means that the entire spring recovery was a pull-forward of demand rather than a sustainable improvement in buyer sentiment. The implication is that the market is not healing; it is cycling through temporary demand bursts that exhaust themselves within a few months. Investors should prepare for a re-test of previous lows in transaction volumes, particularly in cities that saw the strongest spring sales.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: What Happens When Policy Stimulus Is Exhausted?

The report is clear that policy easing has been a key driver of the selective strength in Tier 1 cities. Shenzhen and Guangzhou relaxed policies in late April, and the effect is visible in the May data. But the report does not fully address what happens when the policy toolkit is depleted. Interest rates are already low. Down payment requirements have been reduced. Purchase restrictions have been loosened in multiple cities. Each new round of easing produces a smaller and shorter-lived response.

This raises a second-order question that the report touches on but does not fully resolve: Are we approaching the point where further policy easing becomes ineffective? The diminishing marginal impact of each stimulus round is a well-established pattern in China's property market. If the current policy settings are already priced in and the market is still softening, the next leg of price declines may occur in an environment where policymakers have fewer options to respond.

Investors should consider the possibility that the K-shaped divergence itself is a symptom of policy exhaustion. Tier 1 cities respond because they have the demographic and economic fundamentals to amplify policy signals. Lower-tier cities do not. If that diagnosis is correct, the gap between Tier 1 and the rest will continue to widen, and the national average will continue to drift lower even as a handful of cities stabilize.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the K-Shaped Market

The report provides enough data to construct a practical decision framework for investors. The framework has three layers: city tier, developer quality, and time horizon.

First, city tier is no longer a proxy for risk; it is the primary determinant of return outcomes. Tier 1 exposure is defensible, but it must be monitored for signs that the resilience is fading. Tier 2 exposure requires a selective approach focused on cities with strong net migration and diversified economic bases. Tier 3 and below should be avoided unless there is a specific catalyst that the market has not priced in.

Second, developer quality matters more than ever. The report favors beneficiaries with both industry beta and self-help alpha, meaning companies that benefit from any market improvement but also have company-specific drivers. CR Land and C&D International are cited as examples, with solid EPS outlooks, attractive dividend yields, and medium-term re-rating potential. The logic is that in a K-shaped market, you want exposure to companies that can outperform regardless of the macro backdrop.

Third, the time horizon for any property investment must account for the extended nature of this downcycle. The report expects a broadly soft month-on-month home price downtrend in 2026-27, with only a mild uptrend in select Tier 1 cities. That is a multi-year view. Short-term trades based on monthly data are likely to be whipsawed by policy announcements and seasonal effects. The appropriate horizon is 12 to 24 months, with a focus on whether the structural divergence is narrowing or widening.

The key monitoring indicators for the next three months are sales volumes, home prices, secondary listing volumes, transaction mix, and rental rates. If these indicators show stabilization in Tier 1 but continued deterioration elsewhere, the K-shape is confirmed. If Tier 1 also begins to weaken, the entire thesis of a managed downcycle comes into question.

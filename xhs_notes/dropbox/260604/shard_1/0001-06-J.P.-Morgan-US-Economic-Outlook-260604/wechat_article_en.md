# The U.S. Economy Is Not as Fragile as the Sentiment Data Suggests — But That Is Exactly What Makes It Vulnerable

Since early 2025, the economic policy uncertainty index has surged to levels not seen since the onset of the pandemic. Consumer sentiment, by some measures, has nearly touched historic lows. Professional forecasters have assigned elevated probabilities to a GDP decline for over four consecutive years. And yet, the U.S. economy has continued to grow. Real GDP expanded in 2024, is on track for growth in 2025, and the consensus forecast for 2026 remains positive. This divergence between what people feel and what the data shows is not a statistical anomaly. It is the central strategic fact that investors, corporate leaders, and policymakers must reckon with today.

The conventional narrative — that the economy is teetering on the edge of recession because sentiment is terrible — is wrong. But the opposite narrative — that the economy is fine because growth continues — is equally incomplete. The real story is more nuanced and more consequential. The U.S. economy has entered a regime where structural shifts in productivity, labor supply, and capital investment are masking the weakness in traditional cyclical indicators. This creates a dangerous asymmetry: the economy can withstand a great deal of bad news, but when the structural supports begin to buckle, the adjustment will be sudden and severe.

A major global investment bank's economic research report, published in June 2026, provides the analytical backbone for this argument. The report is not a collection of forecasts. It is a diagnosis of a system operating under unusual stress. The data it presents — on uncertainty, labor markets, productivity, corporate margins, inflation, and AI investment — tells a coherent story that challenges both the pessimists and the optimists. The question is not whether the economy will grow. The question is what kind of growth it will be, who will benefit, and what risks are being accumulated beneath the surface.

## The Widest Gap Between Sentiment and Fundamentals in Modern History Is a Leading Indicator of Regime Change, Not Noise

The report documents that the economic policy uncertainty index, which tracks the frequency of newspaper articles referencing policy uncertainty, reached approximately 540 in 2025 — a level exceeded only by the 2020 pandemic spike. By April 2026, the index had moderated to roughly 380, but this remains historically extreme. Over the same period, the Survey of Professional Forecasters has consistently assigned a probability of real GDP decline in the next quarter that hovers around 30 percent — a level that, in prior decades, was associated with actual recessions.

Yet the economy has not contracted. Real GDP growth in 2024 was approximately 2.5 percent, and the report projects continued growth through 2026. This is not a forecasting error. It is a structural shift in how the economy responds to uncertainty. The traditional mechanism — that rising uncertainty suppresses business investment and consumer spending — has been partially neutralized by two factors. First, corporate balance sheets are extraordinarily strong, with profit margins near historic highs. Second, the nature of uncertainty has changed. In prior cycles, uncertainty was about demand: would consumers stop buying? Today, uncertainty is about policy: what will tariffs look like next quarter? What will immigration policy be? These are supply-side uncertainties, and they affect the composition of growth more than its aggregate level.

The implication for decision-makers is clear: sentiment data cannot be read as a simple recession signal. The economy is not fragile in the traditional sense. It is resilient in a way that creates a false sense of security. When the regime eventually shifts — and it will — the trigger will not be a decline in consumer confidence. It will be a breakdown in one of the structural supports that have kept growth alive despite the noise.

## Slower Population Growth Has Fundamentally Altered the Labor Market's Cyclical Behavior, Making Wage Inflation Stickier and Recoveries Shallower

The report highlights a critical structural shift: private payroll growth has decelerated sharply, from approximately 5 percent year-over-year in 2022 to roughly 0.5 percent by 2025. This is not primarily a demand-side phenomenon. It reflects slower population growth, including a sharp reduction in immigration. The labor force is simply not expanding as fast as it used to.

This has two profound implications. First, the unemployment rate is no longer a reliable cyclical indicator. The report shows that the unemployment rate has remained relatively stable even as job growth has slowed. This is because the labor force is growing more slowly, so even modest job creation is sufficient to keep unemployment from rising sharply. The traditional relationship between job growth and unemployment — Okun's law — has weakened.

Second, and more importantly, wage growth has become stickier. The Employment Cost Index, which peaked at over 5 percent in 2023, has moderated to roughly 3.2 percent by early 2026. But it has not collapsed, as it would have in a typical recession. This is because employers are competing for a smaller pool of available workers. The report notes that the unemployment rate has likely peaked and that wage growth will only slow a bit further.

The strategic takeaway is that the labor market is no longer a source of cyclical cushion. In past downturns, falling wages and rising unemployment provided a natural adjustment mechanism that helped the economy rebalance. That mechanism is now blunted. If demand weakens, the adjustment will come through lower employment rather than lower wages, which means any downturn will feel sharper and more painful than the GDP numbers alone would suggest.

## The Best Productivity Run Since the Internet Rollout Is the Hidden Engine Keeping Growth Alive, But It Is Concentrated and Unsustainable

The report presents a striking chart: nonfarm business productivity growth, measured over twelve quarters annualized, has reached levels not seen since the late 1990s and early 2000s — the period of the Internet rollout. This productivity surge has been the economy's secret weapon. It has allowed GDP to grow even as job growth has slowed. It has kept corporate profit margins at historic highs. It has absorbed the impact of higher wages without triggering a destructive inflationary spiral.

But this productivity boom is not evenly distributed. It is being driven overwhelmingly by AI-related capital expenditure. The report shows that global semiconductor sales have grown from roughly $600 billion in 2021 to an estimated $1.3 trillion by 2026. Consensus forecasts for hyperscaler global capex show growth from $160 billion in 2023 to nearly $1 trillion by 2027. This is an investment cycle of historic proportions, and it is almost entirely concentrated in a small number of technology firms and their supply chains.

The problem is that this concentration creates a vulnerability. The report notes that AI is not yet a major driver of GDP growth in an accounting sense. In 2024, physical tech investment contributed only 0.1 percentage points to GDP growth, and net trade in tech goods subtracted 0.3 percentage points. In 2025, tech investment contributed 0.6 percentage points, but net trade subtracted 0.8 percentage points. The net contribution is small, and it is volatile.

Furthermore, AI adoption remains in the early innings. The report's data on daily AI usage at work shows that only about 12 percent of people used AI at work every day as of early 2026, and this figure has barely budged since mid-2024. The productivity gains from AI are real, but they are concentrated in a narrow slice of the economy. For the broader economy, the benefits are still largely prospective.

The strategic implication is that the current productivity boom is not a reliable foundation for sustained growth. It is a sector-specific phenomenon that could reverse if AI investment slows or if the promised productivity gains fail to materialize in the broader economy. The economy is running on a narrow engine, and that engine is exposed to both technological and geopolitical risks.

## Corporate Profit Margins Are at Record Highs, But This Is a Source of Vulnerability, Not Strength

The report shows that domestic nonfinancial corporate profit margins, measured as pre-tax profits including tariffs, have reached approximately 18 percent — a level that has only been exceeded during the immediate post-pandemic recovery. S&P 500 consensus earnings expectations for 2026 have risen by 7.5 percent year-to-date through May 2026.

At first glance, this looks like unalloyed good news. But the report's data reveals a more complicated picture. Profit margins are high because companies have been able to pass on higher costs to consumers. This is not sustainable in a regime where consumer sentiment is already at historic lows. The gap between high corporate margins and low consumer sentiment is a tension that must eventually resolve — either margins will compress, or sentiment will improve.

The report does not explicitly forecast which outcome will prevail, but the data suggests that margin compression is more likely. Core PCE inflation has been stuck above 3 percent and is not returning to 2 percent quickly. Core producer price inflation has accelerated to 5 percent, and nonfuel import prices are rising. The Fed's concerns have shifted from recession risk to inflation risk, and market-implied expectations now point to the first rate hike in September 2027.

This is a critical inflection point. If the Fed is forced to raise rates to contain inflation, the impact on profit margins will be double-edged. Higher rates will increase borrowing costs, compressing margins directly. And higher rates will slow demand, making it harder for companies to pass on costs. The current level of profit margins is not an equilibrium. It is a cyclical high that will revert, and the reversion will be painful for equity markets.

## The Biggest Downside Risk Is Not in the Data at All — It Is an Oil Supply Shock That the Report Can Only Condition On

The report includes a section that is unusually candid for a major institutional research piece. It identifies the biggest downside risk as an oil supply disruption in the Middle East, and it explicitly states that its predictions are conditioned on the Strait re-opening soon. The report compares the current Middle East conflict, which has resulted in an 18 percent supply loss in crude oil and condensate, to historical disruptions: Iran sanctions in 2018 (4 percent), the Libya civil war in 2011 (3.5 percent), Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in 2005 (1.5 percent), and the Gulf War in 1990 (2 percent). The current disruption is an order of magnitude larger than anything in recent memory.

This is the section of the report that raises the most urgent unresolved questions. How long can the Strait remain closed? What is the contingency plan if it does not re-open? How much strategic petroleum reserve capacity remains? What is the demand elasticity at current prices? The report does not answer these questions, and it cannot. They are fundamentally unknowable.

But the report's decision to include this risk analysis, and to condition its entire economic forecast on a geopolitical assumption, is a signal in itself. It tells the reader that the baseline forecast is fragile in a way that standard economic models cannot capture. The economy's resilience to policy uncertainty, labor shortages, and inflation is real. But it is not resilience to a 20 percent oil supply shock. That is a different category of risk, and it is the one that keeps serious analysts up at night.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Current Regime

For investors and corporate strategists, the report implies a clear decision framework with three dimensions.

First, distinguish between cyclical and structural signals. The sentiment data is a structural signal about the nature of uncertainty, not a cyclical signal about the timing of recession. The labor market data is a structural signal about population dynamics, not a cyclical signal about demand. The productivity data is a structural signal about technological change, not a cyclical signal about the business cycle. Misreading these signals leads to bad decisions.

Second, identify the concentration risks in your portfolio or business. The current expansion is being driven by a narrow set of factors: AI capex, high corporate margins, and a tight labor market. Each of these factors is exposed to a specific vulnerability. AI capex is exposed to a slowdown in technology spending. Corporate margins are exposed to Fed tightening. The labor market is exposed to a sudden demand shock. Diversification across these risk factors is essential.

Third, stress-test for the oil shock scenario. The report's most important contribution may be its explicit acknowledgment that the baseline forecast is conditional on a geopolitical assumption that could break at any time. Every investment thesis and corporate plan should include a scenario where oil prices double and stay high for an extended period. If that scenario is not survivable, the current positioning is too risky.

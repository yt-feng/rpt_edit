# India’s Data Center Boom Is an Electricity Story, Not a Technology Story

The most consequential investment insight for Indian infrastructure over the next decade is not about artificial intelligence models, fiber optic cables, or GPU supply chains. It is about who controls the electrons that power the servers. India’s data center capacity is expected to reach between 5 and 8 gigawatts by 2030, up from roughly 1.5 gigawatts today, and the recent Middle East crisis has pushed industry expectations toward the higher end of that range. This is not a gradual build. It is a structural acceleration that will reshape how power generation, transmission, and real estate assets are valued in the country.

The critical insight from observing the United States data center buildout is this: the biggest winners have not been the technology companies building the AI models. They have been the utilities and infrastructure owners that could deliver reliable, dispatchable power to the right locations. In the U.S. markets of PJM and ERCOT, where data center demand grew at 23% and 26% compound annual rates over the past decade, the utilities that owned gas and nuclear generation in those regions saw their market capitalizations increase fourfold to eightfold. The companies that merely owned land or built facilities without power access did not participate in that rally.

The same dynamic is now playing out in India, but with a distinct local twist. India does not have the same deregulated wholesale power markets as the United States. It does not have the same grid interconnection queue, which in the U.S. now averages six years. And it does not have the same abundance of available land with both transmission access and water supply. What India does have is a small number of conglomerates that control the full stack: land, power generation, transmission connectivity, and the balance sheet to build data center infrastructure at scale. The thesis of this article is straightforward: the asset-heavy data center opportunity in India will be captured by those who own the power ecosystem, not those who own the real estate or the compute hardware.

## The U.S. Playbook Reveals That Dispatchable Power, Not Renewable Capacity, Drives Utility Outperformance

The divergence in U.S. utility performance over the past four years is not random. Vistra, Constellation Energy, and NRG Energy have delivered returns of 400% to 800% while other major utilities like NextEra and Dominion have been flat to down. The differentiating factor is not company size, management quality, or renewable energy exposure. It is the ownership of dispatchable generation capacity in the two largest data center markets: PJM in the eastern United States and ERCOT in Texas.

Consider the pricing signal. In PJM, capacity prices rose from approximately $29 per megawatt-day for the 2024-2025 delivery year to roughly $329 per megawatt-day for 2026-2027. That is an elevenfold increase. The reason is that data centers require 24-hour, 7-day power with near-zero downtime tolerance. Intermittent renewable sources alone cannot meet that requirement without firming capacity from gas, nuclear, or coal. The three winning utilities all had significant dispatchable generation portfolios in PJM and ERCOT. They did not need to build new plants to benefit. They simply owned the assets that became dramatically more valuable as demand surged.

The second-order effect is equally important. Even coal plants that were scheduled for retirement have been kept online. More than 4.4 gigawatts of coal capacity retirements have been pushed out in the United States because the grid cannot afford to lose dispatchable megawatts when data center demand is compounding at over 20% annually. This is a structural shift, not a cyclical one. The era of easy renewable additions without firming capacity is ending in regions with high data center concentration.

For India, the implication is clear. The utilities that will benefit most are those with merchant power capacity, not just long-term power purchase agreements. Merchant plants can capture the scarcity pricing that emerges when data center operators compete for limited grid-connected megawatts. The Indian power market is less deregulated than PJM or ERCOT, but the physics of electricity is the same. When demand surges and supply is constrained, whoever owns the marginal megawatt wins.

## The Co-Location Model Is the Dominant Framework for India, and It Favors Balance Sheet Depth Over Technology Risk

There are two primary business models for data center asset owners globally. The first is the cloud provider model, where companies own the compute hardware, typically graphics processing units, and either own or lease the physical facility. This model generates 8 to 10 times higher revenue per IT megawatt than co-location, but it carries significantly higher capital expenditure requirements, shorter contract durations, and technology obsolescence risk. The second is the co-location model, where companies build and lease the physical data center infrastructure to hyperscalers or enterprises, who bring their own compute hardware.

The co-location model is the one that will dominate in India, and the reasons are structural. First, the capital expenditure for a co-location facility, excluding compute, is approximately 50 crore rupees per megawatt in India, which is lower than in the United States. The payback period is roughly five years. Second, Indian operators do not have the same access to GPU supply chains or the same willingness to take technology risk on rapidly depreciating hardware. Third, the hyperscalers themselves prefer to own the compute layer while leasing the infrastructure, because it allows them to standardize their global operations and avoid the complexity of managing Indian land acquisition, power procurement, and regulatory compliance.

This model shifts the competitive advantage away from technology expertise and toward balance sheet strength and execution capability. The winners will be those who can acquire large land parcels near substations, secure long-term power supply agreements, navigate state-level regulatory approvals, and fund the construction of facilities that cost tens of millions of dollars each. The technology risk sits with the hyperscaler customer, not the Indian operator.

The U.S. market provides a useful valuation benchmark. Co-location operators like Equinix and Digital Realty trade at approximately 22 to 23 times forward enterprise value to EBITDA. Recent private transactions, such as Blackstone’s acquisition of AirTrunk, were completed at roughly 21 times. These multiples reflect the market’s view that co-location assets offer predictable, long-duration cash flows with limited technology risk. Indian operators that can demonstrate similar contract structures and power availability should command comparable valuations over time.

## The Adani Group’s Dominance Is Structural, Not Speculative, Because It Controls the Full Power Stack

The report makes a point that deserves emphasis: the Adani Group’s position in the Indian data center ecosystem is unmatched, and this is not a short-term trading observation. It is a structural reality rooted in the group’s control over the three critical inputs that data centers require.

First, land. The Adani Group is the largest renewable energy, private thermal power, and transmission player in India. This means it has access to large contiguous land parcels that are already connected to the grid. Land acquisition is one of the most time-consuming and politically sensitive aspects of data center development in India, and the group has solved it at scale.

Second, power. The group has merchant thermal capacity, renewable capacity, and the transmission infrastructure to move power from generation sites to load centers. In a market where access to power is the binding constraint, owning generation and transmission is a structural advantage that cannot be replicated quickly. Competitors must negotiate with state utilities, secure open access, and build transmission lines. The Adani Group can simply allocate its own capacity.

Third, balance sheet. Building a data center facility costs approximately 50 crore rupees per megawatt, and a 100-megawatt campus requires 5,000 crore rupees in capital. The group has the financial capacity to fund this buildout without relying on external capital markets that may be skeptical of the returns. This is not an advantage that can be acquired through hiring or partnerships. It is a function of decades of capital allocation.

The report notes that Reliance Industries also has significant land holdings, including 0.5 million acres in Gujarat and over 5,000 acres in Navi Mumbai. But the key difference is that Reliance does not have the same depth in power generation and transmission. The Adani Group’s advantage is that it can offer a hyperscaler customer a complete solution: land, power, connectivity, and facility construction. No other Indian conglomerate can match this today.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Risk of Regulatory Intervention and the Timeline for Merchant Power Monetization

The report provides a compelling framework for why power access is the critical variable, but it leaves several questions unanswered that investors must consider.

The first is regulatory risk. The Indian power market is not a free market like PJM or ERCOT. The government has historically intervened to cap prices, allocate coal linkages, and manage power purchase agreements for state utilities. If data center demand creates the kind of scarcity pricing seen in the United States, will regulators allow merchant power generators to capture those rents, or will they impose price caps or priority allocation mechanisms? The report does not address this, and the answer will determine whether the utility thesis plays out fully or only partially.

The second question is the timeline for merchant power monetization. The report shows that the Adani Group dominates merchant power capacity and future capacity additions. But merchant power revenues depend on spot market prices, which are volatile and subject to seasonal and hydrological factors. A data center operator needs 24-hour power, not just peak-hour power. Can merchant plants structure long-term contracts that capture the scarcity premium without exposing themselves to regulatory backlash? The report suggests this is possible but does not provide a concrete path.

The third question is about the pace of data center construction. The report estimates that industry capacity will reach 5 to 8 gigawatts by 2030, but this depends on factors that are outside the control of Indian operators: global AI capital expenditure cycles, hyperscaler demand for Indian compute capacity, and the availability of fiber connectivity to international cable landing stations. If the global AI investment cycle slows, the Indian buildout could be delayed, and the merchant power thesis would be pushed out.

These are not reasons to dismiss the thesis. They are reasons to monitor the regulatory and demand environment closely. The structural advantage of power ownership is clear, but the timing and magnitude of the payoff depend on variables that the report acknowledges but does not resolve.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Which Asset Owners Will Win

For investors evaluating the Indian data center opportunity, the report’s analysis can be synthesized into a three-question framework that applies to any company claiming exposure to the theme.

First, does the company own or have secured access to power generation in the regions where data centers are being built? The U.S. experience shows that owning generation in the right market is the single most important factor. In India, the right markets are likely to be Navi Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad, and Pune, where fiber connectivity and land availability are concentrated. Companies that own generation in these regions have a structural advantage. Companies that plan to buy power from the grid are competing with every other industrial user for the same limited supply.

Second, does the company have land parcels that are already connected to high-capacity transmission substations? The report highlights that there is a big fight for land in Navi Mumbai, with prices exceeding even recent transactions. Land without substation access is not valuable for data center development, because the interconnection queue in India, like in the United States, can take years. Companies that have already secured substation connectivity have a multiyear head start.

Third, is the company adopting the co-location model or the cloud provider model? The report’s analysis suggests that co-location is the appropriate model for Indian operators, because it limits technology risk and capital intensity. Companies that try to own GPUs and operate as neocloud providers will face shorter contracts, higher leverage, and the risk that their hardware becomes obsolete before it is paid off. Investors should prefer companies that lease infrastructure to hyperscalers, not companies that compete with hyperscalers.

This framework does not guarantee which companies will succeed, but it does clarify which questions matter. The companies that can answer yes to all three questions are the ones that should command premium valuations. The companies that can only answer yes to one or two are taking on risk that may not be priced in.

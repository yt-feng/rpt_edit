# China’s Property Recovery Is Real, But It Only Belongs to a Handful of Developers

The narrative around China’s real estate sector has shifted from crisis containment to selective recovery. After months of cautious观望, the data now supports a more assertive conclusion: the market is stabilizing, but the benefits are accruing almost exclusively to high-quality state-owned enterprises. In May, the six leading SOE developers tracked by a major global investment bank delivered positive year-on-year sales growth, led by CR Land at plus 28 percent and Yuexiu at plus 18 percent. Their share prices rose an average of 7 percent against a Hang Seng Index that fell 2 percent. This is not a broad-based rebound. It is a bifurcation that will define the next phase of China’s property cycle.

The question every serious investor must now answer is not whether the market has bottomed, but which companies are positioned to compound earnings from here. The evidence suggests that the recovery is being driven by structural forces—improving affordability, a functioning land market, and resilient upgrade demand—rather than temporary policy stimulus. Yet the market remains skeptical about sustainability, as reflected in a recent pause in developer share prices. That skepticism creates both risk and opportunity.

This article unpacks the key signals from the latest sales and land market data, explains why the recovery is more durable than it appears, and identifies the analytical gaps that investors need to close before making allocation decisions.

## May Sales Confirm That Execution Capability, Not Market Tailwind, Is the Differentiator

The headline numbers for May are encouraging, but the dispersion beneath them tells the real story. Among the six high-quality SOEs tracked, CR Land grew sales 28 percent year-on-year, Yuexiu grew 18 percent, and COLI grew 14 percent. In contrast, private developers in the coverage universe saw average sales decline 40 percent year-on-year. The gap is not cyclical; it is structural.

What explains this divergence? It is not simply access to financing, though that matters. The key factor is project quality and geographic focus. CR Land and COLI have concentrated their pipelines in Tier 1 and strong Tier 2 cities, where upgrade demand from existing homeowners remains resilient. The strong sell-through of Shenzhen high-end projects in recent months is a case in point. Buyers in these cities are not speculators; they are owner-occupiers trading up for better space and amenities, supported by the best affordability levels in a decade.

The implication is straightforward: developers with rich, high-quality saleable resources in premium locations will continue to outperform, even if the broader market remains tepid. Investors should focus on inventory quality and geographic concentration, not just sales volume. The companies that can sustain positive year-on-year growth into June, on the back of a low base last year, are the ones that have rebuilt their land banks and project pipelines during the downturn. Those that cut land acquisition aggressively are now left with nothing to sell.

## The Land Market Is Sending a Powerful Signal: Developer Confidence Is Returning

Perhaps the most underappreciated development in the current cycle is the early recovery of the land market. On May 29, Shanghai sold five plots for approximately RMB 11 billion, with four of them transacting at 17 to 40 percent above reserve prices. In Suzhou, COLI acquired a residential plot for RMB 1.7 billion, 30 percent above the reserve price, setting a provincial record for price per square meter. Similar high-premium transactions have been observed in Nanjing.

These are not isolated events. National land sale declines narrowed to 36 percent in the first five months of 2026, from 41 percent in the first four months. More importantly, the average premium over reserve price increased to 10 percent, from 6 percent in the prior period. Developers are bidding aggressively for prime sites again, which signals that they have confidence in future selling prices and demand.

This matters for two reasons. First, land acquisition is a leading indicator of future revenue. Developers that are buying land now are signaling that they expect to sell homes at a profit in the next 12 to 24 months. Second, a functioning land market supports asset valuations across the sector. When land prices stabilize or increase, the carrying value of existing land banks improves, which strengthens balance sheets and reduces the risk of impairment charges.

The open question is whether this land market recovery will broaden to lower-tier cities and smaller developers. So far, the evidence suggests it will not. The high-premium transactions are concentrated in Shanghai, Suzhou, and Nanjing. Developers are being highly selective, focusing only on markets where demand is proven. This selectivity reinforces the bifurcation theme: the land market is recovering for quality developers in quality locations, not for the sector as a whole.

## Shanghai’s Secondary Market Is the Canary in the Coal Mine for Organic Demand

One of the most debated questions in China property is whether the recent sales uptick is sustainable or merely a release of pent-up demand following policy easing. The secondary market data from Shanghai provides the clearest answer yet.

May secondary transactions in Shanghai reached a five-year high, sustaining the strong momentum from March and April. Critically, secondary home prices have also risen month-on-month for three consecutive months, with a 0.7 percent increase in April according to the National Bureau of Statistics. This is not a one-off holiday boost or a temporary policy sugar rush. It reflects a genuine improvement in affordability and a wealth effect that is beginning to feed through.

The logic is straightforward. When secondary prices stabilize and begin to rise, homeowners feel wealthier and are more willing to trade up to new homes. This creates a virtuous cycle: secondary market liquidity enables primary market demand. Developers that can capture this upgrade demand with well-located, high-quality projects will benefit disproportionately.

The data also suggests that the recovery is organic rather than policy-driven. While purchase restrictions have been eased in many cities, the improvement in affordability stems from a combination of lower mortgage rates, stable or declining home prices over the past two years, and rising household incomes. According to the same report, affordability is now at a decade-best level. This is a structural support, not a cyclical one.

However, investors should note that the recovery is not uniform across cities. In the ten-city sample tracked, May secondary transactions were up 18 percent year-on-year on average, but Hangzhou saw only 3 percent growth, and Nanjing 5 percent. The strongest growth came from Xiamen (34 percent), Foshan (31 percent), and Shanghai (28 percent). Geographic differentiation will persist, and investors need to look through city-level data to understand where demand is genuinely recovering.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Earnings Recovery Timeline and the C-REITs Catalyst

For all the positive signals in sales and land markets, the report leaves two critical questions unresolved. First, when will the earnings recovery materialize? The current valuation summary shows that for most developers, 2025 and 2026 earnings are expected to remain depressed, with meaningful recovery only projected for 2027. CR Land, for example, trades at 10.0 times 2025 earnings and 9.5 times 2026 earnings, but the multiple drops to 8.9 times 2027 estimates. This implies the market is already pricing in a recovery two years out.

The risk is that the recovery in sales and land markets does not translate into earnings as quickly as expected. Development margins may remain compressed if land costs have risen while selling prices stay flat. Interest costs and impairment charges could continue to weigh on profitability. Investors need to stress-test the earnings recovery assumptions, particularly for developers that are aggressively bidding for land at premiums.

Second, the report highlights the potential for CR Land to benefit from investment property revaluations as the C-REITs market expands, but it does not quantify this catalyst. The C-REITs market in China is still in its early stages, and the ability to list commercial properties as REITs could unlock significant value for developers with large investment property portfolios. However, the timing, valuation, and regulatory framework remain uncertain. This is a meaningful upside that is not fully captured in current valuations, but it is also a source of risk if the C-REITs market develops more slowly than expected.

## A Decision Framework for Allocating Capital in the Recovery

Based on the evidence, investors should approach China property with a clear framework that distinguishes between companies that are structurally positioned to benefit and those that are merely riding a cyclical wave. Three criteria matter most.

First, evaluate land bank quality and replenishment capability. Developers that have maintained or increased land acquisition in premium cities during the downturn are the ones that will have saleable resources when demand returns. The land market data shows that only a handful of developers are actively bidding for prime sites. Those that sat out the land market entirely will struggle to generate sales growth.

Second, assess earnings visibility and margin resilience. The current valuation multiples already reflect an expected earnings recovery in 2027. Investors should look for developers with diversified revenue streams—such as investment property income, property management fees, or C-REITs proceeds—that can support earnings even if development margins remain under pressure. CR Land’s potential C-REITs catalyst is one example. Companies with high net gearing and heavy reliance on development income are more vulnerable to margin compression.

Third, monitor secondary market price trends in key cities. Secondary prices are the best leading indicator of primary market demand. If secondary prices continue to rise month-on-month in Shanghai, Shenzhen, and other Tier 1 cities, the upgrade cycle will sustain itself. If secondary prices stall, the recovery may prove short-lived. Investors should track this data on a monthly basis and adjust exposure accordingly.

The report’s stock preferences—CR Land and C&D International, both rated Buy—align with this framework. Both have strong execution in high-end projects, robust pricing power, and balance sheets that allow them to participate in the land market recovery. The key risk is that the earnings recovery takes longer than expected, or that policy tightening returns if prices rise too quickly. But for investors willing to look through the near-term noise, the structural case for selective exposure to high-quality Chinese developers is stronger than it has been in years.

# Auto Suppliers Are the Hidden Play in the 800V Infrastructure Buildout—and the Market Has Not Priced It In

The next major investment theme in industrial technology is not coming from the usual suspects. While investors have crowded into traditional electrical equipment and semiconductor names to gain exposure to AI data center growth, a largely overlooked group of companies sits on capabilities that could prove equally valuable—and at a fraction of the valuation. Automotive suppliers, long dismissed as cyclical and growth-challenged, have spent years developing deep expertise in 800-volt power electronics for electric vehicles. That expertise is now directly transferable to the two fastest-growing infrastructure markets of the decade: AI data centers and battery energy storage systems.

The thesis is straightforward but not yet widely appreciated. Next-generation AI data centers are migrating from 400-volt to 800-volt direct-current architectures to handle the thermal and efficiency demands of dense GPU clusters. Battery energy storage systems are following a similar voltage evolution. Auto suppliers already manufacture the inverters, converters, connectors, busbars, contactors, and sensors that these new architectures require. They have invested in 800V technology for EVs, mastered high-tolerance manufacturing at scale, and built complex supply chains that can deliver reliability standards far beyond consumer electronics. The opportunity is nearly 100 percent incremental to their existing automotive revenue, and the market has not yet assigned any value to it.

The comparison to the 2018 peak of autonomous-vehicle and EV mania is instructive but incomplete. During that period, investor enthusiasm was driven by speculative adoption curves, uncertain regulation, and technology that was years from maturity. The current interest in AI data center and BESS exposure is different: it is anchored in real demand, visible capital expenditure commitments, and measurable economic returns. A global investment bank report notes that the level of new group interest in AI exposure from both auto and non-auto investors has not been this high since 2018. But this time, the foundation is more solid. The question is not whether the buildout will happen, but which suppliers will capture the content.

## The 800V Architecture Shift Creates a New Addressable Market That Auto Suppliers Are Uniquely Qualified to Serve

Understanding why auto suppliers matter requires understanding why data centers are changing their electrical architecture. Traditional data centers move electricity through a complex chain: grid power arrives as alternating current, is converted multiple times, and is delivered at lower voltages with higher current. This approach requires thicker cables, generates more heat, and loses significant energy along the way. As compute density increases with next-generation GPU clusters, these inefficiencies become unacceptable.

The 800V architecture solves this by converting grid AC into high-voltage DC early in the chain, then distributing that DC power across the facility using busbars, high-voltage wiring, and specialized connectors. Near the servers, the voltage is stepped down using DC-DC converters. The result is lower current, reduced heat, smaller cables, and the ability to pack more compute into the same physical footprint. NVIDIA has explicitly stated that 800V DC will be the essential foundation for next-generation intelligence, with initial rollout expected alongside rack-scale systems in 2027 and acceleration later in the decade.

This is where auto suppliers enter the picture. The technology path for battery electric vehicles was always expected to move from 400V to 800V, and suppliers invested heavily in power electronics to support that transition. They now have production-ready inverters, converters, high-voltage connectors, busbars, and contactors designed for 800V applications. The manufacturing disciplines required—lean production, complex supply chain management, high-tolerance quality standards—are the same disciplines that matter in data center infrastructure and BESS. The capabilities are portable. The investment has already been made.

The market opportunity is substantial. Global data center power capacity that is planned or under construction stands at 181 gigawatts, with 172 gigawatts of that outside China. A global investment bank estimates that as data centers transition to 800V, overall equipment spend could reach approximately four million dollars per megawatt, or four billion dollars per gigawatt. For BESS, global demand is projected to grow from roughly 334 gigawatt-hours in 2025 to 1.6 terawatt-hours in 2030. The cumulative ex-China BESS total addressable market from 2027 to 2030 is estimated at over five hundred billion dollars. Even a small share of this market would represent a material growth avenue for companies that have been viewed as structurally challenged.

## Three Companies Stand Out for Their Product Portfolios and Readiness, but the Full Opportunity Set Is Broader

A detailed portfolio review of ten auto suppliers reveals that while several have capabilities relevant to 800V infrastructure, three are best positioned today. The analysis is based on mapping each company's existing product suite against the components that 800V data centers and BESS systems require.

The first company has already announced a TurboCell product, a battery energy storage system, and a bidirectional microgrid inverter. Its capabilities extend to inverters, converters, and the potential to develop solid-state transformers, which represent the most advanced form of AC-DC conversion at the grid edge. The second company holds strong competencies in high-voltage connectors and busbars for EVs, and these capabilities appear directly portable to data center applications. This is significant because the company has set a non-auto annual growth target of eight to ten percent, and the market has been skeptical about whether that target is achievable. The 800V opportunity could provide the missing credibility. The third company is positioned to benefit from the shift to liquid-cooled data centers, where pressure, temperature, and flow sensors become critical components. It also manufactures 800V contactors for EVs that can be repurposed for new applications.

The critical distinction is that for these auto suppliers, the 800V infrastructure opportunity is nearly entirely incremental. Competitors in the electrical components space, such as Amphenol and TE Connectivity, already have some AI power content in their revenue base. For auto suppliers, this is new business that does not cannibalize existing revenue. The market has not yet reflected this in valuations, which remain depressed relative to industrial AI plays.

## The Report Leaves Several Critical Questions Unanswered, and Investors Should Demand Clarity Before Committing Capital

For all its analytical rigor, the report does not fully resolve several questions that will determine whether this thesis plays out as expected. The first and most important is the total addressable market for each specific product. The 800V DC architecture is still in early-stage development, and the precise configuration of components will likely evolve as hyperscalers settle on standards. The report makes wide-range estimates for certain key products, but acknowledges that the TAM is inherently unclear. Investors need to understand not just whether these companies have capabilities, but how much revenue those capabilities can generate at scale.

The second open question is timing. The report indicates that 800V architecture will not become prevalent until 2028 or later. This creates a gap between current investor enthusiasm and actual revenue realization. Companies may need to invest in sales efforts, product adaptation, and certification processes before they see any return. The risk is that the market prices in the opportunity too early, leading to disappointment if adoption is slower than expected.

The third question is competitive dynamics. While auto suppliers have relevant capabilities, they are not the only players with 800V expertise. Electrical equipment manufacturers, industrial technology companies, and specialized power electronics firms are also investing in this space. The report does not fully address how auto suppliers will compete on cost, reliability, and customer relationships against incumbents who already serve data center operators. The "auto grade" quality standard is an advantage, but it is not a moat.

Finally, there is the question of capital allocation. Auto suppliers are not known for high-margin, high-growth businesses. If they need to invest significantly to capture the 800V opportunity, their return on invested capital may not meet investor expectations. The report notes that some investment will be required, but does not quantify the magnitude or the potential impact on margins.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating Auto Suppliers with Non-Auto Potential

For investors considering exposure to this theme, a structured evaluation framework is more useful than a list of names. The following criteria can help distinguish between companies that have a genuine right to participate and those that are merely adjacent.

First, assess product specificity. Not all 800V capabilities are equally valuable. The components with the highest content per gigawatt and the most defensible technology positions are inverters, converters, solid-state transformers, high-voltage connectors, and busbars. Companies that manufacture these components have a stronger claim than those that produce general-purpose sensors or enclosures.

Second, evaluate manufacturing scalability. The data center buildout will require volume production with consistent quality. Auto suppliers already operate at scale, but they must demonstrate that they can adapt their production lines to non-auto specifications without compromising margin. Companies with flexible manufacturing platforms and experience in multiple end markets have an advantage.

Third, examine customer relationships. Data center operators and hyperscalers have long procurement cycles and rigorous qualification processes. Auto suppliers that already have relationships with industrial or technology customers, or that can partner with established electrical distributors, will face a shorter path to revenue.

Fourth, consider the balance sheet and capital allocation strategy. Capturing the 800V opportunity may require upfront investment in R&D, sales infrastructure, and possibly capacity expansion. Companies with strong free cash flow and a willingness to invest in non-auto growth deserve a premium. Those that are capital-constrained or overly focused on returning cash to shareholders may miss the window.

Fifth, monitor valuation relative to peers. The report's core insight is that auto suppliers offer a less expensive way to gain AI infrastructure exposure than traditional industrial AI names. But as the thesis gains traction, valuations will adjust. The key is to identify companies where the 800V opportunity is not yet priced in, and where the existing auto business provides a margin of safety if the infrastructure thesis takes longer to materialize.

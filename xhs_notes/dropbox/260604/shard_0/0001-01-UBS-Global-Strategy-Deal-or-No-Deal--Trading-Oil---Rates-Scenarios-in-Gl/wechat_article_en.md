# Credit Spreads Are Not Immune to Higher Rates — They Are Simply Waiting for the Right Trigger

The conventional wisdom in credit markets over the past two months has been that higher rates do not matter for spreads. US investment grade and high yield spreads have tightened by 17 and 60 basis points respectively, even as the 10-year yield moved higher. Rates volatility has normalized to pre-conflict levels in the US, and the narrative of a "not too hot, not too cold" economy has kept credit investors comfortable. But this apparent decoupling is not a structural shift. It is a temporary equilibrium built on a specific composition of the rates move — one that is now beginning to change.

The critical question for credit investors is not whether higher rates will eventually matter, but what specific threshold will force a repricing. The answer lies not in the level of yields, but in the mix of their components. When real rates and term premia drove the initial rise, credit could absorb the shock. But as long-term inflation expectations begin to edge higher — and early signs of this shift are already visible — the transmission mechanism from rates to spreads becomes far more dangerous. The market is pricing a benign outcome, but the underlying data on supply chains, positioning, and central bank divergence suggest a different story.

This is not a call for an imminent crash. It is an argument that the current tightness in spreads is fragile, asymmetric, and dependent on a narrow set of conditions that are already eroding. The real risk is not that rates rise further, but that the composition of the rise shifts in a way that undermines the very foundation of current credit valuations. Investors who treat the last two months as proof of a new regime are likely to be surprised by how quickly the relationship between rates and credit can reassert itself.

## The Composition of the Rates Move Determines Whether Credit Feels the Pain

The resilience of credit spreads to higher rates over the past two months was not a mystery. It was a direct consequence of what was driving yields higher. The initial rise in US 10-year yields was dominated by higher real rates and term premia, not by a destabilizing shift in inflation expectations or the policy outlook. This distinction matters because real rates and term premia do not directly challenge the macro or micro fundamentals that underpin credit quality. They do not signal a loss of central bank credibility, nor do they imply that nominal earnings growth is at risk.

Inflation expectations, by contrast, are a different animal. When breakevens rise, they force investors to question whether the central bank has control over the trajectory of prices. This questioning erodes the credibility of the policy path, which in turn raises the risk of a more aggressive tightening cycle. Credit spreads are sensitive to this channel because higher inflation expectations imply higher terminal rates, which compress the margin of safety for leveraged balance sheets. For now, US 5-year breakevens remain below 2.5%, comfortably beneath the threshold that would trigger a meaningful credit response. But the direction of travel matters more than the level, and the direction is no longer benign.

The composition of the rates move has begun to shift. Early signs of a repricing in long-term inflation expectations are emerging, driven by stalled Middle East negotiations and growing recognition that AI-driven capex may be an upside risk to inflation. This is the kind of shift that credit cannot ignore for long. The market has been pricing a deal in risky assets — essentially betting that geopolitical tensions will resolve and that inflation will remain contained. If that bet proves wrong, the transmission to credit will be sudden and non-linear.

## Credit Will Not Adjust Gradually — It Will Reprice Only When a Regime Shift Becomes Visible

Credit markets do not react to marginal adjustments in rates. They react to regime shifts. The evidence from the past several months supports this view: spreads tightened even as yields rose, because the market interpreted the move as a cyclical adjustment rather than a structural change. The threshold for a regime shift in the US appears to be breakeven inflation moving into the 2.65-2.75% range. At that level, investors would begin to question the degree of inflation control and, by extension, the credibility of the current policy path.

The risk that this threshold will be tested is rising, not falling. Supply chain indicators are deteriorating sharply. One global investment bank's supply chain stress index rose 1.2 standard deviations in March and April — the second largest two-month increase since the pandemic-induced surge in July 2020. This is not a marginal data point. It is a signal that the benign inflation narrative may be short-lived. If supply chain disruptions persist or worsen, the pass-through to core goods inflation will accelerate, and the Fed will face pressure to respond.

The non-linear nature of the transmission is crucial to understand. In a regime shift scenario, the Fed would need to deliver multiple hikes, not a single adjustment hike. The market is not pricing this possibility. It is pricing low odds of a sustained hiking cycle. Any change in this pricing would trigger a repricing of the entire rates curve, and credit spreads would widen decisively — not because of the level of rates, but because the policy regime itself would have changed. The lesson from past episodes is clear: credit markets tolerate higher rates as long as the policy path is credible and stable. Once that credibility is questioned, the adjustment is swift and severe.

## Stretched Valuations and Positioning Leave No Room for Further Tightening, Even on a Rates Rally

One of the most dangerous assumptions in current credit markets is that a rates rally would drive spreads tighter. This assumption is almost certainly wrong. Credit valuations are already stretched. US investment grade spreads are at the 2nd percentile on a one-year basis; US high yield is at the 3rd percentile. In Europe, the picture is slightly less extreme but still compressed: IG at the 30th percentile, HY at the 11th. Across US high yield, 10 out of 16 key sectors trade at spreads below the 25th percentile. There is simply not much room for further tightening.

Positioning data reinforces this view. Asset manager net positioning in S&P 500 futures — used as a proxy for broader risk appetite — sits near the 95th percentile over the past decade. This is not a market that is underinvested in credit. It is a market that is fully positioned, with limited capacity for additional institutional buying. Commodity trading advisor positioning in credit has plateaued, and historical analysis shows that the largest spread tightening following geopolitical resolutions occurred when CTAs were previously short credit. They are now long. The implication is clear: even a peace deal or a sharp decline in rates is unlikely to generate meaningful further spread compression.

This asymmetry is the defining feature of current credit markets. The upside from here is limited, while the downside from a regime shift is substantial. Investors who are long credit are not being compensated for the tail risk they are carrying. The risk-reward is skewed toward widening, not tightening, and the positioning data suggests that the market is crowded on the wrong side of the trade.

## The Europe-U.S. Divergence Creates a Clear Opportunity to Take Profit and Reposition

The valuation case for long Europe versus US credit has largely run its course. The trade was opened at the onset of the conflict, reopened in early April, and captured most of the rally back to tights. The fundamental backdrop no longer supports it. From a monetary policy perspective, the divergence between the ECB and the Fed is stark and growing. One investment bank's forecasts show 50 basis points of ECB hikes in 2026, compared to 25 basis points of Fed cuts. The ECB has effectively never reached neutral, and the market is already pricing approximately 60 basis points of hikes for 2026. The Fed, by contrast, is expected to ease.

This divergence matters for credit because it creates asymmetric vulnerabilities. Europe faces weaker-than-expected growth, driven by the energy shock and limited fiscal support for corporates. The eurozone economy is showing clear downside risk, with weak May PMIs suggesting a contraction in Q2 GDP. Companies are building inventories in anticipation of further supply chain disruption, which adds cost and reduces margin flexibility. Political uncertainty in Europe and the UK further complicates the outlook. The combination of tighter monetary policy, weaker growth, and elevated uncertainty is not priced into European credit spreads.

Taking profit on the long Europe versus US trade is the prudent move. The trade has worked, and the risk-reward has shifted. A more attractive entry point will likely emerge as the divergence between the two regions becomes more apparent. For now, the better positioning is to reduce exposure to European credit and wait for a clearer signal.

## Three Scenarios, Three Trades — But the Common Thread Is Downside Protection

Given the elevated uncertainty around both the trajectory of the Middle East conflict and the outlook for inflation, scenario-based positioning is the most sensible approach. Three scenarios capture the range of plausible outcomes, and each implies a different trade.

In the first scenario — energy normalizes but inflation proves sticky — oil declines while rates rise. The trade here is to short US investment grade office REITs against the index, on a duration-adjusted basis. Office REITs have long-duration cash flow structures, higher leverage, and a rising illiquidity premium in stress. They consistently underperform other REIT subsectors during periods of rising rates, and current valuations screen tight because the market has played the hard asset trade.

In the second scenario — energy normalizes and inflation proves transitory — both oil and rates decline. Here, the value lies in Europe. A long position in EU investment grade airlines against the index offers significant convexity, positive carry, and a slightly long duration structure. The sector has not repriced since the rally began in late March, creating an opportunity for catch-up.

In the third scenario — the Strait remains disrupted and inflation proves sticky — oil rises materially and rates follow. The consumer, which has proven resilient so far despite clear signs of a K-shaped economy, would come under pressure. The trade is to short US high yield retail against the index, playing for spread underperformance due to a negative growth shock. This structure is duration neutral and carry positive.

The common thread across all three scenarios is that the current tightness in spreads is not sustainable. Whether rates rise or fall, the asymmetry favors widening. The question is not whether credit will adjust, but which catalyst will trigger the adjustment.

## The Report Raises Questions It Does Not Fully Answer — And Those Questions Are the Real Opportunity

The analysis presented here is rigorous, but it leaves several important questions unresolved. First, what is the precise mechanism by which supply chain stress translates into credit spread widening? The correlation is clear, but the causality is not fully mapped. Is it through earnings revisions, margin compression, or a repricing of default risk? Each channel implies a different timing and magnitude of adjustment.

Second, how should investors distinguish between a cyclical adjustment in rates and a regime shift in real time? The threshold of 2.65-2.75% for breakeven inflation is a useful heuristic, but it is not a hard line. Markets can reprice well before a threshold is reached, especially if the composition of the rates move changes rapidly. The report identifies the risk but does not provide a real-time monitoring framework.

Third, what is the role of liquidity in the transmission from rates to credit? The report notes that liquidity is still ample, but does not explore how quickly liquidity can evaporate in a regime shift scenario. The experience of March 2020 suggests that liquidity can disappear faster than fundamentals deteriorate, amplifying spread moves well beyond what the macro backdrop would justify.

These are not weaknesses in the report. They are the natural boundaries of any analytical framework. The best investors will use the report's insights to build their own monitoring systems and decision rules, filling in the gaps with their own judgment and experience.

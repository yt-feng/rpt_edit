# China's Power Semiconductor Cycle Is Different This Time: Supply Discipline, Not Demand Surge, Is the Real Driver

The prevailing narrative in global semiconductor investing has been dominated by artificial intelligence, advanced logic nodes, and high-bandwidth memory. Yet one of the most structurally compelling opportunities in the sector today sits in a less glamorous but equally critical segment: power semiconductors. A global investment bank report makes a clear and contrarian case that China's power semiconductor cycle is not merely a repeat of past upturns. The controlling insight is this: the current recovery is being driven by sustained supply discipline and capacity constraints, not by a temporary demand spike. This structural shift favors integrated device manufacturers (IDMs) over fabless companies, and it opens the door for significant margin expansion that the market has not yet priced in.

Why now matters. Global power semiconductor stocks have rallied an average of 135% year-to-date, yet their Chinese counterparts have risen only 34%. Within China, power IDMs such as CR Micro and Silan Micro have appreciated by a mere 15-24%. This valuation gap suggests that investors are applying the wrong cyclical framework to Chinese names, treating them as laggards in a mature recovery rather than as beneficiaries of a structurally tighter supply environment. The report upgrades Silan Micro from Neutral to Buy, raises price targets across its coverage, and argues that the combination of disciplined capex, resilient downstream demand, and accelerating silicon carbide (SiC) adoption creates a multi-year opportunity.

The argument is not without nuance. Not all companies in the power semiconductor value chain are positioned equally. The report identifies a clear pecking order, with IDMs at the top, fabless firms in the middle, and fab-lite transition companies facing near-term headwinds. This differentiation is critical for investors seeking to allocate capital with precision.

## Sustained Supply Tightness, Not Demand Elasticity, Is the Structural Backbone of This Cycle

The most important analytical departure in this report is its emphasis on supply-side dynamics. Historically, power semiconductor cycles have been driven by demand volatility: a surge in automotive or industrial end-markets would trigger a capacity scramble, followed by oversupply and a correction. This cycle is different. The report identifies two reinforcing factors that suggest supply will remain constrained for longer than typical.

First, capital expenditure across the global power semiconductor supply chain has remained disciplined. After a period of aggressive capacity expansion during the 2021-2022 shortage, manufacturers pulled back. IDMs and specialty foundries slowed their capex programs over 2023-2025. This is not simply a cyclical pause; it reflects a strategic reassessment. Companies are prioritizing utilization rates and return on invested capital over market share grabs. The result is that even as demand recovers, supply cannot ramp quickly.

Second, the report points to capacity reallocation by major global players. TSMC has announced plans to gradually convert part of its 8-inch and 12-inch foundry capacity to advanced packaging. Infineon has signaled the conversion of some idle IGBT capacity to leading-edge MOSFET production. These moves effectively remove a portion of the addressable supply for traditional power devices, tightening the market further. The implication is clear: the supply constraint is not a temporary bottleneck but a deliberate structural shift in how capacity is deployed.

The downstream demand picture supports this thesis. Rather than a single explosive end-market, the recovery is broad-based. New energy vehicles, energy storage systems, grid infrastructure, and what the report terms "core and physical AI" are all contributing to resilient demand. This diversification reduces the risk of a sudden demand collapse that would overwhelm the supply discipline. The combination of constrained supply and diversified demand creates a pricing environment that benefits incumbents with existing capacity.

## Power IDMs Are Positioned for 5-8 Percentage Points of Net Margin Expansion, While Fabless Firms Face Structural Limitations

The report's most actionable insight is its differentiation between business models within the power semiconductor value chain. Not all participants will benefit equally from the tighter-for-longer supply environment. The report explicitly prefers power IDMs over fabless companies, and the reasoning is grounded in margin mechanics.

IDMs control their own fabrication capacity. When supply is tight, they can capture the full benefit of rising utilization rates and improving pricing. Their fixed costs are spread over a larger revenue base, and they are not dependent on third-party foundries for wafer supply. The report forecasts that CR Micro and Silan Micro will see net margins expand by 5-8 percentage points between 2025 and 2028. Their return on equity is projected to improve from 2.9% and 3.3% respectively in 2025 to 9.6% and 12.0% by 2028. These are not marginal improvements; they represent a fundamental inflection in profitability.

Fabless companies, by contrast, face a structural limitation. They must purchase wafers from foundries that are themselves capacity-constrained. Even if end-market demand is strong, fabless firms cannot scale their output without access to wafer supply. Their margin expansion potential is therefore capped at 2-4 percentage points over the same period. NCE Power, a fabless MOSFET specialist, is expected to see its net margin improve from 21% to 25% by 2028, a respectable but less dramatic trajectory than the IDMs.

The report also identifies a third category: fab-lite transition companies, exemplified by StarPower. These firms are moving from a pure fabless model toward owning some internal capacity. The transition itself is costly. StarPower's earnings estimates have been cut by 25-37% to reflect higher depreciation burdens. However, the report maintains a Buy rating on StarPower, arguing that its strong R&D capability in SiC will eventually drive a re-rating. The near-term pain is the price of long-term strategic positioning.

The so-what for investors is clear. If the supply-tightness thesis is correct, the market is currently undervaluing IDM margins. The report's net profit forecasts for CR Micro and Silan are above Wind consensus, suggesting that the sell-side has not fully incorporated the margin expansion story. For investors who agree with the supply discipline thesis, the entry point is now, before consensus catches up.

## Silicon Carbide Adoption Is No Longer Theoretical; It Is a Tangible Revenue Driver with Measurable Penetration Rates

The report's second major thesis is that SiC adoption has moved from promise to practice. This is not a speculative call on future technology; it is an observation of current production reality. The report notes that SiC penetration is rising across multiple downstream sectors, including electric vehicles, energy storage systems, and AI data centers. This trend is reflected in commentary from the global supply chain and, critically, in the fact that CR Micro and Silan are already running fully loaded SiC production lines.

The key enabler is price-performance. SiC devices have historically been more expensive than silicon-based alternatives, limiting their adoption to high-end applications where efficiency gains justified the premium. Years of price declines have narrowed that gap. The report argues that SiC now offers attractive price-performance versus silicon, which should drive penetration across a broader set of end-markets. This is not a niche story; it is a mainstream substitution cycle.

Within the report's coverage, StarPower and Silan have the highest revenue exposure to SiC. StarPower's SiC revenue exposure is estimated at less than 15% for 2025, while Silan's is less than 10%. These numbers may seem modest, but the growth trajectory matters more than the starting point. The report's earnings revisions for Silan explicitly incorporate SiC-driven upside, with 2026-2028 EPS raised by 5-14% above prior estimates and 8-23% above consensus.

The strategic implication is that SiC capability is becoming a competitive differentiator. Companies that invested early in SiC R&D and production capacity are now positioned to capture a disproportionate share of growth. The report's valuation methodology for StarPower has shifted from a PE-based approach to a PB-based approach, consistent with how it values other IDM and fab-lite semiconductor stocks. This methodological change reflects the view that StarPower's asset base, including its SiC capacity, is becoming a source of competitive advantage that warrants a different valuation framework.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Supply Discipline and the Risk of Demand Concentration

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several meaningful open questions that readers should consider before acting on its conclusions. The most important unresolved issue is the sustainability of supply discipline. The report's thesis rests on the assumption that global power semiconductor manufacturers will continue to exercise capex restraint. But this assumption is untested against a scenario of sustained demand strength. If end-market demand continues to grow at an 18% compound annual rate for China's covered companies, as the report forecasts, how long will manufacturers resist the temptation to add capacity? The history of the semiconductor industry is littered with cycles where discipline collapsed in the face of rising prices.

A second open question concerns demand concentration risk. The report highlights exposure to new energy vehicles, energy storage, and AI data centers. These are all high-growth end-markets, but they are also subject to policy risk, technology substitution, and geopolitical disruption. The Chinese EV market, for example, is facing increasing trade barriers in Europe and North America. If export channels are constrained, domestic demand alone may not be sufficient to sustain the projected growth rates.

Third, the report's preference for IDMs over fabless companies is analytically sound under current conditions, but it does not fully address the risk that fabless firms could develop alternative sourcing strategies. The report notes that TSMC is converting some foundry capacity to advanced packaging, which tightens supply for power devices. But what if foundry players respond by building dedicated power capacity? The supply tightness that benefits IDMs today could be eroded by competitive responses tomorrow.

These are not criticisms of the report; they are the natural boundaries of any forward-looking analysis. The report's value lies in its clear articulation of a thesis and its identification of which companies are best positioned if that thesis plays out. Investors should use the report as a starting point for their own scenario analysis, not as a definitive prediction.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Positioning in China Power Semiconductors

The report's analysis can be distilled into a practical decision framework. Investors evaluating exposure to China's power semiconductor sector should ask three questions.

First, do you believe that supply discipline will persist? If your answer is yes, then the report's preference for IDMs over fabless companies is the logical conclusion. CR Micro and Silan Micro offer direct exposure to the margin expansion story. Their current valuations, with price-to-book multiples below historical averages since 2020, suggest that the market has not yet priced in the ROE improvement that the report forecasts.

Second, do you believe that SiC adoption will accelerate? If yes, then Silan and StarPower are the primary beneficiaries within the coverage universe. Silan offers a combination of tight-supply margin expansion and SiC exposure. StarPower offers higher SiC revenue exposure but carries near-term earnings risk from its fab-lite transition. The trade-off between near-term earnings volatility and long-term strategic positioning is one that each investor must make based on their time horizon.

Third, what is your view on the valuation gap between global and Chinese power semiconductor stocks? The report argues that Chinese names have catching-up potential, particularly IDMs. If you share this view, the asymmetry is in your favor: the downside is limited by already-low valuations, while the upside is supported by earnings revisions that are above consensus.

This framework does not provide a single answer for all investors. It forces a decision on the key variables that will determine outcomes. The report's contribution is to make those variables explicit and to provide evidence for each side of the argument.

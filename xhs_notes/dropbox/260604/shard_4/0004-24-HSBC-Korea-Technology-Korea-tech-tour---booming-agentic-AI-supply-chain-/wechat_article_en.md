# Agentic AI Is Reshaping the Korean Technology Supply Chain Faster Than Physical AI Can Scale

The artificial intelligence investment narrative is undergoing a critical shift. For the past two years, the semiconductor supply chain has been dominated by a single engine: GPU-driven training infrastructure for frontier models. That era is ending, and a more complex, multi-layered demand structure is emerging. The recent Korea tech tour, which brought together fourteen companies spanning memory, semiconductor equipment, materials, and tech hardware, delivered a clear message: the next phase of AI value creation is not about a single chip. It is about the entire ecosystem that supports agentic AI deployment, and that ecosystem is expanding into areas that many investors have not yet fully priced.

This is not a speculative story about robots taking over factories. It is a concrete, measurable shift in capital expenditure patterns. Cloud service providers are increasing their spending at rates that would have seemed implausible two years ago, with total global CSP capex expected to reach USD738 billion in 2026, representing an 80% year-on-year increase. What matters most is where that money is going. The tour revealed that the primary beneficiary is no longer just the GPU supplier. It is the memory makers, the substrate manufacturers, the laser equipment firms, and the materials companies that enable the physical infrastructure of agentic AI.

The distinction between agentic AI and physical AI is the most important strategic frame for understanding this cycle. Agentic AI refers to systems that can autonomously execute tasks, manage workflows, and interact with digital environments. These systems require massive memory bandwidth, high-speed interconnect, and reliable power conversion. Physical AI, which includes robotics and autonomous systems, remains structurally important but is not yet driving the same level of capital deployment. The tour made this hierarchy explicit: the supply chain for agentic AI is booming today, while physical AI waits in the wings. Investors who conflate the two risk missing the timing of the opportunity.

## Memory Earnings Are the Most Solid Foundation of the Current Cycle, But the Real Opportunity Lies in the Equipment and Materials Layer That Has Not Yet Peaked

The memory segment has been the most visible beneficiary of AI-driven demand, and the tour reinforced why. DRAM content per server is accelerating at a rate that demands attention. The latest Vera Rubin platform requires 1.5 terabytes of DRAM per CPU, a 170% increase over the previous generation. That is not a marginal upgrade. It is a structural jump in content per unit, and it is being driven by the specific requirements of agentic AI workloads, which demand low-latency, high-bandwidth memory to manage complex inference tasks.

HBM3e pricing is expected to narrow the gap with commodity DRAM, and the shift from GPU-centric demand to CPU-driven demand is a critical nuance. Agentic AI is more feasible to manage on CPU architectures, particularly x86 and ARM-based designs, which are seeing robust demand for both server and mobile applications. The penetration rate of ARM-based CPUs in the overall CPU market is forecast to reach 20% in 2026 and 40% by 2028. This is not a niche trend. It is a fundamental shift in the compute architecture that underpins AI deployment, and it has direct implications for memory content, pricing, and margin structure.

The memory makers themselves are enjoying output increases and margin expansion, supported by long-term agreement structures that stabilize earnings. But the more important insight from the tour is that the memory cycle is already well understood by the market. The earnings trajectory for Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix is visible. The real alpha, and the area where the tour provided fresh datapoints, lies in the equipment and materials layer that supports memory expansion. Semiconductor equipment and materials companies have not yet fully reflected the coming wave of aggressive capital expenditure by memory makers. That wave is coming, and the companies that supply the tools and materials for capacity expansion are entering a multi-year growth phase that is independent of bit-growth cycles.

## The Package Substrate Sector Is a Clear AI Beneficiary That Most Investors Are Underestimating, and It Is Running Ahead of Supply

The tour highlighted a segment that does not receive enough attention in the AI narrative: package substrates, specifically FC-BGA substrates. This is the physical layer that connects chips to the rest of the system, and it is becoming a bottleneck. High-end FC-BGA demand is running ahead of supply, signaling high utilization rates and extending lead times. Customers are actively seeking long-term agreements and co-investment structures to secure capacity. This is not a cyclical uptick. It is a structural supply-demand imbalance driven by the increasing size, layer count, and complexity of AI processors.

The structural drivers are clear. Larger and higher layer bodies are required to accommodate the growing number of interconnects in advanced processors. Silicon capacitor embedding is adding functionality and value per unit. Glass core substrates represent a technological shift that could redefine the substrate market over the next several years. Each of these trends lifts content per unit, supporting a multi-year growth story that is not dependent on unit shipment growth alone.

The global ABF substrate market is expected to move from a supply surplus of 17% in 2024 to a deficit of 7% in 2026 and a deficit of 35% by 2028. That is a dramatic swing, and it has implications for pricing power, margin expansion, and the competitive dynamics among substrate manufacturers. Investors who focus only on the chip layer are missing a significant part of the value chain. The substrate layer is where the physical constraints of AI infrastructure are becoming most apparent, and the companies that can address those constraints will command premium valuations.

## Semiconductor Equipment and Materials Companies Have Plenty of Runway Left, Driven by Content and Customer Expansion That Is Independent of Bit-Growth

This is the section where the tour provided the most novel insights. The equipment and materials layer is often treated as a derivative of memory capital expenditure, but the tour revealed that several companies are pursuing technology transitions that create independent growth drivers. EO Technics is seeing laser full-cut technology begin to displace mechanical dicing in HBM production. That is a process change, not a volume change, and it has significant implications for equipment content per wafer. The same company is experiencing a sharp ramp in PCB drilling and is co-developing a debonder with a leading foundry. These are application expansions that open new revenue streams unrelated to memory bit-growth.

HPSP is broadening its reach with a new DRAM customer expected in the third quarter of 2026 and is engaging with NAND customers. This is customer expansion, not market expansion, and it demonstrates that the company's technology is becoming a standard part of the memory manufacturing process. Park Systems is winning atomic force microscopy traction in hybrid bonding and advanced packaging metrology. These are high-value, technically demanding applications that command premium pricing and create switching costs for customers.

Hansol Chemical is advancing its precursor technology with optionality into the late-2026 patent expiry of hafnium-based high-K precursors. This is a specific, time-bound opportunity that could reshape the competitive dynamics of the precursor market. The common thread across these companies is that they are not simply riding the memory capex wave. They are creating proprietary technology positions that give them pricing power and customer stickiness independent of the volume cycle. For investors, this is the most attractive characteristic in a cyclical industry.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: How Much of the Current Capex Cycle Is Driven by Genuine Demand Versus Strategic Positioning by CSPs?

The tour data is compelling, but it raises a question that the report does not fully resolve. Cloud service provider capital expenditure is growing at extraordinary rates, with US CSPs alone expected to increase capex by 70% in 2026. Chinese CSPs are adding to the total, pushing global CSP capex toward USD738 billion. The question is whether this spending is justified by current revenue generation or whether it reflects a strategic land-grab mentality driven by competitive dynamics rather than return on investment.

The report provides some data points that suggest the economics are improving. The cost per million tokens has fallen by 35x from the Hopper generation to the Blackwell generation, and gross profit margins at AI service providers are improving, with Anthropic reportedly reaching 70% margins. But the scale of capex is so large that even these improvements may not be sufficient to generate acceptable returns. The report shows that Open AI's cloud infrastructure costs are expected to reach USD85 billion in 2026 and USD205 billion by 2030. Those numbers are staggering, and they imply a level of revenue growth that may not materialize if agentic AI adoption does not accelerate as expected.

This is not a reason to dismiss the opportunity. It is a reason to be selective. The companies that are selling picks and shovels to the CSPs, such as memory makers, substrate manufacturers, and equipment suppliers, are less exposed to the demand risk than the CSPs themselves. Their revenue is driven by capex, not by end-user adoption. But if the capex cycle slows, the equipment and materials layer will feel the impact with a lag. The question is whether the current pace of investment is sustainable, and the report does not provide a definitive answer. Investors should monitor CSP commentary on return on invested capital and capacity utilization as leading indicators.

## The Decision Framework for Investors: Focus on Technology Transitions, Not Volume Cycles, and Look for Companies with Multiple Growth Vectors

The tour provides a clear framework for investment decisions in the Korean technology supply chain. The first principle is to distinguish between companies that are dependent on volume growth and companies that are benefiting from technology transitions. Volume-dependent companies, such as commodity memory manufacturers, will perform well during the upcycle but are exposed to the inevitable downcycle. Technology transition companies, such as EO Technics with its laser full-cut adoption or Hansol Chemical with its precursor optionality, have growth drivers that persist regardless of the volume environment.

The second principle is to look for companies with multiple growth vectors. Samsung Electronics is the most obvious example. The report prefers Samsung not only for its HBM4 potential but also for its ability to narrow the technology gap in commodity DRAM. That is two vectors: one tied to AI-specific demand and one tied to the broader memory market. EO Technics has at least three vectors: laser dicing in HBM, PCB drilling, and foundry debonder co-development. Each vector provides diversification and reduces single-point-of-failure risk.

The third principle is to pay attention to customer concentration and long-term agreement structures. The substrate sector is already seeing customers seek LTAs and co-investment. This is a positive signal for revenue visibility and margin stability. Companies that can secure long-term commitments from customers are more attractive than those that rely on spot market pricing.

The fourth principle is to recognize that the equipment and materials layer is still early in its cycle. Memory earnings are solid and well-understood. Equipment and materials earnings are about to take off. The tour provided fresh datapoints that suggest content expansion and customer wins are happening now, but the financial impact has not yet fully materialized in reported earnings. This creates a window for investors who are willing to look forward.

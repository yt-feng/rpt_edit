## Resilient US Pricing and a Shift Toward High-Margin Product Mix Create a Clear Advantage for Japanese Automakers with Strong US Exposure

Ford’s management explicitly stated that pricing and product mix in the US market remain favorable. Overall demand for pickup trucks is robust, and the share of V8 engines is rising. This is not a temporary phenomenon driven by inventory restocking or fleet sales. It reflects a structural preference among US consumers for larger, higher-margin vehicles, even as interest rates remain elevated and used car prices moderate. The full-year pricing increase of approximately 0.5% may appear modest in absolute terms, but in an industry where a single percentage point of pricing improvement can translate into hundreds of millions of dollars in operating profit, it is a material tailwind.

For Japanese automakers, the implication is straightforward. Honda, Toyota, and Subaru all have significant US sales exposure. They compete directly in the crossover, SUV, and light truck segments that benefit from the same consumer dynamics. More importantly, these companies have invested heavily in hybrid electric vehicle powertrains, which command higher transaction prices and better margins than their conventional counterparts. Ford’s results validate that the US market is not yet saturated for profitable internal combustion and hybrid vehicles. The demand for these products is coexisting with, not being cannibalized by, the electric vehicle transition.

The risk that some observers may overlook is the potential for a demand pull-forward. If US consumers are accelerating purchases in anticipation of future tariff increases or interest rate cuts, the current strength could prove cyclical rather than structural. However, Ford’s guidance, issued in late July, incorporates second-quarter actuals and forward-looking assumptions. The upward revision suggests that management sees the trajectory as durable, not fleeting. This provides a degree of corroboration for the earnings outlooks of Japanese automakers that have yet to report comparable quarterly results.

## USMCA and Currency Dynamics Introduce a Policy Risk That Favors Japanese Automakers with Local Production

Ford’s management did not shy away from addressing the trade policy landscape. The company explicitly stated that it is the brand with the highest US production ratio and argued that the USMCA framework should be discussed with a clear focus on the fact that Japanese and South Korean automakers benefit from weak local currencies and face a 15% export tariff to the US.

This is a pointed reminder that trade policy is not static. The USMCA is scheduled for review in 2026, and the political environment in the United States remains sensitive to automotive trade imbalances. A renegotiation could result in stricter rules of origin, higher local content requirements, or even tariff adjustments that disadvantage import-dependent manufacturers.

For Japanese automakers, the defensive response is already in place. Toyota, Honda, and Subaru all operate significant manufacturing capacity in the United States. Their US production ratios are among the highest for foreign-based automakers. This means that even in a scenario where USMCA terms become more restrictive, these companies are less exposed than peers that rely heavily on imports from Japan or Mexico.

The currency dimension adds another layer of nuance. The Japanese yen has weakened substantially against the US dollar, which creates a competitive advantage for Japanese automakers when they export vehicles to the US. But it also means that their US-sourced profits, when repatriated, translate into higher yen-denominated earnings. This is a net positive for shareholders, but it also invites political scrutiny. Ford’s explicit mention of the weak yen as a benefit for Japanese competitors suggests that the issue is on the radar of US policymakers. Observers should factor in a modest probability of policy changes that could erode this advantage over time.

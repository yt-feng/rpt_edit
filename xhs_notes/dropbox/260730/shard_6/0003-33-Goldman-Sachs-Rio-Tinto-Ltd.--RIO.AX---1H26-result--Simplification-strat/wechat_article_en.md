## The “Stronger, Sharper, Simpler” Agenda Is Not a Rhetorical Framework But a Quantified Cost Transformation That Is Already Delivering Results

At its investor day in late 2025, Rio Tinto outlined a “stronger, sharper, simpler” agenda focused on cost reduction, portfolio simplification, and decentralized decision-making. The 1H26 result provides the first tangible evidence that this agenda is more than aspirational. The cost-out target has been increased from USD 0.65 billion to USD 1.8 billion by year-end, with USD 1.3 billion already achieved on an annualized run-rate basis by the end of the first half. Of the USD 0.87 billion banked year-to-date, USD 0.34 billion came from volume improvements and USD 0.5 billion from cash cost reductions.

Management has indicated that the additional approximately USD 1 billion to be delivered in the second half will come roughly evenly from volumes and costs, and that month-on-month momentum is building. This is significant because the USD 1.8 billion target represents roughly 5% of Rio Tinto’s annual underlying cost base of approximately USD 37 billion. In an environment where inflationary pressures continue to affect mining inputs globally, a cost-out program of this magnitude provides a meaningful buffer against margin compression.

The cost transformation is not limited to operational efficiency. Rio Tinto is shifting from a centralized operating model to a leaner, decentralized structure that pushes decision-making back to mine sites. This organizational change is designed to improve responsiveness and accountability, but it also has a direct financial implication: it enables faster identification and elimination of inefficiencies. The company has identified further cost-out opportunities beyond the USD 1.8 billion run rate, some of which will be realized through divestments of non-core assets such as borates and mineral sands.

The divestment program itself is progressing. Rio Tinto has identified USD 5-10 billion of non-core assets, with approximately USD 5 billion expected to be released by the end of 2026. A potential Pilbara power transaction could unlock an additional USD 3-4 billion in the second half of 2026. These divestments serve a dual purpose: they simplify the portfolio and provide cash to reduce the net debt position, which stood at USD 14.1 billion at the end of the first half. The leverage ratio of 0.5 times net debt to EBITDA is manageable, but the divestment program will accelerate deleveraging and create capacity for either increased shareholder returns or further growth investment.

## Rio Tinto’s Copper Equivalent Production Growth of Approximately 10% Through 2030 Positions It as a Structural Beneficiary of the Energy Transition, Not a Cyclical Iron Ore Play

The most important strategic shift underway at Rio Tinto is the rebalancing of its earnings mix away from iron ore and toward copper and aluminium. The company forecasts copper equivalent production to grow by approximately 10% from 2025 to 2030, with EBITDA growing by approximately 30% over the same period. This growth is driven by a diversified set of assets: the ramp-up of the Oyu Tolgoi underground copper mine, higher grades at Bingham Canyon from 2027, the ramp-up of Simandou iron ore, higher Pilbara iron ore shipments to a medium-term target of 345-360 million tonnes per annum by 2028, expanding aluminium margins, and lithium growth in Argentina.

The copper story is particularly compelling. Rio Tinto’s copper mined production is forecast to increase from 883 kilotonnes in 2025 to over 1,000 kilotonnes by 2028, driven primarily by Oyu Tolgoi. The company also has longer-dated copper growth options including Resolution in Arizona, La Granja in Peru, Winu in Australia, and the Bingham Canyon pit extension. Resolution copper is particularly noteworthy: Rio Tinto has commenced surface drilling in newly accessible areas following a land exchange, with underground drilling scheduled to begin in the third quarter of 2026. This project alone could transform Rio Tinto’s copper profile if it reaches production.

The aluminium business is also undergoing a strategic repositioning. Rio Tinto recently entered into a definitive agreement to acquire a controlling stake in Companhia Brasileira de Alumínio (CBA) through a joint venture with its major shareholder Chalco. The total acquisition cost is approximately USD 903 million, of which Rio Tinto’s share is approximately USD 298 million. This transaction addresses a structural imbalance in Rio Tinto’s alumina portfolio: the company is long 3.3 million tonnes per annum of alumina production in the Pacific Basin but short 2.6 million tonnes in the Atlantic Basin. The CBA acquisition helps close that gap and strengthens Rio Tinto’s position in the Atlantic aluminium market.

The lithium growth story is in its early stages but has significant potential. Rio Tinto’s lithium carbonate production is forecast to increase from 56 kilotonnes in 2025 to 172 kilotonnes by 2030, driven primarily by the Rincon project in Argentina. While lithium prices remain volatile, Rio Tinto’s cost position and project execution capability provide a degree of downside protection that pure-play lithium producers may lack.

On earnings mix, the key question is whether investors are being adequately compensated for the transition from iron ore to copper and aluminium. Rio Tinto’s copper and aluminium earnings are expected to account for 45-50% of group EBITDA by 2026 and 60% by 2030. This shift implies that Rio Tinto’s earnings will become less correlated with Chinese steel demand and more correlated with global electrification and energy transition spending. If investors begin to re-rate the stock based on this earnings mix shift, the valuation could expand from the current 7 times EBITDA to the 8-9 times range that would be more consistent with a diversified base metal company.

On execution risk, the key variables are the Oyu Tolgoi ramp-up, the Simandou project timeline, and the cost-out program. Rio Tinto has recently announced a change to the underground mine plan at Oyu Tolgoi due to a delay in the Mongolian government’s approval of a license transfer from Entrée Resources. This could result in a 50 kilotonne impact to copper and gold production in 2027 and 2028. However, Rio Tinto is advancing lower-grade panels in the interim, and the Mongolian government has agreed to commence work on resolving the license transfer. Execution risk at Oyu Tolgoi is real but manageable, and the project remains on track to deliver significant production growth by the end of the decade.

Simandou is progressing on schedule, with first production already achieved in 2025 and ramp-up continuing through 2028. The project is complex, requiring significant infrastructure investment in Guinea, but Rio Tinto’s track record of project execution provides confidence. The cost-out program is ahead of schedule, with USD 0.87 billion already banked year-to-date against a full-year target of USD 1.8 billion.

The primary risk to the thesis is a sharp downturn in commodity prices, particularly iron ore and copper. However, Rio Tinto’s cost position and balance sheet strength provide a buffer. The company’s Pilbara unit cost guidance of USD 23.5-25 per tonne means it remains profitable even at significantly lower iron ore prices. The copper business benefits from a low-cost position at Oyu Tolgoi and Bingham Canyon. The divestment program provides additional financial flexibility.

Rio Tinto’s 1H26 result demonstrates that the company is executing on its strategic priorities while generating strong financial performance. The valuation has retreated to historical averages despite a clear improvement in the quality and diversification of the earnings base. For investors with a medium-term horizon, the risk-reward profile is attractive.

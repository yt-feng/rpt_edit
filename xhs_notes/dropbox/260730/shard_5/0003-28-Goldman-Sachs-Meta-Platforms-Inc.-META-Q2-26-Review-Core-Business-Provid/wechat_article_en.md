# Meta’s Q2’26 Results Show AI Investment Impact in Core Business, but Questions Remain on Longer-Term Returns

Meta Platforms delivered a Q2’26 earnings report that, on the surface, should have been received with enthusiasm. Advertising revenue grew 28% year-over-year, powered by broad-based engagement gains and the continued adoption of AI-driven tools. Advantage+, the company’s AI-powered advertising suite, reached a $75 billion annualized revenue run rate in the quarter alone. Management guided Q3 revenue above consensus expectations. And yet, the stock has fallen 12.5% over the past three months and trades at roughly 17 times forward GAAP earnings, a discount that reflects a fundamental tension: the company is generating clear, near-term returns on its AI compute investments within its core business, but it has provided virtually no visibility into the scale, duration, or ultimate payoff of the massive capital spending required to build the next generation of AI infrastructure.

This is not a story of failure. It is a story of a company that has successfully demonstrated that its AI investments yield measurable improvements in content recommendation, monetization efficiency, and advertiser outcomes. The problem is that the market now needs to decide whether those returns are sufficient to justify a capital expenditure trajectory that, according to the latest estimates, will push cumulative 2027-2028 capex approximately 14% higher than previously forecast. Management has made clear that it values building and scaling compute capacity in the near term over long-term capital discipline. That is a strategic bet, not a mistake. But it is a bet that demands a much clearer framework for how the company will eventually monetize compute capacity beyond the core advertising business.

The core of the investor debate has shifted. It is no longer about whether AI works inside Meta’s Family of Apps. It works. The question is whether the company can productize that compute into new revenue streams at a scale and pace that will eventually justify the spending trajectory. Until that question receives a more concrete answer, the stock is likely to remain range-bound, caught between strong operational performance and an overhang of unresolved capital allocation uncertainty.

## The Core Business Is Delivering Exactly What AI Investment Promised, But That Alone Cannot Sustain the Current Spending Trajectory

Meta’s Q2’26 advertising performance is the strongest evidence to date that its massive AI infrastructure investments are generating a measurable return within the core business. Advertising revenue growth of 28% year-over-year was not driven by user growth alone. It was driven by algorithmic improvements that increased engagement, recommendation accuracy, and the efficiency of the ad auction. The $75 billion annualized run rate for Advantage+ is particularly instructive: this is a product that did not exist three years ago, and it now represents a revenue stream larger than the entire annual revenue of most major internet companies.

The causal chain is clear. More compute enables larger and more frequent model training cycles. Better models improve content ranking and ad targeting. Improved targeting increases advertiser willingness to pay and user engagement. Higher engagement and pricing feed back into revenue growth. This is a virtuous cycle that Meta has now empirically validated. The company’s Q3 revenue guidance, which came in above consensus, suggests management sees no near-term deceleration in this dynamic.

But there is a structural problem embedded in this success. The revenue growth generated by AI improvements within the core business is happening against a baseline of already-high engagement and monetization. The marginal return on incremental compute for content recommendation and ad targeting is likely to diminish over time. The first wave of AI improvements—moving from heuristic-based ranking to deep learning models, then to large-scale transformer architectures—produced step-function gains. The next wave will require increasingly larger investments to produce increasingly smaller improvements in core metrics. The revenue growth rate of 27.1% estimated for 2026 is impressive, but the capital intensity of the business is projected to reach 56% in the same year, up from 35.9% in 2025. The ratio of revenue growth to capital spending growth is deteriorating, and that is the mathematical reality that the market is pricing.

## The Absence of a Credible Long-Term Monetization Framework for Compute Beyond the Core Is the Central Investor Overhang

Management’s Q2’26 commentary outlined a broad set of potential AI monetization opportunities outside the core advertising business. These include personalized consumer AI agents, AI tools for advertisers and creators, enterprise foundational model APIs, tools for building and scaling AI operations, and direct monetization of compute capacity through cloud licensing deals. The range of possibilities is wide, and the ambition is clear. But the level of specificity is low.

The challenge for investors is that these opportunities exist on very different timelines and require very different levels of incremental investment. Consumer AI agents, for example, could generate subscription revenue through offerings like Meta One, but the adoption curve for paid AI assistants is unproven at scale. Enterprise API access and cloud licensing deals would require Meta to compete directly with established cloud providers that have years of head start in enterprise sales, compliance, and infrastructure reliability. The “business in a box” tools for SMBs and creators are conceptually compelling, but they depend on a distribution advantage that Meta has not yet demonstrated outside of advertising.

Management has been explicit about one thing: it places greater value on building and scaling compute capacity in the near term than on preserving long-term capital flexibility. This is a defensible strategic choice if the company believes that compute itself will become a monetizable asset. But it is a choice that makes the absence of a detailed monetization roadmap more consequential. When a company is spending $130-145 billion in capex in a single year, the market needs more than a menu of possible use cases. It needs a framework for understanding which of those use cases will generate material revenue, on what timeline, and at what incremental cost.

The most telling data point in the report is the free cash flow trajectory. Meta generated $46.1 billion in free cash flow in 2025. That figure is projected to fall to $13.9 billion in 2026, turn negative at negative $26.1 billion in 2027, and recover only modestly to $12.1 billion in 2028. A company with a $1.5 trillion market capitalization is projected to generate cumulative free cash flow of approximately zero over a three-year period. This is not a sign of distress; Meta has a strong balance sheet and can fund these investments through debt issuance. But it does mean that the company is effectively asking investors to accept that the long-term payoff from compute infrastructure will be large enough to compensate for a multi-year period of negligible or negative free cash flow. That is a reasonable ask only if the monetization path is clear. Today, it is not.

## The Capital Spending Cycle Has a Defined Near-Term Shape, But the Outer Years Remain a Black Box That the Market Cannot Yet Price

Management raised the low end of its fiscal 2026 capex guidance to $130-145 billion, a narrow upward revision that signals internal confidence in the near-term investment plan. The analyst estimates projecting capital intensity peaking at 70.9% in 2027 before declining to 62.4% in 2028 are based on the assumption that the current buildout cycle will moderate as infrastructure comes online and depreciation catches up with spending. Depreciation and amortization is projected to rise from $18.6 billion in 2025 to $97.0 billion in 2028, a fivefold increase that will mechanically compress reported earnings even if operating performance remains strong.

This pattern—a surge in capital spending followed by a period of elevated depreciation and margin compression—is not unusual for companies undergoing large-scale infrastructure buildouts. Amazon experienced a similar dynamic during its AWS expansion years, and the market eventually rewarded the investment once the revenue stream materialized. The difference is that Amazon’s cloud business had a clear, proven revenue model before the largest investments were made. Meta is building the infrastructure and searching for the business model simultaneously.

The lack of visibility into 2029 and beyond is the most significant unresolved issue. Management has not provided a framework for how capital spending will evolve after the current cycle peaks. Will capex decline as a percentage of revenue once the infrastructure is built? Will it remain elevated if the company decides to compete directly in the cloud computing market? Will the monetization of AI agents and enterprise services generate enough revenue to fund continued investment without margin dilution? These are not rhetorical questions. They are the central determinants of Meta’s intrinsic value over the next five years, and they remain unanswered.

The analyst estimates in the report extend through 2031 and show capital intensity gradually declining from 70.9% in 2027 to 54.9% in 2031, with revenue growth decelerating from 27.1% in 2026 to 12.9% in 2031. These are reasonable baseline assumptions, but they are precisely that—assumptions. The range of possible outcomes is extraordinarily wide. If AI compute becomes a genuinely new revenue category for Meta, the revenue trajectory could be significantly higher and the capital intensity could decline faster. If the core advertising business matures and the new monetization streams fail to scale, the company could face a prolonged period of sub-20x earnings multiples and negative free cash flow.

## Investors Need a Decision Framework That Separates the Proved Core Returns from the Unproven Compute Monetization Thesis

For investors evaluating Meta at current levels, the appropriate analytical approach is to separate the business into two distinct components and value them independently. The first component is the core Family of Apps advertising business, which has now demonstrated that AI investments produce measurable revenue growth and margin improvement. This business can be valued using traditional discounted cash flow or earnings multiple frameworks, with the caveat that capital intensity will remain elevated for the next two to three years. At 17 times 2027 GAAP earnings, the core business alone may be undervalued if advertising revenue growth continues at 20% or higher and if the company can maintain operating discipline on the expense side outside of depreciation.

The second component is the compute monetization optionality—the potential revenue from AI agents, enterprise services, cloud licensing, and other non-advertising AI products. This component should be valued as an option, not as a base-case cash flow stream. The option has significant upside if any of the identified monetization pathways achieve scale. It also has a defined cost: the incremental capital spending above what would be required to sustain the core business. Investors should ask whether the option premium embedded in the current stock price is reasonable given the range of possible outcomes.

The critical decision point for management will come in the next 12 to 18 months. If the company can provide concrete evidence that one or more of its non-advertising AI monetization initiatives is gaining traction—measured in terms of paying users, contract value, or revenue contribution—the stock could re-rate significantly as the market begins to price the optionality. If, instead, the narrative remains qualitative and the capital spending continues to rise without a corresponding revenue story, the overhang will persist regardless of how well the core business performs.

The report’s conclusion that current stock performance underestimates Meta’s ability to productize compute is a defensible view, but it depends on an act of faith that the company will deliver specificity within a timeframe that matters to the market. The evidence from Q2’26 is that the core engine is running well. The evidence that the new engine exists is still largely circumstantial.

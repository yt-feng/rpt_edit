## CONTEXT

Nike has been losing market share every year since 2020. Nike was one of the earliest international Sportswear brands to establish a presence in the market, entering China in the early 1980s and spending the next several decades building a dominant position and gaining share, mostly coming from domestic brands. Nike and other major international brands together reached a peak of 57% market share in 2020 (Exhibit 2) of which Nike alone was half at 27% (Exhibit 1).

But since then, due to external factors (e.g. the 2021 Xinjiang cotton controversy and 2022-23 COVID-related lockdowns, which disproportionately hurt Western brands that over-indexed to high-tier cities) as well as stronger efforts by domestic brands to gain share, Nike's share of the market gradually declined from $27\%$ to the low-20s.

Then from 2024 onwards, Nike saw sharper declines, driven by specific inventory and clearance issues on major Lifestyle franchises that the brand became over-reliant on, as we have seen across Nike's global markets. Nike undertook a multi-year marketplace cleanup that included buying back inventory from partners, reducing sell-in, liquidating product, and in some cases physically moving inventory out of China into neighboring markets (e.g., Turkey), which resulted in more share losses, down to $16\%$ in 2025.

For more on the broader context of the China market and our views on growth, see our note from June 2026: Global Sportswear: State of the sector in China

Franchise management is largely done — the goal is now to rebuild the brand. While Nike’s efforts in China have moved slower than other regions — e.g. North America was cleaned up in 2024-25 and has been growing since Summer 2025 — the commentary from Mgmt and from retail channel checks suggest that Nike is now in the final stages of inventory cleanup. Meanwhile, marketplace sell-through trends are now positive, and comp trends at 100+ renovated stores in China are positive (see: Nike: Key takeaways from Bernstein's Retail Forum).

The next piece of the strategy is around rebuilding the brand. As Cathy Sparks, Nike's VP and GM of Greater China, noted in her open letter last week, the goal is to return to “a more premium digital marketplace, a stronger retail experience, more locally created product, and teams empowered closer to the consumer, all working together to deliver a consistent, elevated Nike experience.”

The current focus is online distribution and marketplace management, for both DTC and Wholesale. On the DTC side, Nike Mgmt believes the brand has done too much aggressive discounting on platforms, such as during major online shopping festivals like 6.18 and 11.11, and that the DTC online business needs to restore full-price sell-through by limiting participation in deep promotional events. On the Wholesale side, Nike is trying to crack down on resellers and gray market sales that result in widespread availability of discounted Nike product online, including via secondary distributors and unauthorized channels. This glut of discounted online product has dragged down Nike's brand perceptions in the market, a problem that Mgmt is now focused on fixing.

## There are two key actions taking place over the coming quarters:

(1) Cutting online sell-in to Wholesale partners: reductions are already underway, so that by January 2027 Nike product will not be sold online by Wholesale partners. Impact will be felt starting Q1 FY27 as per Nike's guidance. We estimate these cuts reach their full run-rate in H2 FY27, with potentially some small second-year impact in H1 FY28 from lapping some early sell-in in H1 FY27.

(2) Trimming Nike's DTC online business to stop participating in discount events: Reductions have already begun as of FY26 and will continue in FY27 — however, there will be some offsetting share shift that will benefit Nike DTC online, from Wholesale online being cut to zero, so on a net basis we expect DTC online to benefit from these changes.

The goal is to concentrate online traffic into fewer, higher-quality touchpoints. Beginning January 2027, Nike's digital presence will be limited to only direct Web/app sales and official Nike flagship stores on key platforms (e.g., Tmall, JD.com, and Douyin), not including partner-operated online storefronts. In explaining the strategy, Sparks emphasized that the objective is not reducing access but rather “reducing fragmentation and strengthening the consumer journey.” Nike believes these official destinations can provide “clearer product presentation, stronger storytelling and more connected consumer journeys” while helping drive greater consistency across the ecosystem.

Offline correction currently not being discussed. Nike is investing heavily into offline presence including store renovations and localized retail concepts, but a similar “reset” of the offline footprint is not currently under discussion.

## OUR VIEW

## IMPLICATIONS FOR NIKE IN CHINA

This is a double-digit growth headwind for the Nike China business, which has already struggled to grow. Using the disclosures from Topsports and Pou Sheng, Nike's two biggest Wholesale partners in China, as well as our estimates of smaller partners, we estimate that Wholesale online accounts for a high-teens percent of Nike's China business currently. This business is being entirely wound down to zero over the next couple of quarters: a \$1b hit to the China business, which already hasn't grown since FY24 (Exhibit 6). Following the clearing of inventory, this reset creates another major near-term headwind for the region, pushing China growth back yet another year.

Growth is the main data point investors need to see to believe in Nike's longevity. While Mgmt has sounded optimistic on margin recovery (even more so now after this China strategy, as the loss of discount online sales will further boost margins), growth has been elusive and the goalpost has been pushed back multiple times. Based on H1 guidance, the China headwind amounts to roughly 200 bps of total company growth (\~12% of sales x \~15% decline), which we expect should continue through H2. From an investor sentiment perspective, this further delays Nike's path to top line growth, and with it investor confidence in the longevity and sustainability of the business model.

Some of these Wholesale sales will likely be recovered... but we expect the majority to be given up. The natural consequence of Nike DTC being the only available shopping option on Tmall, JD, Douyin, Taobao and other sites is that Nike's online DTC business should capture some of the sales that would have otherwise gone to Wholesale online partners — therefore, we do expect DTC online to grow as a result. However, if the goal is to stop discounting online and replace with full-price product, we expect that the customer for those discounted goods will not be suddenly willing to pay full-price, but will instead switch to another brand within their price range. As a result, we expect the majority of this online market share to be lost to competitors, with some recouped in the DTC online business.

Can Nike gain back this lost share eventually? We are not expecting it. With premium Western brands competing at the high-end of the price spectrum and domestic competitors at the lower-end of the price spectrum, it seems like Nike is being squeezed in the middle. Even after this reset, we model Nike growing in line with the China market, with some possible upside in terms of share gains depending on the success of the new Lifestyle pipeline coming in CY27 (which should benefit FY28-29 sales) but not going back to “fair share” and recouping the market share that was given up. Longer term, we expect Nike to continue to grow in China but about in-line with the market, not regaining its prior position of market share dominance.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR THE COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

Adidas is likely the biggest near-term beneficiary. As Nike removes a meaningful amount of discounted product from China's online marketplace, pricing pressure across the category should ease. At the same time, partners such as Topsports and Pou Sheng will need to replace lost Nike online volume, likely resulting in greater emphasis on Adidas product. Given Adidas is already growing DD% in China and seeing strong traction across categories, we view Nike's distribution reset as another incremental tailwind to FY27 growth.

Domestic brands should also benefit, particularly in lower-price and more promotional channels. A portion of the online demand Nike is intentionally walking away from is likely to migrate toward Anta, Li Ning, and other local players. To the extent Nike is prioritizing quality of sales over volume, domestic competitors appear well positioned to absorb some of the demand at the lower-end of the price spectrum previously captured through heavily discounted online channels.

Premium Western brands should largely be unaffected. Brands such as On and Hoka have limited Wholesale exposure in China and do not sell to a wide range of distributors and resellers, so Nike's exit of those distributors should not affect them. Hoka works with Topsports and should expect to see higher demand from this partner; similarly, On works with 1-2 others of Nike's top Wholesale partners. But the gating factor is the brands controlling distribution, not lack of retailer demand, so higher demand should not make much of a difference.

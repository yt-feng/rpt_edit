# Mercedes Is Not Just Surviving—It Is Reshaping Its Cost Base and Capital Strategy for a Lower-Growth World

Mercedes-Benz Group reported second-quarter 2026 results that beat the company-compiled consensus on adjusted operating income at the Passenger Cars division—4.0 percent versus 3.5 percent expected—and the stock initially rose as much as 6 percent before settling up 2.9 percent on the day. The headline numbers look respectable in an environment that management itself describes as extremely challenging. Group adjusted EBIT reached €2.3 billion, 40 percent above consensus, and free cash flow came in at €1.1 billion, 51 percent ahead of expectations.

Yet beneath the surface, the quarter reveals a company navigating three distinct strategic tensions simultaneously. Mercedes is executing a credible cost transformation away from its high-cost German base, it is absorbing a structural impairment in its China joint ventures without altering its long-term commitment to that market, and it is systematically monetizing its Daimler Truck stake to fund an aggressive capital return programme. The stock trades at roughly 8.7 times forward adjusted earnings with a 7.5 percent dividend yield, but the sum-of-the-parts discount has been widened from 20 percent to 25 percent to reflect the more uncertain outlook for China operations.

The question for observers is not whether Mercedes can beat a low consensus in a given quarter. The question is whether the strategic actions underway—cost relocation, platform modernization, and portfolio simplification—are sufficient to offset the structural margin compression coming from China and the transition to battery electric vehicles. The evidence from the second quarter suggests the answer is conditional: Mercedes is making the right moves, but the headwinds are large enough that the margin corridor of 3 to 5 percent for Passenger Cars may become a structural ceiling rather than a cyclical floor.

## Mercedes Is Shifting Production Geography at a Pace That Changes the Unit Economics of Its European Output

The most consequential strategic action in the quarter was not visible in the profit-and-loss statement as a single line item. It was the official opening of the next phase at the Kecskemét plant in Hungary, where capacity has been doubled to 400,000 units. Factor costs at that site—covering labor, energy, plant overhead, and logistics, but excluding raw materials and purchased parts—are approximately 70 percent lower than equivalent costs in Germany.

This is not a marginal adjustment. Mercedes is targeting an increase in the share of production from low-cost countries from roughly 15 percent to 30 percent of its total footprint. The company also aims to raise local-for-local production from 60 percent to 70 percent by 2027, which reduces currency exposure, logistics expense, and tariff risk simultaneously. The Kecskemét expansion enables European production of the electric C-Class, the GLC, and compact G derivatives on the same lines that can also build internal combustion and hybrid variants.

The productivity programme that underpins this shift has already delivered a 19 percent fixed-cost reduction between 2019 and 2024, net of inflation. The next target is a further reduction of more than 10 percent over the following three years, achieved through headcount optimization, management layer reduction, and process streamlining. Production costs are targeted to fall by 10 percent by 2027 on top of earlier capacity adjustments.

What makes this credible is that the cost reduction is not dependent on volume recovery. It is structural: moving physical production to lower-cost locations, digitizing logistics, and negotiating working-time arrangements with German unions. The Germany-focused Productivity Initiative explicitly targets labour and energy costs through internal efficiency and political negotiation rather than waiting for demand to rebound. For a company that still generates the majority of its value in Europe, this geographic rebalancing is the single most important lever for protecting margins in a lower-growth environment.

## The China Impairment Is a Signal of Lower Future Returns, Not a Change in Strategic Commitment

Mercedes recorded a non-cash impairment of €704 million on its Chinese joint ventures in the second quarter, which reduced reported Passenger Cars EBIT to just €49 million. The equity-accounted contribution from BBAC, the main China joint venture, swung from a positive €115 million in the first quarter to a negative €560 million in the second quarter, entirely driven by the impairment charge.

Management explicitly stated that the impairment reflected lower anticipated future returns from the Chinese operations, based on market developments and cost pressures. The China market itself fell approximately 20 percent in the quarter, materially affecting both Mercedes volumes and joint venture economics. Yet management reaffirmed its strategic commitment to China, citing localization of the long-wheelbase GLE from August, deeper technology partnerships, and strengthened local research and development.

The tension here is real and unresolved. Mercedes continues to lead the premium average selling price hierarchy in China even amid pricing pressure, and it is targeting measured BEV volumes for the new GLC and C-class BEVs to avoid cash-burning price wars. But the impairment is a concrete acknowledgment that the profit contribution from China will be structurally lower than previously assumed. The sum-of-the-parts discount was increased from 20 percent to 25 percent specifically to reflect this uncertainty.

For observers, the China impairment is not a one-time event to look through. It is a recurring risk factor that will need to be reassessed each quarter as local competitors continue to launch new models and price aggressively. Mercedes has the brand strength and product cadence to defend its position, but the equity returns from the joint ventures are unlikely to return to prior peaks. The strategic question is whether the profits from Europe and North America can compensate for this structural drag.

## The Daimler Truck Stake Monetization Is Becoming a Recurring Source of Cash That Changes the Capital Allocation Calculus

Mercedes generated €417 million in proceeds from the sale of Daimler Truck shares in the second quarter, and an additional €600 million in July as the truck maker's stock hit record highs. These disposals are not a one-time event. CFO Harald Wilhelm explicitly described them as phased and opportunistic, with the company retaining flexibility on timing, discount, and market impact—whether through piecemeal sales or accelerated bookbuilds.

The cumulative effect is significant. In the first half of 2026, Mercedes returned €5 billion to shareholders through dividends and share buybacks. It announced an additional €1 billion buyback that will likely be completed before the April 2027 annual general meeting. The Daimler Truck stake provides a source of cash that is independent of automotive operating performance, which means capital returns can be sustained even if Passenger Cars margins remain at the lower end of the guidance range.

This creates a differentiated profile. Mercedes is not just an automaker with cyclical earnings. It is a cash-generating entity that is systematically reducing a non-core holding to fund shareholder distributions while simultaneously investing in its largest-ever product and technology offensive. The net industrial liquidity stood at €30.4 billion at the end of the second quarter, providing ample balance sheet capacity.

The risk is that the Daimler Truck stake is finite. If the disposals continue at the current pace, the holding will be substantially reduced within two to three years. At that point, capital returns will need to be funded entirely by automotive free cash flow, which is currently under pressure from China headwinds, BEV transition costs, and tariff uncertainty. The current strategy buys time, but it does not solve the underlying margin challenge.

## A Decision Framework for Assessing Mercedes as an Investment Case

Observers evaluating Mercedes need to weigh three distinct drivers that are moving in different directions. The following framework organizes the evidence from the second quarter into a set of conditions that would make the equity case work or fail.

First, the cost transformation is real and measurable. The shift of production to Hungary, the reduction in fixed costs, and the target of a 10 percent further reduction in production costs by 2027 are all supported by concrete actions and disclosed metrics. If Mercedes can achieve a weighted average manufacturing cost that is structurally lower than its German-based competitors, it can sustain margins even if volumes do not recover to prior peaks. The key indicator to track is the share of production from low-cost countries, which is targeted to double from 15 percent to 30 percent.

Second, the China exposure is a structural drag that will not resolve quickly. The impairment is a signal that the joint ventures are worth less than previously assumed, and the competitive dynamics in the Chinese premium segment are intensifying. Mercedes has the brand and product to defend share, but the profit contribution from China will be a headwind for the foreseeable future. The key indicator to track is the equity-accounted earnings from BBAC, which swung from positive to negative in one quarter.

Third, the capital returns are attractive but partially dependent on a finite asset. The Daimler Truck stake provides a cushion that allows Mercedes to return cash to shareholders even when automotive earnings are under pressure. But this cushion is being depleted, and the sustainability of the dividend and buyback programme will ultimately depend on the recovery of Passenger Cars margins toward the midpoint of the 3 to 5 percent range. The key indicator to track is the pace of Daimler Truck disposals and the resulting reduction in the holding.

The stock trades at approximately 8.7 times forward adjusted earnings with a 7.5 percent dividend yield, which already embeds a significant degree of caution about the outlook. The sum-of-the-parts discount of 25 percent reflects the market's skepticism about China and the BEV transition. If Mercedes can execute the cost transformation and stabilize China earnings, the current valuation offers a margin of safety. If the headwinds intensify, the discount could widen further.

The second quarter results demonstrate that Mercedes is not passively waiting for conditions to improve. It is actively reshaping its cost base, monetizing non-core assets, and launching new products. The question is whether the pace of action is sufficient relative to the scale of the structural challenges. The evidence so far suggests it is, but only just.

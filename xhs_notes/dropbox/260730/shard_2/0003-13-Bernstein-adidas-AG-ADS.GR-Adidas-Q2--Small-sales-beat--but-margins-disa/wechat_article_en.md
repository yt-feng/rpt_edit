# Adidas Is Winning the Performance Battle but Losing the Profitability War

Adidas delivered a Q2 that perfectly captures the tension at the heart of its turnaround story: top-line growth that beat consensus by 100 basis points, yet an operating margin that missed by 99 basis points. Revenue came in at 6,743 million euros, up 14 percent on a currency-neutral basis, driven by a 39 percent surge in Performance footwear and apparel. But the operating margin contracted 70 basis points year over year to 8.5 percent, well below the 9.5 percent the Street had expected. The culprit was not gross margin, which expanded 80 basis points to 52.5 percent, but a 170-basis-point increase in marketing spend as a percentage of sales, combined with higher DTC-driven shipping costs and labor expenses that prevented operating overhead from delivering the leverage observers had anticipated.

This quarter matters because it is the first since the Yeezy partnership termination in October 2022 with no residual Yeezy impact on year-over-year comparisons. The clean base reveals the underlying economics of the new Adidas. And those economics tell a story of a brand that has successfully rebuilt its product heat in Performance categories but is now facing a strategic trade-off: invest aggressively to sustain momentum in a promotional marketplace, or protect margins and risk losing share. The FY26 guidance update, which raised the sales outlook to 9-10 percent currency-neutral growth but maintained operating profit at approximately 2.3 billion euros, signals that management has chosen investment. Observers must now decide whether that choice is prudent or a prelude to margin compression that the current valuation does not fully discount.

## Performance Growth Is Accelerating, but Lifestyle Has Become a Drag on the Top-Line Narrative

The most striking data point in Q2 is the divergence between Performance and Lifestyle. Performance grew 39 percent currency-neutral, accelerating from 29 percent in Q1 and 27 percent in Q4. This was led by double-digit growth in Football, driven by World Cup product and retro-inspired collections; Running, powered by the Adizero franchise and Hyperboost Edge; and Motorsport. Apparel grew 35 percent, supported by jersey launches, while footwear grew only 1 percent currency-neutral, albeit on top of a 9 percent comp in Q2 of the prior year excluding Yeezy. The footwear number matters because it suggests that the Performance revival is not yet translating into equivalent footwear traction, which is typically higher-margin and more brand-defining.

Lifestyle, by contrast, grew only 2 percent currency-neutral, decelerating from 6 percent in Q1 and 3 percent in Q4. The bright spot within Lifestyle was Originals apparel, which posted double-digit growth, and momentum in Low Profile and Lifestyle Running. But the overall slowing trajectory raises a question: Is Adidas experiencing a natural lifecycle shift as the Samba and Gazelle trend matures, or is the company deliberately deemphasizing Lifestyle to focus on Performance? The data suggests both. The company is investing marketing dollars behind Performance launches like the Adizero Dropset Elite and Pro in Training, which is sensible strategically but creates a near-term revenue hole that Performance must fill.

The strategic implication is clear: Adidas is betting that Performance can become the primary growth engine, replacing the Lifestyle-driven surge that fueled the initial post-Yeezy recovery. That bet is working in absolute terms, but the deceleration in Lifestyle means the company is now relying on a single engine. If Performance growth moderates in the second half, as the implied guidance of 4.2 to 6.2 percent currency-neutral growth for H2 suggests, the company will face a growth gap that neither segment can fill.

## DTC Is Outperforming Wholesale, but the Cost of Direct Control Is Higher Than the Market Anticipates

DTC grew 25 percent currency-neutral in Q2, with e-commerce up 27 percent and own retail up 23 percent. Wholesale grew only 6 percent, constrained by a tough comp of plus 14 percent in Q2 of the prior year and a deliberately conservative sell-in approach in Europe, where management cited an uncertain consumer environment and heightened promotional activity. The DTC outperformance is structurally positive: it gives Adidas greater control over pricing, brand presentation, and customer data. But it comes with a cost that the Q2 margin miss made visible.

Operating expenses as a percentage of sales increased 150 basis points year over year to 44.0 percent, well above the 42.2 percent consensus estimate. The breakdown is instructive. Marketing spend rose 170 basis points as a percentage of sales, driven by World Cup campaigns and major product launches. But operating overhead, which should have provided leverage as sales grew, instead expanded by 30 basis points relative to expectations. Management attributed this to higher DTC-driven shipping costs and labor expenses. In other words, the shift toward direct channels is structurally more expensive than the wholesale model it is replacing, at least in the near term.

This is a critical insight for observers. The bull case for Adidas has long rested on the idea that DTC expansion would improve margins by capturing the wholesale markup. Q2 suggests the opposite may be true in the current environment. Shipping costs, last-mile delivery, and the labor required to staff own retail stores are eating into the gross margin gains from full-price selling. The gross margin did expand 80 basis points to 52.5 percent, driven by healthy full-price sales, favorable channel mix, and a small tariff refund. But the operating expense line more than offset that gain. The net result is that the operating margin declined despite gross margin expansion, a pattern that cannot persist if the company is to reach the high-single-digit margin target it has set for FY26 inclusive of tariff refunds.

## The FY26 Guidance Implies a Sharp H2 Slowdown That the Market Has Not Fully Priced

The FY26 guidance update raised the top-line outlook from high-single-digit to 9-10 percent currency-neutral growth, reflecting the stronger-than-expected first half. But the implied H2 guidance tells a more sobering story. Based on the full-year midpoint of 9.5 percent currency-neutral growth and the actual H1 result of approximately 14 percent, the implied H2 growth rate is 4.2 to 6.2 percent, with a midpoint of 5.2 percent. That is 240 basis points below the consensus estimate of 7.6 percent and 390 basis points below the Bernstein estimate of 9.1 percent.

The implied H2 operating margin is even more concerning. Excluding tariff refunds, the implied H2 margin is 7.6 to 7.8 percent, with a midpoint of 7.7 percent. That is 80 basis points below consensus and 130 basis points below Bernstein's estimate. Even including the expected 250 to 300 million euros in tariff refunds, the implied H2 margin rises only to 9.4 to 9.5 percent, which is still below the 10.7 percent operating margin Adidas delivered in Q1 of this fiscal year.

The source of the H2 slowdown is not difficult to identify. Management explicitly cited macroeconomic challenges and a promotional marketplace, particularly in Europe. The European business grew only 6 percent currency-neutral in Q2, in line with Q1, and the company is deliberately limiting wholesale sell-in to avoid inventory buildup. In North America, growth accelerated to 17 percent currency-neutral from 12 percent in Q1, but that was driven in part by conservative prior-year comparisons and may not be repeatable if the consumer environment changes. Greater China grew 15 percent, decelerating from 17 percent in Q1, and Emerging Markets and Latin America, while strong, are smaller bases that cannot compensate for a slowdown in the core regions.

The strategic takeaway is that Adidas is guiding to a second-half deceleration that is sharper than the market expected, and the margin trajectory implies that operating leverage will remain elusive. The company is investing through the cycle, which may prove wise if the promotional environment eases in 2027. But for the remainder of this fiscal year, the margin story is one of compression, not expansion.

## The Core Tension: Adidas Is a Better Brand Than the Margin Profile Suggests, but Margins Are What Drive the Company

Adidas has successfully rebuilt its brand equity in Performance categories. The 39 percent growth in Performance, the double-digit growth in Football and Running, and the strong DTC performance all point to a brand that has regained relevance with athletes and consumers. The gross margin expansion to 52.5 percent, driven by full-price selling, confirms that the brand has pricing power. These are not the characteristics of a company in structural decline.

But the operating margin tells a different story. At 8.5 percent, Adidas is operating at a margin that is below its historical average and well below the 12 to 14 percent margins that the market had expected the company to achieve post-Yeezy. The gap between gross margin and operating margin is widening, not narrowing, because the cost of running the business is growing faster than the top line. Marketing spend, DTC costs, and labor expenses are consuming the gross margin gains. Until that equation changes, the company will struggle to re-rate.

The FY26 guidance, with its implied H2 margin of 7.6 to 7.8 percent excluding tariff refunds, suggests that management expects the equation to remain challenging for the remainder of the fiscal year. The question for 2027 is whether the investment in Performance and DTC pays off in the form of operating leverage, or whether the cost structure has permanently shifted higher. The answer will determine whether Adidas trades at 20 times earnings or 15 times earnings. The Q2 results do not provide a definitive answer, but they make clear that the margin question is the central strategic issue facing the company.

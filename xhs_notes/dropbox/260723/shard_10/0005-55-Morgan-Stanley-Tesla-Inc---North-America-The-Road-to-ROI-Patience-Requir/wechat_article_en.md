# Tesla’s Capex Supercycle Demands Tangible Proof Points Before the Market Rewards the Physical AI Thesis

Tesla disclosed 380,000 cumulative unsupervised robotaxi miles driven to date across Texas and Florida, with no notable incidents reported and weekly unsupervised miles growing at a double-digit percentage rate week-over-week. That is a genuine operational milestone. Yet the company also confirmed that capital expenditures will increase over the next two to three years, pushing free cash flow deeper into negative territory through the end of the decade. The tension between these two facts defines the current observation dilemma. The market is being asked to fund a multi-year, multi-billion-dollar capex cycle on the promise of returns that remain several years away, and the evidence required to sustain that patience must become more concrete, more frequent, and more transparent with each passing quarter.

The strategic thesis for Tesla has not changed. The company remains positioned to lead in Physical AI—the convergence of autonomous vehicles, humanoid robotics, and distributed energy storage. But the pathway to monetizing that leadership now runs through an extended period of negative free cash flow, rising R&D spending, and compressed margins in the core automotive and energy businesses. The question is no longer whether Tesla is building the right capabilities. It is whether the company can demonstrate sufficiently convincing milestones at a pace that keeps the market from discounting the long-dated optionality embedded in the company's valuation.

A global research report provides the analytical framework for assessing this question, while keeping long-term estimates broadly unchanged. This is a telling adjustment. The report is not reducing its ultimate conviction about the value of Tesla’s autonomous network, robotaxi fleet, energy business, or humanoid opportunity. It is marking down the near-term financial path to those outcomes. The implication is clear: the gap between current financial performance and future valuation must be bridged by evidence, not just narrative.

## A 55% FSD attach rate in North America confirms that consumers are buying Tesla vehicles for the software, not just the hardware

The most important data point from the quarter is not the robotaxi miles or the capex guidance. It is the 55% attach rate for Full Self-Driving capability on incremental vehicle deliveries in North America. The report notes that this figure far surpassed its prior estimate of 25% to 30%. That is not a marginal beat. It is a structural shift in how customers perceive the value proposition of a Tesla vehicle.

For years, the positive perspective on Tesla has rested partly on the idea that the company would eventually monetize its installed base through software subscriptions and network services. That thesis required consumers to actually pay for the software. The 55% attach rate suggests they are doing so at a level that was previously considered unrealistic. The report now projects global attach rates reaching 40% by 2030 and 80% by 2040, subject to international regulatory approval. These are material assumptions. They underpin the $144 per share that the report assigns to Network Services in its base case valuation.

The implication is that Tesla’s competitive moat is widening not just through technology advancement but through consumer behavior. Each vehicle sold with FSD capability represents a recurring revenue stream and a data-generation node that improves the system for every other vehicle. The attach rate data transforms the auto business from a unit-volume story into a platform-adoption story. But that transformation only matters if the software actually delivers on its promise of unsupervised autonomy. The 55% attach rate raises the stakes for execution. It means a large and growing base of customers is paying for a feature that has not yet fully materialized.

## Robotaxi progress is real but must shift from geographic expansion to density and safety data within existing cities

The 380,000 unsupervised miles driven and the double-digit weekly growth rate are encouraging. The report also notes that the robotaxi fleet is already operating early versions of v15 FSD software, suggesting that the technology is iterating rapidly. But the critical metric is not total miles. It is miles per vehicle per day within a given city, combined with a statistically significant safety record that can withstand regulatory scrutiny.

The report explicitly flags this distinction. It states that what is needed is density and a larger sample of safety data within existing cities, not just geographic expansion. This is a crucial nuance. Expanding the robotaxi service to more cities demonstrates operational capability, but it does not prove that the system is safe enough to operate at scale without human supervision. Safety validation requires repetition in the same environment under varying conditions. It requires millions of miles in a single city with a near-zero incident rate. That is the evidence that regulators and the market will ultimately require before the unsupervised service can be commercialized at scale.

The report’s base case values Tesla Mobility at $120 per share, assuming roughly 5 million cars in the robotaxi fleet by 2040 at an average revenue of $1.33 per mile. Those are enormous numbers. They imply that Tesla will not only solve the technical problem of full autonomy but also navigate the regulatory, operational, and competitive challenges of deploying a global robotaxi network. The unsupervised miles data is a necessary first step, but it is not yet sufficient to validate the valuation. Observers should track the miles-per-vehicle metric within the existing operating cities, not just the headline growth rate.

## The capex step-up is a deliberate strategic choice, but it extends the cash burn period and narrows the market’s tolerance for delays

Tesla’s management reiterated a 2026 capex guide of more than $25 billion and now expects capex to increase over the next two to three years as the company expands the robotaxi fleet, Optimus capacity, the semiconductor fab, solar manufacturing, and AI compute. The report notes that this represents a step-up versus its prior assumption of a moderating capex profile. The company has also secured debt facilities that provide capacity to borrow up to $30 billion to accelerate these investments.

The report’s revised estimates reflect the financial impact of this decision. It now assumes nearly $30 billion in capex in 2027, up from $20 billion previously. Combined with lower EBITDA estimates, this drives a free cash flow burn of approximately $14 billion in 2027, compared to $5 billion in the prior estimate. The 2026 and 2027 adjusted EBITDA estimates decline by 7% and 12%, respectively. Operating income margins fall to 1.9% in 2026 and 2.4% in 2027.

These numbers matter because they define the timeline over which the market must remain patient. The report explicitly states that absent consistent, transparent proof points, it would expect the market’s tolerance for incremental capex to narrow. That is the critical consideration. The capex cycle is not a one-year phenomenon. It extends through the end of the decade on the report’s estimates. Every quarter that passes without a clear demonstration of ROI increases the probability that the market will reprice the company to reflect the cash burn rather than the future optionality.

The bull case of $821 implies more than double that. The bear case of $130 implies a 65% decline. This wide dispersion reflects the genuine uncertainty about outcomes. The capex decision is correct strategically, but it creates a period of financial vulnerability that must be managed through execution.

## Optimus is the highest-upside, lowest-visibility component of the thesis and requires supply chain proof points, not just technical demonstrations

The humanoid robot opportunity is the most speculative element of the Tesla story. The report assigns $56 per share to humanoids in its base case, applying a 50% probability discount. In the bull case, the value rises to $199 per share with a 0% probability discount. The range reflects the enormous potential and the enormous uncertainty.

Management confirmed that start of production for Optimus is expected soon but provided limited new commentary on production readiness. The report notes that the complexity of the supply chain is likely to weigh on the pace of the initial ramp. Musk reiterated the challenge of building new supply chains that do not yet exist, designing a robot with human and then superhuman dexterity, and developing the technology that enables a humanoid to perform generalized tasks rather than pre-programmed or remote-controlled actions.

These are not minor engineering challenges. They are foundational problems that have not been solved by any company in the world. The report maintains conservative adoption estimates through the end of the decade, and the valuation reflects that conservatism. However, the bull case assumes that Tesla solves these problems and achieves widespread deployment. For observers to move toward that bull case, the evidence must shift from technical demonstrations to supply chain contracts, manufacturing timelines, and customer commitments. Until that happens, Optimus will remain a high-upside, low-visibility option that the market will discount heavily.

## A decision framework for evaluating Tesla through the capex supercycle

The report provides the raw material for a structured framework that can be used to assess progress over the next two to three years. This framework is organized around four milestones, each of which must be met with increasing precision and transparency.

First, robotaxi safety density. The critical metric is not total unsupervised miles but miles per vehicle per day in existing cities, combined with a statistically significant incident rate that can be benchmarked against human drivers. Observers should expect management to disclose these metrics on a quarterly basis. If the company provides only aggregate mileage growth without per-vehicle density data, that is a point to note.

Second, FSD attach rate sustainability. The 55% North American attach rate is a positive development, but it must be maintained or increased as the price of the software changes and as competition from other autonomous driving systems intensifies. Observers should track attach rates by region and by vehicle model, and they should expect management to provide guidance on how attach rates will evolve as unsupervised FSD is rolled out to passenger vehicles.

Third, capex efficiency. The report assumes nearly $30 billion in capex in 2027. Observers should expect management to demonstrate that this spending is translating into tangible assets: robotaxi vehicles deployed, Optimus units in production, Megapack factories operating at capacity. If capex rises without corresponding asset growth, the efficiency of the spending must be questioned.

Fourth, energy margin stabilization. The report lowered its 2030-plus energy gross margin assumption by 150 basis points to 23.5%, reflecting growing competition and lower average selling prices. The energy business is a critical cash flow contributor during the capex supercycle. If margins continue to compress, the financial pressure on the overall enterprise increases.

Each of these four milestones can be tracked with publicly available data and management commentary. The report’s analysis suggests that the market will reprice the company based on progress against these metrics, not on the narrative alone.

## The energy business faces structural margin compression even as demand grows, creating a headwind to cash flow during the investment phase

Tesla’s energy storage business has been a bright spot in recent years, with robust growth driven by data center buildout and broader electrification. However, the quarter revealed pressure on margins. Normalizing for one-time items, energy gross margin fell approximately 300 basis points quarter-over-quarter to 28% due to lower average selling prices amid growing competition. The report now expects long-term gross margins to settle in the low-to-mid 20% range, a meaningful reduction from prior expectations.

The report’s revised estimates reflect this pressure. It assumes energy gross margins of 27.2% in 2026 and a decline over time to 23.5% by 2030 and beyond. This is significant because the energy business was expected to provide a cushion of profitability while the automotive and robotics investments consumed cash. If energy margins are structurally lower than previously assumed, the overall cash flow profile of the company becomes more challenging.

The bright spot is the “megapod” concept that management detailed—a shippable enclosure pairing Tesla AI4 and x86 compute that can be placed anywhere power is available, including at Tesla’s Supercharger network. This concept effectively pairs distributed power with distributed inference and could add a further demand vector for Megapack over time. But this is an early-stage idea, not a near-term revenue driver. The immediate reality is that the energy business is becoming more competitive, and that competition is compressing margins.

## The valuation rests on long-dated cash flows that require extraordinary execution across multiple businesses simultaneously

The report’s sum-of-the-parts valuation provides a clear picture of what the company's valuation is discounting. In the base case, core auto contributes $45 per share, Network Services contributes $144, Tesla Mobility contributes $120, Energy contributes $35, and Humanoids contributes $56. The total is $400.

Note that the core auto business—selling vehicles—accounts for only about 11% of the base case valuation. The remaining 89% comes from businesses that either do not yet exist at scale or are in their earliest stages of commercialization. Network Services requires 80% FSD attach rate at $240 per month average revenue per user by 2040. Tesla Mobility requires 5 million robotaxi vehicles generating $1.33 per mile. Humanoids requires successful commercialization of a product that has not yet entered production.

These are not conservative assumptions. They are ambitious targets that depend on Tesla executing flawlessly across technology, regulation, manufacturing, and market adoption for the next 15 years. The report acknowledges this by applying a 10.9% weighted average cost of capital and a 10x exit EBITDA multiple to the auto business, which are reasonable discounting mechanisms. But the fundamental structure of the valuation means that small delays or challenges in any one of these businesses have outsized effects on the total.

The bear case, which values Tesla at $130, assumes lower attach rates, a smaller robotaxi fleet, no value for humanoids, and compressed auto margins. That scenario is not implausible. It simply assumes that the execution challenges prove more difficult than expected. The wide gap between the bull and bear cases is not a flaw in the analysis. It is an honest reflection of the uncertainty inherent in a company that is attempting to simultaneously engage with three industries.

# The Oil Market’s Quiet Adjustment Is Masking a Dangerous Regional Divide

The largest supply disruption on record has produced a price chart that looks almost boring. Since the start of the conflict, Brent crude has averaged just $101 per barrel, tracking nearly exactly the path that our analytical framework projected back in early March. At first glance, this suggests that the market has absorbed the shock with remarkable efficiency. A closer look reveals something far more unsettling: the adjustment has not failed, but it has shifted from crude prices to product markets, and from global averages to regional bottlenecks. The real story is not that oil prices have stayed calm. It is that the calm is unevenly distributed, and the most vulnerable points in the system are now carrying a disproportionate share of the burden.

This matters now because the conventional wisdom is settling around a narrative of resilience. Investors, policymakers, and corporate strategists are beginning to treat the current price level as a new equilibrium. That assumption is dangerous. The data show that the market has absorbed the disruption through two mechanisms that are inherently temporary: demand destruction and inventory depletion. Neither can continue indefinitely. And the evidence is already mounting that the next phase of adjustment will be expressed not in Brent futures, but in regional fuel markets that are structurally unprepared for the strain.

The most striking finding from the latest research is that our initial framework was half right and half wrong. We correctly anticipated that crude prices would not spike to extreme levels. But we expected the adjustment to show up in widening product cracks and sharply higher prices for gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel. Instead, after an initial spike, those prices have reset lower, and refining margins have narrowed. The market has absorbed the disruption through a combination of demand losses in the conflict zone, reduced consumption in parts of Asia, faster-than-expected fuel substitution in China, traditional demand destruction in Africa, and massive draws on both crude and refined product inventories. This is not a market that has found a new balance. It is a market that has borrowed stability from the future.

For US consumers, the relief at the pump is real but fragile. The national average for regular gasoline sits at roughly $4.16 per gallon, down more than 10% from the late-May peak. But that national average is a dangerous abstraction. It obscures a deepening regional divide that has profound implications for supply chains, corporate costs, and the broader economy.

## The National Average Hides a West Coast Crisis That Is Structurally Worsening

The most important insight from the data is that the US fuel market is not one market. It is at least three, and the West Coast is operating under fundamentally different conditions than the rest of the country. In California, drivers are paying an average of $5.89 per gallon for gasoline. Truckers are paying $7.16 per gallon for diesel. These are not temporary spikes. They are the product of structural forces that are intensifying.

California faces a unique convergence of constraints. The state’s regulatory environment, including high taxes and stringent environmental mandates, has long made it an outlier. But the current crisis is being driven by supply-side factors that are compounding rapidly. The closure of two major refineries—Valero’s Benicia facility and Phillips 66’s Los Angeles refinery—has removed 17% of the state’s refining capacity. These closures are not cyclical. They reflect a long-term trend of declining local production and increasing import dependence. California was once a major producer and exporter of oil and refined products. Today, it relies on imports for a significant share of its gasoline supply.

The problem is that California cannot easily draw on supplies from neighboring states. Unlike the East Coast, which can receive shipments from the Gulf Coast and Europe, California depends heavily on CARB-compliant gasoline from Asian refiners. These imports account for nearly two-thirds of the state’s gasoline imports and roughly 20% to 30% of total gasoline supply. Since the disruption to crude flows from the Strait of Hormuz began, many Asian refiners have been forced to reduce refined product exports because their feedstock availability has been constrained. The state’s unique fuel specifications and the absence of major product pipelines linking it to the rest of the country mean that there is no easy substitute.

The implications extend far beyond California’s borders. Roughly one in five of all goods entering the United States arrives through West Coast gateways such as the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. Before those imports reach warehouses and distribution centers across the country, they travel their first several hundred miles powered by West Coast diesel and gasoline. This means that a meaningful share of America’s supply chain pays West Coast fuel prices. The cost of moving goods from the ports to inland markets is being set in the most expensive fuel market in the country. That cost does not disappear. It is embedded in the delivered price of everything from electronics to furniture to industrial components.

## Diesel Is the Canary in the Global Supply Chain Coal Mine

If gasoline illustrates regional tightness, diesel tells a story of global scarcity that is accelerating. The US Gulf Coast has become the marginal supplier of diesel to the world, and the strain is showing in the data. US diesel exports averaged 1.2 million barrels per day in January and February, nearly 150,000 barrels per day higher than the same period last year, despite the closure of three US refineries since the start of 2025. Since the conflict began, exports have risen further to 1.5 million barrels per day.

The demand is coming from two directions. Brazil has seen a decline in Russian diesel shipments, creating a gap that US exporters are filling. Europe, where restrictions on products refined from Russian crude came into effect in January, is also drawing on US supply. The result is that US diesel inventories have been drawn down to a 23-year low, even as refinery utilization remains strong. The stock is being drained from both ends: domestic demand and international obligations.

The strategic implication is clear. Diesel is the fuel that powers the global economy. It moves trucks, trains, ships, construction equipment, and agricultural machinery. A sustained tightening in diesel markets does not just raise costs for individual sectors. It raises the baseline cost of economic activity across the board. The current drawdown is not a seasonal fluctuation. It is a structural shift in which the US is being forced to supply a global market that has lost access to Russian barrels and is struggling to replace them.

The question that the research does not fully answer is how long this can continue. US refineries are running at high utilization rates. There is limited spare capacity to increase diesel output. If global demand continues to grow, and if the disruption to Hormuz persists, the only mechanism left to balance the market will be higher prices. That would mean diesel prices rising not just on the West Coast, but nationally and globally. The current pattern of inventory draws is a temporary buffer. Once that buffer is exhausted, the adjustment will be sudden and painful.

## Jet Fuel Is the Wild Card That Could Tighten Everything Else

Jet fuel sits in an ambiguous position between the regional story of gasoline and the global story of diesel. US jet fuel inventories have remained relatively resilient, supported by record refinery yields that have kept pace with demand. Seasonal demand has continued to strengthen with the onset of summer travel and the approach of the World Cup, even as several airlines have already reduced flight schedules through the third quarter.

The resilience of jet fuel inventories is a positive sign, but it also creates a risk. Refiners have been prioritizing jet fuel production, which has constrained gasoline output. If jet fuel demand remains strong, refiners will continue to allocate capacity away from gasoline and diesel. This is a rational response to relative prices, but it means that the tightness in other product markets is being exacerbated by the strength of jet fuel demand.

The open question is whether jet fuel demand will soften as airlines reduce schedules, or whether the World Cup and summer travel will keep it elevated. If demand remains strong, the pressure on gasoline and diesel markets will intensify. If demand softens, refiners can shift production back, easing the pressure on other products. The data do not yet provide a clear signal on which direction the balance will tip.

This uncertainty is not a minor detail. It is a critical variable for anyone trying to forecast fuel prices over the next six months. The jet fuel market is the swing factor that could determine whether the current tightness in gasoline and diesel becomes a full-blown crisis or fades into a manageable disruption.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Next Phase of the Oil Market

The research provides the raw material for a decision framework, but it does not assemble the framework itself. That is the task for the reader. Based on the data and analysis, the following three questions should guide strategic thinking about the oil market over the next six to twelve months.

First, how much of the current adjustment is structural versus cyclical? The demand destruction and inventory draws that have kept crude prices stable are inherently temporary. They cannot continue indefinitely. The question is whether the structural forces that caused the disruption will resolve before the temporary buffers are exhausted. If the Strait of Hormuz reopens soon, the market will have a chance to rebuild inventories and restore balance. If the disruption persists, the adjustment will eventually have to come through higher prices, and the current calm will be remembered as the lull before the storm.

Second, which product markets are most vulnerable to a sudden tightening? The data suggest that diesel is the most exposed, given the combination of low inventories, strong export demand, and limited spare refining capacity. But the answer depends on regional exposure. For companies operating on the West Coast, gasoline is the immediate concern. For companies with global supply chains, diesel is the bigger risk. For airlines, jet fuel is the variable to watch. The framework must be tailored to the specific vulnerabilities of each business.

Third, what is the contingency plan if the current adjustment mechanism fails? The market has so far avoided a sharp price spike by drawing down inventories and reducing demand. If those mechanisms prove insufficient, the adjustment will be disorderly. Companies that have not stress-tested their supply chains for a scenario in which diesel prices rise by 30% or 40% are exposed. Companies that have not considered what happens if California fuel prices remain structurally elevated are exposed. The time to plan is now, while the market is still calm.

## What the Report Does Not Answer: The Duration of the Disruption and the Limits of Inventory Draws

The research is rigorous and detailed, but it leaves two critical questions unresolved. The first is the duration of the Strait of Hormuz disruption. The report provides scenarios for reopening at different dates, but it does not assign probabilities to those scenarios. This is not a criticism. It is a recognition that the outcome depends on geopolitical factors that are inherently unpredictable. But for decision-makers, the lack of a probability distribution makes it difficult to calibrate the level of precaution that is warranted.

The second unresolved question is the limit of inventory draws. The report shows that inventories have been drawn down significantly, but it does not specify how much further they can fall before the market breaks. At some point, inventories reach minimum operating levels, below which the system cannot function. The research does not identify where that threshold lies for each product market. This is a critical gap. Without knowing the floor, it is impossible to know how much buffer remains.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are invitations for deeper investigation. The full report contains additional data on inventory levels, refinery utilization, and demand projections that can help answer these questions. The charts and tables that accompany the research provide a richer picture than any summary can capture.

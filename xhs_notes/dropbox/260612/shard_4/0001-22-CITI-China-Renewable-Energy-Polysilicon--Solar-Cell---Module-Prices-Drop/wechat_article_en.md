# China Solar Is Not a Recovery Play. It Is a Two-Track Market Where Export Winners Will Separate From Domestic Laggards.

The prevailing narrative around China's solar sector has been one of unrelenting supply-side pressure. Prices for n-type polysilicon, cells, and modules all declined again last week, with TOPCon cells dropping 4.6% week-over-week to RMB 0.31 per watt. Polysilicon inventory sits at 295,000 tons, and solar glass inventory days stretch to 53.4 days. These numbers tell a familiar story of overcapacity and margin compression. But a closer look at the data from the recent SNEC conference in Shanghai reveals a more nuanced and strategically important picture. The real story is not that the industry is in trouble. It is that the industry is bifurcating along geographic lines. Domestic demand remains tepid, but overseas sales are surging, driven by large framework agreements with buyers from the Middle East, Africa, and Southeast Asia. This is not a cyclical recovery. It is a structural shift in market access and competitive positioning.

For investors, this means the old playbook of betting on broad-based solar exposure is no longer sufficient. The winners will be those companies that have secured access to non-Chinese demand pools, particularly in regions where energy transition investment is accelerating and where Chinese manufacturers face fewer tariff barriers. The losers will remain trapped in the domestic price war, where prices hover near variable production costs and inventory continues to accumulate. The question is not whether solar demand exists. It does. The question is which companies can reach that demand profitably.

The report from a global investment bank makes this distinction clear. The authors are selective on the China solar sector due to excessive supply, but they maintain Buy ratings on Sungrow Power Supply and Ningbo Deye Technology. Both companies have significant exposure to overseas energy storage and inverter markets. This is not a coincidence. The thesis is that the downstream, export-oriented players in the value chain will outperform the upstream, domestic-focused producers of polysilicon, wafers, and cells. The data supports this view. The week-over-week price declines are most severe in cells and polysilicon, the segments where Chinese domestic competition is fiercest. Meanwhile, the framework agreements signed at SNEC are overwhelmingly for modules, not for upstream components.

This article will unpack the implications of this bifurcation, examine why government intervention is unlikely to change the trajectory, and provide a decision framework for investors navigating this complex landscape.

## The SNEC Conference Revealed a Clear Divide: Export Demand Is Structurally Different from Domestic Demand

The International Photovoltaic Power Generation and Smart Energy Conference & Exhibition, known as SNEC, closed in Shanghai on June 5, 2026. It is the industry's largest annual gathering, and this year's event provided a critical signal about where demand is actually growing. The on-site signings were dominated by gigawatt-scale framework memorandums of understanding for modules. Longi, Jinko, Trina Solar, and JA Solar all signed new supply agreements with overseas buyers primarily from the Middle East, Africa, and Southeast Asia. A small number of integrated PV-storage strategic cooperation deals were also announced.

What was notably absent was significant contracting for polysilicon and silicon wafers. According to the report, very few contracts for these upstream products were signed on site. Transactions for these components are negotiated mostly off-site through annual long-term agreements. This is a telling detail. It suggests that the spot market for upstream solar materials remains fragmented and price-sensitive, while the module market is seeing genuine demand pull from international buyers who are willing to commit to large-scale, multi-year supply relationships.

The implication is clear. The export channel is not just a safety valve for excess domestic production. It is becoming the primary growth engine for the industry. The buyers from the Middle East and Africa are not bargain-hunting for the cheapest available panels. They are signing framework agreements that signal long-term commitment to solar deployment. This is a fundamentally different demand profile from the domestic Chinese market, where price competition is the dominant dynamic.

For investors, the key question is which companies have the commercial relationships, the logistical infrastructure, and the product specifications to serve these export markets effectively. The companies that signed the framework agreements at SNEC are the usual suspects: the large integrated module manufacturers. But the report also highlights that Sungrow and Deye, which are inverter and energy storage companies, are rated as Buys. This suggests that the export opportunity extends beyond modules to the balance-of-system components that are essential for solar projects in emerging markets.

## Government Anti-Involution Measures Will Be Softer in 2026, Removing a Potential Catalyst for Domestic Price Recovery

One of the most frequently discussed topics in the China solar sector is the possibility of government intervention to curb what is often called "involution" — the phenomenon of excessive competition that drives prices below sustainable levels. In 2025, the Chinese government took steps to address overcapacity in several industrial sectors, including solar. The expectation among some market participants was that 2026 would see a continuation or even an escalation of these measures.

The report challenges this assumption directly. It states that anti-involution measures from the government in 2026 would likely be softer than those in 2025. The reasoning is straightforward. China's Producer Price Index (PPI) and Consumer Price Index (CPI) have rebounded partly as a result of higher energy prices driven by the Middle East conflict. When inflation is no longer a deflationary concern, the government has less urgency to intervene in specific industrial sectors to prop up prices.

This is a critical insight for investors. If the government is not going to step in to enforce production discipline, then the domestic market will remain in a state of oversupply for longer. The price floor will be determined by variable production costs, not by regulatory intervention. As the data shows, polysilicon prices are already not far from variable production costs. This means that further downside is limited, but it also means that a meaningful recovery in domestic prices is unlikely without a significant demand pickup or capacity closure.

The strategic implication is that investors should not wait for a government-driven catalyst to improve the domestic pricing environment. The path to profitability for solar companies lies in export markets, not in a domestic rebound. This reinforces the two-track thesis. Companies that can generate revenue and margin from overseas sales will have a buffer against the domestic price depression. Companies that are purely domestic will continue to struggle.

## The Upstream Value Chain Is Trapped in a Structural Glut, While Downstream and Storage Segments Offer Pricing Power

The weekly price data in the report tells a story of divergence within the supply chain. N-type polysilicon prices fell 1.3% week-over-week to RMB 33.3 per kilogram. Granular polysilicon was flat at RMB 33.5 per kilogram. Polysilicon inventory remains high at 295,000 tons, down only 1% week-over-week. Wafer prices were unchanged, but only because the market is already at a low base. There were some price reductions from small manufacturers, indicating that the pressure is still there.

The most dramatic decline was in solar cells. TOPCon cell prices fell 4.6% week-over-week to RMB 0.31 per watt. This is a significant drop in a single week. Module prices declined only 0.3%, which suggests that module manufacturers are absorbing some of the cost declines from cells rather than passing them through to customers. Solar glass prices were flat but remain at low levels, with an industry-wide average inventory period of 53.4 days.

The pattern is clear. The upstream segments — polysilicon, wafers, and cells — are where the capacity oversupply is most acute. These are capital-intensive, commoditized products where Chinese manufacturers have invested heavily. The downstream segments — modules and especially inverters and energy storage — have more differentiation and pricing power. Sungrow and Deye are rated as Buys precisely because they operate in these downstream segments and have strong export exposure.

The report also notes that two space PV industry alliances have been officially launched, led by GCL and JA Solar respectively. Leading manufacturers are developing lightweight tandem cells for satellites. This is a niche but telling development. It shows that the industry is searching for new application markets to absorb capacity. Space solar is not going to move the needle on a gigawatt scale, but it illustrates the mindset of manufacturers who are desperate for any demand channel that is not the saturated domestic market.

For investors, the message is to avoid the upstream commoditized segments and focus on downstream companies that have technology differentiation, brand recognition, and international sales channels. The price data supports this allocation.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Open Question: How Will Trade Tariffs and Geopolitical Risks Reshape Export Access?

The report is bullish on export demand, but it does not fully address the sustainability of this channel. The framework agreements signed at SNEC are encouraging, but they are memorandums of understanding, not firm orders. The buyers are from the Middle East, Africa, and Southeast Asia, which are generally considered friendly markets for Chinese solar products. However, the global trade environment is shifting rapidly.

The report lists trade tariffs as a key risk for both Sungrow and Deye. For Sungrow, the risks include intensification of overseas trade tensions that could lessen exports. For Deye, the risks include higher-than-expected trade tariffs against Chinese inverter products in overseas markets. These are not hypothetical concerns. The United States and the European Union have both imposed tariffs on Chinese solar products in recent years. India has implemented its own protectionist measures. Even in emerging markets, there is growing interest in local manufacturing and import substitution.

The question that the report does not fully answer is whether the current wave of export demand is sustainable in the face of potential trade barriers. The Middle East and Africa are currently less protectionist than Western markets, but that could change. If Chinese manufacturers flood these markets with low-priced modules, local governments may respond with tariffs or local content requirements. The framework agreements signed at SNEC may be a leading indicator of demand, but they could also become a target for trade retaliation.

Investors need to monitor this risk closely. The companies that are best positioned are those that have established local manufacturing or assembly operations in target markets, or those that have joint ventures with local partners. The report does not provide this level of detail, but it is a critical variable for anyone making long-term investment decisions in the sector.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Which Solar Companies Will Survive and Thrive

Given the two-track nature of the China solar market, investors need a systematic way to evaluate companies. The following decision framework is derived from the report's logic and can be applied to any company in the sector.

First, what is the company's revenue exposure to export markets versus domestic China? The report suggests that export demand is more encouraging than domestic sales. Companies with 50% or more of revenue from overseas are likely to have better pricing power and growth prospects. Companies that are heavily dependent on the Chinese domestic market will face continued margin pressure.

Second, where in the value chain does the company operate? Upstream companies in polysilicon, wafers, and cells are facing the most severe price declines and inventory accumulation. Downstream companies in modules, inverters, and energy storage have more differentiation and are better positioned to capture export demand. The report's Buy ratings on Sungrow and Deye, both inverter and storage companies, support this distinction.

Third, does the company have a defensible competitive advantage in its target export markets? This could be in the form of technology leadership, brand recognition, local manufacturing capacity, or long-term customer relationships. The framework agreements signed at SNEC were with major module brands like Longi, Jinko, Trina, and JA Solar. These companies have established distribution networks and customer trust in overseas markets. Smaller players may not have the same access.

Applying this framework, the report's selective stance makes sense. The analysts are not bullish on the sector as a whole. They are bullish on specific companies that meet these criteria. For the rest of the sector, the outlook remains challenging.

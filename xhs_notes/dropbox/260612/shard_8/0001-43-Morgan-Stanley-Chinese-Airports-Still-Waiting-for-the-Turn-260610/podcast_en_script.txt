EN_A: So we just got our hands on a new research report on Chinese airports from a major global investment bank. The title says it all: “Still Waiting for the Turn.” That does not sound very optimistic.
EN_B: It is not. The report is pretty cautious across the board. They downgraded Guangzhou Baiyun Airport from equal-weight to underweight, and kept underweight on Beijing Capital Airport. Only Shanghai Airport stays at equal-weight.
EN_A: Why the downgrade? What changed?
EN_B: Three things. First, higher fuel costs are pushing up airfares, and passengers are switching to trains. Second, duty-free revenue is in a messy transition period. And third, for Baiyun, huge new capacity just came online, but the macro environment is soft, so earnings recovery might take longer than expected.
EN_A: Let’s start with the energy shock. How bad is the traffic impact?
EN_B: Pretty visible. In April, domestic air passenger growth slowed to just 3 to 4 percent year on year, compared to 6 to 10 percent in the first quarter. Over the Labor Day holiday, air traffic actually dropped nearly 6 percent, while railway traffic was still growing.
EN_A: So people are voting with their wallets and choosing trains over planes.
EN_B: Exactly. The report points out that railway passenger growth accelerated to over 10 percent in April. And non-domestic traffic is also softening. So this is not just a blip. It could persist as long as fuel prices stay elevated.
EN_A: What about the duty-free business? I remember the new contracts were supposed to be a big positive.
EN_B: They are structurally positive. The new contracts break the monopoly, introduce multiple operators, and have a more stable rent structure. But in the near term, there is a painful transition. Shanghai Airport gave new operators a three-month rent-free period, so duty-free rent dropped 49 percent year on year in the first quarter.
EN_A: 49 percent drop? That is huge. But the report says per-passenger spending may have bottomed. What does that mean?
EN_B: It means the worst is likely behind us. But a meaningful recovery will take time. New operators need to build inventory, set up supply chains, and introduce new product categories. So medium-term outlook is better, but near-term is still a drag.
EN_B: And there is another uncertainty. For Baiyun Airport, the cost-sharing agreement with its parent company for the new terminal is not finalized yet. The current arrangement involves an asset-use fee of about 900 million yuan per year. But the final deal could be more costly for the listed company.
EN_A: So costs are uncertain, revenue is under pressure. That explains why they cut earnings estimates.
EN_B: Yes, they cut earnings for all three airports and lowered price targets by about 40 percent on average. For Baiyun, their estimates are 1 to 20 percent below consensus through 2028.
EN_A: And what about the other two? Shanghai and Beijing Capital?
EN_B: Beijing Capital remains underweight. Without new flight slots, earnings pressure continues. And its exclusion from Stock Connect is a valuation overhang. Shanghai is neutral because duty-free revenue should normalize from the second quarter.
EN_A: So the whole sector is stuck waiting. But are there any open questions the report doesn’t fully answer?
EN_B: Two big ones. First, what exactly will Baiyun’s final cost-sharing deal look like? That could swing earnings significantly. Second, can the new duty-free operators actually lift per-passenger spending over time? The full report has more detailed scenario analysis on both.
EN_A: Sounds like the summary only tells half the story.
EN_B: Exactly. We covered the main logic here, but if you want the full valuation models, risk-reward analysis, and those deeper questions, you should definitely read the complete report.

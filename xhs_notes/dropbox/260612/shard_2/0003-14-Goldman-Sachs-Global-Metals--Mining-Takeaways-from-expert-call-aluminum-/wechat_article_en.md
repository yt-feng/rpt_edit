# Chinese Aluminum Producers Are Reshaping Global Supply Faster Than Markets Expect

The global aluminum market is undergoing a structural shift that most investors have not fully priced in. Strong profitability is now driving Chinese producers to accelerate capacity expansion outside of China at a pace that exceeds earlier forecasts, particularly in Indonesia and Central Asia. This is not a marginal adjustment. Based on recent expert assessments, the combined capacity additions from Indonesia and Central Asia alone could reach nearly 6.0 million tonnes over 2026 to 2028, with larger targets already planned beyond that horizon. The implications for global aluminum prices, trade flows, and competitive dynamics are profound.

For years, the narrative around aluminum supply was dominated by China's capacity cap of 45 million tonnes and the constraints on ex-China expansion due to power shortages and regulatory hurdles. That narrative is now outdated. The evidence from recent on-the-ground visits to Indonesian projects suggests that earlier power supply bottlenecks are fading faster than expected. Meanwhile, Chinese producers are not waiting for domestic capacity to be fully utilized before building abroad. They are pursuing a dual strategy: maintaining elevated output at home while aggressively building new capacity overseas.

The market should therefore prepare for a world where global aluminum supply grows meaningfully faster than demand in the medium term, unless demand surprises significantly to the upside. The strategic question for investors is not whether this expansion will happen, but which producers, regions, and downstream segments will be most affected by the resulting surplus.

## The Fading Power Bottleneck in Indonesia Unlocks a Wave of New Capacity

The single most important development in the global aluminum supply outlook is the easing of power supply constraints in Indonesia. Earlier this year, the consensus view was that Indonesia's ambitious aluminum expansion plans would be delayed by limited electricity generation capacity and competition from the nickel smelting industry. That view has been upended by recent developments.

According to the expert assessment, two factors have combined to unlock significant power availability for aluminum smelters. First, the suspension of some nickel smelting operations within the Tsingshan industrial park has freed up power capacity that is now being reallocated to aluminum production. This is a tactical rebalancing within the same industrial ecosystem. Second, the construction of new power plants, including 710MW supercritical thermal units, has proceeded more smoothly than anticipated, with fewer constraints on the export of power generator units than previously feared.

The result is a dramatic upward revision to Indonesia's capacity outlook. The expert now estimates that 2.150 million tonnes of capacity will come online in 2026 and another 1.975 million tonnes in 2027. This is 1.6 million tonnes higher than the forecast made just months earlier, with 70 percent of that revision driven by the power reallocation from nickel operations. The ramp-up is expected to accelerate from the fourth quarter of 2026.

What makes this development strategically important is not just the volume but the cost profile. The unit production cost of primary aluminum in Indonesia is estimated to be similar to China, at around RMB 15,000 to 16,000 per tonne. The power tariff in the Tsingshan industrial park is higher than the Chinese average, at approximately RMB 0.46 per kWh compared to RMB 0.41 per kWh in China. Yet the overall cost competitiveness remains intact due to other factors, including proximity to bauxite resources and favorable regulatory treatment.

The implication is clear: Indonesian aluminum will be directly competitive with Chinese output, and it will be produced by Chinese companies. This is not a case of low-cost producers displacing high-cost ones. It is a case of the same producers expanding their low-cost footprint into a new geography, thereby increasing their aggregate market share and reducing their exposure to any single regulatory regime.

## Central Asia Emerges as the Next Frontier for Chinese-Led Expansion

While Indonesia has captured most of the attention, Central Asia is quietly becoming the second pillar of ex-China aluminum capacity growth. Kazakhstan, in particular, is now the site of two massive projects led by Chinese producers, with total planned capacity reaching 5.4 million tonnes.

Project G, with a total planned capacity of 2.4 million tonnes across three phases of 800,000 tonnes each, has already completed site selection and commenced piling work. The first phase is expected to start operation in 2028. Project H is even larger, with a total planned capacity of 3 million tonnes across three phases of 1 million tonnes each, and its first phase is also estimated to start operation in 2028.

Together, the first phases of these two projects alone represent 1.8 million tonnes of capacity coming online by 2028, equivalent to approximately 2.4 percent of the global market. This is a material addition, especially when combined with the Indonesian ramp-up.

The strategic rationale for Central Asia is different from that for Indonesia. Kazakhstan offers access to relatively low-cost energy, proximity to Chinese supply chains, and a stable regulatory environment that is supportive of foreign investment in resource processing. For Chinese producers, diversifying into Central Asia also provides a hedge against any future tightening of export restrictions or trade barriers that could affect shipments from Southeast Asia.

The emergence of Central Asia as a production hub raises an important question: how much more capacity is in the pipeline beyond what has been publicly disclosed? The expert noted that larger targets are planned beyond 2028, suggesting that the current wave of expansion is only the beginning. If Chinese producers continue to replicate their domestic model in new geographies, the global aluminum supply curve could shift downward more permanently than most models currently assume.

## China's Domestic Output Exceeds the Cap, and Environmental Inspections Are Not a Solution

The conventional wisdom that China's aluminum output is strictly capped at 45 million tonnes has been eroding for some time, but the latest data makes the breach undeniable. The expert estimates that China's primary aluminum output will reach 45.5 million tonnes in 2026, one million tonnes higher than the 2025 level. More importantly, the annualized run rate in April 2026 reached 47.1 million tonnes, which is 1.8 million tonnes higher than the average output of 45.3 million tonnes in 2025.

This overproduction is not the result of new capacity approvals. It is driven by unauthorized practices: illegal capacity additions, continued operation of old capacity that should have been suspended under the capacity swap program, and intensification of current potline operations in a way that risks damaging electrolytic cells. The expert estimates that these unauthorized activities account for 1.0 to 1.2 million tonnes of output.

The recent environmental inspections in China have been interpreted by some market participants as a signal that authorities will crack down on this overproduction. The expert's view is different. These inspections are described as routinely arranged and not specifically targeted at the aluminum industry. This suggests that the market should not expect a sudden enforcement-driven reduction in output.

The implication is that China's domestic production will remain elevated for as long as profitability supports it. The incremental output is coming from three main sources: the restart of idled capacity, the ramp-up of Zhalv phase 2, and Tianshan Aluminum. None of these are likely to be reversed quickly.

This creates a complex dynamic. Chinese producers are simultaneously running their domestic operations at above-cap levels and building large new capacity abroad. The combined effect is a global supply expansion that is larger and faster than the sum of its parts would suggest, because the same companies are driving both trends.

## Export Momentum Is Already Reflecting the Coming Supply Wave

The supply expansion story is not just about future capacity. It is already visible in current trade flows. The expert noted a strong orderbook momentum for Chinese aluminum exports since April and May, particularly in unwrought aluminum, aluminum alloys, aluminum sheet and strip, and aluminum sections. This change is expected to lead to double-digit growth in Chinese aluminum exports.

The mechanism is straightforward. As domestic production runs high and domestic demand absorbs only part of the output, the surplus must find a home in international markets. The strong orderbook momentum suggests that Chinese exporters are actively securing market share abroad, ahead of the full ramping of their overseas capacity.

This export push will have two effects. First, it will accelerate the destocking of domestic social inventory in China, which the expert expects to begin in June. Second, it will put downward pressure on aluminum prices in international markets, particularly in regions that are directly exposed to Chinese exports.

The strategic question for non-Chinese producers is whether they can compete with a supply chain that is simultaneously benefiting from scale, low-cost energy in multiple geographies, and aggressive export pricing. The answer will depend on the specific cost position and market positioning of each producer, but the direction of travel is clear.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Demand Side and the Timing of Price Adjustment

For all its detail on supply, the expert assessment leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important of these is the demand side. The report does not provide a forecast for global aluminum demand growth, nor does it address how demand from key end-use sectors such as automotive, construction, packaging, and renewable energy will evolve over the next three years.

This matters because the supply expansion is only bearish for prices if it exceeds demand growth. If global demand accelerates due to factors such as the energy transition, infrastructure spending, or reshoring of manufacturing, the new capacity could be absorbed without a significant price decline. The report's implicit assumption is that supply will grow faster than demand, but this is not explicitly tested.

A second unanswered question is the timing of price adjustment. The expert's estimates suggest that the most significant capacity additions will occur in 2027 and 2028. But the export momentum from China is already building. If prices begin to decline before the new capacity is fully operational, some of the planned projects could be delayed or scaled back. The decision framework for producers is not simply about whether to build capacity, but about when to bring it online relative to the price cycle.

A third open question is the response of incumbent producers outside China. The report focuses on Chinese-led expansion, but it does not address whether non-Chinese producers will respond by closing higher-cost capacity, merging to achieve scale, or lobbying for trade protection. The aluminum industry has a long history of trade disputes, and the current wave of expansion could trigger a new round of anti-dumping measures or tariffs.

These open questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are the natural boundaries of any analysis that focuses on supply. For investors, the key insight is that the supply side is now more certain and more bearish than it was six months ago. The demand side and the policy response remain the key variables that will determine the ultimate market outcome.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Aluminum Supply Wave

Given the structural shift in supply, investors need a clear framework for assessing which parts of the aluminum value chain are most exposed and which may benefit. The following decision logic can help.

First, distinguish between primary aluminum producers and downstream processors. Primary producers with high-cost operations in regions that are directly exposed to Chinese exports, such as Europe and parts of North America, face the greatest risk of margin compression. Producers with low-cost operations in regions that are insulated by trade barriers or logistics costs are better positioned.

Second, assess the exposure of individual producers to the specific geographies where new capacity is being built. Producers that have their own expansion plans in Indonesia or Central Asia may benefit from the same cost advantages that are driving the Chinese-led expansion. Producers that are purely domestic in high-cost regions face a strategic disadvantage.

Third, evaluate the downstream segments that are likely to benefit from lower aluminum input costs. Industries that consume large volumes of aluminum, such as automotive manufacturers, packaging companies, and renewable energy project developers, could see improved margins if primary aluminum prices decline. The key is to identify end-users that have pricing power in their own markets and can capture the benefit of lower input costs rather than passing it through to customers.

Fourth, monitor trade policy developments closely. The aluminum market has been shaped by tariffs and trade agreements for decades. If the current supply wave leads to a sustained decline in prices, it is likely that producing countries will seek protection. The timing and scope of any trade actions will be a critical variable.

Fifth, consider the time horizon. The most significant capacity additions are expected in 2027 and 2028. For investors with a short-term horizon, the current export momentum from China may be the most actionable signal. For investors with a longer horizon, the structural shift in the supply curve is the more important factor.

EN_A: So the May inflation numbers are out for China, and they look pretty mixed. CPI is soft, PPI is firm. What is the real story here?
EN_B: The headline numbers matched expectations, but the structure is shifting. PPI continued its uptrend, driven by industrial upgrading and AI demand, plus seasonal summer strength in coal and cooling. CPI, on the other hand, barely budged. Food deflation offset gains in communication and entertainment.
EN_A: So AI is the big driver behind PPI? That sounds like a shift from the old narrative.
EN_B: Exactly. The report highlights that AI-related cost pass-through is broadening. It is not just data centers anymore. Upstream semiconductors and downstream electronics are feeling the pinch. Communication tool prices rose 1.5 percent month on month, phones and tablets up 1.6 and 1.1 percent. That is memory tightness feeding through.
EN_A: So what does this mean for inflation going forward? Is AI becoming a persistent tailwind?
EN_B: That is the key insight. The report argues this AI impulse could be more durable than the energy shock. The global tech upcycle is still intact, and China's industrial upgrade and AI plus initiatives are multi-year plans. But there is a catch. Excess capacity and weak consumer demand will cap the upside. So PPI has some risk, but CPI stays benign.
EN_A: Why is CPI so weak then? Is it just food prices dragging it down?
EN_B: Food deflation is a big part, especially fresh vegetables and pork. But also transport and communication slowed. Even though it rose 0.2 percent in May, the prior two months averaged 2 percent monthly. Cars and fuel are still falling, offsetting the communication tool gains.
EN_A: What about the PPI side? Are there other structural drivers beyond AI?
EN_B: Non-ferrous metals are a big one. Tin and copper smelting jumped 4.8 and 3.1 percent month on month, driven by industrial upgrade and AI compute demand. Coal and power also got a seasonal boost from summer peak demand. But global oil price swings capped the overall gains. Upstream oil and refining pulled back.
EN_A: So what does this mean for the broader economy? Is deflation risk fading?
EN_B: The report thinks the GDP deflator could turn positive in the second quarter, ending a three-year deflationary stretch. That supports the central bank's price recovery mandate. But core CPI is still around 1 percent, and there are headwinds from energy shocks and tariff uncertainty. So the central bank will likely hold for now. A cut might come back in the second half if growth risks persist.
EN_A: So the divergence between PPI and CPI might continue. Are there any open questions the report does not fully answer?
EN_B: One big one is how durable the AI inflation impulse really is. The report says it could be more persistent than energy, but the magnitude and scope still need validation. Another is the Middle East situation. If the Strait of Hormuz disruption lingers, PPI could see more upside. The full report has a deeper scenario analysis on that.

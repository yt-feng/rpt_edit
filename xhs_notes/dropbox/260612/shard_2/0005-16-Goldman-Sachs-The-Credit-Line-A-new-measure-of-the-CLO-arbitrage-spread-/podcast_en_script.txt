EN_A: So we are looking at a really interesting report today that redefines a key metric for CLO markets, the arbitrage spread. B, give us the headline. What is the main takeaway?
EN_B: The big message is that the traditional way of measuring CLO arbitrage is outdated. The report introduces a new method called the deal-matched arbitrage spread. And what it shows is that the compression in arbitrage, especially in the USD market, is actually much more structural and severe than people think.
EN_A: So what exactly was the old measure missing? Why is this new method better?
EN_B: The old method simply takes the difference between new issue loan spreads and the weighted average cost of CLO debt. But it misses the actual excess spread embedded in each specific deal. When a CLO is reset or refinanced, that gap can change a lot. The new method matches each deal's assets and liabilities directly, giving a much truer picture of what managers are actually earning.
EN_A: And what are the most surprising findings from this new lens? What is the first key insight?
EN_B: First, the USD market has seen much more pronounced compression in arbitrage spreads over the last two years compared to the EUR market. What this means is that equity investors in USD CLOs are facing a structurally tougher environment. The report points to the rise of captive equity capital as a key driver.
EN_A: Captive equity capital. That sounds like a technical term. How does it change the game?
EN_B: Basically, large institutions set up dedicated funds that commit equity to the same CLO manager across multiple vintages. They are less focused on maximizing the arbitrage spread on any single deal. They care more about steady deployment and vintage diversification. This reduces the sensitivity of equity demand to spot arbitrage conditions, effectively lowering the return floor for everyone.
EN_A: So for an equity investor, does this mean returns are systematically lower?
EN_B: Yes, the report is clear that the presence of non-return-seeking participants should depress equity returns, all else equal. But it is not a mechanical relationship because realized returns also depend on credit losses. The report does not fully quantify that trade-off yet.
EN_A: Okay, what is the second key insight? What about resets and refinancings?
EN_B: The second insight is that lower liability costs do not automatically translate into better reset economics. In the USD market, since 2023, asset spread tightening has often kept pace with, or even exceeded, the improvement on the liability side. So the net benefit from resetting is very modest, less than 20 basis points in many cases.
EN_A: And what about the EUR market? Is it different?
EN_B: Completely different. EUR reset economics have held up much better, improving by nearly 50 basis points. That is because loan repricing activity has been less aggressive in Europe, so asset spreads have not compressed as fast. But the report leaves a cliffhanger here: how sustainable is this advantage? It does not fully answer that.
EN_A: And the third insight? Is there a difference between top-tier and lower-tier managers?
EN_B: Yes, and it is counterintuitive. In the USD market, higher-tier managers actually print slightly wider arbitrage spreads. In the EUR market, the opposite is true: lower-tier managers have wider spreads. This suggests that in USD, the differentiation is on the liability side, while in EUR, it is on the asset side. The report offers a preliminary explanation, but the full story is in the complete report.

EN_B: The biggest question is whether the structural compression in USD arbitrage means the golden era for CLO equity is over. And whether the optically cheaper EUR equity is actually a good deal. The report is cautious on the latter because the fundamental backdrop in Europe is weaker.

EN_A: So China has been this huge balancing force in the global oil market recently. But what is this report really trying to figure out?
EN_B: The big question is this: China slashed its crude imports by almost 5 million barrels a day between March and May. But at the same time, its crude inventories were actually rising. That seems backwards, right? The report digs into why that happened and what it means for the global supply picture.
EN_A: 5 million barrels a day is massive. And inventories going up when imports are falling does sound counterintuitive. How do they explain that?
EN_B: The key context is that China had been over-importing crude for most of 2025 and early 2026. They were aggressively building both commercial and strategic reserves. By February, before the conflict escalated, they had a supply surplus of about 2.7 million barrels a day. So the import cuts were coming from a position of strength, not from collapsing demand.
EN_A: So they were drawing down their own stockpiles rather than buying new crude. But what about actual demand? Was it falling too?
EN_B: Yes, but not as much as you might think. The report estimates China's apparent oil demand was down about 1.2 million barrels a year-on-year in April, which is an 8% drop. But that decline was mostly driven by refineries cutting runs and weakness in petrochemicals, not by a collapse in transport fuel demand.
EN_A: Why were refineries cutting runs? Was it just because of lower demand?
EN_B: It was more about margins. By April, refining margins across China had turned negative. High crude prices, restrictions on refined product exports, and a limited ability to pass through cost increases to domestic consumers all combined to crush profitability. State-owned refineries cut runs aggressively. Independent refineries, on the other hand, were told by the government not to cut, so they actually increased their share of supply.
EN_A: That sounds like a policy-driven split. So what happened with diesel and gasoline? Were they both hit equally?
EN_B: Not at all. Diesel demand was relatively resilient, down only 2% year-on-year. Manufacturing activity, while slowing, was still higher than a year ago, which supported industrial diesel use. Gasoline is where the story gets really interesting.
EN_A: What happened with gasoline? I saw that China had record travel numbers during the May Day holiday.
EN_B: Exactly, and that's the paradox. Holiday trips were up 3.5% year-on-year, but gasoline retail sales were down 10% to 30%, and in some developed regions, as much as 50%. The reason is the rapid adoption of electric vehicles and high-speed rail.
EN_A: How fast is that adoption happening?
EN_B: NEV sales penetration hit 61% in April, up from 51% in March and just 39% in January. Highway charging volumes for EVs surged 53% year-on-year during the holiday. So the old correlation between travel data and gasoline demand is breaking down completely.
EN_A: So what does that mean for the market? Is the report saying we can't trust mobility data anymore to predict oil demand?
EN_B: That's a key takeaway. The report also highlights a threshold effect. It says Chinese gasoline demand is relatively insensitive to small price increases, up to about a 5 to 7 dollar per barrel rise. But once you exceed that, demand destruction accelerates. By late April, retail gasoline prices had already risen by 30 dollars a barrel, far past that threshold.

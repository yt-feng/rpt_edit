# Oil Markets Are Repricing Disruption Risk, Not Ignoring It

The conventional narrative that oil markets have shrugged off the Strait of Hormuz disruption misses the deeper structural story. Spot Brent futures have declined roughly 25 percent from their late March peak, but this decline is not a signal that the market believes the crisis is over. It is a repricing of the size, duration, and composition of the disruption itself. The market has learned that the global oil deficit during the Hormuz closure is running at roughly 5-6 million barrels per day in the second quarter, far smaller than the 14-15 mb/d hit to Middle East liquids production. That gap between physical supply loss and actual market tightening is the most important analytical fact of the current cycle.

The difference comes from three offsetting forces that are often misunderstood as separate stories. Nearly 5 mb/d of demand destruction has emerged, concentrated in China, the Middle East, and petrochemical feedstocks. The global market entered the disruption with a pre-war surplus of roughly 3 mb/d. And non-Middle East supply has been upgraded by approximately 1.5 mb/d since hostilities began. These forces have combined to create a deficit that is real but manageable, and the market has priced that reality.

Yet the base case forecast for Brent in the fourth quarter of 2026 remains USD 90 per barrel, nearly USD 30 above pre-shock levels. That persistence tells us something critical about how this market works. The price floor has been structurally lifted, not because of current physical tightness, but because the market is now embedding a permanent security premium into long-dated prices. The question for serious investors is not whether oil prices will fall back to pre-crisis levels. They will not. The question is how to calibrate exposure across scenarios that range from benign to severely adverse, and how to distinguish between transient demand weakness and structural demand erosion.

## The Deficit Is Smaller Than the Supply Hit, and That Changes Everything

The most analytically useful number in the entire report is the reconciliation between the 14-15 mb/d Middle East supply loss and the 5-6 mb/d actual global deficit. This is not a minor adjustment. It is a fundamental reframing of how disruption risk should be modeled. The supply loss is dramatic, but the market does not feel the full force of that loss because demand adjusts, inventories buffer, and non-disrupted supply responds.

The nearly 5 mb/d of demand destruction is the largest single offset. This is not simply economic slowdown. It is a combination of behavioral response to high prices, government measures, and most importantly, accelerating structural shifts in China. The year-over-year decline in Chinese crude import demand of nearly 4 mb/d has been a key driver of the price moderation. This is not a cyclical blip. It reflects China's deepening pivot to electric vehicles and alternative energy sources, a trend that the report assumes will persist beyond the reopening period. The assumption that just over 10 percent of the demand weakness will be permanent is conservative, but it signals a real structural headwind.

The pre-war surplus of roughly 3 mb/d that was forecast for the second quarter before the disruption is another critical cushion. The market was already loose before the crisis hit. That starting condition matters enormously for how tightness propagates through the system. A disruption that would have caused a 10 mb/d deficit in a balanced market creates only a 5-6 mb/d deficit when it lands on an already oversupplied base.

The implication is straightforward: future disruptions will not produce linear price responses. The starting state of the market determines the magnitude of the impact. Investors who model disruption risk as a simple supply-minus-demand calculation will systematically overestimate price spikes when the market enters a disruption from a surplus position.

## A Longer Disruption but a Faster Recovery: The Two-Speed Mideast Supply Story

The report now assumes that Gulf oil exports will normalize by late August, two months later than the previous assumption of late June. This is a meaningful extension, but it is not the full story. The normalization does not require Hormuz flows to return to pre-war levels. It requires Hormuz flows to recover to only 70 percent of pre-war levels, given the current redirections through pipelines to Yanbu, Fujairah, the Gulf of Oman, and Ceyhan.

This is a crucial operational detail. The infrastructure to bypass Hormuz exists and is being used. The East-West pipeline in Saudi Arabia, the Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline, and the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline provide alternative export routes that are already carrying roughly 10 mb/d. The math is simple: pre-war Gulf exports of 23 mb/d minus current redirections of roughly 10 mb/d means that Hormuz needs to carry only about 13 mb/d to restore full exports, compared to a pre-war level of 20 mb/d through the Strait.

The key constraint on reopening is not oil field readiness or worker availability. The active rig count in Saudi Arabia has actually increased since the war began, suggesting that producers are preparing for a restart. The binding constraint is pipeline capacity to destock previously produced oil. This is a logistical bottleneck, not a geological one. Once the Strait reopens, the report expects production to recover faster than the consensus of the IEA and EIA forecasts. The data shows that Gulf producers could recover essentially all lost supply within three to four months of reopening, while other forecasters see a slower, more cautious trajectory.

For investors, this means the downside risk from a rapid supply recovery is larger than consensus expects. If the Strait reopens and pipeline logistics are less constrained than feared, the market could swing from deficit to surplus much faster than current forward curves imply. The benign scenario of USD 70 per barrel in late 2026 and USD 60 in 2027 is not a tail risk. It is a plausible outcome if the reopening is smooth and demand losses prove stickier than assumed.

## Resilient 2027 Prices Despite a Large Surplus: The Security Premium Argument

The report forecasts that 2027 crude prices will exceed their 2025 average by USD 10 per barrel, despite an over 3 mb/d surplus in 2027. This is the most counterintuitive claim in the analysis, and it is also the most strategically important.

Two mechanisms explain this resilience. First, OECD commercial oil stocks are unlikely to reach very high levels following sharp draws in 2026 and over 1 mb/d of global strategic stockpiling in 2027. The inventory trajectory matters more than the absolute surplus number. Even a large surplus does not push prices down if inventories are being rebuilt from low levels. The market is pricing the path of inventories, not the level of the surplus.

Second, and more fundamentally, the market is now embedding a security premium into long-dated prices. This is not a temporary disruption premium that will fade when the Strait reopens. It is a structural reassessment of the risk that spare capacity is not actually spare. The ability of Gulf producers to ramp up production quickly in a crisis has been called into question. The market will demand compensation for that uncertainty going forward, even in a surplus environment.

The report estimates this premium at roughly USD 9 per barrel for long-dated prices. That is a permanent shift in the price floor. Pre-crisis, the market assumed that OPEC spare capacity was a reliable backstop. Post-crisis, that assumption is gone. Every barrel of spare capacity will be discounted for the risk that it cannot be deployed when needed.

For investors with long-duration energy exposure, this is a critical insight. The base case for 2027 is not a return to pre-crisis prices. It is a structurally higher floor, supported by inventory dynamics and a security premium that will not easily dissipate. The bull case for oil is not about a sustained deficit. It is about a permanent repricing of supply risk.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Persistent Demand Destruction Will Be

The most important unresolved question in the analysis is the durability of the demand destruction. The report estimates that roughly 5 mb/d of demand has been lost during the disruption, and assumes that just over 10 percent of that weakness will persist. But the basis for that assumption is thin, and the range of outcomes is wide.

The demand destruction is concentrated in three areas: China, the Middle East, and petrochemical feedstocks. Each of these has a different structural trajectory. China's shift to electric vehicles is accelerating, but it is not linear. The pace of EV adoption depends on policy support, battery costs, and consumer preferences, all of which are uncertain. Middle East demand destruction is partly a function of conflict-related economic disruption, which could reverse quickly if stability returns. Petrochemical feedstock demand is tied to industrial activity and global trade, which are themselves uncertain.

The report does not provide a rigorous framework for distinguishing between temporary demand compression and permanent demand erosion. This is not a criticism of the analysis; it is a genuine analytical frontier. The oil market has rarely experienced a demand shock of this magnitude from a geopolitical event. There is no reliable historical analog.

Investors should treat the assumption of 10 percent persistence as a central estimate with wide error bars. If the persistence is closer to zero, the market will be tighter than the base case, supporting higher prices. If it is closer to 20 or 30 percent, the surplus in 2027 will be larger, and the security premium will be tested. This is the variable that will determine whether the base case is too bullish or too bearish.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Scenario Range

The report provides three explicit scenarios: benign, adverse, and severely adverse. The benign scenario sees Brent averaging around USD 70 in late 2026 and USD 60 in 2027, assuming exports normalize by end-July, stickier demand losses, and stronger non-disrupted supply. The adverse scenario sees Brent averaging just over USD 110 in late 2026, assuming Gulf exports normalize only by end-October. The severely adverse scenario sees Brent averaging USD 140 in 2027, assuming Hormuz remains disrupted through 2027 with only a gradual recovery in exports.

These scenarios are not equally probable, and the report's central view is that risks are two-sided but tilted to the upside. The key question for any investor is how to position across this range. A simple framework has three dimensions.

First, assess your view on the duration of the disruption. The base case assumes normalization by late August. Every month of extension adds roughly USD 5-10 per barrel to the late 2026 price, based on the scenario analysis. If you believe the Strait will reopen sooner, the benign scenario becomes more likely. If you believe the conflict is structurally unresolved, the adverse scenario is the right anchor.

Second, assess your view on demand persistence. The base case assumes 10 percent of demand destruction sticks. If you believe the structural shift in China is larger and faster, the demand baseline is lower, and prices will be softer. If you believe demand bounces back fully, the deficit will be larger and prices will be higher.

Third, assess your view on the security premium. The base case assumes a USD 9 per barrel premium to long-dated prices. If you believe the market will forget this crisis quickly, the premium will fade and 2027 prices will be lower. If you believe the market has permanently repriced spare capacity risk, the premium will persist or even grow.

The decision framework is not about picking a single scenario. It is about understanding which variables matter most and how they interact. The duration of the disruption and the persistence of demand destruction are the two dominant drivers. The security premium is the structural wildcard.

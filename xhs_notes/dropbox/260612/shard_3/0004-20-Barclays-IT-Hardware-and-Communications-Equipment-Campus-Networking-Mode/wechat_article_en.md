# The Campus Networking Boom Is Over: The Next Phase Demands a Strategy of Selective Share Gain, Not Market Growth

The post-pandemic correction in campus networking is now history. The market has absorbed the inventory overhang, normalized demand, and is settling into a new, slower growth trajectory. A global investment bank’s updated model forecasts overall campus networking revenue growth of approximately 8% in 2026 and 7% in 2027, a clear deceleration from the 10% growth seen in 2025 and a far cry from the mid-teens expansion of 2021 through 2023. This is not a cyclical downturn; it is the market finding its structural equilibrium. The strategic question for investors and corporate planners is no longer whether the market will grow, but who will capture the value in a maturing environment where growth is increasingly a zero-sum game.

The implication is stark: the era of a rising tide lifting all boats is over. The 16% market contraction in 2024 was a brutal cleansing, and the recovery that followed has been uneven. The data shows that the total addressable market is returning to a mid-single-digit growth profile, which is the historical norm for this sector. The tailwind from hybrid work and the initial rush of enterprise upgrades has faded. What remains is a market driven by replacement cycles, AI-inferencing preparation, and the gradual adoption of new standards like WiFi 7. These are powerful forces, but they are not explosive. They are steady, and they reward incumbents with strong refresh bases and challengers with compelling architectural advantages.

The central argument of this article is that the campus networking market has entered a phase where aggregate growth is a poor proxy for individual company performance. The winners will be determined by their ability to execute on three distinct vectors: capturing share from the dominant incumbent, capitalizing on the structural shift toward cloud-managed networking, and aligning product roadmaps with the specific demands of AI-ready campus infrastructure. The market is no longer a growth story; it is a share-shift story.

## The Post-Correction Market is Normalizing to a Mid-Single-Digit Growth Baseline, Making Share Gains the Primary Driver of Outperformance

The headline numbers from the updated model tell a story of stabilization, not acceleration. After the 16% decline in 2024, the market rebounded to 10% growth in 2025. The forecast for 8% and 7% in the following two years represents a return to a more sustainable, albeit slower, pace. This is the critical baseline from which all strategic thinking must proceed. The market is not broken, but it is no longer a source of easy, above-trend expansion.

For the largest player, Cisco, which commands approximately 50% market share, this environment presents a dual challenge. The company has reported strong recent order growth, with campus networking orders up over 25% year-over-year and wireless orders up over 40% in its most recent fiscal quarter. This performance reflects a recovery from its own severe inventory correction in 2024. However, the model anticipates that Cisco will continue to be a net donor of share to competitors. The refresh cycle is healthy, but the architectural shifts in the market favor newer, more agile architectures. Cisco’s size means it must defend a massive installed base while simultaneously innovating to prevent erosion. The question is not whether Cisco can grow, but whether it can grow fast enough to offset inevitable share losses in specific segments.

Conversely, for a later entrant like Arista, the math is far more favorable. Starting from a minuscule 0.3% campus market share in 2020, the model projects a rise to 3.4% by 2027. While this absolute share remains small, the growth rate is exponential in relative terms. Arista’s campus revenue guidance of $1.25 billion for 2026 is a concrete signal of its ambition. For a company with a dominant position in the data center, the campus represents a large, underpenetrated adjacency. The key insight here is that for a share-gainer like Arista, a normalizing market is not a headwind; it is a tailwind, because it lowers the bar for organic growth and allows it to compound its gains from a small base without needing the entire market to accelerate.

## The Structural Shift to Cloud-Managed WLAN is Accelerating, Creating a High-Growth Niche Within a Slower-Growing Market

The most compelling sub-plot within the campus networking story is the divergence between the overall WLAN market and the cloud-managed WLAN segment. The total WLAN market is forecast to grow at roughly 12% in 2026 and 8% in 2027, driven by the ramp of WiFi 7. This is healthy. But the cloud-managed WLAN segment is projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of 17% from 2021 to 2026, a significant upward revision from a prior estimate of 15%. This is not just a difference in degree; it is a difference in kind.

This acceleration has profound implications for competitive dynamics. Cloud-managed networking shifts the value proposition from hardware to software and services. It creates recurring revenue streams, increases customer stickiness, and allows vendors to deploy features and security updates continuously. For enterprise customers, the appeal is operational simplicity and the ability to manage a distributed campus network from a single pane of glass. The model suggests that cloud WLAN already represents a mid-teens percentage of total WLAN revenues, and this share is only going to grow.

Vendors with strong cloud platforms are therefore positioned to capture a disproportionate share of future growth. The report’s data on HPE, for instance, shows it maintaining a low-double-digit market share. But the real strategic question is how its Aruba networking business, which has a strong cloud-managed offering, performs relative to competitors who are more reliant on traditional hardware sales. The cloud shift rewards architectural incumbency. A vendor that entered the market with a cloud-native platform has a structural advantage over one that is trying to retrofit cloud capabilities onto a legacy hardware business. The market is not just growing; it is evolving, and the evolution favors the cloud-first architectures.

## AI Inferencing at the Edge is Reshaping Campus Switching Demand, But the Full Impact Remains an Open Question

One of the most intriguing, and least understood, drivers in the updated model is the role of AI inferencing in the campus. The report notes that enterprises are upgrading and preparing their networks for AI inferencing. This is a critical observation because it suggests that the campus network is becoming a downstream beneficiary of the AI infrastructure buildout. The data center side of the networking market has been the primary beneficiary of AI training workloads. The campus, however, is where AI inferencing will be consumed.

The logic is straightforward. As AI applications become embedded in enterprise workflows, the traffic patterns on campus networks will change. Inferencing requires low latency and high bandwidth at the edge. This will drive demand for higher-speed campus switches and more sophisticated network management capabilities. The wired switching segment, which constitutes the largest portion of the campus market, is already seeing robust order and revenue growth, partly driven by refresh activity and partly by this AI preparation.

However, the model does not quantify the specific uplift from AI inferencing. This is a meaningful open question. How much incremental demand will AI create? Will it be a one-time upgrade cycle, or a sustained source of growth? The answer depends on the pace of enterprise AI adoption, which remains highly uncertain. The report’s current forecast may be conservative if AI adoption accelerates, or it may be too optimistic if enterprises delay upgrades. This is the central analytical tension in the report: the market is normalizing, but a powerful new demand driver is emerging. The interaction between these two forces will determine whether the 7-8% growth forecast proves to be a floor or a ceiling.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Four Questions to Assess Vendor Positioning in a Share-Shift Market

The report provides the data; the strategic task is to translate that data into a usable framework for decision-making. The following four questions are designed to help investors and corporate strategists evaluate the relative positioning of vendors in this maturing market.

First, what is the vendor’s share trajectory? The market is growing at 7-8%, but the performance of individual companies will diverge wildly. A vendor that is gaining share, like Arista, can compound growth well above the market average. A vendor that is losing share, even if the market grows, will underperform. The critical metric is not revenue growth, but revenue growth relative to the market. The model’s market share data for each vendor is the single most important piece of information.

Second, how exposed is the vendor to the cloud-managed networking transition? The cloud WLAN segment is growing at nearly twice the rate of the overall market. A vendor with a strong cloud platform is riding a structural tailwind. A vendor that is over-indexed to traditional hardware is fighting a headwind. This is not a binary distinction; it is a spectrum. But the direction of travel is clear, and the model’s upward revision of the cloud WLAN CAGR underscores its importance.

Third, is the vendor’s product roadmap aligned with AI-ready campus infrastructure? The AI inferencing thesis is still nascent, but it is likely to become a dominant theme over the next several years. Vendors that can demonstrate a clear path to handling the bandwidth and latency requirements of edge AI will be better positioned to win enterprise deals. This is a qualitative judgment, but it can be informed by looking at a vendor’s switching portfolio and its investments in network programmability and telemetry.

Fourth, what is the vendor’s exposure to the enterprise data center switching market? The model shows that enterprise data center switching is growing far faster than campus switching, with estimates of 47% and 24% growth in 2026 and 2027 respectively. A vendor with a strong data center franchise can use its credibility there to cross-sell campus solutions. Conversely, a pure-play campus vendor may miss out on the most dynamic part of the overall networking market. The interplay between campus and data center is a powerful strategic lever.

## The Report’s Core Unresolved Question: How Will the Cisco-Arista Share War Reshape the Competitive Landscape Over a Five-Year Horizon?

The report provides a detailed, data-rich snapshot of the current competitive dynamics. But it also leaves a critical strategic question unanswered: what happens when Arista’s campus share reaches a more meaningful level, say 5-10%? The model projects Arista’s share reaching 3.4% by 2027. At that point, it will no longer be a niche player. It will be a direct, legitimate competitor to Cisco in a way that it is not today. How will Cisco respond?

The history of technology markets suggests that incumbents do not cede share passively. Cisco has deep resources, a massive installed base, and a powerful ecosystem of partners. It can compete on price, bundle campus products with its dominant data center and security portfolios, or accelerate its own cloud networking capabilities. The report’s current data shows Cisco’s share stabilizing around 49%. But a multi-year share war could compress margins for all players, even as the market grows. The model does not account for a potential price war, and it does not model the impact of aggressive competitive responses from the incumbent.

This is the most important analytical gap in the report. The next phase of the campus networking story is not just about market growth or technology shifts; it is about the competitive dynamics between a dominant incumbent and a fast-growing challenger. Investors who can anticipate how this battle will unfold will be in a far stronger position than those who simply extrapolate current trends. The answer lies not in the data, but in the strategic choices that management teams will make in the coming years.

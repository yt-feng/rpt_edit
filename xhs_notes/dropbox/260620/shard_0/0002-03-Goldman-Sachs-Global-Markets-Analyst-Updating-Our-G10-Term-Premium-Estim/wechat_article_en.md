# The Term Premium Is Still High, But How You Measure It Changes the Entire Bond Market Narrative

The most important question in global fixed income today is not whether term premium has risen. It has. The real question is *how much* of the recent steepening in G10 yield curves reflects a genuine repricing of the path of short-term interest rates, and how much reflects a pure compensation for bearing duration risk. The answer, it turns out, depends entirely on which model you trust. A major new framework from a global investment bank introduces survey-augmented term premium estimates that systematically produce lower and more stable readings than traditional yield-curve-only models. For the US 10-year, the difference is roughly 40 basis points. For the UK, it is nearly 100 basis points. For Japan, the divergence tells an entirely different story about the Bank of Japan's exit from yield curve control. This is not a technical footnote. It is a fundamental disagreement about the structure of rate expectations that has profound implications for asset allocation, hedging strategies, and fiscal sustainability.

The timing matters. G10 bond markets have endured one of the most dramatic repricing episodes in decades. Yields have risen, curves have steepened, and central bank balance sheets are shrinking. If the yield-curve-only models are correct, then term premium alone accounts for a large portion of the move, implying that bonds are offering unusually attractive compensation for risk. If the survey-based models are correct, then a significant share of the yield increase is actually a structural upward revision to expected policy rates, meaning bonds are less cheap than they appear. The difference is not academic. It determines whether a portfolio manager should be extending duration or staying short.

This article unpacks the new dual-model framework, explains why the divergence between the two estimates is itself an information signal, and offers a decision framework for investors who must navigate this ambiguity. The central argument is that the survey-based estimates are likely more reliable for assessing the current level of term premium, especially after structural shifts in inflation and monetary policy regimes, but the yield-curve-only estimates retain a distinct advantage for predicting future excess returns. Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient on its own.

## Survey-Based Models Reveal That Rate Expectations, Not Just Risk Premium, Are Driving Yields Higher

The core innovation in this new research is straightforward but consequential. Traditional term premium models, such as the widely used Adrian-Crump-Moench framework, extract expected short rates purely from the yield curve's shape. They rely on statistical assumptions about the long-run mean of short rates, which makes them highly sensitive to the estimation window and slow to adapt when the policy regime changes. The new survey-based models, following the Kim-Wright methodology, incorporate direct survey data on where market participants expect short rates to be in the future. This changes the decomposition of observed yields in a systematic way.

When survey data indicate that market participants have revised upward their expectations for the path of policy rates, the survey-based model attributes more of the rise in long-term yields to those higher rate expectations and less to term premium. The yield-curve-only model, by contrast, tends to interpret the same steepening as a rise in term premium because it has no external anchor for rate expectations beyond the historical mean. This difference is not marginal. For the US 10-year, the survey-based estimate currently sits near 70 basis points, while the yield-curve-only estimate is approximately 110 basis points. For the UK, the divergence is even more striking: the yield-curve-only model shows the UK with the highest term premium among the G4, while the survey-based model shows the UK with the lowest. The reason is that UK survey expectations for short rates have risen substantially, reflecting persistent inflation and a more hawkish monetary policy outlook. The yield-curve-only model misses this entirely.

The implication is clear. If you rely solely on yield-curve-only models, you will systematically overestimate the term premium in markets where rate expectations have shifted structurally higher. This matters for valuation. A 40-basis-point overestimate of term premium in US Treasuries makes them appear significantly cheaper than they actually are. An investor acting on that signal might overweight duration, only to find that the expected carry does not materialize because the yield curve is not as rich in risk compensation as the model suggested.

## Japan Is the Ultimate Test Case, and the Survey-Based Model Passes It

Japan provides the most vivid illustration of why the survey-based approach is superior for understanding structural shifts. Since 2023, Japanese surveyed policy rate expectations have risen dramatically, alongside the convergence of longer-term inflation expectations toward the Bank of Japan's 2 percent target. Market pricing of OIS swap rates has moved to similar or even higher levels on a two-to-five-year horizon. At the same time, the JGB yield curve has steepened significantly as the BoJ shrinks its balance sheet and the market reprices duration risk.

The yield-curve-only model interprets this bear steepening almost entirely as a rise in term premium. It sees short rates moving only gradually, anchored by decades of near-zero policy rates, and concludes that the steepening must reflect compensation for risk. The survey-based model tells a completely different story. It shows a substantial rise in rate expectations, with surveyed projections for the short rate climbing by over 100 basis points. Correspondingly, the rise in JGB term premium is much smaller. The implication is that the JGB market is not pricing in a massive duration risk premium; it is pricing in a genuine normalization of monetary policy.

This distinction is critical for anyone investing in Japanese government bonds or cross-asset strategies that depend on JGB yields. If the yield-curve-only model were correct, JGBs would be offering historically generous compensation for risk, potentially signaling a buying opportunity. If the survey-based model is correct, JGB yields are largely reflecting a structural shift in the policy rate path, and there is no such bargain. The survey-based model's ability to capture this shift, which the yield-curve-only model misses entirely, suggests that it is the more economically intuitive framework for assessing the current level of term premium in markets undergoing regime change.

## The Yield-Curve-Only Model Has One Critical Advantage: It Predicts Future Returns Better

Given the clear benefits of the survey-based approach for understanding the current level of term premium, one might be tempted to discard the yield-curve-only model entirely. That would be a mistake. The research shows that yield-curve-only estimates are systematically better predictors of future excess returns to holding duration over medium-term horizons. Across the G4, the R-squared values for predicting one-year excess returns are roughly twice as high for the yield-curve-only model compared to the survey-based model. The advantage persists across six-month, twelve-month, two-year, and five-year holding periods.

Why would a model that is worse at measuring the current level be better at predicting future returns? The answer lies in the nature of the two approaches. The yield-curve-only model is more volatile and more responsive to changes in market pricing. It reacts quickly to shifts in the yield curve's level, slope, and curvature. This volatility, which is a liability for estimating the current level, becomes an asset for forecasting. When the yield-curve-only term premium spikes, it often overshoots relative to the survey-based estimate, and that overshoot creates a subsequent return opportunity. The model is effectively capturing the cyclical component of term premium that mean-reverts over time.

The survey-based model, by contrast, is smoother and more anchored. It incorporates survey data that change only monthly, making it slower to react to day-to-day market moves. This stability makes it better for assessing the structural level of term premium but worse for identifying short-to-medium-term trading opportunities. The practical implication is that investors should use the two models for different purposes. The survey-based estimate is the better guide for strategic asset allocation and assessing whether bonds are cheap or expensive on a structural basis. The yield-curve-only estimate is the better guide for tactical duration positioning and timing entry points.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Puzzle of High Premium and Low Volatility

The research is admirably transparent about the limitations of both approaches, but it leaves one critical question unresolved. In recent years, term premium has remained elevated even as interest rate volatility has declined. This is unusual. Historically, term premium and rate volatility move together. When volatility is high, investors demand more compensation for bearing duration risk. When volatility falls, term premium typically follows. The current environment, in which term premium is high and volatility is moderate, suggests a structural shift in the duration risk premium that goes beyond cyclical factors.

The report notes this puzzle and promises future research to explore the drivers, but it does not yet provide a complete explanation. Several hypotheses are plausible. The first is that the sheer volume of bond supply, driven by large fiscal deficits across the G10, is exerting independent upward pressure on term premium that is not captured by volatility alone. The second is that the unwinding of central bank balance sheets has removed a structural buyer of duration, permanently raising the risk premium required to clear the market. The third is that the regime shift from low inflation to higher and more volatile inflation has changed the correlation between bond returns and other asset classes, making duration less attractive as a hedge and thereby increasing the premium.

Investors need to form their own views on this question because the answer determines whether current term premium levels are sustainable. If the high premium is primarily a cyclical phenomenon driven by elevated volatility and uncertainty, then it should decline as volatility normalizes. If it is a structural phenomenon driven by supply, balance sheet runoff, and changing correlations, then term premium may remain elevated for years, with profound implications for the fair value of government bonds, the cost of fiscal financing, and the attractiveness of duration relative to other risk assets.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Two-Model World

For portfolio managers and strategists, the existence of two credible but divergent term premium estimates creates both a challenge and an opportunity. The challenge is that there is no single "correct" number. The opportunity is that the gap between the two estimates is itself a powerful information signal. When the gap is wide, as it is currently in the UK and Japan, it indicates that the market is pricing in a significant structural shift in rate expectations that the yield-curve-only model is not capturing. When the gap is narrow, as it is in the euro area, the two models are in closer agreement, suggesting that the yield curve is more accurately reflecting the consensus path for policy rates.

The decision framework has three layers. First, for assessing the current level of term premium and making strategic allocation decisions, favor the survey-based estimate. It is more economically intuitive and better captures structural shifts. Second, for tactical trading and timing duration entry, favor the yield-curve-only estimate. Its higher volatility and better predictive power for excess returns make it the more useful tool for identifying overreactions and mean-reversion opportunities. Third, monitor the gap between the two estimates as a regime indicator. A widening gap suggests that the market is experiencing a structural repricing of rate expectations, which has different implications for asset allocation than a pure risk premium shock.

This framework implies that the current environment is particularly challenging for investors who rely on a single model. The survey-based estimate suggests that bonds are less cheap than they appear, while the yield-curve-only estimate suggests that term premium is near local highs and may offer attractive forward returns. The resolution of this tension will depend on whether the structural rise in rate expectations proves durable or whether it reverses as inflation and growth data evolve. The report's own analysis points to a rise in term premium driven by macro risks and bond supply, but the precise mix remains uncertain.

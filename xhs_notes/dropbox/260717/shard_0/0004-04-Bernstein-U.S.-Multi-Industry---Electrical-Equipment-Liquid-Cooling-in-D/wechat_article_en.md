The timing of this development is critical. Two-phase DTC remains in early commercialization stages, with unresolved engineering challenges around fluid dynamics, refrigerant selection, and control complexity. Direct-to-die cooling is even more nascent, with widespread deployment unlikely before 2030. If single-phase DTC can handle 15kW per cold plate, the urgency to solve these problems diminishes. Necessity has been the primary driver of cooling technology evolution in data centers. Remove the necessity, and the adoption timeline for advanced cooling collapses.

## Two-Phase DTC Faces Structural Engineering Hurdles That Make It a Risky Bet for Near-Term Deployment

Two-phase direct-to-chip cooling offers a theoretical heat capture advantage of more than three times that of single-phase systems, driven by latent heat absorption during coolant evaporation. The physics are compelling: while sensible heat transfer requires a temperature gradient, latent heat transfer occurs at constant temperature, enabling uniform thermal profiles and higher heat flux per unit area. Yet translating this principle into a reliable, scalable product has proven extraordinarily difficult.

The most fundamental engineering challenge is concurrent management of fluid and vapor flow within the cold plate. In a two-phase system, the coolant evaporates as it absorbs heat, creating vapor pockets that generate localized high-pressure regions. Because fluids flow from high to low pressure, these vapor pockets can push coolant in the wrong direction, causing flow maldistribution. Some microchannels end up carrying predominantly vapor while others carry liquid, creating non-uniform cooling and localized hotspots. Solving this requires dual-path channel arrays with larger diameters for low-density vapor and smaller diameters for liquid—a geometric constraint that adds manufacturing complexity and reduces design flexibility.

Control complexity represents a second major barrier. Two-phase systems require active management of multiple variables simultaneously: refrigerant temperature, avoidance of vapor superheating, condensate cooling, and overall system pressure. Modern GPU workloads compound this difficulty. Inference batching creates rapid power transients that can go from zero to 100 percent load in milliseconds. A two-phase cooling system must respond to these transients in real time, requiring finely calibrated sensors and sophisticated control algorithms that single-phase systems do not need. The software burden alone could delay commercial readiness by years.

Refrigerant selection adds a third layer of uncertainty. The coolants that meet the required thermodynamic properties for two-phase DTC are predominantly PFAS compounds—per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances that face increasing regulatory scrutiny due to environmental persistence and health concerns. Data center operators are already navigating complex compliance requirements around energy consumption and water usage. Adding PFAS regulation to that mix creates long-term supply chain and reporting risks that many operators may prefer to avoid entirely.

The current state of commercialization reflects these challenges. The leading players in two-phase DTC remain in pilot phases, with funding rounds that suggest meaningful scale is still years away. Vertiv and Accelsius appear closest to having commercially ready products, but the technology remains unproven at the scale required for hyperscale deployments. Given these unresolved issues, a credible single-phase alternative that delays the need for two-phase adoption by five to seven years would fundamentally alter the competitive dynamics of this market.

## Direct-to-Die Cooling Will Not Reshape the Market Before 2030, Removing Near-Term Pressure for Incumbents

Direct-to-die cooling represents the most radical departure from current architectures. By etching microchannels directly into the silicon wafer and flowing coolant through the chip package itself, this approach eliminates the cold plate entirely and brings heat extraction to the source of generation. The theoretical heat flux advantage over direct-to-chip cooling is substantial, and the simplification of the thermal stack could reduce the number of components in the cooling system.

However, the practical barriers to commercialization are formidable. The most critical issue is material compatibility: deionized water, the most practical coolant candidate, causes corrosion when in direct contact with silicon. Solving this problem requires either new coolant formulations that are compatible with silicon or protective coatings that do not compromise thermal performance. Neither solution has been demonstrated at scale.

Manufacturing complexity is equally daunting. Etching microchannels into silicon wafers requires modifications to semiconductor fabrication processes that are already among the most precise and expensive in any industry. Integrating cooling channels into a chip package that must also handle electrical connections, structural integrity, and thermal expansion creates a multi-dimensional engineering problem that no company has solved for volume production.

Microsoft and TSMC have signaled activity in this space, with potential manufacturing timelines around 2026. But signaling activity is not the same as demonstrating commercial viability. Even under optimistic assumptions, widespread adoption of direct-to-die cooling is unlikely before 2030. For data center operators planning capacity additions for the next five years, direct-to-die is not a viable alternative. It is a research program with long-term potential but no near-term relevance.

This timeline matters because it defines the window during which single-phase DTC must prove its staying power. If CoolIT's 15kW cold plate can be commercialized and replicated, the industry has a clear path to meeting cooling requirements for Rubin Ultra and potentially Feynman and Kyber generations without transitioning to either two-phase or direct-to-die. The obsolescence risk that has hung over single-phase cooling equipment for the past two years effectively disappears.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating Cooling Technology Risk in Data Center Capital Planning

Data center operators and equipment buyers face a strategic choice that depends on three variables: the GPU thermal trajectory, the commercialization timeline for advanced cooling, and the replicability of CoolIT's single-phase breakthrough. This framework translates those variables into actionable decisions.

**Scenario One: CoolIT's 15kW cold plate achieves commercial scale and competitors replicate the approach within 12 to 24 months.** In this scenario, single-phase DTC remains the dominant cooling format through at least 2030. Two-phase DTC becomes a niche solution for specialized high-density deployments rather than a mainstream requirement. Direct-to-die cooling remains a long-term research project. Capital allocated to two-phase R&D or pilot deployments should be redirected to optimizing single-phase system efficiency and reliability. The risk of stranded investment in single-phase infrastructure is minimal.

**Scenario Two: CoolIT's cold plate is commercialized but protected by patents that prevent rapid replication.** In this scenario, single-phase DTC remains viable, but CoolIT captures a disproportionate share of the cold plate market. Competitors face a choice: license the technology, invest in alternative single-phase approaches, or accelerate their two-phase programs. Operators should diversify their cooling supplier base and maintain contingency plans for two-phase adoption, but the urgency is moderate. The patent creates pricing power for CoolIT but does not force a technology transition.

**Scenario Three: CoolIT's cold plate proves difficult to commercialize at scale, or GPU TDPs exceed 15kW per cold plate in the next generation.** In this scenario, two-phase DTC becomes necessary by 2027 or 2028. Operators should begin pilot deployments of two-phase systems within the next 12 to 18 months to build operational experience and supplier relationships. Direct-to-die remains a longer-term hedge but should not influence near-term capital allocation. The key risk is moving too late: if two-phase becomes a necessity and the leading suppliers are capacity-constrained, operators face extended lead times and premium pricing.

**Scenario Four: GPU TDPs increase gradually, staying within the 2.5kW to 5kW range for the next five years.** In this scenario, even without CoolIT's breakthrough, single-phase DTC can handle the thermal load with modest improvements in coolant temperature management and cold plate design. The need for advanced cooling is pushed out organically. Operators should continue optimizing single-phase systems and treat two-phase and direct-to-die as watching briefs. No urgent action is required.

The most probable path lies between Scenarios One and Two. CoolIT's demonstration is credible enough to take seriously, but commercialization at scale and patent enforcement are uncertain. Operators should plan for single-phase DTC to remain the primary cooling format through 2030, but they should also develop relationships with two-phase suppliers as a hedge. The cost of maintaining optionality is small compared to the cost of being forced into a technology transition without preparation.

## The Commercialization of a 15kW Cold Plate Has Asymmetric Implications for Cooling Equipment Suppliers

If CoolIT's single-phase breakthrough reaches commercial scale, the implications for white space cooling equipment are mixed and depend heavily on the speed of competitive replication. In the near term, demand far outstrips supply across all cooling equipment categories, so no supplier faces revenue pressure in the next two to three years. The risk is structural and longer-term.

For cold plate manufacturers, the 15kW cold plate eliminates obsolescence risk. The narrative that single-phase cold plates would become obsolete as GPU TDPs exceeded 2.5kW has been a persistent overhang on this sub-sector. If single-phase can handle 15kW, that obsolescence timeline extends well beyond the planning horizon of most data center operators. Cold plate suppliers benefit directly from reduced technology risk and the associated premium that buyers are willing to pay for proven, scalable solutions.

However, the innovation premium that suppliers have enjoyed begins to erode once the technological frontier stops advancing. If the industry converges on a single-phase solution that works for the next five to seven years, cold plates become a more commoditized component. Pricing pressure increases as multiple suppliers replicate the approach and compete on cost rather than performance differentiation. Suppliers with strong patent positions, like CoolIT, can maintain pricing power. Suppliers without proprietary technology face margin compression.

For coolant distribution unit manufacturers, the implications are similar but potentially more pronounced. CDUs are the pumps, heat exchangers, and control systems that circulate coolant through the cold plates. In a single-phase world, CDU design stabilizes and the rate of technological change slows. This reduces the urgency for operators to upgrade CDUs and extends replacement cycles. Unit volume growth remains strong due to overall data center capacity expansion, but revenue per unit may decline as competition intensifies and the technology premium fades.

For suppliers that have invested heavily in two-phase DTC capabilities, either directly or through partnerships, the 15kW cold plate creates a strategic dilemma. Continuing to develop two-phase technology is a hedge against the possibility that CoolIT's approach fails at scale or that GPU TDPs eventually exceed 15kW. But the resources allocated to two-phase R&D could instead be deployed to optimize single-phase systems and capture market share in the near term. The optimal strategy depends on each supplier's assessment of the probability that single-phase DTC can sustain its dominance through 2030 and beyond.

The most exposed suppliers are those that have bet their cooling strategy entirely on two-phase or direct-to-die adoption. If the single-phase paradigm extends by five to seven years, these suppliers face a period of low revenue from advanced cooling products while their competitors capture the single-phase market. The partnerships between JCI and Accelsius and between Carrier and ZutaCore represent significant capital commitments to two-phase technology. Those commitments still have value as long-term options, but the near-term return on that capital is diminished if single-phase remains viable.

For the equipment ecosystem as a whole, the 15kW cold plate is a stabilizing force. It reduces technology uncertainty, simplifies supply chain planning, and allows operators to standardize on a single cooling architecture for a longer period. Standardization drives volume, and volume drives cost reduction. The beneficiaries are the largest suppliers with the broadest product portfolios and the strongest manufacturing scale. The losers are niche players whose value proposition depends on solving problems that CoolIT's cold plate may have already solved.

Earlier this week, we met with the head of procurement at a leading data center developer, who also recently worked at a top 5 US electrical contractor. Below are the top 3 takeaways from our conversation, which focused on the growing obstacles to data center construction, the supply/demand environment in power generation (BTM + standby) and how China's electrical equipment manufacturing footprint is expanding into the US under the radar.

It is harder to build a data center today than a year ago. More delays/cancellations in the first 4-5 months of this year than entirety 2025, driven by public aversion (power prices, water usage, job losses). This uncertainty is forcing developers to hedge by multi-tracking the same data center build in different geographic locations. The expert anticipates 60-65% of projects in pipeline to proceed as scheduled. As such, OEMs, particularly power gen (CAT, CMI, etc.), are stepping efforts up to protect backlog quality: higher project viability standards (permits, financing & offtake), OEMs adding first right of refusal to buy back equipment into contract if a project gets delayed. Although power remains a critical bottleneck, the skilled labor shortage is getting worse, especially amongst high voltage electricians, commissioning specialists, electrical/utility engineers and substation construction crews – the shortage of immigrants, which usually represent 1/3 of the total labor, is a massive hindrance. As a result, data center construction is becoming more modular and the data center in a box concept will grow as rack density increases. OEMs and MEP contractors like ETN, HUBB, LGN, PWR, FPS who either are expanding their in-house fabrication capacity or sell equipment that enables modularization (e-houses, tc.) will benefit.

Power Gen lead times remain long, pricing growth moderating. Power redundancy for this expert is 1.3-1.5x the IT load for behind the meter and 1.0x for standby diesel generation, which is slightly above what we model (1x & 0.8x, respectively). This expert increasingly prefers turbines over reciprocating engines as data center size increases, though reciprocating engines remain an attractive option if time to power is the priority or if data center scale is in the 20-80 MW range. Lead time remain elevated – out to 2030 for turbines, 2029-30 for large recips (10-20 MW), while Tier 4 diesel standby has a 2 year lead time, Tier 2 diesel standby is 12-18 months, while <2MW diesel standby is <1 year. Pricing growth is slowing across the board, with the expert citing modest inflation vs. the +30-50% previously, driven by expanding capacity, new competitors and equipment becoming available from canceled/delayed projects (mainly talk thus far). Though, data center operators remain willing to pay a premium on the grey market to sidestep lead times. As for contract terms, OEMs are requiring higher down payments, 20% vs. the more traditional 10%.

China finding the back door into the US? Domestic OEMs are forging offshore partnerships for capacity. Siemens is now directing China-manufactured transformers into the US (recent development). GEV working with XD for high voltage breakers out of China, while ETN has taken a stake in a transformer manufacturer in China. This approach gives customers a piece of mind – they are getting a tier 1 design, but with offshore manufacturing capacity.

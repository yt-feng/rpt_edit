## Spare Cathode Capacity at Spence Can Process Sierra Gorda’s Oxide Stockpile, Generating Roughly $200 Million in Annual EBITDA

Sierra Gorda holds an oxide ore stockpile containing approximately 320,000 tonnes of copper—about 90 million tonnes at 0.35% grade. Spence has roughly 130,000 tonnes per year of spare cathode capacity at its dynamic heap leach and cathode plant. The logic is straightforward: truck the oxide ore the short distance to Spence, stack it, and let the leaching process—which takes three to six months for oxides with an average recovery of about 70%—produce cathode copper.

The financial impact is significant. Over a five-year period, this arrangement could generate approximately $200 million per year in EBITDA, split between the two parties. For context, that is roughly equivalent to the annual EBITDA of a mid-tier copper producer. The synergy here is not about cutting costs—it is about unlocking revenue from an asset (the stockpile) that currently generates no cash flow, using capacity (the cathode plant) that is already built and underutilized.

This is a textbook example of what operational collaboration can achieve when two assets are physically adjacent and have complementary processing capabilities. The key risk is that the stockpile is finite—once processed, the revenue stream ends—but over the assumed five-year window, the cash flow is material.

## Combining Mine Plans and Sharing Sulphide Ore Feed Can Lift Concentrator Utilization and Extend Mine Life

Spence’s concentrator is operating at only 90% of its 95,000 tonnes-per-day nameplate capacity due to clay-related processing challenges. At the same time, the mining fleet at Sierra Gorda appears more efficient based on site visits, and Sierra Gorda’s recently approved fourth grinding line will increase its processing capacity from roughly 130,000 to 160,000 tonnes per day. The opportunity is to share sulphide ore feed between the two concentrators, optimizing mine plans so that higher-grade ore from Sierra Gorda can be processed at Spence when its concentrator has spare capacity, and vice versa.

The potential impact goes beyond short-term throughput. Sierra Gorda recently announced an exploration target on the Catabela Northeast prospect—adjacent to its existing Catabela pit—of between 1.1 and 2.9 billion tonnes at 0.45-0.48% total copper. A technical assessment is due in 2026. If this resource is confirmed, it could extend Sierra Gorda’s mine life well beyond the current approximately 20 years. Combined mine planning would allow both operations to maximize production and lower unit costs over a much longer horizon.

The financial upside is substantial. A 10% uplift in production at Spence alone would be worth roughly $2.5 billion in net present value at a long-run copper price of $5.20 per pound. The synergy here is not just about filling spare capacity—it is about creating a shared resource base that neither asset could access independently.

## Tailings, Water, and Power Synergies Can Defer Over $500 Million in Capex While Enabling Future Expansion

BHP is spending more than $1 billion from fiscal 2026 through 2030 on extra drainage and higher tailings dam walls at Spence. If Spence can deposit some of its tailings at Sierra Gorda’s tailings facility instead, a significant portion of that capex could be deferred. The analysis assumes that by 2030, some tailings could be redirected, with Sierra Gorda receiving a fee of $2-4 per tonne. The net present value of this capex deferral to BHP is estimated at roughly $500 million.

The technical feasibility is plausible. Sierra Gorda already operates high-rate thickeners that increase tailings solid density, and Spence is increasing its own tailings density from 53% to 65% solids. The fourth milling line at Sierra Gorda requires even higher density—62% solids—so the two operations are converging on similar tailings management practices. Permitting and approval changes would be required, but the operational logic is sound.

Water and power synergies add another layer. Sierra Gorda has access to 1,500 liters per second of seawater through a third-party provider, and its fourth milling line will consume about 80-85% of that capacity. A potential fifth milling line would push consumption beyond 90%, but the pumps could be upgraded to increase flow. Spence operates a desalination plant with 1,000 liters per second capacity, expected to be fully utilized by 2028. Critically, Spence’s plant cannot process seawater, while Sierra Gorda can handle both. This complementarity means that additional water from Sierra Gorda could support a future fifth milling line at Sierra Gorda itself, or even be used on Spence’s heap leach to assist its salt-catalyzed leaching technology for secondary sulphides.

On power, Sierra Gorda has 20-30 megawatts of spare capacity in its contract that could be used by Spence. These are not large numbers in absolute terms, but in a capital-intensive industry where every percentage point of margin matters, they compound.

## A Decision Framework: How to Evaluate Asset-Level Joint Ventures in Copper Mining

For industry observers and corporate strategists evaluating similar opportunities, the Spence-Sierra Gorda case offers a replicable framework. The first question is physical adjacency. Are two assets within trucking distance—say, 20-30 kilometers—such that ore, water, or tailings can be moved economically? The second question is complementarity. Does one asset have spare processing capacity that the other lacks? In this case, Spence had spare cathode capacity and Sierra Gorda had an oxide stockpile. The third question is infrastructure sharing potential. Can water, power, or tailings systems be connected to defer or avoid capex? The fourth question is resource extension. Does one asset have exploration potential that could feed both operations, extending combined mine life?

The fifth and most strategic question is ownership structure. The MoU involves BHP as sole owner of Spence and a joint venture between KGHM and South32 at Sierra Gorda. Aligning incentives across three parties is inherently complex, but the potential value is large enough to justify the effort. For companies with adjacent assets held through different ownership vehicles, this deal shows that a formal joint venture or collaboration agreement can unlock value without requiring full M&A.

The framework also highlights what not to do. If assets are too far apart, if processing technologies are incompatible, or if regulatory barriers to sharing water or tailings are prohibitive, the synergies may not materialize. The Spence-Sierra Gorda case works because the distance is short, the processing gaps are clear, and the regulatory environment in Chile, while not simple, is navigable.

## The Broader Strategic Implication: Asset-Level Joint Ventures May Become the Primary Vehicle for Copper Supply Growth

The mining industry has long debated whether to build new mines or acquire existing ones. The Spence-Sierra Gorda MoU suggests a third path: integrate adjacent assets without changing ownership. This approach avoids the premium typically paid in M&A, the execution risk of greenfield development, and the political friction of entering new jurisdictions. It also allows each party to retain its balance sheet and strategic independence while capturing a material share of synergies.

For BHP, which has been actively seeking to expand its copper exposure, this deal is consistent with a pattern of pursuing asset-level joint ventures rather than corporate takeovers. For South32 and KGHM, the collaboration unlocks value in an asset that represents roughly 35% of South32’s group net asset value. For the broader industry, the message is clear: in a world where copper demand is rising but new supply is hard to bring online, the lowest-hanging fruit may be sitting right next door.

The $2-4 billion in potential synergies is not a forecast—it is a scenario. But it is a scenario grounded in specific, measurable operational changes that have been assessed in detail. If executed, this collaboration could add 10-20% to the net present value of two already valuable assets, without the risks of a major acquisition or a greenfield build.

EN_A: So, we hear a lot about geopolitical risks these days. But is the real story about short-term shocks or something much slower and deeper?
EN_B: That is exactly the question a new report from a global investment bank tries to answer. The title is "Geopolitics and GDP: Structural Alignment Matters More than Near-Term Risk." And the key insight is that we might be focusing on the wrong thing.
EN_A: What do you mean by "the wrong thing"?
EN_B: The report breaks it down into two concepts. First, there are geopolitical risk events, like wars or terrorist attacks. These are sharp, highly visible, but usually short-lived. Second, there is geopolitical alignment, which measures how close or distant countries are from each other. That is a slow-moving, persistent process.
EN_A: And the report argues that the second one matters more for the economy in the long run?
EN_B: Exactly. For example, a big risk event, like the Iran war, might only shave off about 0.3 percentage points from global GDP growth in the near term. That is not nothing, but it is temporary. In contrast, a one-standard-deviation improvement in a country's geopolitical alignment can boost its long-run GDP by about 3%. That effect builds up over almost a decade.
EN_A: Three percent is huge. So how does that actually happen? Through what channels?
EN_B: Mostly through trade and investment. Better alignment reduces trade barriers, increases bilateral trade volumes, and makes foreign direct investment flow more freely. The report also finds that emerging markets benefit even more than developed markets, because closer alignment helps them move away from resource dependence and improve their institutions.
EN_A: That makes sense. But is there any surprise in the findings?
EN_B: Yes, one big one. When the researchers looked at which bilateral relationships deliver the biggest economic gains, the UK came out on top. Not the US, not China. The reason is that the UK is a proxy for alignment with the entire Western bloc, because of its deep ties to both the US and mainland Europe.
EN_A: That is a fascinating result. But the report must have some limitations, right? What questions does it leave open?
EN_B: Two important ones. First, the data only goes up to 2024, so it does not capture the full impact of the 2025 tariffs or the Iran war. The fragmentation we see today might be worse than what the model shows. Second, the causality is tricky. Does better alignment cause economic growth, or does economic growth lead to better alignment? The report admits this is hard to fully prove.

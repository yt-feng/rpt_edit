# The Optical Market Is Entering Its Most Consequential Growth Phase, But the Real Debate Is Not About Speed—It Is About Architecture

The latest datacom optical market forecast from LightCounting, embedded in a recent investment bank research report, revises the total addressable market sharply upward. The datacom segment is now expected to grow at nearly a 35% compound annual rate through 2028, reaching $47 billion. That is a considerable increase from prior estimates, and it reflects genuine demand from AI infrastructure buildouts. But the headline number, while impressive, masks a deeper strategic question: which optical architectures will capture that growth, and when?

The report makes clear that the near-term story is dominated by pluggable transceivers at 800G and 1.6T data rates. These are the workhorses of the current AI cluster expansion. However, the more interesting—and contentious—part of the forecast concerns co-packaged optics (CPO). Industry analysts project CPO will reach only $3 billion by 2028, a fraction of the total market. Yet some component suppliers are already guiding to revenue figures that imply a much larger CPO total addressable market. This gap between consensus forecasts and company-level signals is where the most important investment debate now sits.

The timing of the inflection matters enormously. If CPO adoption accelerates earlier than the consensus expects, the competitive landscape for optical component suppliers will shift dramatically. If it does not, the current leaders in pluggable transceivers will continue to capture the majority of value. The report provides the data to frame this debate, but it does not resolve it. That is precisely why readers need to dig deeper into the original charts and assumptions.

This article interprets the report's findings through a strategic lens, identifies the unresolved questions, and offers a decision framework for investors and executives navigating this market.

## The Upward Revision in Datacom Optics Is Real, But Its Composition Reveals Where Value Is Concentrating

The datacom market forecast has been revised upward by an average of $9 billion per year through 2028, relative to the prior outlook. That is a material change. But the composition of that revision tells a more nuanced story than the aggregate number suggests.

The upward revision is driven primarily by 1.6T data rates, which account for approximately $5 billion of the increase, and 800G, which contributes another $4 billion. Lower data rates—400G and below—are essentially flat or declining over the forecast period. This is not a broad-based recovery in optical demand. It is a concentrated surge at the highest speeds, tied directly to the bandwidth requirements of AI training and inference clusters.

What does this mean for investors? It means that companies with exposure to 1.6T and 800G transceivers are positioned to capture the bulk of near-term growth. Suppliers that remain heavily dependent on 400G or legacy telecom products face a different, less favorable trajectory. The report's data implies a sharp divergence in revenue growth across the optical component universe, and that divergence will likely widen as the forecast period progresses.

The attach rate of optical solutions per XPU is also rising. According to the report, the blended attach rate has increased from approximately 2.7x per XPU in 2023 to an estimated 4.0x in 2025, and is projected to reach 4.4x by 2027. This is not a marginal change. It represents a structural increase in the optical content required to support larger cluster sizes. However, the report also notes that attach rates vary significantly by architecture: Google's deployments using optical circuit switches run closer to 1.5x per TPU, while Nvidia-based clusters approach 6.0x per GPU. This variance means that the overall market forecast is sensitive to the mix of AI architectures deployed, which is itself uncertain.

The key takeaway is not simply that the market is growing. It is that the growth is highly concentrated in specific data rates and architectures, and that the companies best aligned with those concentrations will see disproportionate benefits. The rest will face headwinds.

## The CPO Debate Is Not About Whether, But When—And the Timing Gap Is the Most Important Unknown

Co-packaged optics have been a topic of intense discussion for years, often framed as a technology that is perpetually "two years away." The report's data suggests that inflection is now visible on the horizon, but the timing remains contested.

Industry analysts forecast the CPO market will grow from negligible revenue in 2025 to approximately $3 billion by 2028, and then accelerate sharply to $11 billion by 2030. The inflection point is around 2028, when scale-up use cases—particularly dual- and multi-rack deployments—begin to ramp. After that, CPO is expected to account for roughly 20% of the total datacom market by the end of the decade.

But here is where the report's data becomes strategically interesting. One of the covered companies, Lumentum, has provided guidance implying that its own CPO revenues could reach approximately $2 billion by calendar 2028. If that single company's forecast is accurate, the total CPO market would need to be significantly larger than the $3 billion that industry analysts currently project. Either Lumentum is being aggressive in its guidance, or the consensus forecast is too conservative. The report leans toward the latter view, noting that "estimates from industry analysts are conservative and are likely to be revised higher."

This gap is not a minor forecasting disagreement. It has direct implications for which companies are positioned to lead in the next phase of optical growth. If CPO adoption accelerates, companies with strong CPO roadmaps could capture a disproportionate share of value, potentially at the expense of traditional transceiver suppliers. If adoption remains on the consensus trajectory, the pluggable transceiver leaders will continue to dominate.

The report does not resolve this debate. It provides the data points but leaves the reader to assess the probability of each scenario. That is the right approach, because the uncertainty is genuine. The timing of CPO adoption depends on factors that are inherently difficult to predict: the pace of AI cluster scaling, the evolution of electrical-optical interface standards, and the willingness of hyperscalers to commit to new architectures.

## Telecom and DCI Growth Is Being Pushed Out, Creating a Divergence Between Near-Term and Long-Term Optical Demand

While the datacom forecast is being revised upward, the telecom and data center interconnect (DCI) segment is being revised modestly downward. The telecom and DCI market is now expected to grow at a 14% CAGR through 2028, reaching $6 billion, compared to prior estimates that were approximately $1 billion higher per year.

The downward revision is driven primarily by 1.6T ZR and embedded optics, where the market outlook has been pushed out by roughly a year. The reason, according to the report, is that end customers are waiting for the availability of multi-rail amplifiers and multi-rail line systems before committing to high-speed deployments. This is a classic infrastructure adoption pattern: the technology is ready, but the supporting ecosystem is not yet mature.

This divergence between datacom and telecom demand has important implications. It means that optical component suppliers with diversified exposure to both segments may face a mixed near-term picture: strong datacom growth offset by telecom weakness. Companies that are more concentrated in datacom are likely to see more consistent momentum. The report's data suggests that this divergence will persist for at least the next 12 to 18 months, until the telecom ecosystem catches up.

The longer-term outlook for telecom and DCI remains positive. The fundamental drivers—increasing data traffic, cloud expansion, and the need for higher-speed interconnects—are intact. But the timing shift means that investors should not expect a synchronized recovery across all optical end markets. The datacom segment is the clear leader, and that leadership will likely widen before it narrows.

## Optical Circuit Switching Is a Sleeper Opportunity That Deserves More Attention

One of the most interesting sections of the report concerns optical circuit switching (OCS). The OCS market is forecast to grow from approximately $1.3 billion in 2026 to $4.3 billion by 2031, a CAGR of about 26%. That is a robust growth rate, but it is the use case that makes this segment strategically important.

Google is expected to remain a dominant user of OCS throughout the forecast period, leveraging 300-port and 128-port switches for connecting up to 9,000 TPUs and as a replacement for electrical spine switches. The key attributes driving adoption are high bandwidth, low latency, low power consumption, and the ability to reuse switches across multiple interconnect generations.

The "future-proofing" attribute is particularly important. In a world where AI cluster architectures are evolving rapidly, the ability to deploy a switch that can be repurposed across multiple generations of interconnect technology is a significant advantage. It reduces the risk of stranded assets and lowers the total cost of ownership over time.

For investors, OCS represents a thematic opportunity that is somewhat distinct from the transceiver and CPO markets. It is a networking architecture play, not a component play. The companies that benefit from OCS adoption may be different from those that benefit from transceiver or CPO growth. The report provides the data to identify this opportunity, but it does not fully explore the competitive dynamics or the potential for OCS to displace traditional electrical switching in large-scale AI clusters.

This is an area where readers should seek additional analysis. The OCS market is still relatively small, but its growth trajectory and strategic importance suggest it could become a meaningful part of the optical ecosystem by the end of the decade.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Optical Market

For investors and executives trying to make sense of this data, the report implies a clear decision framework. It is built around three questions:

First, what is your exposure to 1.6T and 800G? The near-term growth in the datacom market is concentrated at these data rates. Companies with strong positions in these segments are likely to see revenue acceleration. Companies that are more exposed to 400G or lower data rates face a different trajectory, with declining addressable markets over the forecast period.

Second, what is your CPO roadmap and timeline? The debate between the consensus forecast and company-level guidance creates a strategic fork. If you believe the consensus, the current transceiver leaders will continue to dominate through 2028. If you believe the company-level signals, CPO leaders could capture significant value earlier than expected. Your investment or strategic positioning should reflect your view on this timing question.

Third, how much of your revenue comes from telecom versus datacom? The divergence between these segments means that diversified suppliers face a mixed near-term picture. The report's data suggests that datacom-focused companies will outperform telecom-heavy companies over the next 12 to 18 months. If you are managing a portfolio or a business, this divergence should inform your allocation decisions.

These three questions are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive for the key strategic choices facing participants in the optical market. They do not provide easy answers, but they frame the debate in a way that allows for disciplined decision-making.

## The Report Raises Questions That Only Deeper Analysis Can Answer

The report provides a wealth of data, but it deliberately leaves several important questions open. The most significant is the timing of CPO adoption. The gap between the consensus forecast and company-level guidance is large enough to create materially different outcomes. Resolving this question requires a deeper understanding of hyperscaler architecture roadmaps, the readiness of the CPO supply chain, and the pace of standardization.

A second open question concerns the role of optical circuit switching in the broader market. The report provides a forecast but does not fully explore the competitive dynamics or the potential for OCS to disrupt traditional electrical switching. This is an area where additional research could yield significant insights.

A third question is about the NeoCloud capital spending visibility. The report notes that third-party analysts "continue to highlight their conservatism in the forecasts, particularly starting in 2027, with limited capital spending visibility from NeoClouds to support the growth trends in capex towards AI Infrastructure." This is a critical caveat. The entire optical market forecast depends on continued investment in AI infrastructure. If that investment slows, the growth trajectory could change materially.

These are not weaknesses of the report. They are the natural boundaries of any forecast. But they are also the questions that readers need to explore further to develop a complete investment or strategic thesis.

ZH_A: 最近有一份外资投行的研报，提出了一个很有意思的概念，叫“火箭与羽毛”。这到底是在说什么？

ZH_B: 这个比喻很形象。它描述的是能源价格冲击向核心通胀传导时的一种不对称现象。就像火箭升空，能源价格一涨，核心通胀就跟着快速上升；但价格下跌时，核心通胀却像羽毛一样，慢慢飘落，幅度小得多。

ZH_A: 所以这意味着，如果能源价格再涨一次，通胀压力会很大，但如果它跌了，我们可能也指望不上它来缓解通胀？

ZH_B: 完全正确。这正是这份报告的核心判断。它用欧元区1999年到2025年的数据做了统计，结果非常清楚：正向的能源和食品价格冲击，对核心通胀有显著且持续的传导效应；而负向的冲击，效果几乎可以忽略不计。

ZH_A: 那为什么会出现这种“火箭与羽毛”的现象呢？报告里有没有分析背后的原因？

ZH_B: 报告总结了三个主要机制。第一是企业的定价行为。当成本上升时，企业为了保利润、避免破产，会毫不犹豫地涨价；但当成本下降时，它们更倾向于保留利润空间，而不是降价，尤其是在有市场定价权的时候。

ZH_A: 这不就是“涨价容易降价难”吗？那第二点呢？

ZH_B: 第二是“菜单成本”。在正通胀环境下，频繁调价有成本，企业会吸收小的成本下降，而不是去调整价格标签。第三是工资和价格的向下刚性。能源涨价侵蚀购买力，工人会要求加薪；但成本下降时，企业很难去降低名义工资，所以节省的成本不会通过工资传导出去。

ZH_A: 这些理由听起来都挺合理。但报告有没有提供一些新的、更扎实的证据呢？

ZH_B: 当然有。报告除了用传统的总量数据，还做了两个重要的拓展。第一个是跨国面板分析，利用了欧元区不同国家能源冲击的差异，结果同样证实了这种“折线”关系：能源通胀对核心通胀的影响，只在能源价格为正时才显著。

ZH_A: 那第二个拓展呢？

ZH_B: 第二个是利用了欧盟委员会的企业调查数据，分析不同行业企业的定价预期。结果发现，能源密集型行业的企业，在能源价格上涨时，报告涨价预期的概率明显更高；但在能源价格下跌时，它们的降价预期却没有显著变化。这从微观层面再次印证了不对称性。

ZH_A: 那这份报告对未来的通胀和央行政策有什么具体的判断吗？

ZH_B: 报告认为，这种不对称性给核心通胀前景带来了上行风险。即使未来能源价格因为某些原因比如霍尔木兹海峡重新开放而下跌，对核心通胀的缓解作用可能也很有限。反过来，任何能源价格的进一步上涨，都会更猛烈地推高通胀。

ZH_A: 所以这对欧洲央行意味着什么？

ZH_B: 报告明确说，这会增强欧洲央行加息的理由。一方面，它降低了能源价格突然下跌能显著改善通胀前景的可能性；另一方面，也为应对通胀上行风险而进行“风险管理式加息”提供了支撑。报告因此上调了核心通胀预测，认为峰值会达到2.7%，并维持了6月和9月两次加息的基准预测。

ZH_A: 等等，你说“风险管理式加息”，这个概念很有意思。能再展开讲讲吗？

ZH_B: 这个在完整报告里有更详细的讨论。简单说，就是即使当前通胀看起来在回落，但考虑到未来风险不对称，央行可能宁愿先加息来防范风险，而不是等风险变成现实再行动。

ZH_A: 我明白了。那这份报告有没有留下一些没有完全解答的问题？比如，这种不对称性在未来会不会发生变化？

ZH_B: 这正是报告最值得深读的地方。比如，它提到2022到2023年的那次巨大能源冲击，对统计结果影响很大。如果排除那次冲击，样本回到2019年之前，这种不对称性就不那么明显了。这说明，这个“火箭与羽毛”规律，可能只在极端冲击下才会被激活。那未来如果能源价格只是温和波动，这个规律还成立吗？报告没有给出最终答案。

ZH_A: 这确实是个关键的悬念。看来要真正理解通胀和央行政策的走向，这份完整报告里的细节是必不可少的。如果想获取这份报告，或者加入我们的讨论社群，可以看看节目简介里的指引。感谢收听，我们下期再见。

ZH_B: 再见。

EN_A: So, I came across this really interesting research report from a global investment bank. It uses this vivid metaphor, "Rockets and Feathers." What is that all about?

EN_B: It's a great way to describe an asymmetric pattern in how energy price shocks pass through to core inflation. When energy prices go up, core inflation shoots up like a rocket. But when they fall, core inflation comes down like a feather—slowly and only a little.

EN_A: So, in plain English, this means if energy prices spike again, we get a big inflation problem. But if they drop, we can't really count on that to give us much relief?

EN_B: Exactly. That's the report's main takeaway. They ran the numbers on Euro area data from 1999 to 2025, and the finding is very clear. Positive energy and food price shocks have a big and persistent impact on core inflation. Negative shocks? The impact is tiny and statistically insignificant.

EN_A: That's a huge asymmetry. Why does this happen? Does the report give any reasons?

EN_B: They point to three main mechanisms. First, how firms protect their margins. When costs go up, companies raise prices aggressively to avoid bankruptcy. But when costs fall, they're more likely to keep prices high to expand their profit margins, especially if they have market power.

EN_A: So, "prices up like a rocket, but down like a feather." That makes sense. What's the second reason?

EN_B: Menu costs. In an environment of positive trend inflation, it's costly for firms to change prices. So they tend to absorb small cost decreases rather than go through the hassle of lowering prices. Third is downward wage and price stickiness. An energy spike erodes purchasing power, so workers demand higher wages. But when energy costs fall, firms find it very hard to cut nominal wages. So the savings don't get passed through.

EN_A: Those are all good theoretical reasons. But does the report have any fresh, hard evidence?

EN_B: Definitely. Beyond the simple top-down model, they did two really clever extensions. First, a cross-country panel model. By looking at how different Euro area countries experienced energy shocks differently, they confirmed the same "kinked" relationship. The pass-through is only significant for positive energy inflation.

EN_A: And the second extension?

EN_B: They used firm-level survey data from the European Commission. They looked at what sectors expect to do with their selling prices. The result is striking. Energy-intensive firms are much more likely to report plans to raise prices when energy goes up. But when energy falls, there's no significant change in their plans to lower prices. It confirms the asymmetry at the micro level.

EN_A: So, what does this all mean for the inflation outlook and for central bank policy?

EN_B: The report argues this asymmetry creates clear upside risks for core inflation. Even if energy prices fall—say, because of a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz—the relief for core inflation might be very limited. But any further energy price increases would push inflation up much more sharply.

EN_A: So for the ECB, this is a big deal.

EN_B: It is. The report says this strengthens the case for rate hikes. It reduces the chance that a sudden drop in energy could significantly improve the inflation outlook. And it makes a stronger argument for "risk management" hikes to guard against the upside risks. This analysis supports their recent upgrade to their core inflation forecast, which they now see peaking at 2.7 percent, and their baseline call for two ECB rate hikes in June and September.

EN_A: Wait, "risk management hikes." That's an interesting concept. Can you elaborate?

EN_B: The full report goes into more detail on that. The basic idea is that even if current inflation looks like it's coming down, because the risks are so asymmetric to the upside, a central bank might prefer to hike now to insure against a bad outcome, rather than wait and have to act more aggressively later.

EN_A: That makes sense. But does the report leave any big questions unanswered? For example, could this "rocket and feather" pattern change in the future?

EN_B: That's exactly the most interesting part. The report notes that their main finding is very sensitive to the inclusion of the massive 2022-2023 energy shock. If you cut the sample at 2019, the asymmetry is no longer statistically significant. So is this pattern only activated during extreme shocks? If energy prices just drift up and down modestly, does the rule still hold? The report doesn't fully answer that.

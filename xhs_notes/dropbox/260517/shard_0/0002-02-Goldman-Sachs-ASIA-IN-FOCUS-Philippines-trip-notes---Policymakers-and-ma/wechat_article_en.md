# The Philippines Is Facing a Stagflationary Energy Shock That Markets Are Not Fully Pricing In

The Philippines has become the most exposed economy in emerging Asia to the current energy supply shock, and the policy response is creating a more complex macro environment than many investors appreciate. A global investment bank report based on recent meetings in Manila reveals a country where inflation has already surged to 7.2 percent year-on-year, growth has collapsed to 2.8 percent, and the central bank is being forced into a tightening cycle that will further pressure an already fragile economy. The combination of a conservative fiscal stance, political uncertainty, and a currency that has been the worst performer in the region since the conflict began suggests that the risks are skewed toward further deterioration rather than recovery.

The critical insight that emerges from these meetings is that policymakers and market participants hold fundamentally different views on the growth outlook. Policymakers expect a second-half rebound driven by recovering investment and stronger external demand. Market participants are far more skeptical. The evidence from the ground supports the skeptics. Investment and construction had already softened before the energy shock, partly due to a corruption scandal in the flood-control sector. Anti-corruption measures in other economies have consistently shown that the recovery in investments takes time, as heightened scrutiny creates administrative bottlenecks that delay project implementation. The assumption that public spending will simply be back-loaded into the second half of the year ignores the structural friction that now exists in the approval and disbursement process.

This divergence between official optimism and on-the-ground reality is the central tension that investors need to navigate. The report makes clear that the path forward involves monetary tightening, a conservative fiscal approach that rules out broad stimulus, and a currency that will continue to underperform regional peers. Each of these factors reinforces the others, creating a feedback loop that makes the stagflationary environment more entrenched than headline numbers suggest.

## The Monetary Tightening Cycle Has Further to Run Than Markets Currently Expect

The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas has already acted early, hiking 25 basis points in April, but the report indicates that the tightening cycle is far from complete. Market participants expect at least two more hikes, with some projecting the policy rate could reach as high as 5.50 to 6.00 percent from the current 4.50 percent. The report itself forecasts two additional hikes, one in June and another in the third quarter, but acknowledges that risks are skewed toward more rather than fewer hikes.

The subtle shift in central bank communication is particularly telling. Following the April CPI print, the BSP issued its usual inflation statement. But after the inflation surprise proved larger than expected, a second statement was released just hours later with notably stronger language. The original statement committed to taking the "necessary actions" to return inflation to target "within a reasonable time." The revised statement dropped the temporal qualifier and committed to taking "all necessary monetary actions." This change has fueled market speculation about a possible off-cycle move, with the most likely window being the last two weeks of May.

The bond market has already priced in aggressive tightening. The two-year government yield is around 6 percent, which is 150 basis points above the 4.50 percent policy rate, indicating that sharp rate hike expectations are embedded in the front end of the curve. Even if not all the priced-in hikes fully materialize, there is scope for the curve to shift higher if inflation continues to print above expectations. The curve shape also matters. The 2/10 year spread has widened to around 160 basis points from approximately 95 basis points before the war. This spread is likely to bear flatten, as the front end remains more sensitive to higher inflation prints and shifts in rate hike expectations, while the long end is less vulnerable given that the government is not currently planning to increase debt financing.

## Fiscal Discipline Is Constraining the Policy Response and Will Amplify the Growth Slowdown

The fiscal approach emerging from Manila is remarkably conservative for an economy experiencing a sharp growth deceleration. Policymakers appear committed to the medium-term fiscal framework, viewing fiscal consolidation as the priority even as the economy struggles. The measures announced since the conflict began have been restrained, focused on targeted assistance for transport and agriculture rather than broad sUBSidies. Critically, these measures are budget neutral, funded through re-allocations from contingency provisions and a mandated 20 percent reduction in non-essential spending across ministries.

This fiscal conservatism reflects a deliberate strategic choice. The Philippines does not operate under hard fiscal rules, but policymakers are focused on the benefits of a credible fiscal posture, including lower borrowing costs. This becomes more important as the Philippines moves to a higher income classification and loses access to concessional lending from international financial institutions. The implicit trade-off is that short-term growth support is being sacrificed for long-term fiscal credibility.

The implications for investors are significant. Without fiscal stimulus to offset the monetary tightening and the negative terms-of-trade shock from higher energy prices, the burden of adjustment falls entirely on the private sector. Corporate sentiment and capex plans are already being affected by the slow recovery in public investment. The report notes that local market participants are more cautious than policymakers on the recovery in public investment in the second half of 2026. If fiscal spending remains tightly managed and the recovery in public investment disappoints, the growth outlook could deteriorate further than the consensus expects.

## Political Uncertainty Is a Structural Headwind That Markets Are Underestimating

The report was written against the backdrop of dramatic political developments that have introduced significant uncertainty about the medium-term policy outlook. The breakdown of the alliance between Ferdinand Marcos Jr. and Sara Duterte, the arrest of former President Rodrigo Duterte and his transfer to the International Criminal Court, the impeachment of Vice-President Sara Duterte, and the unsealing of an ICC arrest warrant against Senator Ronald dela Rosa all point to a political environment that is becoming increasingly unstable.

The immediate impact on investment is clear. The report notes that the Portfolio Strategy team downgraded the Philippines equity market to underweight in March, and local equities have declined both on the week and year-to-date. But the deeper question is what this political uncertainty means for the structural reform agenda and the country's ability to attract foreign direct investment over the medium term. The Philippines has been positioning itself as a beneficiary of supply chain diversification away from China. Political instability undermines that narrative.

The next presidential election is scheduled for May 2028, and presidents are not eligible for re-election. This creates a natural political cycle that introduces additional uncertainty about policy continuity. The current political turmoil will likely reinforce the cautious stance of both domestic and foreign investors, further delaying the recovery in investment that policymakers are counting on for the second half of the year.

## The Currency and Bond Markets Are Signaling More Pain Ahead

The Philippine peso has been the most underperforming currency in emerging Asia since the start of the conflict, followed by the Indian rupee and the Thai baht. This is consistent with the fundamental exposure of these economies to higher energy prices. The Philippines is a heavy net importer of energy and is not part of the high-end technology supply chain that has provided a buffer for currencies like the Korean won and the Taiwanese dollar.

The decline in foreign exchange reserves has been among the highest in the region as a percentage of GDP. Gross FX reserves fell from USD 113 billion at the end of February to USD 104 billion in April. This reserve drawdown reflects the combination of higher import costs and capital outflows, and it limits the central bank's ability to defend the currency through intervention.

The report's commodities team expects Brent crude oil to end the year at around USD 90 per barrel under their baseline scenario, which assumes flows through the Strait of Hormuz fully recover by the end of June. Even at this level, which is slightly above current forwards, the oil price remains adverse for the peso. In more adverse scenarios, oil prices could settle at higher levels by year-end. The combination of weak growth and a deteriorating current account underpins a bearish view on the peso, which is expected to continue underperforming regional peers.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Political Risk and Policy Credibility

The report provides a detailed analysis of the macro policy framework and the political situation, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most significant gap is the lack of a framework for understanding how the political uncertainty might interact with the credibility of the policy framework. The conservative fiscal approach is premised on the assumption that maintaining fiscal discipline will lower borrowing costs and support long-term growth. But if political instability leads to a sustained increase in risk premiums, the benefits of fiscal discipline may be eroded.

A second unanswered question concerns the potential for second-round inflation effects to be more persistent than the historical experience suggests. The report notes that policymakers are drawing on experience from the 2022 episode, where second-round effects peaked around two quarters after the initial shock. But the current environment is different. The energy shock is superimposed on an economy that was already slowing, with a currency that has depreciated more sharply than in 2022. The pass-through from fuel prices to broader inflation could be faster and more complete this cycle.

A third question relates to the implications of the GBI-EM Global Diversified Index inclusion scheduled for January 2027. The report notes that given current sentiment and the target index weight of just 1.78 percent, significant pre-positioning is not expected in the near term. But the inclusion itself could provide a catalyst for foreign inflows if the macro environment stabilizes. The timing of the inclusion relative to the political cycle and the monetary tightening cycle will determine whether this becomes a supportive factor or an opportunity for further outflows.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Philippine Macro Environment

For investors trying to position themselves in this environment, the report points to a framework based on three intersecting variables: the inflation trajectory, the growth trajectory, and the political timeline.

The inflation trajectory is the most immediately observable variable. The April CPI print of 7.2 percent year-on-year was a significant upside surprise, and the risk is that inflation continues to print above expectations through the third quarter as second-round effects materialize. If inflation remains elevated, the BSP will be forced to continue hiking, which will further pressure growth and the currency. The key data points to watch are the monthly CPI releases and the quarterly consumer inflation expectations survey.

The growth trajectory is more contested. The official view expects a second-half recovery driven by public investment and external demand. The market view is more skeptical. The resolution of this disagreement will depend on the pace of fiscal spending, the recovery in private investment, and the strength of the electronics export cycle. The report notes that the regional export upturn has been driven by high-end semiconductor demand, where spillovers to the Philippines may be less direct given its position in the back-end of the supply chain.

The political timeline is the most uncertain variable. The impeachment trial of Vice-President Sara Duterte will play out in the Senate over the coming months. The arrest warrant for Senator dela Rosa and the broader ICC investigation into the Duterte administration's war on drugs will continue to generate headlines. The next presidential election is not until May 2028, but the political maneuvering has already begun. The risk is that political uncertainty becomes a persistent headwind to investment rather than a temporary disruption.

The intersection of these three variables determines the outlook for the peso and the bond market. In the base case scenario where inflation moderates, growth stabilizes, and political uncertainty does not escalate further, the peso should stabilize and the bond curve should bear flatten as rate hike expectations are gradually priced in. In an adverse scenario where inflation remains elevated, growth disappoints, and political uncertainty intensifies, the peso could weaken further and the bond curve could shift higher across the board.

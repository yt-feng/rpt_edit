# Europe’s Economy Is Not Repeating 2022, But the Shock Is More Insidious

The Middle East conflict has delivered a measurable economic shock to Europe, but the nature of that shock differs fundamentally from the one triggered by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022. This is not a rerun of the energy crisis that sent European natural gas prices to 230 EUR/MWh and crushed industrial output. The current episode is more contained in its immediate energy price spike, yet it may prove more stubborn in its persistence and more damaging in its second-order effects on inflation expectations and policy credibility.

A global investment bank’s research report, published in mid-May, provides the most granular daily tracking of this dynamic available to institutional investors. The report leverages proprietary tools including a Financial Conditions Index, high-frequency consumer sentiment data, and a growth model that jointly considers energy prices, financial conditions, and foreign demand. The core finding is that the current shock is expected to subtract roughly 0.25 percentage points from euro area growth this year, while simultaneously adding nearly 2 percentage points to end-2026 inflation forecasts. This stagflationary tilt is the defining feature of the current environment, and it demands a strategic response from investors, corporate treasurers, and policymakers alike.

The data tells a story of asymmetry. Energy markets have reacted, but not with the panic of 2022. Consumer confidence has softened, but not collapsed. Financial conditions have tightened, but only modestly. Yet the inflation channel is already transmitting more aggressively than many expected. The report’s proprietary model shows that selling price expectations in the European Commission’s survey have surged by 1.5 standard deviations in manufacturing and 1.3 standard deviations in retail trade. This is not a repeat of the old playbook. It is a new, more subtle, and perhaps more dangerous phase of the cycle.

The critical question for decision-makers is not whether Europe will suffer a recession — the baseline forecasts do not show one — but whether the region is entering a period of structurally higher inflation persistence that will force central banks to maintain restrictive policy longer than markets currently price. The answer to that question will determine the trajectory of bond yields, currency spreads, and equity sector rotation for the remainder of this year and into 2026.

## The Energy Price Channel Is More Muted Than 2022, But the Oil Shock Is Expected to Last Longer

The most immediate comparison investors make is to the energy price explosion of early 2022. That comparison is illuminating, but it can also be misleading if applied uncritically. The report provides a direct visual comparison of forward curves for both natural gas and oil across the two episodes, and the differences are stark.

European natural gas forwards, as measured by the TTF benchmark, peaked at 230 EUR/MWh in March 2022. At the time of this report, the spot price was around 50 EUR/MWh, and the forward curve was backwardated, with contracts for delivery 25 months out trading near 28 EUR/MWh. The current shock has not produced anything close to the vertical spike in gas prices that paralyzed European industry two years ago. This reflects both a different supply-demand configuration and the significant progress Europe has made in diversifying its gas supply away from Russian pipeline flows.

For oil, the picture is reversed. In March 2022, oil forwards peaked around 125 USD/bbl and then declined sharply, with the curve moving into contango within months. The current shock shows oil forwards near 110 USD/bbl at the front end, but the curve is flatter and the backwardation less extreme. Critically, the report’s baseline forecast for oil remains above 90 USD/bbl for the next 12 months, and the forward curve does not fully converge to that level until 18-24 months out. This suggests that markets are pricing a more persistent, if less acute, oil premium.

The so-what is clear. For European corporates, the energy cost shock is not the existential threat it was in 2022, but the margin compression will be more prolonged. For central banks, the oil channel feeds directly into headline inflation and, through transportation and input costs, into core goods and services inflation. A slower-burning oil shock is harder to look through than a sharp spike that quickly reverses. The report’s data implies that the energy tailwind to disinflation that Europe enjoyed through most of 2023 and 2024 has now become a headwind, and that headwind is likely to persist.

## Consumer Confidence Has Stabilized, But Price Expectations Are Decoupling From Sentiment in a Dangerous Way

One of the most striking findings in the report is the divergence between consumer sentiment and inflation expectations. The report tracks two high-frequency consumer confidence indicators — the Morning Consult daily index and the European Commission’s monthly survey — and compares their evolution in the current episode to the 2022 baseline.

Consumer confidence has declined since the onset of the conflict, but the drop is roughly one-third to one-half the magnitude of the collapse seen in March 2022. The Morning Consult index, which had been trending upward through early 2026, has flattened rather than fallen off a cliff. This suggests that households are not panicking, and that the direct hit to disposable income from energy prices is more manageable this time.

However, the report’s data on inflation expectations tells a different story. The European Commission’s survey shows that selling price expectations in manufacturing, retail trade, and services have all risen sharply. The ECB’s Consumer Expectations Survey, released in late April, showed a 1.5 percentage point rise in median one-year-ahead inflation expectations to 4.0%. This is a critical data point. It means that households and firms are beginning to incorporate the current energy shock into their forward-looking pricing and wage-setting behavior.

The danger is that inflation expectations become unanchored from the ECB’s target. In 2022, the energy shock was so large and so clearly temporary that central banks could argue it would pass through to headline inflation without embedding itself in core prices. The current shock is smaller, but its persistence — especially on the oil side — makes it more likely to feed into wage negotiations and pricing decisions. The report’s data on the ECB’s wage tracker and the Corporate Telephone Survey both show wage growth expectations edging higher. This is the mechanism by which a supply shock becomes a self-fulfilling inflation cycle.

For investors, this means that the inflation risk premium in European bond markets may be underpriced. If the ECB is forced to maintain or even raise rates in response to rising inflation expectations, the current pricing of rate cuts in 2026 will need to be unwound. That would have significant implications for duration positioning and for the relative performance of European versus US fixed income.

## Financial Conditions Have Tightened Modestly, But the Transmission to Growth Is Already Visible

The report constructs a GS Financial Conditions Index for the euro area, which captures the combined effect of interest rates, equity prices, credit spreads, and exchange rates. Since the start of the conflict, this index has tightened by approximately 25 basis points. That is a modest move compared to the roughly 100 basis point tightening seen after the 2022 invasion, but it is not negligible.

The report’s growth model, which combines the FCI with energy prices and foreign demand, estimates that the current shock will subtract about 0.25 percentage points from euro area GDP growth this year. This is a relatively small drag, and it is consistent with the baseline view that Europe will avoid a recession. But the model also shows that the drag is concentrated in the first half of the year, and that the growth trajectory for 2026 has been downgraded by a more significant 0.8 percentage points.

This downgrade is not driven solely by energy. The report notes that pre-war data in the UK surprised to the upside, suggesting that some of the weakness is specific to the euro area’s industrial structure and its exposure to trade disruptions through the Suez Canal and Red Sea. The combination of higher energy costs, tighter financial conditions, and supply chain frictions is producing a growth headwind that will persist even if energy prices stabilize.

The implication for corporate strategy is that the current environment favors sectors with pricing power and low energy intensity, and penalizes those with high fixed costs and exposure to global trade. The report does not name specific sectors, but the pattern is clear: the manufacturing PMIs have softened while services have held up relatively better, and within manufacturing, the divergence between output prices and input costs is widening. Companies that can pass through cost increases will protect margins; those that cannot will face a prolonged squeeze.

## The Report Raises an Unresolved Question About the Persistence of the Inflation Shock

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves one critical question unanswered: how much of the current inflation impulse is temporary, and how much reflects a structural shift in the European economy’s inflation dynamics? The report provides the data to frame this question but does not resolve it.

On the one hand, the energy price shock is clearly exogenous and may fade as supply adjusts. The report’s own forward curves show oil and gas prices declining over the next 12-24 months. On the other hand, the rise in selling price expectations and wage growth indicators suggests that the shock is propagating through the economy in ways that may not fully reverse. The ECB’s Consumer Expectations Survey showing a 4.0% one-year-ahead inflation expectation is particularly concerning, because it implies that households are beginning to doubt the central bank’s ability to deliver on its 2% target.

The unresolved tension is between the model-based forecast and the survey-based evidence. The model, which is backward-looking and based on historical relationships, suggests a manageable inflation overshoot. The surveys, which capture forward-looking behavior, suggest a more entrenched inflation psychology. Which one will prove correct depends on whether the energy shock persists long enough to become embedded in wage contracts and pricing strategies.

This is the analytical frontier that the report opens but does not close. For readers who want to make their own assessment, the key data to watch are the ECB’s wage tracker, the May and June consumer confidence surveys, and the flash PMI prints. If selling price expectations continue to rise while consumer confidence stabilizes, the stagflationary narrative will strengthen. If both decline, the disinflation trend may resume.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Current Environment

For investors and corporate strategists, the report’s data can be synthesized into a three-part decision framework that helps clarify the key trade-offs.

First, assess the energy exposure of your portfolio or business. The current shock is asymmetric across energy types. Natural gas is less of a concern than in 2022, but oil is more persistent. Companies and sectors with high direct or indirect oil exposure — transportation, chemicals, agriculture, and any business with significant logistics costs — face a margin headwind that will last into 2026. Those with high natural gas exposure, such as European industrial manufacturers, face a smaller but still meaningful risk.

Second, monitor the inflation expectations channel rather than headline CPI. The report’s most valuable contribution is its focus on survey-based inflation expectations and selling price plans. These are leading indicators of actual inflation, and they are currently moving in the wrong direction. If the May data releases show a further increase in price expectations, the market will need to reprice the ECB’s policy path. That would be a signal to reduce duration and increase exposure to inflation-linked assets.

Third, distinguish between growth and inflation risks when positioning. The baseline is a mild growth slowdown with persistent inflation. This favors sectors that benefit from pricing power and nominal GDP growth — such as financials, energy, and select services — over those that depend on volume growth and low input costs. It also favors a barbell approach in fixed income, with short-duration instruments to hedge inflation risk and long-duration instruments only if growth deteriorates sharply enough to force the ECB to cut rates.

The report’s framework suggests that the biggest risk is not a recession but a policy error. If the ECB keeps rates too high for too long because it fears unanchored inflation expectations, the growth slowdown could deepen. If it cuts rates too early because it believes the inflation shock is temporary, it could lose credibility and allow expectations to drift higher. The data in this report points to the former risk as more likely, but the balance is fine.

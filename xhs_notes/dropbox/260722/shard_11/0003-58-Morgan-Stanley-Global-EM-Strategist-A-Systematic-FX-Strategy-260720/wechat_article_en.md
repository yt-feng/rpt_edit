## Systematic Signals Add the Most Value as a Guard Against Confirmation Bias, Not as a Standalone Trading System

The most common mistake a discretionary manager can make with a systematic strategy is to treat it as a black box that must be followed or ignored in its entirety. That is a false choice. The real value of a systematic signal is that it provides an independent reference point that can be consulted selectively. A manager who has been long a particular currency for months and is inclined to keep the position should pay attention when the systematic strategy suddenly starts sending the opposite signal. That is the moment to re-examine the thesis and check for blind spots.

This is not cherry-picking in the pejorative sense. A discretionary manager has never promised to follow a systematic model. The manager's job is to synthesize multiple inputs—positioning data, central bank commentary, macro data, and now systematic signals—into a coherent view. Using a systematic signal selectively is normal discretionary practice. The key is to use it systematically in the decision process, not to follow it systematically in the trading process.

There are three specific use cases where systematic signals add the most value. The first is guarding against confirmation bias. Any discretionary process is vulnerable to the tendency to favor information that supports an existing view and discount information that contradicts it. A systematic signal that has no emotional attachment to the position can serve as a useful check. The second is timing and sizing trades. Validation from a systematic strategy can increase conviction and provide the signal needed to enter or exit a trade, or to adjust its size within the overall portfolio. The third is filtering a market view. Multi-factor systematic strategies are agnostic to the market regime, but a discretionary manager can take a view on whether markets are trending or range-bound and tune into the factor that is likely to dominate. In a range-trading environment, momentum signals can be filtered out. In a trending environment, carry signals may be less relevant.

## A Decision Framework for Integrating Systematic Signals into a Discretionary Process

For a macro portfolio manager who wants to operationalize this approach, a simple three-step framework can replace ad hoc judgment with a repeatable process. The first step is to identify divergence. Compare the systematic strategy's current positioning with the manager's own portfolio. Where are the largest differences? Those are the positions that require a written justification. The manager must articulate why the discretionary view overrides the systematic signal. If the justification is weak or relies on an unverifiable assumption, the position should be reduced.

The second step is to assess signal extremity. Not all systematic signals are equal. A position that is at a one-year z-score of 2.5 is more informative than one at 0.5. The manager should focus on the extreme signals, because they are the ones most likely to revert or to indicate a regime change. The z-score transformation provided by the MS team is a practical tool for this assessment.

The third step is to integrate the systematic signal into position sizing. A manager who is considering a new trade should check whether the systematic strategy supports it. If the strategy is also long that currency, the position can be sized more aggressively. If the strategy is short, the position should be sized more conservatively or avoided entirely. This does not mean the manager must agree with the systematic strategy. It means the manager must have a clear reason for disagreeing.

The framework is not a trading system. It is a discipline. And discipline is exactly what discretionary processes most often lack.

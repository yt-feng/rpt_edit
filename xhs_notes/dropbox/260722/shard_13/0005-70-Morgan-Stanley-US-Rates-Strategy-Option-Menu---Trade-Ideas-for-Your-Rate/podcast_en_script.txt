EN_A: Welcome back to the podcast. Today we are digging into a global investment bank report on USD rates options. It is called Option Menu: Trade Ideas for Your Rates View. B, what is the big takeaway here?
EN_B: The big takeaway is that the current pricing in the USD rates option market has some clear dislocations. Investors have a view on where rates are going, but they often end up taking risks they do not want or paying for outcomes they do not expect. So the report scans the option surface for expiries under one year and picks the best expressions for four key views: higher rates, lower rates, steeper curves, and flatter curves.
EN_A: Four views. That sounds like a menu. But what is the core question this report is really trying to answer?
EN_B: It is not just giving trade ideas. The real question is: given where volatility is priced today, how can you use options to express your rates view more efficiently? Each trade is designed to balance cost and unwanted risk.
EN_A: Let us start with higher rates. The report recommends a three-month 30-year payer spread. Why that one?
EN_B: It exploits two anomalies. First, at-the-money volatility is low, so entry cost is cheap. Second, high strike payer skew is expensive because there is persistent demand for tail hedges. By using a spread, you sell that expensive skew and lower your net cost. And the three-month expiry captures three CPI prints, three payroll reports, and one FOMC meeting. That is a lot of catalysts.
EN_A: So it is a cheap bet that long end yields break higher. What is the downside?
EN_B: The main risk is that yields do not sell off in three months, for example if inflation data softens and eases long-term inflation concerns. But the report does not fully spell out one thing: if yields do break higher, the maximum payout is roughly three times the premium paid.
EN_A: For lower rates, the report picks a six-month five-year low strike receiver. Why that one?
EN_B: The front end is pricing in 35 basis points of additional Fed hikes by end of 2026. The report argues that for rates to rally, you need data to challenge that pricing. Six months gives enough time for softer inflation or weaker jobs data to emerge. Also, five-year volatility is the flattest across all tenors, so extending to six months costs very little extra.
EN_A: So it is betting the market is too hawkish on the Fed. What is the catch?
EN_B: The catch is that if data stays resilient and the Fed actually delivers one or more hikes, you lose the entire premium. The report does not fully explore how to adjust if that scenario plays out. That is in the full report.
EN_A: Now the curve trades. There is a three-month 5s30s curve cap for steepening and a one-month 2s5s bear flattener for flattening. Those seem to point in opposite directions.
EN_B: They target different tenors and different drivers. The steepener bets on front end repricing during a Fed easing cycle and a potential breakout in the long end. The flattener is all about geopolitical risk over the next month, specifically Iran. If energy supply is disrupted, inflation expectations rise, and the Fed could turn more hawkish. That would flatten the 2s5s curve.
EN_A: And the bear flattener is a zero premium structure. Sounds too good to be true.
EN_B: Zero premium does not mean zero risk. If the Fed hikes in July and signals a more hawkish path, the curve could actually bear steepen, and that trade would lose money. The report does not fully detail how much you could lose in that scenario. That is in the full report.
EN_A: So the report is like a menu, but each dish comes with its own risks and assumptions.
EN_B: Exactly. And there is one more thing the report hints at but does not fully explore. It shows that investors have resumed selling volatility. Dealer gamma exposure is only about 50 percent of the maximum level over the past year. That means if rates move sharply, the market could be very fragile. But the report does not explain how that gamma dynamic affects these trades in practice.

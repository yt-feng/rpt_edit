# Orbital Compute Is Not a Physics Problem;It Is a Scale Problem

The most persistent objection to space-based AI infrastructure has been thermal management. A vacuum, the argument goes, cannot conduct heat away from dense computing hardware, so orbital data centers will inevitably overheat and fail. This objection is wrong. The physics of radiative cooling is well understood, experimentally validated, and already demonstrated by decades of spacecraft operations, including nuclear reactors launched into orbit as early as 1965. The real question is not whether heat can be rejected in space, but whether the economic, logistical, and reliability challenges of building megawatt-scale orbital compute can be solved faster and more cheaply than terrestrial alternatives. For SpaceX, the answer appears to be yes, and the implications for the AI infrastructure landscape are profound.

A global research report published in July 2026 provides the most detailed engineering and financial analysis yet of SpaceX's orbital compute ambitions. The report models a trajectory in which SpaceX reaches gigawatt-scale orbital compute by 2030, with orbital capacity surpassing terrestrial deployments by 2032 and reaching 364 GW by 2040. Critically, the analysis shows that orbital compute becomes cheaper than current industry terrestrial costs on an all-in operating basis by 2031, driven by the near-elimination of power, cooling, land, and maintenance expenses that dominate terrestrial data center economics. This is not a speculative long-shot. It is a plausible path built on known physics, existing satellite engineering, and a launch vehicle that already exists.

The debate over orbital compute has been framed around the wrong constraint. Thermal management is solvable. The binding constraints are cost, reliability, scalability, and time-to-power.

## The Stefan-Boltzmann Law Makes Orbital Compute Thermally Feasible, and Higher Operating Temperatures Dramatically Reduce Radiator Mass

The governing physics is the Stefan-Boltzmann law, which states that the power radiated by a surface is proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature. For a spacecraft radiator, this means that a modest increase in operating temperature yields a disproportionate reduction in required radiator area. The report calculates that running an AI chip at 20% higher temperature requires 50% less radiator mass. This is not a theoretical curiosity; it is an engineering lever that SpaceX intends to exploit aggressively.

SpaceX is targeting an eventual operating temperature of 370 Kelvin (97 degrees Celsius) for its AI satellites, compared with approximately 360 Kelvin (87 degrees Celsius) for the initial generation expected to launch in 2028. For context, terrestrial GPUs typically operate below 85 degrees Celsius. Running chips hotter in space is possible because the failure mechanisms are different when there is no humidity, no corrosion, and no mechanical vibration from cooling fans. The trade-off is that higher temperatures reduce transistor longevity, but in an orbital context where hardware is replaced every five to seven years anyway, the optimization shifts toward minimizing radiator mass rather than maximizing chip lifetime.

The report provides a concrete example: a 1-kilowatt computer operating at 90 degrees Celsius in space requires a radiator panel of approximately 0.57 square meters, assuming an emissivity of 0.90 from white thermal control paint and two radiating sides. Scaling this to SpaceX's Gen 1 AI satellite, which is expected to achieve 150 kilowatts of peak power and 120 kilowatts of compute, implies a radiator size of 110 square meters per side, roughly half the area of a standard singles tennis court. That is large, but it is not prohibitive, especially when radiator mass already accounts for more than one-third of the total satellite mass.

The environmental heat load in low Earth orbit is also manageable. Deep space is 2.7 Kelvin, but Earth's infrared radiation and reflected sunlight raise the effective sink temperature seen by a radiator in LEO. NASA guidance suggests an equivalent sink temperature between 200 and 300 Kelvin. The report assumes 250 Kelvin, or minus 23 degrees Celsius. That is still far colder than the radiator surface, ensuring a substantial temperature differential and efficient heat rejection. The physics works.

## The Cost Advantage of Orbital Compute Emerges from Eliminating Terrestrial Operating Expenses, Not from Cheaper Hardware

The most common intuition about orbital compute is that it must be more expensive because launching hardware into space is costly. That intuition is correct for capital expenditure but misleading for total cost of ownership. The report estimates that SpaceX's orbital compute capex per watt will be significantly higher than terrestrial in the early years, falling to within 50% more than terrestrial by 2031 and reaching parity around 2035. But capex is only half the story.

The critical insight is that orbital compute has essentially no recurring power, cooling, land, staffing, or maintenance costs. Terrestrial data centers consume enormous amounts of electricity, require water-intensive cooling systems, occupy valuable real estate, and demand ongoing human oversight. A satellite in orbit has no electricity bill, no water bill, no property tax, and no night shift. The vast majority of its ongoing expense is depreciation on upfront capital. When the report calculates all-in operating costs, orbital compute becomes cheaper than current industry terrestrial costs in 2031, at approximately $6.50 per watt per year versus an industry average of $6.80 per watt per year for Blackwell-class hardware.

The report's model shows overall cash costs per watt declining from roughly $35 to $40 today to less than $9 by 2035 and $3.70 by 2040. This trajectory is driven by falling launch costs from Starship reusability, cheaper satellite manufacturing at scale, and GPU costs that converge toward terrestrial parity. Electricity costs for SpaceX's AI compute, which are only relevant for its terrestrial deployments, decline from approximately 10% of revenue in 2028 to 1% by 2040, reflecting the growing share of orbital capacity.

But cost alone is not the full argument. The report notes that SpaceX's two recent neocloud deals commanded a meaningful premium to industry averages, likely reflecting the scarcity value of large-scale compute that can be deployed quickly. In AI infrastructure, time-to-power is as important as cost-per-watt. Terrestrial data centers face grid interconnection queues that can stretch years, permitting risk, equipment availability constraints, and local opposition. Orbital compute, by contrast, can be deployed in parallel batches, subject only to launch cadence and satellite manufacturing throughput. Customers may pay a premium for near-term access, even if orbital compute is not yet cheaper on a pure cost basis.

## Orbital Compute Reaches Gigawatt Scale by 2030 and Becomes the Majority of Deployed Capacity by 2032, Driven by Starship Launch Cadence and Satellite Manufacturing Learning Curves

The report's deployment model is aggressive but grounded in specific engineering assumptions. It assumes first orbital compute deployments in 2028 at 160 megawatts, rising to approximately 2.7 gigawatts in 2030, 21.2 gigawatts in 2032, 111 gigawatts in 2035, and 364 gigawatts in 2040. Orbital capacity becomes the majority of total deployed compute in 2032, reaching 58% of the total, and grows to 96% by 2040. The long-term vision is that terrestrial compute primarily supports model training, while most inference moves to orbit.

This trajectory depends on three interdependent factors: Starship payload capacity, satellite compute density, and manufacturing throughput. The report models three generations of orbital compute satellites, aligned with estimated variants of Starship. The Gen 1 satellite, based on design parameters shared by SpaceX, achieves 70 kilowatts per ton of total satellite mass, or 56 kilowatts per ton for compute only, with a peak power of 150 kilowatts and a mass of approximately 2.1 tons. Future generations are expected to be both larger and more compute-dense, eventually exceeding the company's target of 100 kilowatts of compute per ton.

The satellite design is divided into three major subsystems: the thermal radiator, the solar array, and the compute payload. Each subsystem has known engineering solutions derived from existing satellite technology, particularly Starlink. The radiator uses fluid loops with ammonia, similar to the International Space Station, pumped out to large radiator panels that glow in infrared. The solar array must generate enough power to sustain compute loads, which at 150 kilowatts per satellite is far beyond typical communications satellite power levels but within the capability of large deployable arrays. The compute payload is the most novel element, requiring chips designed to operate at higher temperatures and in a radiation environment beyond Earth's protective magnetosphere.

The report acknowledges that orbital compute still faces significant challenges beyond thermal management: radiation damage to electronics, the physical security of assets in an ungoverned domain, the lack of on-orbit maintenance requiring extensive built-in redundancy, a launch cadence that initially yields only single-digit megawatts per flight, reliance on the same capacity-constrained chip supply chains as terrestrial AI, and a growing orbital debris hazard. None of these are showstoppers individually, but together they create a risk profile that observers must weigh against the potential rewards.

## The Decision Framework for Observers: Evaluate Orbital Compute on Time-to-Power, Cost Trajectory, and Premium Pricing Power, Not on Physics Objections

For those evaluating SpaceX as an AI infrastructure play, the analysis suggests a clear decision framework. The first filter is whether the physics works. It does. Radiative cooling is a solved problem, and the engineering parameters are consistent with decades of spacecraft experience. The second filter is whether the economics work. They appear to, but only on a multiyear trajectory that depends on Starship achieving its intended cost and cadence targets. The third filter is whether orbital compute offers a differentiated value proposition that terrestrial infrastructure cannot match. It does, primarily through time-to-power and scalability.

The report's cost model implies that orbital compute achieves cost parity with terrestrial on an all-in basis by 2031, but that is not the only relevant comparison. Even if orbital compute remains more expensive than terrestrial for several years, SpaceX may still be able to charge a premium because it can deliver large-scale compute capacity faster than any terrestrial alternative. This is analogous to SpaceX's launch business, where prices have continued to rise despite steadily declining underlying costs, reflecting the scarcity value of reliable, frequent access to orbit.

The key debate, as the report frames it, is whether SpaceX can use Starship, satellite manufacturing, space-based solar power, and optical networking to become one of the most scalable sources of AI compute in the 2030s. The thermal management question is a distraction. The real questions are about execution: Can SpaceX manufacture thousands of AI satellites at the required quality and cost? Can Starship achieve the launch cadence needed to deploy gigawatts of compute annually? Can the company solve radiation hardening and on-orbit reliability without driving costs too high? Can it secure enough chip supply to build its satellites?

These are hard questions, but they are the right ones. The physics is not the bottleneck.

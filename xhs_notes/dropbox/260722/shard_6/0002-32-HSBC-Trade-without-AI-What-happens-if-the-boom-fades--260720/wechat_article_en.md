# AI Is Not Just Driving Trade Growth—It Has Become the Trade Cycle

Global goods trade has been remarkably resilient over the past 18 months. Volatile US tariff policy, Middle East shipping disruptions, and broader geopolitical fragmentation should have produced a much weaker picture. Instead, trade has held up. The reason is not diffuse economic strength across sectors. It is one concentrated force: the artificial intelligence boom.

Consider the magnitude. In 2025, AI-related goods drove more than 40 percent of global merchandise trade growth, according to WTO data, despite representing only about one-sixth of all traded goods. So far this year, that share has doubled. AI-related goods now account for roughly 80 percent of global export growth in nominal terms. The non-AI half of the global trade economy has largely flat-lined since 2024.

This concentration creates a strategic vulnerability that few market participants have fully internalized. The trade cycle and the AI investment cycle are no longer correlated; they have become nearly synonymous. If the AI cycle weakens, trade growth weakens with it—not in some peripheral subsector, but at the core of the expansion. Understanding the mechanics of this linkage, the exposure asymmetries across economies, and the plausible scenarios for deceleration is no longer an academic exercise. It is the central question for anyone positioning around global trade exposure.

## Asia's Export Profile Has Become an AI Leverage Play, Not a Diversified Growth Story

The most striking feature of the current trade regime is how deeply it has rewired Asia's export structure. AI-enabling goods now account for nearly 20 percent of global goods trade, up from 14 percent on average in 2024. But that global average masks extreme regional concentration.

Asia dominates AI-related exports. Mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong together represented 44 percent of total global AI-related exports in the first quarter of 2026 in value terms. Taiwan is the most extreme case: approximately 80 percent of its total exports are now tied to the AI value chain. South Korea is not far behind. For these economies, the AI cycle is not a tailwind to an otherwise diversified export base. It is the entire story.

The implications for risk assessment are straightforward but often underapplied. A diversified exporter can absorb sector-specific shocks. Taiwan and Korea cannot. Their export growth trajectories are effectively derivatives of hyperscaler capital expenditure decisions made in California. This is not a temporary cyclical correlation. It reflects a structural transformation in which semiconductor fabrication, advanced packaging, and high-bandwidth memory have become the dominant export categories, and those categories have no meaningful demand driver outside AI infrastructure buildout.

For investors and policymakers evaluating Asian trade exposure, the relevant question is no longer "How is domestic demand in the region?" or "What is the tariff outlook for consumer goods?" The question is: "What is the trajectory of AI capex among the top six hyperscalers?" Everything else is second-order.

## Non-AI Trade Has Flat-Lined, Exposing the Fragility of the Broader Recovery Narrative

It is easy to look at aggregate trade numbers and conclude that global commerce is healthy. That conclusion is misleading. The headline growth is almost entirely a function of AI-related goods, and within that category, a significant portion is price-driven rather than volume-driven.

Consider the US import data. AI-enabling goods now account for 27 percent of total US imports, nearly double the share from the beginning of 2025. But the value of US AI imports was up 60 percent year-on-year in the first five months of this year, while volumes were up just 8 percent. Soaring component prices are inflating the trade numbers. Strip out the price effect and the underlying volume growth in AI trade is modest.

The non-AI picture is different. Export growth for goods outside the AI category has largely flat-lined since 2024. This is not a temporary soft patch. It reflects a structural stagnation in traditional trade drivers: consumer goods, industrial machinery, automotive products, and raw materials ex-AI inputs. Tariff uncertainty has suppressed investment and inventory building. Middle East shipping disruptions have raised costs and reduced reliability. But the deeper issue is that there is no organic demand engine outside AI to drive trade expansion.

The strategic implication is noteworthy. The current trade expansion rests on a single pillar, and that pillar is partially inflated by price effects that could reverse. If AI component prices normalize—which is likely as production capacity expands and competition intensifies—the nominal trade growth numbers will compress even if volumes remain steady. The risk of a double effect, from both price normalization and volume deceleration, is real.

## A Measurable Scenario Exists for How AI Deceleration Could Shave Global Growth

The report provides a concrete stress test. If Taiwan's and South Korea's export growth forecasts were halved this year and next, global export growth would decline by 0.2 to 0.3 percentage points annually. That may sound modest, but it is a partial scenario. It only considers two economies, albeit highly exposed ones. The full impact would be larger if mainland China's export engine slowed, given the economy's weight in global trade.

The mechanism is straightforward. Taiwan and Korea are the upstream suppliers of the AI hardware stack. If hyperscaler capex growth decelerates—as it is expected to, from breakneck rates to roughly 25 percent year-on-year by 2027—demand for semiconductors, memory, and advanced substrates softens. That directly reduces export volumes from the two economies most leveraged to AI hardware. The 0.2 to 0.3 percentage point estimate is a lower bound, not an upper bound.

The Bank for International Settlements has drawn the historical parallel explicitly. In its June 2026 Annual Economic Report, the BIS noted that previous investment booms—canal mania in the 1830s, the dotcom boom of the late 1990s—all ended with an eventual reversal in investment and induced economy-wide recessions. The scale and pace of current tech investments make the AI boom a candidate for a similar trajectory. The BIS is not forecasting a downturn. It is warning that the historical pattern is consistent enough to warrant serious attention.

For anyone building trade exposure forecasts, the prudent approach is to run multiple scenarios that vary the AI capex assumption, not just the tariff or GDP growth assumption. The sensitivity of trade growth to AI capex is now the dominant variable.

## Hyperscaler Capex Provides a Bridge, But the Deceleration Path Is Already Visible

The good news is that the bridge is long. HSBC technology analysts forecast that capex of the top six hyperscalers—AWS, Google, Oracle, CoreWeave, Microsoft, and Meta—could reach over USD 1 trillion by 2027. Even as the pace of growth decelerates to around 25 percent year-on-year next year, the absolute level of spending remains enormous.

This provides a multiyear demand floor for AI-related components. Data centre construction, semiconductor procurement, and networking infrastructure investment will continue at elevated levels. The boom is not ending tomorrow. But the rate of change is decelerating, and markets often price deceleration as a negative catalyst even when absolute levels remain high.

The tariff environment for AI goods remains benign. Simple average most-favored-nation tariff rates on AI-enabling products range from less than 2 percent for high-income OECD economies to roughly 9 percent for least developed economies. Non-tariff measures are rising, but the direct cost burden remains low. This is not a source of disruption in the near term.

The strategic question is whether the deceleration in capex growth is already priced into trade expectations. If markets are still extrapolating current growth rates, the downside surprise from normalization could be significant. If markets have already discounted a capex slowdown, then the trade impact may be manageable.

## A Decision Framework for Assessing Trade Exposure in an AI-Dominated Regime

The following framework translates the report's analysis into actionable questions for anyone managing trade-related exposure, whether as an investor, corporate treasurer, or supply chain strategist.

**Step one: Identify your exposure to AI-related trade flows.** For portfolio managers, this means calculating the share of revenue or earnings in your holdings that depends on AI hardware demand, directly or indirectly. For corporate treasurers, it means mapping your supply chain's reliance on AI-enabling components, particularly semiconductors and memory from Taiwan and Korea. If the exposure is above 20 percent, you are running a concentrated bet on hyperscaler capex.

**Step two: Stress-test your base case against a capex deceleration scenario.** Do not assume that current AI trade growth rates persist. Model a scenario in which hyperscaler capex growth slows to 15 percent year-on-year, not 25 percent. Then model a scenario in which it slows to single digits. Calculate the implied impact on your exposure. If the downside is unacceptable, you need a hedge or a diversification strategy.

**Step three: Distinguish between price-driven and volume-driven exposure.** If your exposure is concentrated in components where prices are soaring, you face a double risk: volume normalization and price normalization. The US import data shows that AI trade value growth is far outstripping volume growth. That gap will close. The question is whether it closes through higher volumes or lower prices.

**Step four: Monitor non-AI trade as a separate indicator.** Do not rely on aggregate trade data to assess global demand. The non-AI component has flat-lined. If you are exposed to traditional trade sectors, your risk is not correlated with the AI cycle. It is correlated with tariff policy, consumer demand, and industrial production—all of which remain subdued.

**Step five: Reassess geographic concentration risk.** Taiwan and Korea are not just AI-exposed; they are AI-dependent. Any disruption to the AI cycle will hit these economies disproportionately. If your portfolio or supply chain has heavy exposure to these markets, you are effectively short a put option on hyperscaler capex. That is a risk worth quantifying explicitly.

This framework is not a prediction. It is a diagnostic. The report's contribution is to show that the AI trade linkage is not a marginal factor to be acknowledged in a footnote. It is the governing variable of the current trade cycle. Acting on that insight means treating AI capex not as a sector-specific data point but as the macroeconomic variable it has become.

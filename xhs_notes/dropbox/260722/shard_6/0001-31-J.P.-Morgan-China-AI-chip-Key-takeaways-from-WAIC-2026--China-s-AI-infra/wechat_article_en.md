The strategic question for observers and technology leaders is no longer which chipmaker can close the gap with Nvidia, but which parts of the supply chain are positioned to benefit from the transition to a token-driven, system-centric infrastructure model.

This shift matters now because it changes the logic for the entire indigenous AI supply chain. The old framework, which rewarded companies that could demonstrate incremental improvements in single-chip floating-point operations or inference latency, is being replaced by a framework that rewards integration, interconnection, and the ability to deliver affordable compute at scale. Training remains the next scalable market, but it is not yet the one generating revenue.

The implication is clear: the companies that will win are not necessarily those with the best single-chip specifications, but those that can deliver effective system-level performance, manage heterogeneous scheduling across different chip types, and reduce the total cost of producing a token. ## Super-Node Architectures Have Become the Industry’s Answer to Process-Node Constraints and Trillion-Parameter Models

The most significant architectural development at WAIC 2026 was the emergence of super-node solutions as a deliberate strategy to address two structural constraints simultaneously. The first constraint is the ceiling on domestic process nodes, which limits the absolute performance of any individual chip. The second is the arrival of trillion-parameter large language models, such as Kimi K3’s 2.8-trillion-parameter architecture, which cannot be served by any single chip, regardless of its performance. Super-nodes solve both problems by creating unified compute domains that span dozens to thousands of cards, using different approaches to chip-to-chip interconnection, switch and fabric design, and software orchestration.

This is not a marginal improvement. It represents a fundamental rethinking of what a compute platform is. Instead of trying to replicate the performance of a single advanced chip through brute-force engineering, the industry is now designing systems that aggregate many less-capable chips into a coherent whole. The effectiveness of this approach depends entirely on the quality of the interconnection fabric, the efficiency of the cooling system, and the sophistication of the software layer that manages data movement and workload distribution. Liquid cooling, optical interconnects, and orthogonal cabinet architectures are likely to become minimum requirements rather than differentiators for next-generation deployments. Any vendor that cannot provide these capabilities will be structurally disadvantaged, regardless of the performance of its individual chips.

The strategic implication for observers is that the value in the supply chain is shifting away from pure chip design and toward the components and services that enable system-level integration. This includes advanced packaging, testing, wafer fabrication equipment, and interconnection technology. The report identifies back-end service providers such as JCET as beneficiaries of increasing demand for advanced packaging and testing, and wafer fabrication equipment companies such as NAURA and AMEC as beneficiaries of strong capital expenditure for memory and advanced logic fabs. These are not peripheral plays. They are central to the super-node thesis.

## Agentic AI Is Reshaping Infrastructure Demand by Creating an Exponential Token-Consumption Curve

The most underappreciated dynamic in China’s AI infrastructure build-out is the role of agentic AI. Agents functioning as digital proxies consume tokens at rates several orders of magnitude higher than human users. A human user might generate a few hundred tokens in a single query. An autonomous agent, operating continuously across multiple tasks, can consume millions of tokens per day. This creates an exponential demand curve that fundamentally changes the economics of AI infrastructure.

The report notes that the promotion of the token factory concept in China represents a deliberate industrialization strategy aimed at making AI tokens affordable and ubiquitous across numerous real-world applications. This is not a marketing slogan. It is a recognition that the unit economics of AI compute must improve dramatically to support the scale of usage that agentic AI implies. If tokens remain expensive, the agent economy cannot scale. If they become cheap enough, the demand curve could steepen faster than supply can respond.

This dynamic has direct implications for infrastructure deployment. Capacity build-out alone is not enough. The industry must also solve for cost reduction, quality measurement, and heterogeneous scheduling across different chip types. The report acknowledges that the ecosystem remains immature in critical dimensions such as industry standards for heterogeneous scheduling, token quality measurement, and the optimal super-node scale across different verticals. These are not minor technical issues. They are structural bottlenecks that will determine how quickly the token economy can scale. Companies that can help solve these bottlenecks, whether through software, interconnection hardware, or packaging technology, will capture disproportionate value.

## A Decision Framework for Observers: Three Questions to Determine Which Parts of the Supply Chain Will Benefit Most

The transition from chip-centric to system-centric infrastructure creates both opportunities and risks. To navigate this transition, observers should evaluate companies based on three criteria.

First, does the company benefit from the shift toward disaggregated architectures and super-node deployment? This favors companies that provide interconnection technology, advanced packaging, liquid cooling, and wafer fabrication equipment. These are the enablers of system-level performance, and their addressable market expands as the industry moves away from single-chip benchmarking. The report’s observations on JCET, NAURA, and AMEC reflect this logic. These companies are not competing to design the fastest chip. They are providing the building blocks that make large-scale deployment possible.

Second, does the company have secured supply and design wins at top cloud service provider customers? The report highlights Iluvatar CoreX as best positioned among tier-2 AI chip players, backed by secured supply and design wins at leading Chinese cloud service providers. This is a crucial distinction. In a market where capacity is constrained and customers are prioritizing reliability over peak performance, the ability to secure supply and demonstrate deployment at scale is a competitive advantage that transcends raw chip specifications.

Third, does the company’s business model benefit from the industrialization of tokens, meaning that its revenue scales with the volume of inference or training workloads rather than with the number of chips sold? This is the most forward-looking criterion. As the token economy matures, companies that can capture value from ongoing compute usage, rather than from one-time hardware sales, will have more durable revenue streams. This favors companies in the back-end service and equipment sectors, where demand is driven by capacity expansion and utilization rates, not by product cycles.

Applying this framework suggests that the most attractive opportunities lie in the enabling layers of the supply chain, not in the chip design layer. The chip designers face intense competition, rapid obsolescence, and the constant risk that their products will be commoditized by the shift to system-level thinking. The enablers, by contrast, benefit from secular tailwinds that are independent of which specific chip wins the benchmark race.

## The Next Phase of China’s AI Infrastructure Will Be Defined by Ecosystem Co-Innovation, Not by Hardware Benchmarks

The report’s most important strategic observation is that making AI compute affordable and ubiquitous requires sustained ecosystem co-innovation. No single company can solve the interconnection problem, the cooling problem, the heterogeneous scheduling problem, and the token quality measurement problem on its own. The industry is moving toward a model in which chip designers, packaging houses, equipment manufacturers, cloud service providers, and software developers must collaborate to deliver integrated solutions. This is a structural shift from the earlier phase, in which each layer of the stack operated relatively independently.

For observers, this means that the winners will be those companies that can position themselves as essential partners in the ecosystem, not as isolated vendors. Companies that can provide standardized interfaces, interoperable software layers, and scalable manufacturing processes will have pricing power and recurring revenue. Companies that try to build proprietary, closed systems will find themselves increasingly marginalized as the industry converges on shared standards for super-node design and token economics.

The report does not resolve the question of which vertical-specific super-node scale will prove optimal, or how token quality will be measured across different applications. These are open questions that will be answered through deployment, not through theoretical analysis. But the direction of travel is clear. China’s AI infrastructure is no longer a collection of chip companies trying to catch up with Nvidia. It is a system-level ecosystem that is building its own architecture, its own economics, and its own competitive logic. The opportunity lies in understanding that architecture and observing the companies that enable it.

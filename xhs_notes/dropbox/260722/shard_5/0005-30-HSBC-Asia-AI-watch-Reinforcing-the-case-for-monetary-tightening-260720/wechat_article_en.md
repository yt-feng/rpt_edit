# AI Is Rewiring Asia's Inflation Dynamics, Forcing Central Banks in Korea and Taiwan Toward Tighter Policy

The AI build-out is no longer a distant growth story for Asia. It is a present-day macroeconomic force that is reshaping the region's inflation dynamics in ways that traditional oil-and-food cycle models cannot capture. As US hyperscalers—Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft—prepare to spend more than USD 700 billion on AI capital expenditure this year, roughly the size of Sweden's entire GDP, the economic effects are concentrating in a narrow set of upstream inputs before filtering into broader prices. This creates an asymmetric inflation risk across Asian economies, one that will force some central banks toward tighter monetary policy while leaving others relatively unaffected.

The conventional wisdom holds that AI is deflationary over the long run, boosting productivity and lowering costs. That may prove true eventually, but it is the wrong framework for the next one to two years. During the current policy cycle, the AI infrastructure build-out functions more like a negative supply-side shock, concentrating demand in semiconductor fabrication, memory chips, data centre equipment, and specialized materials. Upstream cost pressures build first, and only later feed into broader inflation via higher compute and services prices. Central banks in economies positioned at the top of the AI tech stack—those that produce the hardware and components—will feel these pressures earlier and more acutely than downstream adopters.

This analysis leads to a clear strategic conclusion: Korea and Taiwan are the two Asian economies where AI most strengthens the case for monetary tightening. Both sit upstream in the AI supply chain, both are experiencing significant growth upside from AI hardware exports, and both will face cost pass-through pressures before their peers. For investors and policymakers watching Asia's monetary trajectory, understanding which economies are upstream producers, which are midstream assemblers, and which are downstream adopters is now as important as tracking consumer price index releases.

## The AI Infrastructure Build-Out Is Already Delivering Measurable Growth Upside Across Asia, But the Benefits Are Highly Concentrated

The first-round macro effects of the AI boom are visible in two channels: surging hardware exports and accelerating data centre investment. These are not hypothetical projections. They are already embedded in trade and investment data across the region.

Taiwan stands out as the most exposed economy. AI-enabled exports are equivalent to nearly half of its GDP. This is not surprising given that TSMC, the world's leading semiconductor foundry with roughly 70 percent market share, is the only company mass-producing leading-edge two-nanometre chips at commercially viable yields. Every Nvidia Hopper and Blackwell GPU—the workhorses of AI training and inference—depends on TSMC fabrication. The breadth of this dependency means that Taiwan's export cycle is now tightly coupled to hyperscaler capex intentions rather than traditional end-market demand.

Korea is the primary beneficiary of the memory chip supercycle driven by AI. High-bandwidth memory, which stacks DRAM vertically to deliver the extreme speeds required by large language models, is essential for AI data centres. Samsung and SK Hynix together account for roughly 70 percent of the global DRAM market and over half of NAND Flash storage. As hyperscalers scale their data centre footprints, demand for these components is structurally elevated, creating a sustained export tailwind that is distinct from the cyclical memory market of previous decades.

Japan and Malaysia occupy different positions in the AI value chain but are nonetheless beneficiaries. Japan is a major supplier of chipmaking equipment, chemicals, and materials, and is experiencing an AI data centre boom supported by a proposed JPY 370 trillion strategic investment plan. Malaysia has emerged as a key destination for data centre investment, leveraging its existing semiconductor assembly and testing infrastructure, particularly in automotive chips. The common thread across these economies is that their growth uplift is directly tied to the physical infrastructure of AI rather than to downstream adoption or productivity gains.

## Mainland China Serves as a Bellwether for AI's Labour Market Impact, But Labour Disruption Is Not Yet a Macroeconomic Concern for Most Asian Economies

The second-round effects of AI on labour markets have been modest across Asia so far. There have been no mass layoffs and no step-change in productivity that would register in aggregate data. This is consistent with the historical pattern of general-purpose technologies: adoption is gradual, productivity rises over time, some roles are displaced while others emerge, and widespread joblessness is avoided.

However, earnings call analysis reveals meaningful variation across economies. Mainland Chinese companies report the highest potential labour impact in Asia, measured by both explicit mentions of layoffs linked to AI and implicit signals of substitution or slower hiring. This reflects China's rapid AI adoption rate, which exceeds even the United States according to the analysis of English-language earnings call transcripts. The concentration of impact in services sectors—particularly information technology, financials, and consumer discretionary—suggests that China may serve as a canary in the coal mine for other Asian economies as their own AI adoption accelerates.

For central banks, the labour channel is not yet a primary policy consideration. Even in economies with higher occupational exposure, such as developed Asian markets with concentrated services and technology employment, the displacement effects are being offset by reskilling and redeployment. AI can augment labour as well as substitute it, raising output per worker in many occupations. The risk is that if adoption accelerates faster than anticipated, the displacement side of the equation could become more pronounced, particularly for entry-level white-collar roles. But that scenario remains hypothetical for now.

## Inflation Dynamics Are Being Reshaped by the AI Tech Stack Position, Creating Asymmetric Risks Between Upstream Producers and Downstream Adopters

This is the most consequential finding for monetary policy. The AI build-out is changing how inflation propagates through Asian economies, and the traditional framework of tracking food and energy prices is insufficient.

The mechanism works as follows. Hyperscaler capex concentrates demand in a narrow set of inputs: semiconductor wafers, memory chips, cooling systems, fibre-optic networks, and specialized construction materials for data centres. These upstream costs rise first. They then feed into broader prices through two channels: higher compute costs for downstream services that rely on AI infrastructure, and higher prices for the equipment and materials used in data centre construction. The inflation impact is therefore sequential, not simultaneous.

The critical variable is where an economy sits in the AI tech stack. Upstream hardware producers, such as Korea and Taiwan, feel cost pressures earliest because they are directly exposed to the input price increases. Midstream assemblers, such as Malaysia and Thailand, experience second-round effects as component costs are passed through. Downstream pure adopters, such as India and Indonesia, feel the least direct pressure because they are buyers of AI services rather than producers of AI hardware.

This asymmetry has direct implications for central bank policy. In upstream economies, the inflation signal arrives earlier and is more directly tied to the AI capex cycle. Central banks in these economies cannot afford to wait for broad-based consumer price inflation to confirm the trend. They must anticipate cost pass-through from upstream inputs to intermediate goods to final prices. In downstream economies, the inflation signal is weaker and arrives later, giving policymakers more time to assess whether the AI build-out is genuinely inflationary or whether productivity gains will offset cost pressures.

## A Decision Framework for Asian Central Banks: Assess Growth Upside, Tech Stack Position, and Labour Exposure to Determine the Monetary Policy Stance

For investors and policymakers seeking to navigate this environment, a three-factor framework is more useful than a one-size-fits-all view of AI and monetary policy.

The first factor is growth upside from AI. Economies where AI hardware exports or data centre investment represent a significant share of GDP are experiencing a demand-side boost that strengthens the case for tighter policy. Taiwan and Vietnam, where AI-enabled exports approach half of GDP, are clear examples. Malaysia, with its data centre investment boom, is another.

The second factor is tech stack position. Upstream producers face the earliest and most direct cost pressures from the AI build-out. Korea and Taiwan are the prime examples. Midstream economies face second-round effects. Downstream economies face minimal direct pressure. This factor determines the timing and intensity of inflation signals.

The third factor is labour market exposure. Economies with high AI adoption rates and concentrated services employment may face displacement pressures that could weigh on wages and aggregate demand over time. Mainland China is the most exposed, but developed Asian markets with large services sectors also rank highly. This factor is more relevant for the medium-term outlook than for the current policy cycle.

Applying this framework to the current policy environment yields a clear hierarchy of tightening pressure. Korea and Taiwan sit at the top: both have significant growth upside, both are upstream producers, and both will face cost pass-through pressures before their peers. Japan, with its equipment and materials exposure and data centre boom, is in the middle. India and Indonesia, as downstream adopters with lower direct exposure to the AI hardware cycle, face the least pressure to tighten on AI grounds alone.

# The Polarisation Pause in Luxury Is a Strategic Turning Point, Not a Reversal

For more than two decades, the largest luxury brands consistently captured a disproportionate share of industry growth. Between 2016 and 2025, the Big Five fashion and leather goods houses—Vuitton, Chanel, Dior, Hermès, and Gucci—accounted for roughly 80 percent of revenue expansion and approximately 85 percent of profit growth among the top 21 brands. That pattern, long treated as a structural law of the sector, has now paused. In 2025, on aggregate, the Big Five slightly lost market share within a contracting industry. The cause is not a sudden fragmentation of demand or the rise of challenger brands. It is a concentrated market share loss at Gucci, combined with a second consecutive year of turnover decline at Vuitton and a broader downturn at Dior. The story of luxury in 2025 is not about the end of concentration. It is about which brands have the structural insulation to withstand a cyclical downturn and which do not.

The data make this distinction stark. Vuitton remains the largest brand by turnover at an estimated €20.8 billion, but that figure has fallen roughly 6 percent year-on-year after a 1.5 percent decline in 2024. EBIT dropped a sharper 13 percent to €9.5 billion. Chanel, by contrast, saw its brand turnover decline only 1 percent to €16.2 billion. More revealing is the comparison in total retail sales, which adjusts for channel mix. On that measure, Chanel now trails Vuitton by just €0.1 billion, down from a €0.6 billion gap in 2024 and a much wider margin in 2023. If current trends continue—credit card data point to a relatively flat turnover year-on-year for Vuitton in the first half of 2026 versus an estimated 10 percent increase for Chanel—Chanel could overtake Vuitton on retail sales as soon as 2026. The gap at the top is closing not because Chanel is accelerating, but because Vuitton's DTC-heavy model is more exposed to the volume compression affecting the broader market.

This moment demands a re-examination of what brand resilience actually means in luxury. The conventional wisdom has been that scale and heritage provide automatic buffers. The 2025 rankings confirm that heritage is necessary but not sufficient. All ten of the top brands by turnover were founded over a century ago, with the most recent, Dior, established in 1946. Yet six of the ten saw turnover decline. The brands that grew—Hermès, Rolex, Cartier, and Van Cleef & Arpels—share a specific structural trait: they operate in categories or with distribution models that limit their exposure to the middle-market demand that has softened most sharply. The strategic question for executives is no longer whether their brand has history. It is whether their brand has a defensible position in the demand stack that the current cycle is reshaping.

## The Vuitton-Chanel Convergence Reveals the Limits of the DTC Model in a Demand Downturn

Vuitton's position as the largest and most profitable luxury brand is not in dispute. Its estimated EBIT of €9.5 billion is more than double that of any other brand in the top ten, and its 100 percent direct-to-consumer channel mix has historically been a source of margin superiority. But that same structure becomes a liability when demand contracts. Vuitton cannot rely on wholesale partners to absorb inventory or smooth revenue volatility. Every decline in foot traffic or conversion rates flows directly to the top line. Chanel, by contrast, sells the vast majority of its cosmetics through third-party retailers. Its cosmetics division accounted for an estimated 23 percent of total sales in 2025, and those wholesale revenues provide a cushion that Vuitton lacks. The result is that Chanel's brand turnover declined only 1 percent while Vuitton's dropped 6 percent, despite Chanel being roughly 22 percent smaller on that metric.

The retail sales comparison makes the point even more sharply. When wholesale markups are included, Chanel's total retail value of €20.7 billion is nearly identical to Vuitton's €20.8 billion. This convergence is not a function of Chanel outperforming on brand heat. It is a mathematical consequence of channel structure. Vuitton captures 100 percent of its retail value at the DTC price point. Chanel captures only its wholesale revenue from third-party retailers, while those retailers book the end-consumer markup. In a rising market, Vuitton's model maximizes margin capture. In a falling market, Chanel's model absorbs less of the downside.

The implication for brand strategy is uncomfortable but clear. The DTC model that has been the gold standard of luxury distribution for the past decade carries asymmetric risk. It amplifies upside in growth phases but magnifies downside in contractions. Brands that are considering full DTC conversion should model not just the margin uplift in a base case but the revenue volatility in a stress scenario. The Vuitton-Chanel data suggest that the trade-off between margin level and margin stability is more significant than most strategic plans acknowledge.

## Hermès and Rolex Demonstrate That Scarcity Discipline Outperforms Scale Ambition in a Cyclical Contraction

Hermès and Rolex were two of only four top-ten brands to grow turnover in 2025. Hermès Group revenue rose 5.5 percent to €16 billion, and brand-level EBIT increased 6.8 percent to €6.6 billion, yielding a 41.1 percent operating margin. Rolex's estimated turnover grew 5.4 percent to €11.7 billion, driven entirely by a 6 percent average selling price increase that more than offset a 2 percent decline in units sold. These two brands share a strategic discipline that sets them apart from peers: they have consistently chosen scarcity over volume, even when demand was strong enough to support faster growth.

Rolex's approach is the more extreme case. The brand has now recorded two consecutive years of volume decline, the first time this has happened in over two decades. Its estimated 1.15 million units sold in 2025 represent a deliberate reduction in supply that reinforces price power and waitlist dynamics. The result is that Rolex now commands an estimated 33 percent of the Swiss watch market by retail value, up from 26 percent in 2019. No other brand in any luxury category approaches this level of category dominance. Rolex competes in only one product category—Swiss watches—while every other top-ten brand operates across ten or more categories. Its single-category concentration would normally be a vulnerability. But because the brand has trained the market to expect scarcity, it has inverted the usual risk. Demand exceeds supply at any price point the brand chooses to set.

Hermès operates on a similar principle but through a different mechanism. The brand does not publicly disclose production constraints, but its consistent single-digit volume growth over decades, combined with pricing power that has lifted revenue even in slow years, reflects a deliberate cap on supply growth. In 2025, Hermès grew at its slowest pace since 2013, excluding the pandemic year, yet it still outperformed every other top-five brand. The lesson is that scarcity discipline is not a growth constraint. It is a margin and resilience strategy that becomes most valuable when the cycle turns down.

For brands currently debating whether to accelerate distribution expansion or tighten supply, the Hermès and Rolex data provide a clear answer. In a market where the top five brands have paused their share gains, the ones that held or grew share did so by making their products harder to obtain, not easier. That counterintuitive logic is the most actionable strategic insight from the 2025 rankings.

## Dior's Two-Year Decline Shows That Creative Appointments Cannot Substitute for Structural Category Exposure

Dior has now posted two consecutive years of turnover contraction, with estimated revenue falling from €12.8 billion in 2023 to €11.2 billion in 2025, a cumulative peak-to-trough decline of close to 15 percent. The brand slipped from fourth to fifth in the turnover ranking, overtaken by Rolex. Management has responded with a creative leadership change, appointing Jonathan Anderson as sole Creative Director in 2025. Early indicators suggest a modest inflection: constant-currency sales turned positive by 1 to 2 percent year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, after an estimated decline of 2 to 3 percent in the first quarter. But the pace of recovery is expected to be pedestrian, with full-year 2026 sales likely up only low single digits.

The structural issue is not creative direction. It is category exposure. Dior's turnover split is roughly 65 percent fashion and leather goods and 35 percent cosmetics. That cosmetics exposure is higher than at Vuitton or Hermès but lower than at Chanel. More importantly, Dior's cosmetics sales are generated through Dior Parfums, which is reported within LVMH's cosmetics division rather than its fashion and leather goods division. This reporting structure obscures the fact that Dior is more dependent on fragrance and makeup than its direct competitors. In a market where cosmetics demand has proven more volatile than leather goods demand—particularly in the Chinese travel retail channel—that exposure has been a headwind.

The report's analysts explicitly note that competitor Chanel is capturing a disproportionate share of the segment's growth. This is not a coincidence. Chanel's cosmetics business is larger in absolute terms, more deeply embedded in department store and specialty retail relationships, and benefits from a fragrance portfolio that includes some of the most iconic scents in the industry. Dior's fragrance business is strong, but it lacks the same breadth of heritage bestsellers. The implication is that creative leadership changes can improve the trajectory of the fashion and leather goods business, but they cannot quickly rebuild a cosmetics distribution network or accelerate the development of new fragrance icons.

For executives evaluating turnaround candidates, the Dior case is a caution against over-weighting creative appointments as a recovery catalyst. The creative director can influence the fashion business, which typically accounts for 60 to 70 percent of revenue at a house like Dior. But the remaining 30 to 40 percent is driven by licensing, distribution agreements, and category-specific demand cycles that are largely outside the creative director's control. A full brand turnaround requires a coordinated strategy across both halves of the business, and the cosmetics half takes longer to move.

## A Decision Framework for Brand Portfolio Strategy in the Current Cycle

The 2025 rankings translate into a three-dimensional framework for assessing any luxury brand's position and trajectory. The first dimension is channel structure and its impact on revenue volatility. Brands with high DTC exposure, such as Vuitton, capture more margin per unit sold but absorb full downside in a contraction. Brands with significant wholesale exposure, such as Chanel in cosmetics or Rolex in watches, sacrifice some margin for revenue stability. The optimal mix depends on the brand's category and the management team's tolerance for earnings volatility. A useful heuristic is to compare the brand's DTC ratio to its category's historical demand volatility. High-volatility categories such as watches and cosmetics should have lower DTC ratios. Low-volatility categories such as leather goods can support higher DTC exposure.

The second dimension is supply discipline relative to demand. Brands that have consistently constrained volume growth, such as Hermès and Rolex, have built pricing power that protects margins even when the industry contracts. Brands that have pursued volume growth, such as Gucci in its pre-reset phase, are more vulnerable when demand softens. The relevant metric is not the absolute volume number but the trend in volume relative to the brand's own history and to category growth. A brand whose volume growth has consistently lagged category growth by a meaningful margin is building pricing power. A brand whose volume growth has matched or exceeded category growth is building market share but at the cost of future pricing flexibility.

The third dimension is category diversification and the quality of that diversification. Not all category exposure is equal. Dior's cosmetics business is a source of revenue but also a source of volatility, because it competes in a category with lower barriers to entry and faster trend cycles. Saint Laurent, by contrast, has turned its cosmetics license into a structural advantage. The brand's estimated 2025 retail sales of €9.6 billion rank it seventh among all luxury brands, far ahead of its turnover ranking of thirteenth. This is because Saint Laurent Beauté, licensed to L'Oréal, generated an estimated €3.1 billion in turnover and roughly €6.2 billion in retail sales after retailer markups. For the first time, Saint Laurent's cosmetics turnover exceeded its fashion and leather goods turnover. That is not a sign of weakness in fashion. It is a sign of exceptional execution in licensing.

The framework suggests that the most resilient brand portfolios in the current cycle will be those that combine low DTC exposure in volatile categories, a demonstrated history of supply discipline, and at least one category where the brand has a structural advantage that competitors cannot easily replicate. By this measure, Chanel, Hermès, and Rolex are the best-positioned brands among the top ten. Vuitton remains the most profitable but faces a higher risk of continued revenue compression if the demand environment does not improve. Dior and Gucci are in the most challenging positions, requiring both creative and structural fixes that will take multiple years to execute.

## Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels Are Reshaping the Jewellery Category While Tiffany Struggles with the Middle-Market Trap

The jewellery segment within the top ten tells a story of divergence that has strategic implications beyond the category itself. Cartier's estimated turnover rose 8 percent to €11.0 billion in 2025, with retail sales of €12.3 billion and EBIT of €3.5 billion. Van Cleef & Arpels grew 10 percent to an estimated €4.8 billion, with EBIT of €1.5 billion. Both brands have outperformed the broader market consistently. Cartier's turnover compound annual growth rate between 2015 and 2025 is estimated at roughly 10 percent in euro terms. Van Cleef has grown from approximately €750 million in 2013 under former CEO Nicolas Bos to its current level, a roughly 20 percent compound annual growth rate over twelve years.

The contrast with Tiffany is instructive. Tiffany's estimated turnover fell 3 percent to €4.9 billion in 2025, and the brand dropped out of the top ten by retail sales and EBIT. LVMH has pursued an elevation strategy since acquiring Tiffany in 2021, investing in product, communication, and distribution upgrades. But the brand has faced a specific structural challenge. Its sales to middle-income consumers, particularly in the United States, have been under pressure, compounded by steep price increases that were among the highest in the jewellery industry. The brand has not yet been able to fully offset this shortfall by selling higher-priced iconic gold lines such as Lock, Tiffany T, and Hardware.

This is the middle-market trap. A brand that tries to elevate its positioning by raising prices without simultaneously transforming its product mix to appeal to higher-income consumers risks losing its existing customer base before the new one is secured. Tiffany's experience suggests that the price elasticity of demand for aspirational jewellery is higher than many strategists assume. A 10 to 15 percent price increase may be absorbed if accompanied by a clear product story. A steeper increase, implemented over a short period, can destroy volume faster than the brand can replace it with higher-value sales.

Cartier and Van Cleef have avoided this trap because their elevation was gradual and product-led rather than price-led. Cartier's turnaround began in 2016 under CEO Cyrille Vigneron and has continued under Louis Ferla since 2024. The brand's 10 percent CAGR over a decade reflects consistent product innovation, disciplined distribution, and a pricing strategy that has moved upward in step with perceived value rather than ahead of it. Van Cleef's growth has been even more remarkable given its smaller base, driven by the Alhambra collection's sustained popularity and the brand's ability to create waitlist dynamics in a category that has traditionally been more transactional.

The jewellery category now offers a clear strategic template. Brands that can generate scarcity through product design and controlled distribution, as Cartier and Van Cleef have done, can achieve growth rates that rival or exceed those of leather goods houses. Brands that attempt to force elevation through price alone, as Tiffany has attempted, risk a prolonged period of revenue compression while they rebuild their customer base. For conglomerates evaluating acquisition targets or internal brand investments, the jewellery category remains attractive, but only for brands that already have a proven scarcity mechanism or a clear path to building one.

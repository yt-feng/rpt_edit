## Glass Bridges Solve the Mode-Field Mismatch That Limits Optical Coupling Density, and They Are Technologically Mature

The fundamental problem in optical interconnect is that photonic integrated circuits and optical fibers operate at different mode-field diameters. The chip's waveguide confines light to a tiny cross-section, while the fiber core is much larger. Without an intermediate structure, the coupling loss is prohibitive. Glass bridges address this by acting as a tapered waveguide that expands the mode field from the chip scale to the fiber scale. The expert identifies two manufacturing methods: ion exchange, which uses sodium and silver ions to create a refractive index gradient, and laser modification, which is less efficient and introduces higher optical loss. The critical fact is that ion-exchange glass bridge technology is relatively mature and has been under development for years. Corning's glass bridges use this method, meaning the supply chain for the core component already exists in a production-capable form.

This maturity is the first reason why glass bridges, not glass substrates, will drive the near-term deployment cycle. A technology that has already passed the proof-of-concept stage and is being sampled by leading optical component manufacturers does not require a fundamental manufacturing breakthrough. It requires process optimization for loss control, stability, and coupling accuracy. These are engineering challenges, not science projects. The expert explicitly lists loss control, process stability and consistency, and high requirements for coupling accuracy and deformation as the current technical challenges. None of these are showstoppers. They are the normal friction points of scaling a known process to higher volumes and tighter tolerances. The implication is clear: companies that invest in glass bridge integration capabilities today will have a deployable solution before those waiting for glass substrates.

## Glass Bridges Do Not Replace Fiber Array Units; They Add a Critical Intermediate Layer That Enables Higher-Density Architectures

A common misunderstanding in the market is that glass bridges will eliminate the need for fiber array units. The expert corrects this directly: glass bridges add a layer of bridge structure in the middle to achieve mode-field matching and the transition from high density to sparse density. The FAU remains essential for the final connection to the fiber ribbon. What changes is the interface between the PIC and the FAU. Instead of a direct, lossy coupling, the glass bridge provides a controlled transition that allows for higher channel counts without catastrophic failure in coupling efficiency. This is not a replacement technology. It is an enabling technology that raises the ceiling on what FAU-based architectures can achieve.

The strategic implication for the supply chain is that FAU manufacturers are not threatened by glass bridges. They are, if anything, beneficiaries. The glass bridge creates a larger design space for FAU geometry and alignment tolerance, which means FAU suppliers that can adapt their processes to work with glass bridge interposers will capture more value in the next generation of optical modules. Conversely, FAU suppliers that cannot adjust their alignment and assembly precision to accommodate the bridge layer will find themselves locked out of high-density applications. The expert notes that glass bridges are mainly applicable in high-density optical communication scenarios. This is a volume driver, not a niche. Every hyperscaler data center upgrade cycle from 400G to 800G to 1.6T and beyond will require exactly this kind of density improvement.

## Edge Coupling Wins on Efficiency and Bandwidth, but Surface Coupling Wins on Consistency for High-Channel-Count CPO

The coupling architecture debate reveals a nuanced trade-off that the market often simplifies into a binary choice. Edge coupling has relatively high luminous efficiency and a larger optical bandwidth, making it more favorable for wavelength division multiplexing and high-bandwidth couplers. Surface grating coupling has a smaller vertical grating bandwidth and poorer coupling accuracy. On paper, edge coupling is the superior technology. Yet NVIDIA uses surface coupling for its CPO implementations. The expert explains why: NPO and CPO require many couplers—a 6.4T CPO at 200G per lane needs 32 fibers—and the coupling consistency across all channels is paramount. Edge coupling uses a one-dimensional array, which cannot guarantee uniform loss control across all channels. Surface coupling uses a two-dimensional array, which provides better loss control at the cost of lower per-channel efficiency.

This trade-off has direct implications for which companies and architectures will succeed. In low-channel-count, high-performance applications such as long-haul telecom or single-lane test equipment, edge coupling will remain the preferred solution because efficiency matters more than channel uniformity. In the data center switch market, where channel counts are exploding and the cost of non-uniform loss is amplified across dozens of lanes, surface coupling will gain share despite its lower per-channel efficiency. The expert's explanation reveals that NVIDIA's choice is not a vote for surface coupling's inherent superiority but a pragmatic response to the scaling challenge of CPO. As channel counts continue to rise, the consistency advantage of two-dimensional arrays will become even more pronounced, potentially making surface coupling the default architecture for any switch ASIC with 32 or more optical lanes. This is a structural shift that favors companies with expertise in two-dimensional grating design and high-precision surface coupling assembly.

## Glass Core Substrates Remain in Early Development, While Glass Carriers Are Already Essential for 2.5D Packaging

The most significant divergence in the expert call is between glass carriers and glass core substrates. Glass carriers are already a necessity in 2.5D packaging because plastic encapsulation materials cause shape changes, and glass carriers are required to control deformation during the molding process. This is not a future technology. It is a current production requirement. Any company doing 2.5D packaging at scale is already using glass carriers, whether they publicize it or not. The value chain for glass carriers is established, and the bottleneck is not technology but capacity and cost.

Glass core substrates, by contrast, are still far away from commercialization. The expert is blunt: there are multiple technology challenges, including low yield rates for processing through-glass vias because glass is fragile. Intel is a pioneer in developing GCS, and some Korean companies are also working on it, but no reliability test data have been released yet. Chinese companies such as AKM Meadville have been sampling GCS products, and BOE is cooperating with Corning in the development stage. The absence of published reliability data is a red flag. In semiconductor packaging, reliability testing is not optional. It is the gating factor for adoption by hyperscalers and OEMs. Without data showing that GCS can survive thermal cycling, moisture exposure, and mechanical stress over a 10-year product lifetime, no serious buyer will qualify it for production. The expert's framing suggests that GCS is at least three to five years away from production readiness, and possibly longer if yield rates do not improve.

The strategic takeaway is that the glass substrate narrative, which has generated significant interest, is premature. The real action in glass-based packaging is in glass bridges and glass carriers, both of which are already in or near production. Companies that are building their optical packaging roadmaps around GCS availability are taking on substantial timeline risk. Companies that are designing around glass bridges and existing glass carrier solutions have a much clearer path to deployment.

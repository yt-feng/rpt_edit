## The Supply-Demand Arithmetic Points to a Sustained Deficit Through 2026, Not a Transitory Shock

The most important structural feature of the current market is not the Iran disruption itself but what it reveals about the fragility of global LNG supply. Even before the latest escalation, approximately 20% of global liquefaction capacity was offline or operating at reduced rates. The Qatar situation alone represents a multi-million-tonne supply gap that cannot be quickly replaced. CEO Saad Al-Kaabi stated on July 9 that QatarEnergy had halted plans to resume output after an attack on one of its tankers raised safety concerns about Strait of Hormuz transit. The timeline for restoration remains uncertain: QatarEnergy previously indicated it could reach 50% of capacity one month after safe passage is restored and 80% within two months, but this assumes no damage to key infrastructure. That assumption is now questionable.

Outside the Middle East, the supply picture offers little relief. Golden Pass continues its commissioning process but feedgas flows remain volatile, with only four cargoes exported so far—one of which was diverted from the Netherlands to Korea and then to Brazil, signaling persistent logistical inefficiency. Freeport LNG began planned maintenance on July 10, with feedgas flows declining accordingly, and the turnaround is expected to last until late August. While global liquefaction utilization ex-Middle East stood at 95% in July, above the five-year average of 83%, this high utilization rate actually underscores the problem: there is virtually no spare capacity to absorb additional shocks. The system is running hot, and any further disruption will have outsized price consequences.

The supply-demand model confirms this tightness. The global LNG market is projected to be in a 13 million tonne per annum shortfall in 2026, a deficit that only gradually moves toward balance in 2027-2028 before swinging into surplus later in the decade. This is not a one-quarter anomaly. It is a multi-year structural condition driven by demand growth that has been underestimated. Global imports ex-Europe are already 3% higher year-over-year over the last 30 days, with India up 12% and Taiwan up 10%. Japan's imports have accelerated sharply, rising 25% year-over-year over the last two weeks as the country works to refill inventories. These demand trends are not weather-driven aberrations; they reflect structural industrialization and gas-for-coal switching that will persist regardless of seasonal temperature patterns.

## European Storage Deficits Create a Forced Buying Dynamic That Will Sustain Price Support Through Winter

The most consequential demand-side development is unfolding in Europe, where the continent's LNG imports have fallen 24% year-over-year over the last 30 days. This decline is not a sign of weakening demand but of supply scarcity. Asia's recovery has pulled cargoes away from the European market, leaving storage only 54% full compared to 64% at this time last year and well below the 2016-2025 average of 69%. July is expected to be the second hottest in Europe since 2000, with cooling degree days 26% above the ten-year normal, further straining inventories.

The arithmetic of winter preparation is unforgiving. Even with a near-term restart of Qatari exports, normalizing storage levels ahead of winter appears increasingly unlikely. Europe will need to accelerate imports significantly in the coming quarters, and that demand will compete directly with Asia for available cargoes. This creates a forced buying dynamic that should sustain price support well beyond any near-term de-escalation in geopolitical tensions. The European Commission has limited tools to mandate storage fills without creating political backlash, and the market knows it.

For investors, the implication is clear: pullbacks in JKM and TTF prices driven by headline-driven de-escalation should be viewed as buying opportunities, not as signals of a structural shift. The fundamental imbalance will take time to resolve, and the price floor has moved higher.

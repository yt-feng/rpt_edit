# The LCD Panel Price Cycle Has Turned: Why the Second-Half Demand Pull-Forward Creates Asymmetric Risk for Observers

TV panel prices fell 2% month-over-month in July 2026, with mainstream sizes from 32 to 75 inches declining between 1% and 3%. This marks the first notable drop after six months of flat to slightly positive pricing and confirms that the demand pull-forward ahead of the Winter Olympics, FIFA World Cup, and China's 618 shopping festival has exhausted its momentum. For those who have been pricing panel companies based on hopes of sustained pricing power or on the distant promise of glass-based advanced packaging, the July data should serve as a clear signal: the cyclical window has closed, and the second half of 2026 will be defined by below-seasonal shipments, not structural re-rating.

The timing of this inflection matters because it is not being driven by a sudden demand collapse. The mechanism is more subtle and more significant for those who have positioned for a sustained upcycle. TV brands restocked aggressively in the first half of 2026, pulling forward orders that would normally have been placed in the second half. The historical split between first-half and second-half TV panel shipments has been roughly 49-to-51 percent over the past decade. In 2026, that ratio is expected to skew meaningfully toward the first half. This means that even if global TV demand holds steady, second-half shipments will fall below seasonal norms simply because the inventory pipeline is already full. The risk is not that demand deteriorates, but that the order book contracts relative to historical patterns, and panel makers lose pricing leverage precisely when they need it most.

The IT panel segment has not yet cracked, but the logic that has kept monitor and notebook prices flat in July is fragile. Panel makers are holding the line on pricing because they face elevated component costs, and they are unwilling to concede margins in a softening demand environment. This standoff cannot last indefinitely. As brand customers in the IT segment also pulled forward restocking into the first half, the same second-half order deceleration that is now visible in TV will likely hit monitors and notebooks starting in the fourth quarter of 2026. When that happens, the combination of falling TV prices and the beginning of IT price erosion will create a synchronized downward pressure across the entire LCD panel product spectrum for the first time since late 2023.

## The Pull-Forward in TV Shipments Has Created a Second-Half Order Deficit That Will Weaken Panel Makers' Bargaining Power

The mechanism behind the July price decline is not a mystery. TV panel shipments in the first half of 2026 were elevated because brands prepared for a dense calendar of major events. The Winter Olympics in February, the FIFA World Cup from June through July, and China's 618 shopping festival all created incentives for early procurement. But there is no evidence that these events materially expand global TV demand. What they do is shift the timing of purchases across quarters. Brands that stocked heavily in the first half do not need to order again in the second half unless end-market demand surprises to the upside. The most likely scenario is that second-half TV panel shipments fall below seasonal levels, and when brands sit out of the market, panel makers lose the ability to push through price increases or even defend existing prices.

This dynamic is already visible in the July data. The 32-inch and 43-inch panels, which are the most price-sensitive and most exposed to promotional retail channels, fell 3 percent month-over-month. The 65-inch and 75-inch panels, which had been the strongest performers during the upcycle due to premiumization trends, fell 1 to 2 percent. The declines are broad-based across sizes, which suggests a systemic shift in buying behavior rather than a product-specific issue. The next critical checkpoint will be August through September, when brands begin placing orders for year-end promotions. If those orders come in below normal seasonal volumes, the downward pressure on TV panel prices will accelerate into the fourth quarter.

## IT Panel Prices Are Flat for Now, but the Same Demand Timing Distortion Will Hit Monitors and Notebooks in the Fourth Quarter

Monitor and notebook panel prices remained unchanged in July, but the stability is deceptive. The IT segment is experiencing the same phenomenon as TV: brand customers pulled forward restocking into the first half of 2026, and second-half demand is slowing as a result. The difference is that panel makers in the IT segment are currently absorbing component cost pressures rather than passing them through or accepting lower prices. This creates a temporary floor under IT panel prices, but it is not a sustainable equilibrium.

The component cost pressure comes from multiple sources. Driver ICs, timing controllers, and other semiconductor components embedded in LCD modules have not seen the same degree of price relief that the panel makers themselves have experienced. Glass substrate costs, particularly for Corning, have been supported by currency-related price increases and disciplined supply. Panel makers are caught between softening demand from their brand customers and sticky input costs from their upstream suppliers. The only way they have maintained IT panel pricing in July is by choosing to absorb margin compression rather than lose market share. That calculus will change when order volumes decline further. Once TV panel prices have established a clear downward trend and IT brand customers begin to push back on pricing in earnest, the flat pricing of July will give way to declines. The most likely timing for this transition is the fourth quarter of 2026, consistent with the expectation that IT panel prices will begin to follow TV prices downward.

## Supply Discipline Will Limit the Downside but Cannot Prevent a Cyclical Correction

One of the structural changes in the LCD industry over the past three years is that Chinese panel makers have shifted their strategic priority from market share growth to profitability. Lower government subsidies have reinforced this discipline. The industry's production-to-order model, which shortened panel price cycles from multi-year swings to quarterly fluctuations in 2023 and 2024, has become the new normal. This is not a return to the boom-bust cycles of the previous decade.

The evidence for this discipline is visible in fab utilization rates. Industry average utilization stayed at 80 to 85 percent in the second quarter of 2026, broadly flat from the first quarter. This is not a sign of concern. Panel makers are not flooding the market with excess supply. They are managing output carefully, and that should prevent the kind of significant price declines that characterized previous downturns. The consolidation actions that have already occurred, including LG Display's sale of its Gen 8.5 Guangzhou fab to TCL and Sharp's closure of its Gen 10 Sakai fab, have removed capacity that would otherwise be chasing orders in a softening market.

But supply discipline is a buffer, not a cure. It can prevent a 10 percent quarterly price decline from becoming a 20 percent decline, but it cannot prevent a decline when demand is structurally below seasonal norms. The quarterly average expectation for TV panel prices in the third quarter of 2026 is a low single-digit percentage decline quarter-over-quarter. That is a controlled correction, but it is still a correction. For those who have been treating LCD panel companies as if the cycle had been permanently tamed, this distinction matters.

## The Glass Substrate Opportunity in Advanced Packaging Is Real but Distant, and Valuations Do Not Reflect the Execution Risk

A significant portion of the recent interest in panel companies, particularly Taiwan-based names such as AUO and Innolux, has been driven by the potential for glass core substrates in advanced semiconductor packaging. The logic is straightforward: glass offers superior electrical and thermal properties compared to organic substrates for high-performance computing and AI accelerators, and panel makers have the manufacturing infrastructure to produce large-format glass panels. If this technology scales, it could open a new revenue stream that is decoupled from the commoditized LCD panel cycle.

The July report provides a measured assessment of this thesis. For Innolux, which is participating in a foundry customer's glass core substrate project, meaningful contributions are not expected before 2028. That is more than two years away. In the meantime, the industry is moving toward mass production, and multiple supply chain players are targeting the same opportunity. Competition will intensify, and the first-mover advantage may be limited if multiple players achieve comparable technical results. For AUO, which has been diversifying its business into non-display areas, the earnings contributions from advanced packaging and other new initiatives are likely to take time to materialize.

The valuation context matters here. AUO trades at 1.3 times price-to-book, and Innolux at 1.7 times. These multiples are not distressed, but they are not cheap either, given that the core LCD business is entering a cyclical downturn. The argument that these companies deserve a premium because of the glass substrate opportunity assumes that the opportunity will materialize on schedule and that the panel makers will capture a disproportionate share of the value chain. Both assumptions carry significant execution risk. The report's conclusion is direct: it is still premature to value panel companies based on the potential glass opportunities in advanced packaging. Those who have been pricing in this optionality may need to reassess.

On supply discipline, the key variable is whether panel makers maintain utilization rates at 80 to 85 percent or are forced to cut more aggressively. The report's expectation is that supply discipline will hold, which limits the downside but does not eliminate it. Observers should watch utilization data from industry sources. If utilization drops below 80 percent, it would signal that panel makers are struggling to balance supply with demand and that price declines could accelerate.

On non-cyclical optionality, the key variable is the timeline for glass core substrate revenue. For Innolux, the expectation is no meaningful contribution before 2028. For BOE, the progress is faster, which is one reason the report prefers BOE over TCL among Chinese panel makers. But even for BOE, the glass substrate opportunity is a multi-year story, not a near-term earnings driver. Observers should discount any valuation premium that is based on advanced packaging optionality until there is concrete evidence of production scale and customer adoption.

The framework leads to a clear conclusion for portfolio positioning. In the near term, the LCD cycle is moving against panel makers. The price inflection has already occurred for TV, and IT will follow. Supply discipline will cushion the blow but will not prevent it. The glass substrate story is a long-duration option that is not yet priced with adequate risk adjustment. The most prudent approach is to treat panel companies as cyclical plays that are entering a down-phase, and to value them on their core LCD earnings power rather than on speculative optionality. The risk-reward is broadly balanced after the recent correction, but it is not attractive enough to justify incremental exposure.

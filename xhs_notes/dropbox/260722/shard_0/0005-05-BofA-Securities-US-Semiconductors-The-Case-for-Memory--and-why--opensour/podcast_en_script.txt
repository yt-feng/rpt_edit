EN_A: So Chinese open-source AI models are charging a fraction of what Western models do. Does that mean they need less hardware?
EN_B: That is exactly the misconception this report wants to correct. Pricing is a business model choice, not a reflection of hardware cost.
EN_A: So even though they charge less, the memory requirement stays the same?
EN_B: Correct. Take Kimi K3 for example. Each serving instance still needs about 1.4 terabytes of HBM. That memory footprint does not change whether they charge three dollars or thirty dollars per million tokens.
EN_A: Then where does their pricing advantage come from?
EN_B: Three main factors that are not hardware related. First, architectural efficiency like MoE sparsity and MLA attention, which gives them two to three times more efficiency per token. Second, infrastructure costs. Electricity and engineering salaries in China are 40 to 60 percent lower. Third, competition and subsidies. Some companies are likely pricing below cost just to grab market share.
EN_A: What does this mean for investors? What is the key takeaway?
EN_B: The key insight is that these open-source models are actually expanding the total addressable market for memory. Every enterprise that downloads the weights needs to deploy its own hardware, creating new memory endpoints.
EN_A: Can you explain that mechanism?
EN_B: With a closed model, all users share one HBM pool in a few data centers. But with open models, every company that self-hosts needs its own copy of the weights in its own HBM. Ten thousand enterprises mean ten thousand separate HBM deployments.
EN_A: So it is a multiplier effect for memory demand.
EN_B: Exactly. And model weights are growing fast. Kimi K3 has 2.8 trillion parameters, more than four times larger than DeepSeek-V3. Even with quantization, memory demand is still rising.
EN_A: But MoE only activates a fraction of parameters. Doesnt that save memory?
EN_B: This is a common misunderstanding. MoE saves compute, not memory. Because the router selects experts dynamically, all expert weights must stay in HBM at all times. Double the total parameters, double the HBM requirement.
EN_A: So bigger models always mean more memory, regardless of active parameters.
EN_B: Precisely. And larger weights also drive multi-tier memory architectures. Hot experts in HBM, cold experts spilled to CPU memory, cold KV cache to enterprise NAND. Every tier gets more content per deployment.
EN_A: What about Chinese memory maker CXMT? Could that be a competitive threat?
EN_B: The report sees limited risk. CXMT mainly serves consumer DRAM, not the HBM3E or HBM4 needed for AI. And US OEMs are unlikely to get government approval to source from CXMT anytime soon.
EN_A: So the AI memory market still belongs to companies like Micron.
EN_B: Yes. And the report highlights a catalyst. Microns CHIPS Act buyback restrictions expire around December 2026, which could unlock large share repurchases.
EN_A: How large are we talking?
EN_B: At a 40 percent payout ratio, annual buybacks could be 50 to 60 billion dollars, or about 5 to 6 percent of market cap per year.
EN_A: What questions does the report leave unanswered?
EN_B: Two things worth watching. First, how sustainable are the Chinese model subsidies? If capital dries up, pricing and demand could shift. Second, how far does the multi-endpoint effect really go? At even larger model sizes, we might see new bottlenecks.

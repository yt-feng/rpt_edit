EN_A: So the Fed has a new chair, Kevin Warsh, and his first meeting just happened. How did the market react?
EN_B: The market read it as hawkish. The statement and the press conference both reinforced expectations of a rate hike this year. But here is the thing: that might not be the full story.
EN_A: Why do you say that?
EN_B: Because Warsh deliberately gave almost no guidance on the path of policy. Reducing forward guidance is central to his philosophy. So what looks like a hawkish signal might just be a lack of signaling.
EN_A: So what is this report really trying to answer?
EN_B: It asks a bigger question: what will the Fed actually look like under this new chair? Not just where rates are going next quarter, but whether the entire operating logic changes.
EN_A: That is much more interesting than guessing the next rate move. What is the first big insight?
EN_B: The rate path itself might be a puzzle. The dot plot shows only one hike this year. But that one dot is what pushes the median to one. Without it, the median would be zero hikes.
EN_A: Wait. So it is possible we get no hikes at all?
EN_B: Exactly. And the report points out a contradiction. The Fed forecasts core inflation at 3.3 percent for 2026. But the tariff-driven price surge is mostly done, and oil prices are falling. If inflation undershoots significantly, why hike once this year only to cut next year? The logic does not hold.
EN_A: That is a real tension. What is the second insight?
EN_B: The bigger changes are on the balance sheet. Warsh has a clear preference for a smaller balance sheet. The report outlines a concrete path: just halving the Treasury's account at the Fed would shrink the balance sheet by roughly half a trillion dollars, with literally no market impact.
EN_A: Half a trillion? That sounds massive. What does that mean in practice?
EN_B: It means the balance sheet could shrink more than most expect, but the market impact could be less than most fear. The key exception is if the Fed becomes an active seller of mortgage-backed securities. If they just let maturities roll off, it is much less disruptive.
EN_A: And the third insight?
EN_B: It is about the inflation target itself. Warsh set up a task force to study inflation. He reaffirmed the two percent target, but the TIPS market is already showing a disconnect between PCE and CPI. If that study leads to any change in the target, that would be a fundamental shift.
EN_A: That is a huge open question. What about communication? The statement was much shorter this time.
EN_B: Yes, the statement was overhauled. But the report argues this is less revolutionary than it seems. Economists have long said forward guidance only really matters at the effective lower bound. So cutting it now might actually improve communication, because markets stop treating every word as a commitment.
EN_A: So what will the Fed look like a year from now?
EN_B: Very different in communication and probably very different in balance sheet size. But on the rate side, I am less convinced the change will be as stark. And that is exactly where the report leaves some questions open.
EN_A: What questions are still unanswered?
EN_B: What will the inflation task force actually recommend? Could the target shift? What is the exact timeline and method for reducing the balance sheet? The report hints at these but does not fully resolve them. You need to read the full report for those clues.

# China's AI GPU Market Is About to Be Reshaped by Geopolitics: The $91 Billion Opportunity Has a New Variable

The conventional view that China's AI chip market is a closed, domestic-only story is no longer tenable. A recent tightening of US export controls—specifically the move to halt advanced chip shipments to Chinese companies' overseas subsidiaries—has introduced a structural shift that changes the total addressable market in ways most investors have not yet priced in. The bull case for China's AI GPU ecosystem is no longer just about import substitution; it is about the emergence of an entirely new demand category: Chinese cloud providers deploying domestic AI chips in their overseas data centers.

This is not a marginal adjustment. A global investment bank has raised its forecast for China's AI chip TAM to $91 billion by 2030, up 36% from a prior estimate of $67 billion, implying a 23% compound annual growth rate over 2025-2030. That revision is not driven by incremental improvements in domestic chip performance alone. It reflects a fundamental redefinition of what counts as "addressable" in this market. The new TAM now includes overseas capex from Chinese cloud service providers, a category that was previously assumed to be served entirely by Western suppliers.

The timing is critical. Domestic AI infrastructure is entering what the report calls a "critical deployment window" in 2026. Major Chinese CSPs continue to face compute shortages despite ongoing capacity expansion. Vendor qualification activity is accelerating. The question is no longer whether China will build its own AI infrastructure, but how fast, at what scale, and which suppliers will capture the most value. The answer depends on a set of variables that extend well beyond chip performance alone.

## The New TAM Is Not Just Larger—It Is Structurally Different

The upward revision to China's AI chip TAM is not a simple linear extrapolation of past trends. It reflects four distinct changes to the underlying model, each with its own implications for which companies will benefit and how.

First, and most importantly, the report now includes "CSP's overseas capex" as a new category. The logic is straightforward: if US export controls prevent Chinese companies from buying NVIDIA's Blackwell processors for their overseas subsidiaries, those companies will eventually have to source AI chips from domestic suppliers for those same overseas data centers. In the short term, Chinese CSPs may turn to GPU rental to meet immediate demand. But by 2028, the report assumes that 3% of overseas capex will be addressed by local chips, rising to 10% in 2029 and 20% in 2030. This is a conservative starting point. If the geopolitical environment deteriorates further, these percentages could accelerate.

Second, ByteDance's aggressive capex plans have been incorporated. The company is reportedly planning to sharply increase capital spending in 2026 and 2027, with a potential 2027 capex of $100 billion under favorable conditions. The report uses a more conservative $80 billion figure, but even that represents a massive increase in demand for AI infrastructure. ByteDance alone could reshape the demand curve for domestic AI chips.

Third, the addition of Kingsoft Cloud to the database matters. Its capex has already surged to RMB 3 billion, with full-year 2026 capital investments projected to exceed RMB 15-20 billion. This is not a marginal player. Kingsoft Cloud's ramp-up signals that second-tier CSPs are also entering the domestic AI chip procurement game.

Fourth, sovereign and SOE-related TAM has been revised up from $7 billion to $9 billion. Recent news reports indicate that China is preparing RMB 2 trillion over the next five years for data center construction. While specific government guidance has not yet been issued, the direction is clear: state-owned enterprises and local governments will spend more on AI infrastructure, and they will prioritize domestic suppliers.

The combined effect is a market that is not only larger but also more fragmented in its buyer base. The old assumption that China's AI chip market was a simple two-player game between domestic CSPs and sovereign buyers is outdated. The new reality includes overseas deployment, aggressive hyperscaler spending, and a growing role for second-tier cloud providers and SOEs.

## 2026 Is the Inflection Point for Domestic Suppliers, But the Bar Is Higher Than Chip Performance

The report's field trip findings are telling: despite ongoing capacity expansion, major CSPs continue to face compute shortages, while vendor qualification activity is accelerating. This creates a window of opportunity for domestic AI GPU suppliers, but the window is not wide open for everyone.

The key insight is that competition is increasingly driven by ecosystem maturity, software optimization, and cluster deployment capabilities, not just raw chip performance. A domestic AI GPU that matches NVIDIA's specifications on paper but cannot be integrated into a 10,000-node cluster with stable software support will not win procurement contracts. The CSPs are not just buying chips; they are buying deployable AI infrastructure.

On the supply side, access to leading-edge foundry capacity remains a critical differentiator. Vendors that have received CCATS approval from the BIS for TSMC manufacturing—such as Iluvatar with its 7nm/6nm node—benefit from better cost and power efficiency. Meanwhile, domestic advanced-node capacity continues to ramp, with industry participants expecting a more stable local supply chain to emerge in 2027-28 beyond SMIC South.

This creates a two-tier market in the near term. Vendors with access to TSMC's advanced nodes will have a clear advantage in performance and cost. Those relying entirely on domestic foundry capacity will need to compete on software ecosystem, customer relationships, and deployment support. The winners will be those that can offer a complete solution, not just a chip.

## The Self-Sufficiency Trajectory Is Real, But the Bottlenecks Are Shifting

The report expects China's AI-chip self-sufficiency to rise from 42% in 2025 to 70% in 2030. This is a dramatic increase, but it masks important nuances about where the bottlenecks will be.

The first bottleneck is advanced packaging. As chip performance improves, the ability to package multiple dies together becomes a critical constraint. ASMPT is positioned as an enabler of China's advanced packaging capabilities, and its inclusion in the report's stock recommendations underscores this point. Without advanced packaging, even the best-designed AI chips cannot achieve the performance density required for large-scale training clusters.

The second bottleneck is semiconductor equipment. China's semi equipment import value was $2.4 billion in April 2026, down 6% year-over-year, with imports from the US, Netherlands, Korea, and Japan all declining. Only imports from Singapore increased. This suggests that domestic equipment vendors are gaining market share, but the pace of substitution is uneven. The report's constructive view on Chinese semi equipment players like Naura, AMEC, and ACMR reflects the thesis that the localization cycle in equipment is accelerating, but it also implies that the supply chain for advanced nodes remains vulnerable.

The third bottleneck is talent and software. Building a competitive AI chip is one thing. Building the software stack, compiler, and deployment tools that make it usable at scale is another. The CSPs are increasingly sophisticated buyers. They will not adopt a domestic chip simply because it is available. They will adopt it if it reduces their total cost of ownership, integrates with their existing software frameworks, and can be deployed at scale without operational headaches.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer Yet

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several important questions unresolved. These are not weaknesses in the analysis but rather areas where the future is genuinely uncertain and where readers should focus their own research.

The first open question is the pace of overseas adoption. The report assumes that Chinese CSPs will begin deploying domestic AI chips in overseas data centers from 2028 onward, starting at 3% of overseas capex. But this assumption depends on a chain of events that is far from certain. Will Chinese CSPs actually be willing to deploy domestic chips in markets where they compete with global hyperscalers? Will the performance of domestic chips be sufficient for inference workloads in overseas data centers? And what about the regulatory and security implications of deploying Chinese-designed chips in data centers located in jurisdictions like Southeast Asia or the Middle East? These questions are not addressed in the report, and they could materially change the overseas adoption trajectory.

The second open question is the role of ByteDance. The report incorporates ByteDance's aggressive capex plans, but it does not fully explore the implications of ByteDance becoming the dominant demand driver in the domestic AI chip market. If ByteDance accounts for a disproportionate share of total demand, then the market becomes dependent on a single customer's strategic decisions. What happens if ByteDance's AI strategy shifts, or if it decides to develop its own chips in-house? The report's TAM assumptions would need to be revisited.

The third open question is the pace of domestic foundry capacity expansion. The report notes that industry participants expect a more stable local supply chain to emerge in 2027-28, but it does not provide a detailed assessment of SMIC's ability to ramp advanced-node capacity. If domestic foundry capacity falls short of expectations, the self-sufficiency trajectory could be delayed, and vendors reliant on TSMC access would maintain their competitive advantage for longer.

The fourth open question is the competitive dynamics among domestic AI GPU suppliers. The report highlights Cambricon and Iluvatar CoreX as key beneficiaries, but it does not fully analyze how the competitive landscape will evolve as more players enter the market. MetaX Integrated Circuits is rated Equal-weight, suggesting that the analyst sees it as a capable but not dominant player. But as the market grows, consolidation or shakeout is inevitable. Which companies have the staying power to survive a price war or a technology transition?

## A Decision Framework for Investors

Given the uncertainties outlined above, investors need a framework for evaluating opportunities in China's AI GPU ecosystem. The following questions can help structure that analysis.

First, assess the demand side. Which buyer segments are most likely to drive growth? The report identifies three groups: CSPs (including overseas deployment), sovereign buyers, and a smaller group of AI start-ups and auto OEMs. Each segment has different procurement criteria, budget cycles, and sensitivity to geopolitical risk. CSPs are the largest and most dynamic segment, but they are also the most demanding in terms of performance and ecosystem support. Sovereign buyers are more predictable but slower to adopt new technologies. Investors should focus on suppliers that have demonstrated the ability to serve multiple buyer segments.

Second, evaluate supply chain positioning. The critical differentiator in the near term is access to advanced foundry capacity. Vendors with CCATS approval for TSMC manufacturing have a clear advantage. But this advantage is not permanent. As domestic foundry capacity improves, the competitive landscape will shift. Investors should monitor the pace of SMIC's advanced-node ramp and the emergence of alternative domestic foundry options.

Third, analyze ecosystem depth. The report emphasizes that competition is increasingly driven by software optimization and cluster deployment capabilities. Investors should look beyond chip specifications to evaluate the completeness of each supplier's software stack, the quality of its customer support, and its track record of successful large-scale deployments. A chip that works well in a lab but fails in production is not a viable product.

Fourth, consider the geopolitical scenario. The report's bull case depends on continued US export control tightening. If the geopolitical environment stabilizes or improves, the overseas adoption thesis weakens, and the TAM would need to be revised downward. Conversely, if controls tighten further, the overseas adoption trajectory could accelerate beyond the report's assumptions. Investors should stress-test their positions against multiple geopolitical scenarios.

Fifth, evaluate valuation. The report raises price targets for Cambricon and Iluvatar CoreX, but these stocks have already appreciated significantly. Cambricon's market cap is over $115 billion, implying that the market is already pricing in a substantial portion of the TAM upside. Investors need to assess whether the current valuations adequately reflect the risks and uncertainties identified above.

EN_A: So India just hit a big milestone with ethanol. What happened?
EN_B: Yeah, India crossed 20 percent ethanol blending, or E20, in late 2025, about five years ahead of schedule. That’s a big deal.
EN_A: So what’s next? Can they go to 25 or 30 percent?
EN_B: That’s exactly what this report asks. And the answer is: going from E20 to E25 or E27 is directionally sensible, but it’s really hard. Over 80 percent of cars on Indian roads are built for E10.
EN_A: Wait, so the current E20 is already causing problems for older cars?
EN_B: Yes. The report mentions mileage loss and fuel system complaints. Some owners report a 10 percent drop in mileage. The government just assigned ARAI to study the impact of E25 on the existing fleet.
EN_A: So, it’s doable for new cars, but a big headache for old ones.
EN_B: Exactly. New cars from April 2025 are E20-ready. But for the old ones, you either need retrofit kits or wait for them to be scrapped. And current scrappage rates are too low.
EN_A: What about going beyond E25 to flex-fuel vehicles and E85?
EN_B: That’s a much bigger project. The report says building a national E85 supply chain is a long industrial project. And when you model the payoff, it’s surprisingly small.
EN_A: How small? How much crude oil imports would it save?
EN_B: The model shows that even with aggressive adoption, flex-fuel cars would only save about 1 to 2 percent of India’s oil imports by 2030. If you add blending, EVs, hybrids, and CNG, the total saving is about 4 to 5 percent.
EN_A: That’s not much for all that effort. And it’s not cheap, is it?
EN_B: No. Ethanol costs about 60 to 62 rupees per liter, while the petrol it replaces costs about 55 to 58 rupees. It’s only cheaper when crude oil prices spike.
EN_A: So it’s basically an insurance policy against oil price spikes?
EN_B: That’s the only real argument. Brazil does this, but they started in the 1970s, long before electric cars existed. India now has better alternatives: EVs, hybrids, and CNG, which all reduce oil dependence.
EN_A: And what about the raw material? What’s the feedstock risk?
EN_B: India’s ethanol comes from sugarcane, molasses, and maize. All are dependent on monsoon rains. In 2023, a poor harvest forced India to import maize from Myanmar and Ukraine to make ethanol. That’s burning foreign exchange to save foreign exchange.

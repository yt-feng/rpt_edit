# India’s Ethanol Programme Has Hit Its Ceiling: The Case for Stopping at E25 and Letting EVs Finish the Job

India crossed the 20 percent ethanol blending milestone in late 2025, five years ahead of plan. The obvious next question is how much further this programme can go. Can it move to E25, E27, and then beyond to flex-fuel vehicles and E85? The answer, after a rigorous examination of the physics, economics, and alternatives, is that the easy gains are behind it. Stretching to E25 or E27 by 2030 is directionally sensible but hard. Going beyond—building a national flex-fuel and E85 supply chain—is a long industrial project whose payoff, when modeled honestly, is small, increasingly duplicated by technologies arriving anyway, and justified mainly by a hedging logic that no longer fits India’s moment.

The first thing to understand about India’s ethanol programme is why it succeeded so fast, because the reasons are precisely why the next leg is hard. Blending rose from under 2 percent a decade ago to 20 percent in 2025, ahead of every official timeline, on three favourable conditions: the blend went into engines India already owned, the feedstock came from crops India already grew, and the consumer was asked for nothing. A programme that asks nothing of anyone scales easily. It was not entirely costless—the government puts the mileage loss at E20 at 1–6 percent, but a meaningful share of owners of pre-2023 vehicles report closer to 10 percent, alongside complaints of corrosion and fuel-system wear. The political signal is real, and every step up the blend ladder sharpens exactly these complaints.

Now the easy part is finished. More than 80 percent of petrol vehicles currently on Indian roads were manufactured before April 2023 and are only E10-compliant. E20 alone is already triggering rubber seal degradation, fuel pump complaints, and mileage losses of 6–10 percent in legacy vehicles—issues that sharpen materially with every percentage point higher. The government has only just assigned ARAI to study E25’s impact on the existing fleet, with a 60,000 to 70,000 kilometre durability study. Until those results are available, and until either retrofit solutions scale efficiently or original equipment manufacturers upgrade a much larger share of the vehicle base, the transition remains uneven. E25 appears achievable for new vehicle sales, but only a conditional outcome for the broader parc.

The deeper problem is that the economic case for pushing further than E25 rests on a single idea: a hedge against crude oil price spikes. But that hedge is expensive, narrow in its benefits, and increasingly duplicative of alternatives that India already has. This article argues that the optimal path is to stop at E25–E27, let the existing fleet turn over naturally, and redirect policy and capital toward electrification, hybrids, and CNG—alternatives that reduce oil dependence more durably and at lower cost.

## The Blend Wall Is Physical, Not Political: E25 Is the Ceiling for the Current Fleet

There is a physical ceiling to how much ethanol a conventional petrol engine can accommodate, and it is not a question of policy ambition. Ethanol contains roughly 65 to 67 percent of petrol’s energy by volume, is hygroscopic, and can be corrosive to materials that petrol typically does not affect. At low blend levels, these properties have limited impact. As the blend rises, however, the effects become progressively more significant. Both global experience and India’s own engineering standards converge on a threshold of around 20 percent for the existing vehicle base. This is the blend wall, and it is defined by underlying physical and chemical constraints rather than regulatory choice.

India does have some room beyond this level. From April 2025 onward, all new vehicles are required to be equipped with E20-tuned engines. These engines are designed to take advantage of ethanol’s higher octane rating through higher compression ratios, partially offsetting the energy penalty. As the fleet gradually transitions, the tolerable blend level can edge higher, which forms the basis for a measured progression toward E25, and potentially E27, by 2030. The regulatory framework to support this transition is already taking shape. Fuel specifications up to E30 have been notified, and excise duties have been adjusted to support higher blends. However, this framework remains largely regulatory rather than mechanical.

The underlying question is one of fleet readiness. More than 80 percent of the current fleet is still not fully E20-optimised. In this context, E25 appears achievable for new vehicle sales, but considerably more challenging across the existing stock. The government’s recent decision to task ARAI with a durability study reflects a clear recognition that moving from E20 to E25 is a meaningful technical step, not a routine extension. Until either retrofit solutions scale efficiently or legacy fleet turnover accelerates beyond current scrappage rates, the transition remains uneven. We therefore view E25 as a credible base case for new additions to the fleet, but only a conditional outcome for the broader parc.

The so-what for investors is clear: the ethanol story is not a linear growth trajectory. The first 20 percentage points were the easy part. Every additional point requires new engine hardware, cold-start systems, and ECU calibration for new vehicles, and either retrofit kits or scrappage for old ones. The cost and complexity rise non-linearly, while the benefits diminish.

## Feedstock Is the Invisible Constraint: Ethanol Is a Monsoon-Dependent Fuel

India does not face a shortage of distillation capacity. Installed capacity stands at roughly 18 to 20 billion litres, well above the approximately 11 billion litres required for E20. Capacity is not the constraint. If anything, the system is operating with surplus capacity. The real limitation lies in sustainable feedstock. Independent estimates place this at around 6 to 10 billion litres, while the government’s more optimistic assessment is about 13.5 billion. This gap is critical. It is the difference between a view where E20 already represents the practical ceiling and one where E25 still offers headroom. The answer is not fixed. It shifts each year with rainfall patterns and water availability.

The events of 2023 illustrate this dynamic clearly. A weak monsoon and drought-like conditions hit the key cane-producing states, with Maharashtra and Karnataka seeing yields fall sharply from 120 to 130 tonnes per hectare to closer to 80. As sugar availability tightened, the government restricted the diversion of sugarcane juice toward ethanol. Distilleries pivoted toward maize, but the outcome ran counter to the programme’s intent. India, historically a maize exporter of 2 to 4 million tonnes annually, turned into a net importer for the first time in many years. By 2024, it was importing close to a million tonnes from countries such as Myanmar and Ukraine, even as maize-based ethanol became the most expensive option at roughly INR72 per litre. In effect, a programme designed to reduce oil imports ended up relying on imported grain.

The subsequent reversal in September 2025 reinforces the same point. With a recovery in the monsoon, two consecutive good rainfall years replenished reservoirs and revitalized cane output. Sugar moved back into surplus, and cane juice and molasses returned to the ethanol stream in large volumes. The key takeaway is not about whether the system is cane-led or grain-led. It is neither in a structural sense. It is fundamentally driven by water availability. As blending targets rise, the system becomes increasingly exposed to a single weak monsoon year.

This vulnerability is amplified by India’s underlying water constraints. India has a water stress score of 4.11 on a scale of 1 to 5, compared to Brazil’s 1.04. The higher the blending ambition, the more sensitive the programme becomes to rainfall variability. At elevated blend levels, even one year of poor rainfall can disrupt the balance, forcing a shift toward imports and undermining the programme’s core objective. The so-what is strategic: any investor or policymaker relying on ethanol as a reliable tool for energy security must account for its dependence on a resource that is itself increasingly variable.

## The Economic Case for Going Beyond E25 Rests on a Single Idea: A Hedge—And It Is a Weak One

Confront the fact that quietly undermines the economic premise. At $75 a barrel, a litre of crude costs India about INR 45, and a litre of petrol costs around INR55–58 to make before tax. A litre of ethanol is procured at about INR60–62 today on the cane-led mix, and rose above INR71 during the grain pivot. On every comparison that matters, ethanol costs as much as or more than the petrol it displaces, before a rupee of tax is added. Set aside the additional volume required due to mileage loss, and the arithmetic only worsens.

Strip away the rhetoric and one serious answer remains for why to push further at all: a hedge. Brazil’s genius is to let the anhydrous blend float and to put flex-fuel in 90 percent of its cars, so drivers switch to ethanol en masse when crude spikes and petrol turns dear, then switch back when crude falls. When the Strait of Hormuz crisis drove crude above $100 in May and June 2026, a country with Brazil’s fleet could have leaned on ethanol precisely when oil turned expensive. This argument is serious—it is the strongest case for a flex-fuel future. But the question is not whether the hedge has value. It is whether it is worth what India would have to spend to build it.

Grant the hedge its due, then weigh it honestly. The first cut: the hedge pays only in a crude spike. Because ethanol is not cheaper than petrol at ordinary crude prices, the flex driver switches to ethanol only when oil climbs high enough that petrol’s making-cost finally exceeds ethanol’s. That is a real but occasional event—tail-risk insurance, not an everyday saving. For most of any given decade, with crude in the $60–80 range, an Indian flex fleet would run mostly on petrol, exactly as Brazil’s does in cheap-oil years. You would have built an entire parallel fuel system to capture a benefit that materialises in the minority of years when oil spikes.

The second cut is about timing. Brazil began building this hedge in 1975 with Proálcool, at a moment when there was no alternative to oil for a car—no EVs, no lithium-ion cost curve, no charging networks. If you wanted to insure a transport system against oil, home-grown ethanol was the only instrument available. The hedge made sense because it was the only hedge on offer. That is no longer true, and it is the heart of our scepticism. India in 2026 has alternatives Brazil in 1975 could not imagine. Battery costs are falling year on year; strong hybrids cut petrol consumption 30–40 percent with no new fuel and no new infrastructure; CNG penetration is rising across the fleet. Each of these reduces oil dependence—the very thing the ethanol hedge is for—and several decarbonise more durably as the grid greens. The opportunity cost of a single-purpose E85 supply chain is everything that same capital could do to accelerate these alternatives.

## The Numbers Do Not Add Up: Even an Aggressive Flex-Fuel Push Delivers Only Marginal Crude-Import Savings

This is the calculation that should anchor the investment decision. A stock-and-flow model of India’s vehicle fleet through 2040 reveals the hard truth. Even with aggressive adoption—phasing out new petrol passenger vehicle sales by the end of this decade and replacing them with flex-fuel vehicles—stock petrol PVs would still make up 45–50 percent of the fleet by end-2030, down from 65–70 percent today. The net crude-import saving from flex-fuel cars alone is around 1–2 percent of India’s oil imports. Overall savings reach approximately 4–5 percent, driven overwhelmingly by blending to E25–E27 and the rise of EVs, hybrids, and CNG.

The breakdown is instructive. More than two-thirds of the crude-import savings by 2030 come from blending and the adoption of EVs, hybrids, and CNG. Less than one-third comes from flex-fuel vehicles. By 2035, total savings could reach 7–8 percent, and by 2040, 10 percent. But the flex-fuel contribution remains the smallest component in every scenario. The message is clear: you do not need a flex-fuel system to achieve meaningful oil-import reduction. The alternatives that are already scaling—EVs, hybrids, CNG—will do the heavy lifting.

The global track record reinforces this scepticism. The United States built E85 infrastructure and saw it languish at less than 2 percent of stations. China scrapped its ethanol mandate the moment grain turned scarce. Thailand’s E85 demand collapsed when subsidies were cut in early 2026, with EVs stepping in. Only Brazil sustains high blends—but Brazil started in the 1970s, long before EVs existed, and has far more suitable conditions, including abundant water, cheap land, and a sugarcane industry that is among the most efficient in the world. India does not replicate Brazil’s conditions. It replicates the conditions of every other country that tried and failed to push beyond the blend wall.

The so-what for strategy is decisive: a flex-fuel push is not a bad idea in isolation. It is a bad idea in competition with alternatives that are cheaper, simpler, and already scaling. The question is not whether ethanol can reduce oil imports. It can, modestly. The question is whether the marginal rupee spent on ethanol infrastructure delivers more oil-import reduction than the same rupee spent on EV charging, hybrid incentives, or CNG expansion. On every plausible set of assumptions, the answer is no.

## The Report’s Open Question: What Happens When the Hedge Is No Longer the Only Hedge?

The report leaves several meaningful questions unanswered, and these are precisely the questions that should drive further analysis. First, what is the true cost of a flex-fuel supply chain in India? The report models the savings side but does not fully cost the infrastructure required: new pumps at every retail outlet, dedicated storage tanks, logistics for a fuel that is corrosive and hygroscopic, and the cold-start systems needed for high-ethanol blends in India’s climate. These costs are not trivial, and they are not included in the headline procurement price of ethanol.

Second, what is the interaction between ethanol blending and the grid? As EVs scale, the carbon intensity of the transport fleet depends on the grid’s carbon intensity. Ethanol, by contrast, has a fixed lifecycle emission profile that does not improve over time. The report does not model this trade-off, but it is central to any long-term decarbonisation strategy.

Third, what is the political economy of scaling ethanol when the monsoon fails? India has already seen one episode of maize imports to sustain the programme. If blending targets rise to E25 or beyond, the exposure to a single bad monsoon year grows. The report notes this vulnerability but does not model the probability or impact of a multi-year drought scenario. This is the kind of tail risk that matters for a programme that is supposed to enhance energy security, not undermine it.

These open questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are the natural boundaries of any analysis that takes the evidence seriously. They point to the need for a more integrated framework—one that treats ethanol, EVs, hybrids, and CNG not as separate policy domains but as competing investments in a portfolio of options for reducing oil dependence. That framework does not yet exist in public discourse, and it is the most important analytical gap to fill.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Policymakers: Treat Ethanol as a Tactical Tool, Not a Strategic Bet

The report’s evidence points to a clear decision framework. For investors and policymakers, the question is not whether ethanol has a role. It does, and E20 to E25 is a sensible near-term target. The question is where to place the next marginal rupee of capital and policy attention.

The framework has three layers. First, for the existing fleet, the priority should be to complete the transition to E20 and manage the legacy vehicle complaints through retrofit kits and accelerated scrappage. Pushing beyond E25 for the existing parc is not worth the engineering cost or consumer friction. Second, for new vehicles, E25–E27 is achievable and should be mandated as the standard blend, but the design of new engines should prioritise compatibility with higher blends only as a secondary objective. The primary objective should be to optimise for the alternatives—hybridisation, electrification, and CNG—that deliver larger oil-import savings per rupee of investment. Third, for the long term, the case for a flex-fuel and E85 supply chain is weak. The hedge it provides is narrow, expensive, and increasingly duplicative. The capital required to build it would be better spent on scaling EV charging infrastructure, expanding CNG networks, and incentivising hybrid adoption.

The framework also implies a clear set of risks to monitor. The most important is feedstock volatility. If India pursues E25 or beyond, it must invest in drought-resistant feedstock sources, including second-generation ethanol from agricultural waste. The second risk is technology obsolescence. A flex-fuel supply chain built today may look stranded in 2035 as EVs and hybrids dominate new vehicle sales. The third risk is policy reversal. If a poor monsoon forces a retreat from blending targets, the credibility of the entire programme is damaged, and the capital already sunk into distillation capacity may not earn its expected return.

For investors in Indian auto OEMs, the implications are modest but real. The direct impact on the supply chain is small because ethanol blending does not require fundamentally different components. However, there could be indirect benefits for select players. Maruti Suzuki, for example, remains relatively underpenetrated in electric vehicles. A stronger ethanol pathway may help bridge this gap by supporting its positioning in internal combustion and hybrid segments, buying time for its EV transition. The broader message is that the ethanol story is not a growth driver for auto stocks. It is a supporting factor that reduces the risk of regulatory disruption for legacy ICE and hybrid platforms.

## The Verdict: Stop at E25 and Let the Alternatives Finish the Job

India’s ethanol programme has been a genuine policy success, achieving E20 five years ahead of plan. But the easy part is finished. The next leg—moving to E25, E27, and beyond—requires confronting physical, economic, and structural constraints that do not yield to ambition alone. The physics of the blend wall, the monsoon dependence of feedstock, and the unfavourable cost comparison with petrol all point in the same direction: the incremental reduction in crude imports from pushing further is small, and the cost and complexity are high.

The strongest argument for going further is the hedge against crude price spikes. But that hedge is expensive, pays only in tail-risk events, and is increasingly duplicative of alternatives that India already has. Battery costs are falling, EV TCO is converging on petrol, charging infrastructure is expanding, and hybrids cut petrol consumption by 30–40 percent with no new fuel system. The capital required to build a national flex-fuel and E85 supply chain would deliver larger oil-import savings if invested in these alternatives.

The optimal path is clear: stretch to E25–E27 by 2030, manage the legacy fleet transition, and then stop. Let the natural turnover of the vehicle fleet and the scaling of EVs, hybrids, and CNG do the rest. The ethanol programme has served its purpose as a bridge. It is time to cross the bridge, not keep building it.

# The Summer Rollover Tells a Divergent Story: PJM Is Tightening While ERCOT Softens, and Coal Policy Will Not Bridge the Gap

June 1 marks the moment when power markets stop trading June contracts and begin pricing July. This annual rollover is more than a mechanical calendar shift. It is the market's clearest forward-looking signal of how tight the summer will be, because it embeds both demand expectations and generation fuel costs into a single price move. This year, that signal is unusually informative — and unusually divergent.

The PJM market in the Mid-Atlantic region experienced a May-to-June price increase of 28 USD/MWh, nearly double the historical average of 15 USD/MWh over the past decade. In percentage terms, the jump was 44 percent versus a 30 percent average. This occurred despite only modest increases in the prices of natural gas and coal, the marginal generation fuels. The implication is clear: the price move is being driven by structural tightness in the power market itself, not by fuel cost passthrough.

In ERCOT, the Texas market, the picture is reversed. The absolute price rise of 24 USD/MWh was below the historical average of 30 USD/MWh, and while the percentage increase of 61 percent was slightly above the 56 percent average, the absolute price levels in both May and June were lower than in 2024 and 2025. This is consistent with a market that has entered a softening phase since late 2025, driven by substantial expansion in power supply.

The federal government has responded to concerns about power market tightness by refreshing its support for coal power. Earlier this year, the administration announced a plan to allocate nearly 700 million dollars to support coal power and coal exports, primarily by financing delays or cancellations of scheduled coal plant retirements. The Department of Energy has also issued emergency orders directing coal plants to remain online. Yet the question that matters for investors and strategists is whether these measures will meaningfully alter the supply-demand balance. The evidence suggests the answer is no.

## The Divergence Between PJM and ERCOT Is Not a Seasonal Anomaly; It Reflects Structural Forces That Will Persist Through Multiple Summers

The contrast between PJM and ERCOT is not a one-month fluke. It is the manifestation of two fundamentally different supply-demand trajectories. PJM hosts 39 percent of US data center power demand capacity. Data center buildout in the region has continued to accelerate, with Virginia alone showing steadily rising estimated power demand. The capacity additions in Ohio, Texas, and Virginia during the second quarter of 2026 confirm that the data center expansion is broad-based and ongoing. Meanwhile, power supply growth in PJM has not kept pace. The result is a market that is becoming structurally tighter with each passing summer.

ERCOT, by contrast, has seen substantial new power supply come online. The softening that began in late 2025 has continued into 2026. The effective spare capacity metric, which measures daily peak-hour power demand against effective generation capacity, has remained in a comfortable range for ERCOT. When spare capacity falls below 15 percent, the market is considered critically tight. ERCOT has stayed well above that threshold during the early summer period, even as PJM and MISO have dipped closer to it.

The divergence matters because it has direct implications for power procurement strategies, hedging decisions, and asset valuation. A power purchaser with exposure to PJM faces a fundamentally different risk profile than one exposed to ERCOT. The same is true for investors in generation assets, transmission infrastructure, or data center development. A one-size-fits-all view of US power markets is no longer tenable.

## Federal Coal Policy Will Offer Only Marginal Relief Because the Economics and Physics of Retirement Delays Are Running Into Hard Constraints

The 700-million-dollar coal support plan and the DOE emergency orders are not trivial. They have already pushed a significant amount of scheduled coal retirements from 2025 and 2026 out to the 2026-2029 window. But the marginal impact of further delays is diminishing for two reasons.

First, the plants that have already been delayed once face a higher bar to be delayed again. The original retirement schedules were based on economic and operational assessments that assumed a certain remaining useful life. Once a plant has been kept online beyond its original retirement date, the cumulative maintenance costs begin to escalate. The 700-million-dollar fund is finite, and it is unlikely to cover the cost of keeping multiple aging plants running for years beyond their planned closures.

Second, most coal plants in the United States are old. Many lack extensive maintenance histories because the original retirement schedules did not justify such investment. The physical condition of these plants — boilers, turbines, cooling systems, and pollution control equipment — deteriorates when maintenance is deferred. Keeping them online requires not just money but also engineering work, spare parts, and skilled labor that are in limited supply.

The TVA market in Tennessee provides a concrete example. A total of 4.1 gigawatts of coal generation capacity, all originally scheduled for retirement by the end of 2028, has been delayed. But the retirement schedules beyond that window remain unchanged. This pattern suggests that the policy intervention is pushing retirements out by a few years, not eliminating them. The market should therefore expect a wave of coal retirements in the late 2020s and early 2030s, even with current policy support.

## The Data Center Demand Wave Is Not Uniform Across Regions, and Its Acceleration in 2026 and 2027 Will Widen the Divergence Between Tight and Soft Markets

The report's data on data center capacity additions is one of its most strategically important elements. US data center capacity continued to grow in early 2026, and the report expects a significant acceleration in June and the second half of the year. The state-level data shows that Texas and Virginia led expansions over the past year, followed by Arizona, Indiana, Ohio, and Georgia. In the second quarter of 2026, Ohio, Texas, and Virginia are the leaders.

This geographic dispersion is not random. It reflects the interplay of electricity prices, tax incentives, fiber connectivity, and proximity to major population centers. But the key insight is that data center demand is not a uniform national wave. It is concentrated in specific regions, and those regions are precisely the ones where power markets are already showing signs of tightness.

PJM, which covers the Mid-Atlantic region including Virginia, is the most exposed. The report estimates that PJM hosts 39 percent of US data center power demand capacity. As data center buildout accelerates in 2026 and 2027, the demand pressure on PJM will intensify. The market is already pricing this in, as evidenced by the above-average June rollover.

ERCOT, by contrast, has seen significant data center expansion in Texas, but the supply response has been more robust. The market is softening despite growing data center demand, because new generation capacity is coming online faster than in PJM. This suggests that the divergence is not just about demand growth rates; it is also about the elasticity of supply in each region.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: How Long Can PJM Stay Tight Before Demand Destruction or Policy Intervention Changes the Trajectory?

The report makes a compelling case that PJM is tight and will remain tight for the next few years. But it leaves a critical question open: what is the mechanism that will eventually resolve the tightness? In most commodity markets, sustained high prices lead to demand destruction, substitution, or new supply. In power markets, these mechanisms operate differently.

Demand destruction in power markets is not a clean economic process. Industrial and commercial users cannot easily reduce consumption without cutting production. Residential users have limited ability to shift usage away from peak hours. Data centers, which are the fastest-growing demand segment, have the least flexibility because they operate 24/7. The report shows that commercial sector power demand grew 2.7 percent year-over-year in the first three months of 2026, the strongest among all sectors. This suggests that demand is not responding to price signals in the way a textbook model would predict.

New supply, meanwhile, faces long lead times. Natural gas plants can be built relatively quickly, but permitting, financing, and construction still take two to three years. Renewables are faster but intermittent, and they require transmission upgrades that take even longer. The report notes that solar generation has continued to ramp up seasonally, driven by over 30 gigawatts of year-over-year capacity additions. But solar does not solve peak summer tightness, because peak demand occurs in the early evening when solar output is declining.

The unresolved question is whether PJM tightness will eventually trigger a policy response — such as accelerated permitting for new gas plants, or subsidies for battery storage — or whether it will lead to a more disruptive outcome, such as rolling blackouts during extreme heat events. The report does not answer this, but it provides the analytical framework for readers to assess the probabilities.

## A Decision Framework for Power Market Stakeholders: Four Questions That Determine Your Exposure and Strategy

For readers who need to translate the report's findings into actionable decisions, the following framework captures the key variables.

First, what is your regional exposure? If your operations, investments, or power procurement are concentrated in PJM, you are facing a structurally tightening market with above-average price risk. If your exposure is in ERCOT, the risk is lower, but the market dynamics are different. A national average view will mislead you.

Second, what is your time horizon? The report's data suggests that PJM tightness will persist for at least the next two to three years. Coal retirement delays provide only marginal relief in the 2026-2028 window. Beyond 2028, the uncertainty increases because of regulatory and policy risks that disincentivize long-term investment in coal. If your planning horizon extends beyond 2028, you cannot rely on coal as a backstop.

Third, how sensitive is your business to power price spikes? The report shows that PJM prices rallied into the 90 USD/MWh range with the forward contract rollover. If your business has a power cost structure that is exposed to spot or short-term forward prices, you need to consider hedging strategies that lock in prices before the next summer rollover. The historical pattern suggests that the May-to-June rollover is a reliable signal of summer tightness, and this year's signal is stronger than average.

Fourth, what is your exposure to data center demand growth? The report's data shows that data center capacity additions are accelerating and will continue to do so through 2027. If you are a developer, a lender, or an investor in data center infrastructure, you need to assess whether the power markets in your target regions can support the expected load growth. The divergence between PJM and ERCOT suggests that some regions will be more accommodating than others.

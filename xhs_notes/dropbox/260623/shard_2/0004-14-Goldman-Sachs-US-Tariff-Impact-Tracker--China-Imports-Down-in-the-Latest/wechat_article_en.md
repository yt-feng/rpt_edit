# The Tariff Shock Has Passed, But the Structural Reshuffling of Global Freight Has Only Just Begun

The weekly data from a global investment bank’s US Tariff Impact Tracker delivers a clear message: the immediate, dramatic pull-forward of Chinese imports following the Liberation Day tariff pause last year is now fading. Laden vessels from China to the US fell 4% week-over-week and 6% year-over-year in the latest reading, and TEU volumes into the Port of Los Angeles are projected to turn negative next week. This is not a surprise. It is the predictable arithmetic of tough year-ago comparisons meeting a normalization of ordering behavior. But the real strategic question for investors, logistics executives, and supply chain planners is not whether the weekly numbers will bounce. It is whether the trade and transport system is entering a prolonged period of structural realignment—one that will permanently alter sourcing patterns, freight flows, and the profitability of carriers, forwarders, and logistics providers.

The report’s high-frequency data, covering ocean container rates, rail intermodal volumes, truckload availability, and air cargo trends, points to a regime that is no longer defined by a single tariff shock, but by a more complex, multi-polar uncertainty. The administration’s recent announcement of new tariffs under Section 122, combined with a Supreme Court ruling that has reset the legal landscape, creates a 150-day window of clarity followed by an opaque future. This is not the kind of environment that encourages long-term contracts or steady volume growth. It is an environment that rewards agility, optionality, and the ability to shift capacity across geographies and modes. The companies that will thrive are those that can treat tariff volatility not as a risk to be hedged, but as a source of competitive advantage.

The argument of this article is straightforward: the freight cycle narrative has shifted from a binary “tariff or no tariff” debate to a more nuanced, multi-year story of supply chain reconfiguration. The data in the tracker provides the leading indicators to navigate this transition. But the data must be interpreted with a strategic lens, not a reactive one. The weekly noise is real, but the signal lies in the underlying trends: the divergence between China and Asia-ex-China volumes, the behavior of ocean rates relative to capacity, and the early signs of reshoring investment. Each of these tells a part of a larger story about where the global logistics industry is heading.

## The China-to-US Corridor Is Normalizing, But the Divergence with Asia-Ex-China Reveals a Deeper Shift

The most immediate takeaway from the latest weekly data is that the China-to-US corridor is settling into a post-pull-forward pattern. After weeks of elevated volumes as shippers rushed to beat tariff deadlines, the numbers are now retreating. Laden vessels from China to the US fell 6% year-over-year in the week ending June 18, a sharp reversal from the +10% growth seen the prior week. TEUs from China to the US were down 4% year-over-year. This is the mechanical effect of tough comps: last year’s Liberation Day pause created a surge that is now rolling off. But the sequential decline matters more. It suggests that the inventory restocking impulse is fading, and shippers are reverting to a more cautious, just-in-time posture.

The more interesting pattern, however, is the divergence between Mainland China and the rest of Asia. In the latest week, laden vessel growth from Mainland China was down 5% year-over-year, while Asia-Ex Mainland China was down 10%. TEU growth from Mainland China was down 3%, versus a 9% decline for Asia-Ex Mainland. This is counterintuitive. If the tariff story were simply about shifting sourcing away from China, one would expect Asia-Ex China volumes to hold up better. Instead, they are weakening more sharply. The explanation lies in the nature of the pull-forward. The Liberation Day pause last year created a massive front-loading of orders across all of Asia, but the subsequent uncertainty over Section 122 tariffs has created a wait-and-see dynamic that is suppressing new orders everywhere. The difference is that China’s manufacturing ecosystem is larger and more diversified, allowing it to absorb some of the volatility through domestic demand and alternative export markets. Asia-Ex China, which includes Vietnam, South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan, is more dependent on US demand and therefore more exposed to the current pause.

This divergence has a second-order implication for freight investors. If the Asia-Ex China corridor is more volatile, then the carriers and forwarders with exposure to those lanes will face greater earnings uncertainty. Conversely, companies with diversified networks that can flex capacity between China and other Asian origins may be better positioned. The tracker’s data on ocean container rates, which rose 19% week-over-week, suggests that capacity is tightening even as volumes soften. This is a classic sign of supply-side discipline, but it also reflects the cost of uncertainty: carriers are pricing in the risk of sudden demand shifts, and they are passing that cost to shippers. The result is a market where rates are sticky on the upside, even when volumes are soft.

## Ocean Rate Volatility and Intermodal Strength Signal a Market in Transition, Not Collapse

The 19% week-over-week increase in ocean container rates from China/East Asia to the US West Coast is the most striking data point in the tracker. On the surface, it contradicts the narrative of weakening demand. But it is entirely consistent with a market that is adjusting to structural shifts in capacity and routing. The report notes that “global capacity potentially shifts given ongoing geopolitical events,” a reference to the ongoing disruption in the Red Sea and the rerouting of vessels around the Cape of Good Hope. This rerouting has absorbed significant capacity, pushing up rates even as underlying demand softens. The implication is that ocean rates are now being driven more by supply-side constraints than by demand. For shippers, this means that the cost of moving goods is likely to remain elevated even if volumes plateau. For carriers, it means that profitability may hold up better than expected, provided they can maintain pricing discipline.

But the risk is that the capacity situation could reverse quickly. Should the Red Sea reopen to normal traffic, the release of pent-up capacity could cause rates to collapse. The report flags this explicitly, noting that “should the Red Sea re-open, that could further add to effective capacity.” This is the single biggest unresolved variable in the ocean freight market. The tracker’s weekly data provides an early warning system: if ocean rates start to decline while volumes remain flat, it would signal that capacity is returning faster than demand. Investors should watch this metric closely, as it would have direct implications for the earnings of container shipping lines and freight forwarders.

On the domestic side, the intermodal and trucking data tells a more optimistic story. West Coast intermodal carloads were up 11% year-over-year, and truckload load availability on the West Coast surged 79% year-over-year. These are not the numbers of a recession. They suggest that once goods arrive at US ports, the domestic distribution network is absorbing them efficiently. The truck spot rate on the West Coast was up 50% year-over-year ex-fuel, indicating tight capacity in the drayage and short-haul market. This is consistent with the report’s broader thesis that the transport cycle is near a bottom, and that volume growth could inflect positively in 2026. The key catalyst, as the report argues, is the combination of Fed rate cuts, the lap of Liberation Day, and the bonus depreciation revival in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. These factors could stimulate investment and consumption, driving a sustained recovery in freight demand.

## The Reshoring Narrative Is Real, But It Will Take Years to Materialize in Freight Data

The tracker’s discussion of corporate investment in US manufacturing—citing companies like Apple, Nvidia, IBM, Pfizer, and Johnson & Johnson—is the most strategically important section of the report, even though it is not supported by weekly data. The argument is that tariff uncertainty is accelerating reshoring and nearshoring, which will ultimately boost domestic freight flows. This is a long-duration thesis, not a near-term trading idea. The report’s own data shows that the current freight flows are still dominated by the pull-forward effect and the normalization of China imports. The reshoring story will take quarters, if not years, to show up in port volumes, intermodal traffic, and trucking demand.

But the direction of travel is clear. The combination of tariff policy, the bonus depreciation revival, and the broader geopolitical push for supply chain resilience is creating a structural tailwind for US domestic logistics. Companies that own US-based warehouse, distribution, and trucking assets are likely to benefit disproportionately. The report’s upgrade of the trucking sector last year was based on this logic, and the data so far supports it. However, the report also cautions that the recovery may be “subseasonal” in the near term, meaning that peak season demand could be weaker than historical norms. This is a nuanced view: the cycle is turning, but the turn will be gradual and uneven.

For investors, the implication is that the current weakness in transport stocks may be a buying opportunity, but only for those with a multi-year horizon. The report’s scenario roadmap identifies volume growth as the single most important driver of earnings upgrades. The weekly data provides the earliest possible read on whether that volume growth is materializing. The recent improvement in trucking share prices, as the report notes, may reflect the market’s growing confidence that volumes are stabilizing. But the data is still too choppy to confirm a trend. The next few weeks will be critical, as the year-over-year comparisons become even tougher and the impact of Section 122 tariffs becomes clearer.

## What the Tracker Does Not Yet Reveal: The 150-Day Clock and the Question of Shipper Behavior

The report leaves several important questions unanswered, and these are precisely the questions that will determine the direction of the freight cycle. The most pressing is the impact of the Section 122 tariffs, which have a 150-day expiration window. The administration has not disclosed how it will proceed after that period, and this uncertainty is likely to suppress medium-term freight planning. Shippers do not know whether to restock aggressively, to postpone orders, or to shift sourcing permanently. The tracker provides excellent high-frequency data on what is happening, but it cannot yet answer the question of why. The weekly data shows volumes declining, but it does not distinguish between a temporary pause and a structural shift.

A second open question is the behavior of countries with lower effective tariff rates. The report notes that “countries that now have a lower effective tariff rate may pick up exports into the U.S.,” but it cautions that this may take time to show up in the data, particularly for freight out of mainland China. The tracker’s data on Asia-Ex China volumes is already showing weakness, which suggests that the substitution effect is not yet materializing. But it is too early to conclude that it will not happen. The next three to six months will be critical for observing whether Vietnam, India, Mexico, or other alternatives begin to capture market share from China. Investors should watch the tracker’s country-level data closely, as any divergence would signal a permanent reshaping of global trade routes.

A third unresolved question is the impact of air cargo. The tracker reports that air cargo weights from Asia Pacific to North America were up 3% on a two-week-over-two-week basis, and rates were up 2%. This is a small uptick, but it could be the leading edge of a shift toward faster-cycle logistics as shippers seek to reduce inventory risk. The report notes that “the sequential weight movements are figures to watch to ascertain the need for faster cycle logistics.” If shippers become more reluctant to hold inventory due to tariff uncertainty, they may increasingly turn to air freight for time-sensitive goods. This would be a structural positive for air cargo carriers and forwarders, but it would also indicate that the ocean freight recovery may be slower than expected.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Use the Tracker to Navigate the Freight Cycle

For investors and logistics professionals, the weekly tracker is not a crystal ball, but it is a decision-support tool. The key is to focus on the relationships between the data points, not the individual numbers. Here is a framework for interpreting the tracker’s signals:

First, watch the divergence between China and Asia-Ex China volumes. If China volumes stabilize or recover while Asia-Ex China continues to weaken, it would suggest that the tariff-driven shift away from China is stalling. If the opposite occurs—China weakens further while Asia-Ex China holds up—it would confirm that the substitution effect is accelerating. This divergence is the single most important leading indicator of long-term sourcing patterns.

Second, monitor ocean rates relative to volumes. If rates remain elevated while volumes decline, it indicates that supply-side constraints are the dominant factor, and carriers will maintain pricing power. If rates begin to fall alongside volumes, it signals that capacity is returning faster than demand, which would be negative for carrier earnings and positive for shippers.

Third, track the intermodal and trucking data as a lagging confirmation of the ocean freight trends. If intermodal volumes remain strong even as ocean volumes soften, it suggests that domestic distribution is absorbing inventory efficiently, which is a positive sign for the broader economy. If both ocean and domestic volumes weaken, it would point to a broader demand slowdown.

Fourth, use the air cargo data as an early warning for supply chain stress. If air cargo weights and rates spike, it indicates that shippers are resorting to faster, more expensive logistics to manage uncertainty. This would be a negative signal for ocean freight volumes in the near term, but a positive signal for air freight operators.

Finally, keep a close eye on the Port of Los Angeles forward data. The tracker provides two-week forward projections for TEUs, which offer a glimpse into the near-term trajectory. The current projection shows a decline next week followed by an increase two weeks out. If this pattern holds, it would suggest that the current weakness is a temporary dip, not a trend. If the forward data continues to weaken, it would confirm that the pull-forward effect is fully exhausted and that the normalization phase is deeper than expected.

The freight cycle is entering a new phase, one defined not by a single shock but by a prolonged period of structural adjustment. The weekly tracker is the best available window into this transition. The data is noisy, but the signal is there for those who know how to read it.

# China’s Housing Market Is Not Recovering — It Is Fracturing Into A K-Shape That Policy Cannot Easily Mend

The dominant narrative that China’s property market is staging a broad recovery is misleading. What the data actually reveal is a deepening geographic fracture: a handful of “smart” cities — Shanghai, Shenzhen, Beijing, Guangzhou, and a few tech-oriented second-tier cities — are showing genuine price stabilization, while the vast majority of lower-tier cities are experiencing worsening declines. This is not a recovery. It is a K-shape divergence, and it carries profound implications for aggregate demand, fiscal policy, and the limits of the AI boom as a macroeconomic cure.

The recent housing data from April and May 2026 make this pattern unmistakable. Tier-1 cities have recorded three consecutive months of existing home price gains averaging 0.4% month-on-month. Shanghai and Shenzhen led with 0.6% monthly increases in May. Yet the national average for the 70 surveyed cities fell by 0.26% in May, a wider decline than April’s 0.23% contraction. The headline numbers obscure the real story: stabilization in a few cities is not offsetting the drag from everywhere else.

This matters because the property sector remains the single largest transmission mechanism from asset prices to household consumption, local government revenue, and banking system health. If the K-shape persists — and the evidence suggests it is intensifying — then policymakers face a strategic dilemma. Stimulus that works in Shanghai will not work in a tier-3 city. The AI boom, while real, is geographically concentrated. And the wealth effects from rising stock markets are accruing disproportionately to the same narrow set of cities that are already stabilizing. The policy toolkit, meanwhile, remains largely uniform.

The question is not whether China’s property market will recover. It is whether the divergence itself becomes a structural feature of the economy — and what that means for investors, developers, and local governments that are being left behind.

## The Stabilization in Tier-1 Cities Is Real, But It Is Driven by Local Factors That Cannot Be Replicated

The data from Shanghai and Shenzhen are genuinely encouraging, but the reasons for their improvement are idiosyncratic. Shanghai’s existing home prices have risen for four consecutive months, with a cumulative gain of 1.9% from January lows. Transaction volumes surged: secondary home sales grew 22.7% year-on-year in April, 30.9% in May, and 26.1% in the first 20 days of June. On 10 May, Shanghai recorded 1,664 secondary home transactions in a single day, a five-year high.

Crucially, the composition of demand is shifting. Lianjia data show that transactions of properties priced between RMB 5-8 million jumped 9.3% month-on-month in May, while transactions of properties below RMB 3 million dipped 2.6%. This suggests that the stabilization is being driven by higher-end demand, not a broad-based improvement in affordability. The sharp decline in active listings — down nearly 20% year-on-year — also points to a supply-side tightening that may not be sustainable.

Shenzhen’s story is similar. Existing home prices climbed 0.6% month-on-month in May, with three consecutive months of expansion. Transaction volumes turned positive after a 15.3% year-on-year decline in Q1, reaching 17.2% growth in May. The recovery was partly attributable to the relaxation of home purchase restrictions announced on 30 April. But that is a policy lever that has already been pulled in most tier-1 cities. Further easing may yield diminishing returns.

The point is not to dismiss these improvements. It is to recognize that they are the product of a specific confluence of factors: tech-driven employment growth, stock market wealth effects concentrated in financial centers, and policy easing that primarily benefits high-income buyers. None of these conditions exist in most Chinese cities.

## Outside the Top Tier, the Data Are Unambiguously Worse — and the Divergence Is Widening

The contrast could not be starker. While tier-1 cities logged average gains of 0.4% month-on-month in May, tier-2 city prices fell by 0.19%, a worsening from April’s 0.10% decline. Tier-3 and tier-4 cities remained the weakest, with declines of 0.35%. Among the 70 surveyed cities, only 10 recorded month-on-month increases in existing home prices in May, down from 12 in April and 13 in March.

A small number of second-tier cities showed tentative improvement. Hangzhou, home to DeepSeek and Alibaba, saw its existing home price decline narrow consistently since November 2025, finally stabilizing in May. Hefei, a hub for robotics and memory fabrication, posted its first sequential gain in three years in March. But these are exceptions that prove the rule. They are cities with concentrated tech sectors — the same narrow driver that is lifting tier-1 markets.

The national aggregates tell the real story. Property investment growth worsened to -24.3% year-on-year in May from -20.1% in April, far below the consensus forecast of -15.0%. New home sales fell 13.1% by floor space and 9.5% by value in May. For the first five months of 2026, new home sales by floor space contracted 10.8%, worsening from an 8.7% decline in 2025. Compared to January-May 2021 levels, sales are down 47.7% by floor space and 55.5% by value.

The top 100 developers fared even worse. Their contract sales by floor space fell 20.1% year-on-year in January-May, and by value 15.3%. These figures are 84.3% and 77.1% below January-May 2021 levels. The developer distress that characterized 2024-2025 has not been resolved; it has merely become more concentrated in the firms that lack exposure to tier-1 markets.

## The AI Boom Is a Poor Substitute for a Broad-Based Property Recovery

A key argument in the original report is that the AI-driven stock market rally is not a cure for China’s property bust. The data support this view. From July 2025 to mid-June 2026, the Wind All-A Index rose 33.2%. Tech stocks now account for more than 30% of A-share market capitalization. But the wealth effects from this rally are narrowly distributed.

The stock market gains have been driven primarily by high-net-worth individuals, not household savings inflows. The surge in trading volumes — daily averages reached RMB 2.8 trillion in Q2 2026, up from RMB 1.2 trillion in Q2 2025 — has boosted brokerage revenues and compensation for financial professionals. But these professionals are overwhelmingly concentrated in Shanghai and Shenzhen. The wealth effect that is lifting housing demand in those cities is not reaching the broader population.

This creates a self-reinforcing cycle that exacerbates the K-shape. Rising stock prices boost housing demand in financial centers. Higher housing prices in those centers attract more capital and talent. Meanwhile, cities without a tech or financial sector anchor continue to see outflows of both people and capital. The AI boom, far from being a national stimulus, is acting as a geographic amplifier of existing inequalities.

The report’s authors are careful to note that the nationwide wealth effects from the stock rally have been minimal, consistent with their earlier September 2025 forecast. This is a crucial insight. If stock market gains were broadly distributed, they might support consumption and housing demand across the country. But they are not. The mechanism that is working in Shanghai is simply not available to most other cities.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Policy Transmission Problem

The original report is strong on diagnosis but leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is whether policy can address the K-shape directly, or whether the divergence is now structurally embedded.

The report notes that Beijing may eventually be impelled to step up measures to clean up bad debt in the property sector and speed up fiscal reform to support left-behind cities. But it does not specify what those measures would look like, nor does it assess their likelihood. This is not a criticism — it is an honest recognition of uncertainty. But it is the right question for readers to focus on.

Consider the policy options. One approach would be to attempt to replicate the tier-1 playbook in lower-tier cities: relax purchase restrictions, lower mortgage rates, provide subsidies for homebuyers. But these measures have already been tried in many cities with limited effect. The problem is not a lack of demand-side stimulus; it is that demand does not exist at current price levels in cities with declining populations and weak economic fundamentals.

A second approach would be to accelerate fiscal transfers to support local governments in weaker cities, enabling them to maintain public services and avoid further economic deterioration. This is politically difficult, as it would require richer provinces to subsidize poorer ones on a larger scale. It also raises the question of moral hazard: should cities that overbuilt during the boom be bailed out?

A third approach would be to accept the K-shape as a structural outcome and focus policy on managing the transition. This would mean allowing housing prices in lower-tier cities to continue declining until they reach market-clearing levels, while using social safety nets to cushion the impact on households and banks. This is economically rational but politically painful, especially given the role of housing in household wealth and local government finance.

The report does not choose among these options, and it is right not to. The data do not yet provide a clear signal on which path policy will take. But the question is now the central one for investors and analysts monitoring China’s macro outlook.

## A Decision Framework for Readers: How to Monitor the K-Shape

For those trying to navigate this environment, the report’s data suggest a structured approach to monitoring the divergence. The framework has three layers.

First, track the tier-1 cities as leading indicators. Shanghai and Shenzhen are the most sensitive to stock market wealth effects and policy easing. If their price gains begin to slow or reverse, it would signal that even the strongest markets are losing momentum. The Iceberg index, a leading indicator based on the lowest listing prices, already showed a 0.4% month-on-month decline in early June, suggesting limited rebound in the near term.

Second, monitor the second-tier cities that have shown tentative improvement. Hangzhou and Hefei are the key cases. If their stabilization broadens to other tech-oriented cities — such as Nanjing, Chengdu, or Xi’an — it would suggest that the K-shape is not purely a tier-1 phenomenon. If they relapse, it would confirm that the divergence is deepening.

Third, watch the national aggregates for signs of stabilization in the lower tiers. The most important metric is property investment growth, which is a direct channel to GDP and employment. If investment continues to contract at 20% or more year-on-year, the drag on the economy will intensify. If the decline narrows, it would signal that policy stimulus is beginning to work.

The report’s data also highlight a useful distinction between new home prices and existing home prices. New home prices can be distorted by the rising share of luxury housing and regulatory factors. Existing home prices are a better gauge of true market conditions. Readers should focus on the existing home price data for each tier, not the national headline.

## The K-Shape Is the Story — And It Raises Questions That Deserve a Deeper Look

The evidence is now clear: China’s property market is not recovering uniformly. It is fracturing into a K-shape, with a narrow set of cities stabilizing and the rest deteriorating. This pattern is being driven by the geographic concentration of the AI boom, the uneven distribution of stock market wealth, and the limits of uniform policy in a structurally diverse economy.

The report from which this analysis is drawn provides the data and the framework. But it leaves several open questions that are worth exploring further. How much further can tier-1 city prices rise before they become unaffordable even for high-income buyers? Will the tech-driven demand in Hangzhou and Hefei spread, or remain isolated? And most importantly, will Beijing shift its policy approach to address the divergence directly, or will it continue to rely on measures that work only in the cities that need them least?

These are not academic questions. They have direct implications for portfolio allocation, sector exposure, and macroeconomic forecasting. The K-shape is not a temporary anomaly. It may be the defining structural feature of China’s property market for the next several years.

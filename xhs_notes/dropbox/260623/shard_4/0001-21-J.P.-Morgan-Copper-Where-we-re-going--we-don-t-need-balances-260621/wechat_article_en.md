# Copper’s Next Move Depends on Tariff Escalation, Not Supply-Demand Balances

The global copper market has entered a regime where traditional fundamentals no longer dictate prices. At approximately $13,600 per metric ton, copper has risen more than 40% year-over-year despite the world operating in a surplus refined market. This apparent contradiction is not a temporary anomaly—it is the new reality. The central driver of copper prices over the next 12 to 18 months will not be mine supply disruptions, Chinese demand fluctuations, or inventory levels. It will be the structure and communication of U.S. tariffs on refined copper cathode imports.

This argument runs counter to decades of commodity investing orthodoxy, where supply-demand balances were the primary price-setting mechanism. But the data is unambiguous: in 2025, the global refined copper market showed a surplus exceeding 500,000 metric tons, yet LME prices surged 42%. The disconnect is not a puzzle to be solved—it is a signal that the market's center of gravity has shifted. Understanding why this shift occurred, and where it leads, is essential for anyone positioning in copper today.

The mechanism behind this shift is a tug-of-war between the United States and China for copper metal. Since early 2025, the U.S. has been pulling copper from the rest of the world through an attractive COMEX-LME arbitrage, effectively tightening the ex-U.S. market and stripping China of its historical role as the marginal price setter. This dynamic has forced Chinese buyers to accept materially higher prices to secure necessary imports. The result is a market where prices have both higher floors and higher ceilings, with the potential for a very bullish pinch point as ex-U.S. inventories dwindle.

The next major catalyst arrives by June 30, 2026, when the U.S. Commerce Secretary must update President Trump on domestic copper markets following the Section 232 tariff review. How the administration communicates its intentions around tariff escalation will determine whether copper prices push toward $15,000 per metric ton or correct sharply lower. This is not a question of whether tariffs will be imposed—it is a question of how they will be structured and what expectations they create.

## The U.S.-China Tug-of-War Has Broken the Traditional Price-Setting Mechanism

The global refined copper market is currently in surplus, yet prices are near all-time highs. This paradox is explained entirely by the geographic dislocation of inventory driven by U.S. import behavior. Since early 2025, U.S. monthly refined copper imports have averaged approximately 140,000 metric tons, nearly double the 2024 monthly average of 75,000 metric tons. The cumulative effect is staggering: the U.S. has built a stockpile of roughly 1.2 million metric tons over 18 months, absorbing metal that would otherwise have flowed to other markets.

This matters because the U.S. accounts for only about 6% of global refined copper demand. For the past year and a half, it has been pulling metal from global markets as if it consumed closer to 9% of global demand. That 3-percentage-point shift in effective demand may sound modest, but in a market where even small changes in inventory can trigger large price moves, it has been transformative. The U.S. is not consuming this copper—it is stockpiling it in anticipation of potential tariffs. But the effect on global pricing is identical to a genuine demand surge.

The consequence for China has been profound. Before this U.S. pull began, China was the dominant force in seaborne copper markets. When LME prices rose too high, Chinese buyers would strike—refusing to purchase at elevated levels—which would close the SHFE-LME import arbitrage window and force prices lower. This mechanism gave China effective price-setting power. That power has been severely diminished. In early 2022 and mid-2024, when LME copper pushed toward $11,000 per metric ton, Chinese buying floors were around $9,000-$10,000. Today, China's buying floor has shifted to approximately $12,500-$13,500 per metric ton.

The reason is straightforward. China still needs to import roughly 2.6 million metric tons of copper cathode this year to meet its domestic demand. Concentrate supply remains tight, limiting China's ability to ramp up domestic refined production despite increased scrap usage. With the U.S. vacuuming up available metal, China cannot afford to wait for lower prices—it must import at whatever level is necessary. This has structurally raised the price at which Chinese demand re-enters the market, creating a higher floor for LME prices.

The practical implication for investors is that traditional supply-demand balance analysis has lost much of its predictive power. A global surplus of 360,000 metric tons in 2026, as current estimates suggest, tells you almost nothing about where prices are headed. What matters is the geographic distribution of that surplus and the policy-driven incentives that determine where metal flows.

## Tariff Escalation, Not Tariff Levels, Is the Critical Variable for Price Direction

The most important analytical distinction for copper investors in the second half of 2026 is not whether the U.S. imposes tariffs on refined copper cathode, but how those tariffs are structured and communicated. The difference between a bullish and bearish outcome hinges entirely on whether market participants expect tariff rates to escalate over time.

Consider the two extreme scenarios. In the first, the U.S. announces an immediate, high tariff on copper cathode imports—say, 50% effective immediately. This would close the COMEX-LME arbitrage window instantly, stopping the U.S. import pull. The U.S. would then face a destocking period lasting many months, as the 1.2 million metric tons of inventory accumulated over 18 months would have no incentive to remain onshore. China would regain pricing power, and LME prices would likely correct toward $10,000-$11,000 per metric ton as Chinese buyers reassert their traditional price-setting behavior.

In the second scenario, the U.S. announces a forward-starting, escalating tariff regime—for example, 15% starting January 1, 2027, rising to 30% on January 1, 2028. This creates a powerful incentive for market participants to rush additional copper into the U.S. before the tariff takes effect. The import arbitrage window remains open and becomes even more attractive as the tariff deadline approaches. LME inventories would likely draw dramatically as metal is redirected to the U.S., pushing prices higher and potentially creating a sharp backwardation in the futures curve.

The bullish case rests on escalation expectations, not on the tariff rate itself. Even a relatively high tariff, if it is structured to escalate further over time, maintains the incentive to import now before rates rise. Conversely, even a modest tariff, if it is announced as a one-time, permanent measure, removes the urgency to import and risks triggering a prolonged destocking cycle.

Between these extremes lie numerous intermediate scenarios. The U.S. could announce a tariff but delay its implementation, effectively maintaining the current dynamic. It could announce a tariff that applies only to certain grades or origins of copper. It could signal future escalation without formally enacting it. Each variation would produce different price outcomes, but the unifying analytical principle remains: the direction of price movement depends on whether the policy creates expectations of rising future tariffs.

The U.S. administration has strategic reasons to choose an escalatory approach. Having accumulated over 1 million metric tons of copper inventory, the U.S. has an interest in keeping that metal onshore rather than seeing it destocked over the next two years. An escalating tariff achieves this by making it costly to re-export copper while maintaining incentives for continued imports. It also generates tariff revenue on those imports. With 50% tariffs already in place on downstream semi-finished copper products, the administration has established a precedent for escalation on cathode imports.

## China's Response Creates a Structural Floor That Traditional Analysis Misses

One of the most underappreciated aspects of the current copper market is how China's behavior has fundamentally changed in response to the U.S. import pull. This is not a temporary adjustment—it represents a structural shift in the price level at which Chinese demand operates.

Historically, China's copper buying followed a predictable pattern. When LME prices rose above a certain threshold, Chinese buyers would withdraw from the market, closing the import arbitrage window. Prices would then correct until the arbitrage reopened, at which point Chinese buying would resume. This mechanism kept prices within a relatively narrow range and gave China effective control over the global copper price.

That mechanism has broken. The combination of tight concentrate supply and the U.S. import pull means China cannot afford to wait for lower prices. When Chinese buyers attempted a buyer's strike in late 2025 and early 2026 as prices pushed toward $14,000 per metric ton, they eventually had to re-enter the market at $12,500 per metric ton—a level that would have been unthinkable as a buying floor just two years earlier. The arbitrage window has since fluttered open on dips to $13,500 per metric ton, suggesting the floor continues to rise.

This structural shift has several important implications. First, it means that even if the U.S. import pull moderates, prices are unlikely to return to pre-2025 levels. The Chinese buying floor has been permanently raised by the combination of concentrate constraints and the demonstration effect of the U.S. import competition. Second, it means that any scenario that reduces the U.S. pull—such as a non-escalatory tariff regime—would not necessarily cause a catastrophic price collapse. Chinese demand would still provide support, albeit at lower levels than current prices.

Third, it means that Chinese policy responses could become an increasingly important variable. If Chinese policymakers become concerned about supply security, they may impose more stringent restrictions on copper exports, which would further tighten ex-U.S. markets. Alternatively, they could increase strategic stockpiling, adding another source of demand that traditional balance models do not capture.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Magnitude of the Pinch Point

The analysis presented in this report is compelling in its logic but leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important is the timing and magnitude of the potential bullish pinch point that could push prices well above $15,000 per metric ton.

The report identifies the mechanism: if the U.S. announces a forward-starting, escalating tariff, there could be an immense rush to get copper to the U.S. in the months before the tariff start date. LME inventories would draw dramatically as swap agreements are struck to free up U.S.-deliverable metal. The futures curve would likely punch into a sharp backwardation, creating conditions for a price spike.

But the report does not quantify how high prices could go in such a scenario, nor does it specify the exact conditions that would trigger the pinch point. Would it require a specific level of LME inventory? A particular backwardation magnitude? A clear signal from the U.S. administration about tariff timing? The answers to these questions would significantly affect portfolio positioning.

Similarly, the report acknowledges that Chinese opportunistic exports could ramp up to deliver into any backwardation, potentially capping the upside. But it does not fully explore how quickly Chinese exports could respond, or what constraints might limit their ability to arbitrage the price differential. If Chinese smelters are already operating at capacity, their ability to increase exports may be limited, making the pinch point more acute.

The report also leaves open the question of how the U.S. administration's broader trade policy objectives interact with copper-specific tariffs. Copper tariffs do not exist in a vacuum—they are part of a broader set of trade measures that could affect everything from currency markets to industrial production. A comprehensive trade escalation could reduce global copper demand, offsetting some of the supply-side effects of tariffs. The report acknowledges but does not fully integrate these macro considerations.

For serious investors, these unanswered questions are not weaknesses in the analysis—they are the most valuable parts. They define the range of outcomes that need to be hedged and the signals that should trigger portfolio adjustments.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in Copper Over the Next Six Months

Translating this analysis into actionable positioning requires a framework that links specific policy outcomes to price scenarios. The following decision matrix captures the key variables.

The first variable is tariff structure. Is the U.S. tariff on copper cathode imposed as a one-time, permanent measure, or does it escalate over time? Escalation supports higher prices; a one-time tariff supports lower prices.

The second variable is communication timing. Does the U.S. administration clearly signal its intentions before the June 30 review, or does it maintain ambiguity? Clear communication of escalation supports immediate price appreciation; ambiguity supports continued volatility with a bullish bias.

The third variable is China's response. Does China accept higher prices and continue importing, or does it impose export restrictions or increase strategic stockpiling? Acceptance supports current price levels; Chinese policy intervention could push prices in either direction depending on the specific measures.

Using these three variables, investors can construct a scenario tree:

In the base case—a forward-starting, escalatory tariff with clear communication and continued Chinese acceptance—copper prices should push toward $15,000 per metric ton over the coming quarters, with risks skewed to the upside. This is the scenario the report favors.

In the bear case—a one-time, high tariff with no escalation expectations—prices could correct toward $10,000-$11,000 per metric ton as the U.S. import window closes and destocking begins. This scenario becomes more likely if the U.S. administration prioritizes immediate tariff revenue over strategic stockpiling.

In the bull case—an escalatory tariff with an aggressive forward start date and limited Chinese ability to respond—prices could overshoot $15,000 per metric ton significantly as LME inventories draw to critically low levels. This scenario requires both U.S. policy aggressiveness and Chinese supply constraints to align.

The most important risk to all scenarios is the possibility of a broader economic or geopolitical shock that overwhelms copper-specific dynamics. A sharp global recession, a major Chinese economic slowdown, or a sudden resolution of trade tensions could all render the tariff analysis moot.

For portfolio construction, the implication is clear: copper positions should be sized to reflect the asymmetry of outcomes. The downside is limited to roughly 20-25% from current levels in the bear case, while the upside could be 30-50% or more in the bull case. This asymmetry favors being long copper or copper-related equities, with hedges against the bear case through put options or short positions in copper miners with high cost bases.

# The Oil Shock Reversal Has Cut Recession Risk, But a Deeper Growth Malaise Remains Unresolved

The agreement between the United States and Iran has fundamentally altered the risk landscape for the global economy in ways that go far beyond the price of crude. Our analysis now places Brent crude at $80 per barrel by the end of 2026, with risks balanced in both directions. The immediate consequence is a material reduction in downside economic risk. We have cut our 12-month US recession probability to 15%, bringing it back to the long-term norm. This is a lower estimate than the 20% we held on the eve of the war, and the reduction is justified not only by the oil price decline but also by the labor market's demonstrated resilience since then. Yet this headline improvement masks a more troubling structural picture. The economy is not accelerating; it is merely avoiding a crash. The real question for investors is whether the market is correctly pricing the persistence of below-trend growth and the fragility of the consumer, or whether the positive impulse from lower energy costs will prove more durable than our baseline suggests.

The macro picture is now one of a carefully balanced tension. Lower gasoline prices provide a clear positive impulse to real disposable income, which has led us to nudge our second-half GDP growth forecast to 2% on a sequential basis. This is a welcome revision, but it should not be mistaken for a robust expansion. The economy continues to benefit from the AI boom through elevated equity wealth and strong capital expenditure, yet these forces are not translating into broad-based growth in the way many market participants assume. A significant portion of AI-related capex is directed at imported goods from Asia, and in the case of semiconductors, these investments do not even register in GDP accounts. Meanwhile, the consumer remains under genuine pressure. Even with the drop in gasoline prices, real income defined on a cash-flow basis is likely to decelerate in the second half of the year as the positive impact of higher tax refunds and lower final tax payments fades. We expect real consumer spending growth to hold at just 1.5%, a pace that is supportive but hardly booming. The economy is skating on thin ice, and the thaw from lower oil prices may not be enough to prevent a crack.

## The Recent Payroll Surge Is an Outlier That Masks a Gradually Loosening Labor Market

The most critical analytical error an investor can make right now is to extrapolate the recent payroll numbers into a sustained trend. Over the past three months, payroll gains have averaged an outsized 188,000 per month, a figure that appears to confirm economic resilience. But this single data point is inconsistent with virtually every other measure of labor market health. Household employment has grown far more slowly than the payroll survey suggests. The employment-to-population ratio has trended sideways to lower. Most importantly, our updated slack tracker, which synthesizes ten key indicators of labor market utilization, has resumed its gradual loosening after a period of stabilization. This pattern is consistent with an economy that is still operating modestly below potential. We expect payroll gains to slow to below our breakeven estimate of 60,000 per month in the coming quarters. The implication is clear: wage growth and unit labor cost increases will remain muted, even if headline inflation prints have been elevated. The labor market is not a source of inflationary pressure; it is a source of caution. For investors, this means the Fed has more room to remain patient than the market currently prices.

## Inflation Is Headed Lower, But the Composition of the Decline Will Create Divergent Signals Across Measures

The plunge in gasoline prices in June should produce an outright decline in seasonally adjusted consumer prices, a rare and powerful signal. We expect core CPI inflation to average just 0.17% per month over the next three months. The drivers are straightforward: airfares are declining as jet fuel costs fall, hotel rates are dropping from their post-World Cup highs, and rent inflation is reversing a high-side outlier that appeared in the Northeast in May. This is a genuinely disinflationary episode. However, the story is more complicated for the Federal Reserve's preferred measure, core PCE. Core PCE inflation is likely to remain more stubborn, partly because of ongoing upward pressure from imputed financial services prices driven by the equity market rally, and partly because price increases in software and accessories carry a weight in core PCE that is roughly 30 times larger than in core CPI. This divergence matters. The Fed will see a more benign picture in trimmed-mean PCE measures, but the headline core PCE print may stay elevated enough to keep hawks on the committee uneasy. The market must therefore navigate a period where CPI looks dramatically better than PCE, and where the central bank's internal debates become more, not less, intense. The risk is that the Fed overweights the stickier PCE data and delays a pivot that the real economy needs.

## The Fed's New Communication Strategy Risks Increasing Financial Volatility Just When Stability Is Most Needed

The first FOMC meeting under Chairman Warsh delivered a hawkish surprise that the market has digested with remarkable calm, but the structural implications of his communication approach deserve far more scrutiny. Half of the 18 participants who submitted interest rate projections penciled in one or more rate hikes for the remainder of 2026, and the median projection for core PCE inflation in the fourth quarter of 2027 rose to 2.5%. On the surface, this looks like a clear hawkish tilt. Yet we believe the probability of actual hikes is lower than the market prices. A significant portion of those projecting hikes are non-voting regional bank presidents, meaning a majority of actual voters likely project unchanged or lower rates. More importantly, the projections were likely compiled before the rapid improvement in Middle East news and the plunge in energy prices. If inflation comes down in the coming months, the case for hikes evaporates. But the deeper issue is Warsh's explicit desire to reduce the Fed's role in guiding market expectations. His comments suggest he wants markets to focus on incoming data rather than on how the Fed will react to that data. This is a philosophically coherent position, but it is operationally dangerous. Since the Fed controls short-term interest rates, markets cannot avoid asking how the central bank will respond. A significant reduction in transparency will not eliminate that question; it will simply make the answer more uncertain. Financial conditions could become more volatile as market assumptions about the Fed's thinking become subject to fewer reality checks. For investors, this means the traditional playbook of parsing Fed guidance may become less reliable, and the premium on macroeconomic analysis will rise.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How the AI Trade Can Sustain Itself Without Broader Economic Acceleration

The most significant analytical gap in this report is the unresolved tension between the AI-driven equity market and the sluggish real economy. The report acknowledges that AI-related stock valuations are now high even relative to optimistic views about the economic value AI will create over the next decade. It also notes that AI capex largely consists of imported goods that do not meaningfully contribute to GDP. This creates a paradox. The equity market is rallying on an AI narrative that is not translating into broad-based economic growth, consumer income improvement, or labor market tightening. The report's strategists suggest that ongoing upside surprises in AI capex, strength in S&P earnings, and the absence of private sector financial imbalances should support further equity gains. But this reasoning feels incomplete. If the AI boom is not generating commensurate domestic economic activity, then the equity rally is increasingly a story about multiple expansion and sector concentration rather than fundamental earnings breadth. The report leaves open the question of what happens when the narrative of AI-driven growth collides with the reality of a consumer who is running out of fiscal tailwinds and a labor market that is loosening rather than tightening. Investors must ask themselves whether the market is correctly discounting a future where AI transforms productivity but fails to boost aggregate demand in the near term. The answer to that question will determine whether the current equity resilience is a foundation for further gains or a prelude to a correction.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Divergence Between Financial Markets and the Real Economy

The report provides the raw material for a strategic framework, but it does not synthesize it into actionable decisions. Here is how investors should think about the current environment. The first dimension is the trajectory of inflation. If the decline in headline CPI continues and core PCE eventually follows, the Fed will have room to hold rates steady or even cut, which supports both equities and bonds. If core PCE remains stubbornly elevated, the risk of a hawkish surprise rises, and the bond market will reprice accordingly. The second dimension is the sustainability of the consumer. The report makes clear that real income growth is set to slow in the second half of the year. Investors should monitor retail sales and consumer confidence data closely. A break below the current 1.5% spending growth trend would signal that the consumer is cracking under the weight of depleted savings and normalizing fiscal support. The third dimension is the AI capex cycle. If corporate investment in AI continues to surprise to the upside, it will support equity valuations in the technology sector even if the rest of the economy slows. But if AI spending peaks or if the returns on that spending disappoint, the entire market narrative shifts. The most prudent positioning is to be overweight the disinflation trade and underweight the consumer recovery trade, while maintaining a close watch on the AI capex data for any sign of deceleration. The market is pricing a soft landing, but the report suggests the landing may be softer and more uneven than the consensus expects.

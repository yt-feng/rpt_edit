# Gold’s New Regime: Monetary Policy Dominance Has Replaced the Old Drivers, and the Path to $4,800 Runs Through the Fed

The old narrative for gold is broken. For months, the yellow metal traded on a familiar set of macro relationships—geopolitical risk, oil prices, inflation hedging, and central bank accumulation. That framework has collapsed. Since mid-May, gold has decoupled from oil and re-coupled to Federal Reserve policy pricing with an intensity not seen in years. The result is a market that is no longer driven by what happens in the Gulf or in Ukraine, but by the minute-by-minute repricing of the Fed funds rate. This is a fundamentally different regime, and investors who fail to recognize it will be caught on the wrong side of the trade.

The shift is not subtle. A global investment bank report now frames gold’s outlook through a single lens: the Fed’s reaction function. The base case calls for gold to reach USD 4,800 per ounce by the fourth quarter of 2026, consistent with an indefinite Fed hold near neutral. But the risk case—should the market price in three to four rate hikes—could drive gold all the way down to USD 3,800. That is a 21% range from the current spot level. The asymmetry is real, and it is driven by a single variable that has become dominant.

The implications for portfolio construction are stark. Gold can no longer be treated as a static hedge against tail risks. It is now a dynamic instrument that reflects the market’s evolving view of the terminal rate. The question every allocator must answer is whether they are positioned for the dovish path or the hawkish one. The answer will determine not just returns, but the structure of risk across a multi-asset portfolio.

## The Breakdown of Gold’s Traditional Correlations Signals a Regime Change That Investors Cannot Ignore

The relationship between gold and oil was once one of the most reliable in commodity markets. Both assets responded to similar macro forces: supply shocks, inflation expectations, and geopolitical risk. From March through April of this year, that relationship held. Gold and oil moved together, and the narrative was coherent. Then it broke.

Around mid-May, gold’s link to oil weakened significantly. Instead, a new correlation emerged—one between gold and Fed pricing. The report documents that gold’s linkage to the market’s expectation of the Fed funds rate in June 2027 tightened dramatically, with an R-squared of 0.80 since March, compared to just 0.28 since December. This is not a minor statistical shift. It is a structural change in how the gold market processes information.

What caused the decoupling? The proximate trigger was the US-Iran memorandum of understanding and the subsequent increase in Gulf oil exports. Oil prices dropped sharply, and with them, market-based measures of inflation expectations. But gold did not follow oil lower in a straightforward way. Instead, gold began to reflect the market’s repricing of Fed policy—specifically, the risk that sticky inflation would force the Fed to tighten further. The energy price shock that had been gold’s primary driver was replaced by the monetary policy shock.

The so-what here is that gold’s traditional diversifying properties are under threat. If gold is now primarily a monetary policy bet, then it will behave more like a short-duration fixed-income instrument than a real asset hedge. During periods of Fed tightening or hawkish repricing, gold will underperform. During periods of easing or dovish holds, it will rally. This is a regime in which gold’s correlation to equities may rise, reducing its portfolio diversification benefit precisely when it is most needed.

## The Fed’s New Leadership Has Removed Forward Guidance, Making Gold a Pure Data-Dependent Bet

The June FOMC meeting was a watershed moment for gold. The new Fed Chair, in his first meeting, made clear that the era of forward guidance is over. His statement that “we have missed [our inflation target] for five years, and we are going to fix that” was not merely rhetorical. It signaled a regime in which the Fed will not pre-commit to a path, but will react to incoming data. For gold, this means the metal is now data-dependent in a way it has not been for years.

The implications are profound. Without forward guidance, the market must guess the Fed’s reaction function in real time. Every payrolls number, every CPI print, every core PCE release becomes a potential trigger for a repricing of the entire gold curve. The report highlights that the Taylor rule prescription, using consensus forecasts for core PCE and unemployment, implies a policy setting 80 basis points above the current upper bound. Across three standard policy rules, the upside risk to rates is between 30 and 75 basis points.

But the dovish side also exists. Market-based measures of inflation expectations are declining with oil prices. The US-Iran memorandum and the steep drop in oil have pushed breakevens lower. If core PCE disinflation follows consensus forecasts, the case for a hold at the July meeting strengthens, and the market may reduce its pricing of hikes. The report notes that the current market pricing of 44 basis points of tightening by March 2027 could unwind quickly if disinflationary data appear.

The strategic question for investors is whether they believe the hawkish or dovish scenario is more likely. The report does not resolve this. It presents both cases with equal rigor. But the framework it provides is clear: gold’s path is now a function of the Fed’s data-dependent reaction function, and that function is two-sided. Investors who want to position for gold must first take a view on the inflation and growth data over the next six months.

## Investment Demand Has Collapsed, and the Traditional Support Mechanisms Are Notably Absent

If the Fed is the primary driver, the secondary question is whether investment demand will amplify or dampen the move. The answer, at present, is that it will amplify the downside. The report documents a troubling picture across three key metrics.

First, ETF assets across the US, Europe, China, Japan, and India have reached a new low for the year. Since June 11, ETF investors have been sellers into the rise in gold prices. The one exception—a single day of accumulation on June 19—is worth watching as a possible turning point, but it is too early to call a reversal. Second, futures open interest sits at a 17-year low. Third, futures net long positioning is closer to the low of the year than the high. The combination suggests that speculative and institutional investors are not positioned for a rally.

The report also highlights a troubling structural change. The sensitivity of gold prices to ETF flows has increased dramatically. Over the 2003-2022 period, 1 million troy ounces of ETF investment or disinvestment could drive a 1% change in gold. In the January-September 2025 period, that sensitivity rose to 1.8%. In the October 2025 to current period, it has risen to 2.9%. This means that smaller flows are having larger price impacts, likely because of unobserved investment in less transparent venues like the OTC market.

The implication is that to reach the base case of USD 4,800, ETF year-to-date inflows would need to rise to 5 million troy ounces—just above the year-to-date high. That is not impossible, but it requires a significant shift in investor sentiment. If the Fed delivers a dovish hold, that shift could happen. If the Fed tightens, ETF selling could accelerate, pushing gold toward the risk case of USD 3,800.

## China and India Demand Are Fading, Removing the Two Largest Physical Market Supports

For years, Chinese and Indian demand provided a floor under gold prices. That floor is now cracking. The China SGE premium over Comex has reversed to a small discount after a long period of premiums. Since there is a positive relationship between the SGE differential and China import demand, the reversal suggests that Chinese imports will no longer support the market.

The rationale is straightforward. The Chinese yuan is on a strengthening trend, which means Chinese investors have less reason to diversify into gold as a hedge against currency depreciation. At the same time, evidence points to a possible property market bottoming, which reduces the need for gold as an investment alternative to real estate. The report also notes that Chinese authorities have moved to close informal channels between households and global capital markets, which may further reduce the flow of capital into gold.

India’s situation is even more constrained. Prime Minister Modi’s May 10 speech urging citizens to stop buying gold for a year was followed by a hike in the import VAT from 6% to 15%. The policy is explicitly designed to limit demand for foreign currency. Indian import demand was already on the lower end of the range in April at 1.2 million troy ounces, and there is room to fall further. The one potential offset is a grey market discount of as much as USD 200 per ounce, which suggests illegal imports may rise. But that is hardly a reliable source of support for the official market.

The combination of fading Chinese and Indian demand removes two of the most important physical supports for gold. If investment demand also remains weak, the burden of supporting prices falls entirely on central bank purchases. That is a heavy weight for one pillar to bear.

## Central Bank Demand Remains the One Structural Positive, But It Cannot Compensate for Weakness Elsewhere on Its Own

The one bright spot in the gold market is central bank demand. Since 2022, central banks have been accumulating gold at an unprecedented pace, driven by EM central banks seeking to catch up to their DM counterparts. The report expects this trend to continue for some time, as EM central banks still hold only half the gold held by DM central banks.

The Q1 data show that gold purchases rose to a new record high of USD 38.9 billion in real terms. The World Gold Council’s 2026 survey indicates that reserve managers have a similarly strong inclination to add to gold reserves. The Q1 data also show that unreported official demand was higher than expected, suggesting that some central banks are accumulating gold through less transparent channels.

But there is a limit to how much central bank demand can do on its own. The report notes that official demand has not accelerated as of Q1, and it will not compensate for otherwise slower investment demand. The math is simple: if ETF investors and futures speculators are selling, and if Chinese and Indian physical demand is fading, then central bank purchases alone cannot push gold to USD 4,800. They can provide a floor, but they cannot generate a rally.

The structural positives remain. US public debt is growing at an 8% annual pace, above the CBO’s long-term projection of 6%. The report’s model assumes a roughly one-to-one impact on gold prices from debt growth, which supports the view that gold will rise in real terms over 2026 and 2027. But that is a multi-year story, not a near-term catalyst. For the next six months, the dominant variable is the Fed.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Nature of the Gold-Fed Relationship in a Regime of Uncertainty

The report makes a compelling case that gold is now data-dependent and Fed-driven. But it leaves several critical questions unanswered. First, how stable is the gold-Fed relationship? The report itself notes that gold has shifted drivers before—most notably in 2022-23, when gold diverged from a formerly tight relationship with Fed rates pricing around November 2022, well before the last hike of the cycle. If that pattern repeats, the current correlation could break as quickly as it formed.

Second, what is the role of real yields? The report mentions the 2013 taper tantrum, when gold did not meaningfully diverge from 10-year TIPS yields. But it does not fully explore whether the current regime is more like 2013 or 2022. If gold’s relationship to real yields is more stable than its relationship to Fed pricing, then the current focus on the Fed may be a distraction.

Third, how should investors think about the tail risk of a fiscal dominance scenario? The report notes that US debt growth is a structural positive for gold, but it does not integrate that into the near-term forecast. If the Fed is forced to tighten in response to fiscal profligacy, gold could face a headwind from higher rates even as the debt story supports it. The tension between these two forces is unresolved.

These are not criticisms of the report. They are the natural open questions that arise from a rigorous analysis. Any investor who claims to have a definitive view on gold at this point is either overconfident or underinformed. The report provides the framework; the data will provide the answer.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in Gold Through the Second Half of 2026

For investors who need to make a decision now, the report suggests a simple but powerful framework. The gold outlook for the second half of 2026 can be summarized in three scenarios, each tied to a specific Fed outcome.

Scenario one: The Fed holds near neutral. Core PCE disinflation follows consensus forecasts. Oil prices remain low, and breakevens decline. The market reprices its tightening expectations downward. In this scenario, gold rallies toward the base case of USD 4,800. Investment demand returns as ETF flows and futures positioning improve. This is the bullish case.

Scenario two: The Fed tightens by 30 to 75 basis points, consistent with the standard policy rules. Core PCE remains sticky around 3.1%. The Taylor rule prescription of 80 basis points above the current upper bound becomes the market’s focus. Gold falls toward the risk case of USD 3,800. ETF selling accelerates, and futures net long positioning collapses further. This is the bearish case.

Scenario three: The Fed holds, but the data do not improve. Core PCE remains elevated, but the Fed chooses to wait. Gold trades in a range between USD 4,000 and USD 4,400, supported by central bank demand but capped by the risk of future tightening. This is the neutral case.

The framework is simple, but the execution is not. Investors must decide which scenario they believe, and they must be prepared to change their view as the data evolve. The report’s key insight is that gold is now a play on the Fed, and the Fed is a play on the data. There is no shortcut.

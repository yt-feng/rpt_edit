EN_A: So there’s this new China macro report from a global investment bank titled “All About Tech.” It makes a pretty unsettling point. B, what do you think is the real story here?
EN_B: The big picture is that China’s economy is being driven by tech, but domestic demand is really weak. And the report argues this creates a structural problem. The boom in tech and AI might actually end up hurting jobs and consumption, not helping them.
EN_A: So you’re saying the tech push itself could become a new risk for the economy?
EN_B: Exactly. The report points out that exports are the only bright spot, contributing about 3 percentage points to real GDP growth. But domestic demand, like retail sales and fixed asset investment, actually went negative in May. That’s only the second time in history, outside of the 2020 Covid lockdowns.
EN_A: That sounds serious. But why is the report all about tech? How does tech connect to this weakness?
EN_B: The key is that the government is firmly pushing a structural shift away from property and toward technology. The report shows a stark contrast. Compared to 2019, production of industrial robots has tripled, and semiconductor output has more than doubled. But materials like glass and cement are declining. The stock market shows the same thing. The tech sector index is up over 50 percent, while the consumer sector is down more than 25 percent.
EN_A: So it’s a tale of two economies. What’s the most worrying insight from the report for you?
EN_B: The potential impact of AI on jobs. The report asks a very real question. What happens if AI replaces workers quickly, especially during an economic downturn? History from the US shows that nearly all job losses from automation in routine occupations happen during recessions.
EN_A: So in China’s case, it could be even more complicated?
EN_B: Right. The report notes that labor is relatively cheap in China, and the government has more tools to intervene. But on the other hand, Chinese companies face fierce competition and thin margins. They might be even more willing than US firms to replace workers with AI. If that happens, the housing market, which is already looking for a bottom, could face more pressure from falling rents and home prices as incomes stagnate.
EN_A: That’s a potential vicious cycle. Is the government aware of this risk?
EN_B: Yes. The report mentions a State Council document from June that tries to balance AI development with labor market stability. But it cautiously notes that it’s unclear what concrete measures can create more jobs when youth unemployment is already high and AI is threatening entry-level positions.
EN_A: What about the near-term outlook? Did the report change its GDP forecast?
EN_B: Yes. Because April and May data were disappointing, it cut its Q2 sequential GDP growth forecast from 4.0 percent to 3.5 percent. But interestingly, it raised the Q3 forecast from 4.5 percent to 5.0 percent, citing lower energy prices, faster fiscal spending, and normalizing weather.
EN_A: So they expect a rebound in the second half. Does that mean the full-year target is still achievable?
EN_B: The report keeps its full-year forecast at 4.7 percent. But that’s just the headline number. What it really wants to discuss is the structural problem. If you only target headline GDP, you might mask the ongoing weakness in domestic demand.
EN_A: That’s the core of the third part of the report, right? It talks about three policy channels.
EN_B: Exactly. First, job displacement from AI could disrupt the tech-driven growth model. Second, focusing only on the GDP target effectively caps domestic demand growth, because strong exports already push up GDP, so there’s less need to stimulate the domestic side. Third, the frequent stop-and-go policy shifts between quarters are terrible for building confidence.
EN_A: That sounds like what we’ve seen in the past few years. Good data leads to policy tightening, and then weak data forces a reversal.
EN_B: Right. The report argues that this short-term, target-driven zigzag hurts business and household confidence. And confidence is crucial for consumption and investment. It leaves a key question hanging. If long-term planning becomes the only focus, and cyclical management is ignored, can China really achieve a virtuous cycle between tech-driven productivity and strong domestic demand?

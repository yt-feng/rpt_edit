# The Next Regime Shift in US Rates Is Not About the Level of Yields. It Is About How the Curve Moves.

The most consequential question for fixed-income investors over the next several years is not whether the Federal Reserve will cut or hike rates to a new level. The level of yields is a cyclical artifact, a function of the inflation and growth regime of the era. It tells you little about the character of the market you are operating in. The real question is whether the Fed will return to a toolkit that makes the front end of the curve the noisiest it has been in a generation. The evidence from 36 years of data across four Fed chairs is unambiguous: the toolkit determines the rates regime, and a return to a short statement, minimal forward guidance, and a small balance sheet will bring back a volatility profile the market has not seen since the late 1990s.

This is not a forecast about the path of the federal funds rate. It is a forecast about the behavior of the curve itself. The distinction matters because most investors are positioned for a world that is ending. The era of the anchored front end, where forward guidance pinned short-dated yields and the long end did all the work, is fading. The next chair, whether it is the current one or a successor, will preside over a market where the 2-year yield moves more, the slope of the curve becomes a battleground, and the shape of the curve—its curvature—becomes a source of persistent volatility. If you are managing a duration book, a relative-value portfolio, or a liability-driven strategy, you need to understand this shift now, because the positioning that worked in the guidance era will be the source of the largest losses in the no-guidance era.

The core argument is simple. A global investment bank report analyzed the volatility of the front end, the slope, and the curvature of the US Treasury curve under the last four Fed chairs. The data shows that the level of yields does not separate the chairs. What separates them is the volatility of the front end and the shape of the curve. Under Alan Greenspan, front-end volatility was roughly 110 basis points annualized, and the front end drove the entire curve. Under Janet Yellen, front-end volatility collapsed to 21 basis points, and the long end led. Under Jerome Powell, front-end volatility recovered to about 60 basis points, partway back toward the Greenspan regime. The pattern is not about the person. It is about the tools. Greenspan used a short statement, little forward guidance, and a small balance sheet. Yellen used extensive forward guidance and a large balance sheet. Powell started with the Yellen toolkit and is moving toward the Greenspan toolkit. The next chair will likely complete that move.

## The Level of Yields Does Not Define a Rates Regime. Front-End Volatility Does.

The first mistake most market participants make is to sort rates regimes by the level of yields. It is intuitive: high yields under Greenspan, low yields under Yellen, rising yields under Powell. But that sorting is just a reflection of the macroeconomic cycle. The 1990s were a high-inflation, high-growth era. The 2010s were a low-inflation, zero-bound era. The 2020s are a post-pandemic normalization era. The level of yields tells you what the economy was doing, not what the Fed was doing.

The report makes a cleaner cut. It measures front-end volatility by taking the 3-month yield, measuring its daily changes over rolling 63-day windows, and annualizing the result. The ranking across the four chairs is stark. Greenspan runs the highest front-end volatility at roughly 110 basis points. Bernanke and Powell sit in the middle. Yellen runs the lowest by a wide margin at 21 basis points. The long end, measured by the 10-year yield, barely moves across the four chairs. It sits near 90 to 100 basis points for everyone except Yellen, where it is lower. The chairs differ at the front end, not the back.

The implication is direct. If the next Fed chair returns to a Greenspan-style toolkit, the front end will become the primary source of volatility in the US rates market. That means the 2-year yield will move more, the 3-month yield will move more, and the entire short end will become a trading arena rather than a parking lot. Investors who have built portfolios around the assumption that the front end is a stable anchor will need to rethink their entire approach. The front end is about to become the engine of the curve again.

## Under Greenspan, the Front End Drove the Whole Curve. Under Yellen, the Long End Did. The Difference Is Guidance.

The most powerful insight in the report is not about the level of volatility. It is about the structure of volatility. In some regimes, the front end leads and the rest of the curve follows. In others, the front end sits anchored and the long end moves the curve around it. The report measures this directly by looking at the correlation between daily changes in the 2-year yield and daily changes in the 2s10s curve slope.

When the front end drives, a selloff in the 2-year flattens the curve, so the two move in opposite directions and the correlation turns negative. When guidance pins the front end, the long end sets the slope, and the correlation turns positive. The data is a near-perfect mirror image. Greenspan averages about -0.28. The front end led. Yellen averages about +0.29. The long end led while the front end sat at zero. Powell sits at -0.13, partway back toward the Greenspan pattern. Bernanke sits near zero, the handoff between the two worlds.

The report also compares how much the front of the curve moves against how much the whole curve moves over rolling one-year windows. Under Greenspan, the front end accounted for about 90 percent of the curve's variation. Under Yellen, it accounted for roughly 10 percent. Readings above 100 percent mean the front end moved more than the curve as a whole. Put differently, the front end was the curve under Greenspan and barely mattered to it under Yellen.

The strategic implication is profound. If the next chair guides less, the front end will once again become the dominant driver of the curve. That means the 2-year yield will not just be more volatile. It will determine the direction of the entire yield curve. A selloff in the 2-year will flatten the curve. A rally in the 2-year will steepen it. The relationship between the front end and the slope will become negative again, and investors who have built curve trades around a positive correlation will be caught wrong-footed. The entire relative-value playbook of the guidance era, where you could trade the long end independently of the front end, will need to be rewritten.

## Less Guidance Does Not Just Lift Front-End Volatility. It Lifts the Volatility of the Entire Curve Shape.

The report makes a point that is easy to miss but critically important. Most investors watch front-end volatility. Fewer watch the volatility of the curve's shape. But a chair who guides less hands more of the work back to the market, and that uncertainty does not stay in the front end. It moves out along the curve and shows up in the shape. A step back toward the Greenspan toolkit should therefore lift the volatility of the slope and the curvature, not only the level.

The data already shows it. The report measures how much the slope moves over rolling 63-day windows and annualizes it. It does the same for curvature, the 2s10s30s butterfly, a standard gauge of how bowed the curve sits. Curvature gives the cleaner read. Greenspan ran the highest curvature volatility of the four chairs at about 80 basis points. Yellen ran the lowest at about 44 basis points. Powell sits in between near 52 basis points. The forward-guidance era did not only calm the front end. It pressed down on the whole shape of the curve.

Investors underappreciate this part of the story. If the next chair guides less, both front-end volatility and curve-shape volatility rise together. The report shows that the Greenspan months sit to the upper right of a scatter plot, high on both measures. The Yellen months sit to the lower left, low on both. Powell sits between them, nearer to Greenspan. The scorecard that ranks all four chairs on the three volatility measures at once reinforces the same pattern. Greenspan ranks high across front-end, slope, and curvature volatility. Yellen ranks low across all three. Bernanke and Powell land in the middle.

The implication is that a regime shift toward less guidance will not be a single-variable event. It will be a multi-dimensional increase in volatility. The front end will move more. The slope will move more. The curvature will move more. Investors who are positioned for a quiet curve, who have sold volatility or built tight range-bound strategies, will face losses from multiple directions. The distribution of outcomes for the entire curve will widen, and the strategies that worked in the low-volatility, high-guidance era will become the most dangerous positions to hold.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Speed of the Transition and the Role of the Balance Sheet

The report makes a compelling historical case that the toolkit determines the regime. But it leaves several meaningful open questions that are critical for investors to think through. The first is the speed of the transition. The data shows that the shift from the Greenspan regime to the Yellen regime did not happen overnight. It was a gradual process that unfolded over years as the Fed expanded its balance sheet and deepened its forward guidance. The shift back to a Greenspan-style toolkit could also be gradual. The next chair may not announce a sudden abandonment of guidance. They may simply communicate less, let the balance sheet run off more aggressively, and allow the market to gradually take back the work that guidance has done. The speed of the transition matters because it determines whether investors have time to reposition or whether the shift will catch them off guard.

The second open question is the role of the balance sheet. The report focuses on the short statement and little forward guidance as the key elements of the Greenspan toolkit. But the balance sheet is equally important. A small balance sheet means the Fed is not a dominant player in the Treasury market, which means the market has to absorb more supply and price it more actively. That increases volatility. The report does not fully explore how the pace of quantitative tightening interacts with the guidance regime. If the next chair runs a small balance sheet but still provides some forward guidance, the front end may be noisier than under Yellen but quieter than under Greenspan. The exact mix of tools matters, and the report leaves room for investors to debate the relative importance of each tool.

The third open question is the global context. The report focuses on the US front end and the US curve shape. But the US rates market does not operate in a vacuum. The volatility of the US front end will affect global funding conditions, currency markets, and capital flows. If the US front end becomes noisier, it will attract more speculative capital and increase the volatility of cross-border flows. The report does not address how the global backdrop might amplify or dampen the domestic regime shift. That is a question for investors to explore in their own analysis.

## A Decision Framework for the Noisy Front End

For the investor who wants to act on this insight, the report provides a clear decision framework. The first step is to assess the probability of a regime shift. The key variable is not the level of yields or the path of the economy. It is the toolkit. If the next Fed chair uses a short statement, provides little forward guidance, and runs a small balance sheet, the regime shift is highly probable. If the next chair continues with extensive guidance and a large balance sheet, the current regime will persist. The investor's job is to monitor the communication style of the Fed and the trajectory of the balance sheet, not the dots or the median forecast.

The second step is to position for the shift. The report suggests that a move back toward the Greenspan toolkit argues for owning curve-shape and front-end volatility rather than fading it. It argues for less conviction in the tight, range-bound trading that defined the guidance era. If the next chair guides less, realized volatility in the front end and in curve shape rises from today's compressed levels, and positioning built for a quiet curve carries the most risk. The report would rather own low front-end and curvature volatility here than lean against it. This speaks to the distribution of outcomes, not the level of yields.

The third step is to identify the specific trades that express the view. The report suggests maintaining SFRZ6M7 curve flatteners and long 2-year UST SOFR swap spreads. These trades are not about predicting the level of yields. They are about positioning for the front end to lead the curve and for curve shape to become more volatile. The risks are clear: a fiscal stimulus that justifies currently elevated levels of near-term inflation expectations, or firmer funding conditions than expected. But the direction of the trade is consistent with the regime shift.

The fourth step is to monitor the exit. If the regime shift does not materialize, if the next chair continues with extensive guidance and a large balance sheet, the current low-volatility regime will persist, and the trades will need to be unwound. The investor must be disciplined about the entry level, the stop, and the target. The report provides specific levels for both trades. The discipline of the trade is as important as the insight.

## The Next Frontier: Understanding How the Noisy Front End Changes Every Asset Class

The report focuses on the US Treasury market, but the implications extend far beyond it. A noisy front end in the US will change the behavior of every asset class that is sensitive to short-term interest rates. Currency markets will become more volatile as the carry trade becomes more risky. Equity markets will become more sensitive to the front end as the discount rate for near-term cash flows becomes more uncertain. Credit markets will see wider spreads as the cost of hedging interest rate risk rises. The regime shift in the front end is not a fixed-income story. It is a macro story.

They show the data in a way that makes the regime shift feel immediate and real.

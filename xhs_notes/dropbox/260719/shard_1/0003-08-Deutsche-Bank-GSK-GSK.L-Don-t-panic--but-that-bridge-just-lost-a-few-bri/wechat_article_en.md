## The Mixed Trial Data Reveals a Reproducibility Problem That Was Already Visible in the P2X3 Class

The CALM-1 and CALM-2 trials enrolled 825 and 975 patients respectively, testing 25 mg and 50 mg twice-daily doses of camlipixant against placebo. The primary endpoint was 24-hour cough frequency at 12 weeks. CALM-1 hit. CALM-2 did not. The company has not yet disclosed the numerical data, but the binary outcome is unambiguous: two identical trials produced diverging results, and the asset is dead.

This pattern is not new for P2X3 antagonists. Merck’s gefapixant, the only approved drug in this class, succeeded in its COUGH-1 and COUGH-2 trials but received two Complete Response Letters from the U.S. FDA, which cited insufficient evidence of effectiveness. The approved doses in Europe, Japan, and Switzerland are 45 mg twice daily, yet even at that dose the placebo-adjusted reduction in 24-hour cough frequency was only about 15% in both trials. The 15 mg dose showed no separation from placebo. The drug’s commercial prospects remain constrained by a narrow efficacy-safety window dominated by dose-dependent dysgeusia, or taste disturbance, which reached 21% at the 45 mg dose and drove discontinuation rates of 15-20%.

GSK had positioned camlipixant as a potential best-in-class alternative with a superior safety profile. In its Phase 2b trial, camlipixant showed placebo-adjusted cough reductions of 21-34% across doses, with dysgeusia rates of only 5-7% and zero drug-related discontinuations. That profile was compelling enough to justify a $2 billion acquisition of Bellus Health in 2023. But Phase 3 replication has now failed, and the entire P2X3 class now faces a credibility question: can any molecule in this mechanism consistently demonstrate robust, reproducible efficacy across multiple large-scale trials?

Bayer’s eliapixant met its primary endpoint in Phase 2b but was discontinued due to safety risks. Shionogi’s sivopixant failed to meet its primary endpoint in Phase 2. Hengrui’s HRS-2261 has been removed from the pipeline. The only approved asset, gefapixant, has marginal efficacy and significant tolerability issues. The class is not dead, but its commercial ceiling is now far lower than the peak sales estimates that justified GSK’s acquisition price and the market’s prior expectations.

## The Competitive Landscape in Chronic Cough Now Belongs to Merck by Default

With camlipixant terminated, the chronic cough market effectively consolidates around Merck’s gefapixant. That is not a strong competitive position for Merck, but it is an uncontested one. Gefapixant is approved in Europe, Japan, and Switzerland, but has failed twice to gain U.S. approval. The FDA’s concerns about insufficient efficacy are unlikely to be resolved without new data, and Merck has not indicated plans for additional Phase 3 trials.

The remaining pipeline is thin. Nocion Therapeutics’ taplucainium, a sodium channel blocker, is in Phase 2 recruitment for chronic cough but has no clinical data yet. Trevi Therapeutics’ haduvio, a kappa/mu-opioid receptor agonist, is being pursued specifically in IPF-related chronic cough, a narrower indication. Every other asset in the chronic cough landscape has either failed to meet its primary endpoint, been discontinued for safety reasons, or been deprioritized by its sponsor.

This means the chronic cough market will remain small and fragmented for the foreseeable future. Peak sales estimates of £800 million for camlipixant, and similar estimates for gefapixant, now look optimistic. The addressable patient population is large, but the efficacy bar for regulatory approval has proven difficult to clear consistently, and the tolerability bar for commercial adoption is even higher. GSK’s exit from this market is strategically sensible given the data, but it removes a tangible growth option from the portfolio at a time when options are scarce.

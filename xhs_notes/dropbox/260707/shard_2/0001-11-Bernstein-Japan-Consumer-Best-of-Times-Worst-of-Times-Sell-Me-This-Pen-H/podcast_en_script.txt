EN_A: A new report on Japan’s consumer sector starts with a quote from Charles Dickens: “It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.” That sounds like a contradiction. What’s it really about?
EN_B: It’s about a consumer market that is splitting apart. On one side, you have the wealthy. On the other, the mass market. And in the middle, the largest group of consumers is now using AI to make their shopping decisions. The report argues that Japanese brands are perfectly positioned to win in this new world.
EN_A: So what is the big question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It asks: when AI becomes a consumer’s shopping assistant, do traditional brand persuasion techniques still work? Brands that rely on marketing hype and artificial scarcity to charge a premium—will AI just call them out?
EN_A: Wait, AI calling out brands? That sounds a bit futuristic. How does the report explain it?
EN_B: There’s a great analogy in the report: “Sell me this pen.” In the old world, a salesperson finds the customer’s need and lets the pen fill it. But now, the buyer might be an AI. It already knows what it needs. It ignores the pitch. It just reads the spec sheet and matches the price.
EN_A: So that means the era of brands charging a premium just for storytelling and hype might be coming to an end?
EN_B: Exactly. The report says this shift is early, but the direction is one-way. In a K-shaped economy, the squeezed middle class no longer accepts a trade-off between value and quality. They demand both. And AI helps them enforce it.
EN_A: So which brands are best positioned to win in this new world?
EN_B: The report points to Japan’s “global compounders.” These are companies that survived three decades of domestic deflation. They were forced to invest heavily in product quality while keeping margins high. For example, Uniqlo puts about 31% of its price into the product itself. GAP? Only about 15%.
EN_A: So when an AI starts comparing specs, Uniqlo’s advantage becomes obvious. Which specific Japanese brands does the report like?
EN_B: They give clear ratings. Fast Retailing, the parent of Uniqlo, is rated “Outperform.” So are ASICS and Food & Life. Ryohin Keikaku, which runs Muji, is rated “Market-Perform.” That’s because its execution in Western markets still needs to be proven.
EN_A: Does the report leave any questions unanswered? For example, what about luxury brands? Can AI touch them?
EN_B: The report does touch on the upper part of the K-shaped market—luxury goods. It argues that when a price crosses a certain threshold, value moves from the product to scarcity and meaning. AI may not be able to penetrate that, because the consumer isn’t buying specs.

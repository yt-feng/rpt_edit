# The K-Shaped Consumer Divide Is Reshaping US Retail — And Most Observers Are Still Reading the Wrong Signals

The US retail landscape is no longer a single market. It has become two parallel economies operating under the same roof, and the divergence is accelerating. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, spending growth across income cohorts has decoupled in ways that pre-COVID data never anticipated. The lowest-income households have seen cumulative real spending growth of roughly 8 percent between 2018 and mid-2024, while the highest-income group posted nearly double that rate at approximately 17 percent. The gap is even more pronounced when measured by education level.

This is not a temporary blip. It is a structural shift driven by sustained inflation in essentials, a wealth effect that has disproportionately benefited the top quintile, and a downward spiral for cash-constrained consumers who now spend 30 to 40 percent more than they earn. Retailers that continue to manage their businesses as if the consumer base is homogeneous are making a strategic error. The question is not whether the K-shaped economy exists — it does — but which retailers are positioned to win in each branch of the K.

The implications for portfolio construction are direct. Value-oriented retailers like Walmart and Costco are best positioned to gain share in a persistent inflation environment, while dollar stores may benefit from trade-down behavior among higher-income consumers seeking value. Meanwhile, retailers with heavier discretionary exposure — Five Below, Dollar Tree, and Target — will likely see their fortunes tied to a recovery in discretionary spending that may take longer to materialize than consensus expects.

The critical insight for observers is this: the average consumer data point is misleading. It hides the reality that low-income and low-education cohorts are experiencing completely different macroeconomic circumstances than the general population. Understanding which retailers serve which branch of the K is now a prerequisite for generating alpha in US broadlines and hardlines.

## The Post-COVID Decoupling Is Not Cyclical — It Is Structural, and It Will Take Years to Reverse

Before the pandemic, retail spending growth across income groups moved in a tightly clustered pattern. Each cohort grew at a similar rate, albeit from a different base. That changed abruptly with COVID. The initial shock, followed by stimulus payments, enhanced SNAP benefits, and then post-COVID inflation, created a permanent divergence.

Analysis by Federal Reserve researchers using Numerator data shows that the decoupling is visible across both income and education dimensions. Between 2018 and mid-2024, the lowest-income group's real retail spending barely grew on a cumulative basis from mid-2020 to mid-2024. The only period of significant growth for this cohort came in 2021, driven by the disproportionate impact of stimulus checks and SNAP increases. Once that support faded, real spending stalled.

The structural nature of this divergence matters because it cannot be reversed quickly. The most obvious path to normalization is disinflation and the return of purchasing power to lower-income households. But that process is slow and uncertain. The alternative path — normalization "from the top" through a market collapse and wealth destruction — is neither desirable nor likely in the near term. Observers should therefore plan for a multiyear period in which the K-shaped dynamic remains the dominant feature of US retail demand.

This has a direct implication for revenue growth assumptions. Retailers serving the lower branch of the K cannot rely on the same organic volume growth that their peers serving higher-income customers can. Their addressable market is effectively shrinking in real per capita terms. Conversely, retailers serving the upper branch benefit from a customer base that has seen wealth growth far outpace inflation, enabling continued discretionary spending and the ability to absorb price increases.

## Low-Income Consumers Are in a Downward Spiral That Inflation in Essentials Continues to Worsen

The data on low-income household finances is stark. On average, households in the lowest income quintile spend approximately 30 to 40 percent more than they earn. This is not a new phenomenon — it was true before COVID — but the gap has widened since 2019. Meanwhile, the wealthiest cohort has been able to save and invest, with the growth in assets for the top quintile far outstripping that of lower-income groups.

The mechanism driving the downward spiral is straightforward. Low-income consumers are cash constrained and must prioritize everyday essentials: food at home, housing, and healthcare. These categories have experienced the most significant inflation since COVID. Housing costs, in particular, have been a major driver of expenditure increases for households earning under $50,000, many of whom are renters. Staple foods, transportation, and healthcare have also contributed meaningfully.

As a result, the proportion of total expenditures going to essentials has increased for low-income households since 2019, while the share allocated to discretionary categories — entertainment, education, and savings — has declined. This is not a choice; it is a necessity. The inflationary pressure in essentials has also made low-income consumers more susceptible to further inflation, creating a self-reinforcing cycle.

Critically, this dynamic is invisible in aggregate inflation data. The headline CPI masks significant variation across income cohorts. Wealthier households spend a greater proportion of their expenditures on discretionary categories, many of which have been deflationary until recently. As a result, the highest-income quintile has experienced below-average inflation, while the lowest quintile has faced sustained above-average inflation. The gap between the two has been persistent since COVID and shows no sign of narrowing.

For retailers, this means that the same macroeconomic environment is producing opposite effects on different customer segments. A retailer whose customer base is skewed toward the lower quintile is facing a demand environment that is fundamentally weaker than what the headline numbers suggest. A retailer serving the upper quintile is operating in a much more favorable demand environment.

## Inflation Creates a Tailwind for Value-Oriented Retailers but a Margin Headwind for Discretionary-Focused Players

Inflation is generally positive for retailer top lines, particularly for value-oriented players. When inflation rises and other retailers take price, value-oriented retailers remain cheaper by comparison. This creates a natural share gain opportunity. Historical correlations between inflation and same-store sales at Walmart, Sam's Club, Costco, and Target confirm this pattern. Comp sales at these retailers show strong positive correlations with US food inflation and overall inflation.

Walmart and Costco are best positioned to gain share in an environment with persistent inflation. Their everyday low-price positioning becomes more attractive as consumers across income cohorts seek value. Dollar stores could also benefit from higher-income consumers trading down, a trend that has been visible over the last 12 months.

However, the mix shift toward staples is a double-edged sword. Since 2019, Walmart US, Target, and Costco have each seen their staples mix increase by 679, 770, and 118 basis points respectively as a percentage of net sales. For more discretionary-oriented retailers like Target, this mix shift has had a negative impact on gross margins. Staples carry lower margins than discretionary categories, so a higher proportion of staples in the sales mix compresses overall profitability.

The implication is that retailers with higher discretionary exposure are facing a double squeeze: weaker demand from their core customer base and margin pressure from the mix shift toward lower-margin categories. This is the current reality for Five Below, Dollar Tree, and Target.

Looking ahead, the path to recovery for discretionary spending is likely to be led by smaller-ticket items and higher-income consumers first. As inflation normalizes and consumer spending power recovers, the discretionary mix should improve. Retailers with more discretionary exposure in their coverage will be better positioned when that recovery arrives. But the timing is uncertain, and the recovery may be slower than the market expects.

## The Report Leaves One Critical Question Unanswered: How Long Will the K-Shaped Dynamic Persist?

The report provides a thorough analysis of the current state of the K-shaped economy and its implications for retailers. But it does not fully resolve the question of duration. How long will the current divergence persist? What conditions would cause it to narrow?

The report identifies two potential paths to normalization: disinflation and the return of purchasing power, or a market collapse that destroys wealth at the top. The first path is slow and uncertain. The second is undesirable and unlikely to be policy-driven. But there may be a third path that the report does not explore in depth: structural changes in the labor market that improve wage growth for lower-income workers.

If wage growth at the bottom of the income distribution accelerates meaningfully, it could narrow the gap without requiring disinflation or a wealth shock. The tight labor market of the post-COVID period has already produced some wage gains for lower-income workers, but these have been more than offset by inflation in essentials. If the balance shifts — if wage growth continues while inflation moderates — the downward spiral could begin to reverse.

Alternatively, policy changes such as expanded SNAP benefits, housing subsidies, or tax credits for low-income households could provide a direct boost to real spending power. The report notes that stimulus and SNAP increases were the primary drivers of low-income spending growth in 2021, suggesting that policy interventions can have a meaningful impact.

Observers should monitor these variables closely. The duration of the K-shaped dynamic will determine which retail strategies succeed over the next two to three years. A prolonged divergence favors value-oriented retailers with exposure to higher-income consumers. A faster normalization would benefit discretionary-focused players.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the K-Shaped Retail Landscape

The report's analysis can be synthesized into a practical decision framework. The framework has three dimensions: customer income exposure, product mix sensitivity, and balance sheet resilience.

First, assess a retailer's customer income exposure. Retailers whose core customer base is skewed toward the lowest income quintile face a structurally weaker demand environment. Their same-store sales growth will be constrained by the downward spiral in low-income household finances. Retailers serving the upper quintile benefit from a customer base with growing wealth and purchasing power. Walmart and Costco serve a broad cross-section but are well-positioned to capture trade-down from higher-income consumers. Dollar General and Dollar Tree serve a predominantly lower-income customer base and face headwinds from the K-shaped divergence, though they may benefit from trade-down from middle-income consumers.

Second, evaluate product mix sensitivity. Retailers with a higher proportion of discretionary categories in their sales mix are exposed to margin compression from the staples mix shift. This is a near-term headwind. Over time, as discretionary spending recovers, these retailers will benefit from margin expansion. The timing of that recovery is uncertain, so observers should demand a margin of safety in valuations for discretionary-heavy retailers.

Third, consider balance sheet resilience. Retailers with strong balance sheets and the ability to invest through the cycle will be better positioned to gain share during the K-shaped period. They can afford to absorb margin pressure on staples while maintaining price investments and store-level improvements. Weaker balance sheets may force retailers to cut costs or raise prices, which could accelerate share loss.

Applying this framework, Walmart and Costco emerge as the most resilient names in the current environment. Their value positioning, broad customer base, and strong balance sheets give them multiple levers to navigate the K-shaped economy. Dollar General's low-income customer base is a structural headwind, but its value proposition could attract trade-down traffic. Target, Five Below, and Dollar Tree face the most challenging combination of customer income exposure and discretionary mix sensitivity.

We are uranium & nuclear bulls (e.g., Bernstein 2026 Uranium/Nuclear Outlook: Glow up or blow up?) and one catalyst around uranium demand we hear is the success of SMRs (Small Modular Reactors). In this note we review and rank the 79 SMR projects in the pipeline, highlight potential commercial winners, but ultimately see other catalysts as more important to uranium demand over the next decade. GEV (Outperform) and CCJ (Outperform) are on the leaderboard for commercial SMR.

We don't think so. We lay our reasons below -

\- If the load is large, urgent, and 24/7, the first nuclear answer is likely the existing fleet: life extensions, uprates, restarts, and eventually large reactors. SMR still matter, but mostly as a second-wave option for smaller grids, phased industrial loads, coal-site replacement, etc.

\- We introduce our weighted scorecard across licensing, financing, fuel, siting, supply chain and engagement (Exhibit 4- Exhibit 7).

\- We like SMR projects with high licensing and financing scores, but those projects are mostly government-backed nuclear or US defense and DOE supported demo (Exhibit 8).

\- Fuel supply is a gating Item, not a footnote. Many advanced SMRs require HALEU, TRISO, metallic, or molten-salt fuel systems. We rank these fuels from high to low supply certainty and find that for most SMR designs, fuel availability is a gating constraint, not a secondary consideration (Exhibit 11).

\- Lastly, we discuss the unavoidable math problem: If SMRs cost \~2-3x an AP1000 on a per-kW basis yet deliver less than 1/3 the capacity, the economics demand justification (Exhibit 12). SMRs may retain valid use cases: small grid deployment, coal-site replacement, remote power, defense, and industrial heat, but these are far narrower theses than the AI trade that the market is pricing in.

The near-term trade is existing nuclear: uprates, restarts and life extension. That is where the megawatts are real, the assets are known and the customer need is immediate. Constellation Energy (CEG) and Vistra (VST) are the first major beneficiaries and are working on several large restart projects. Positive news about commercial deployment of the BWRX-300 is a clear benefit for GEV, given its ownership of the GEV Hitachi Nuclear Energy joint ventures that are leading BWRX-300 projects such as the first unit now under construction at Ontario Power Generation (OPG)'s Darlington site.

The longer-term trade is uranium because CCJ and KAP do not need SMRs to work to win. SMRs will not be dead. They are just not the answer to every AI power question.

## THE SMR LANDSCAPE

Some definitions. We follow convention and argue that SMRs (Small Modular Reactors) are fission-based nuclear power plants intended to generate electricity at a scale of $< 300$ MW (specifically MWe). Recall that typical reactors are 1,000 MW (1 GW) such as the Westinghouse AP1000. Also recall that MWe is megawatts of electricity equivalent - a measure of power (the ability to generate energy, in this case electrical energy). We also quote MWth or megawatts of thermal equivalent (the ability to generate thermal energy). A good rule of thumb is a reactor's MWe is roughly a third of its MWth.

A large number of SMR projects exist across the world in different stages of maturity (Exhibit 3). We would expect the vast majority to either not move forward or to move forward in the mid 2030s at best.

We highlight that SMRs can be non-commercial (tests, pilots, demonstrations) or commercial (generating electricity from which revenue is earned). As equity investors, our focus is on the latter.

We also remind investors that non-commercial SMR technology has been deployed at scale by various militaries (see the appendix on nuclear vessels). There are currently two civilian operable projects: Rosatom's KLT-40s (providing power to a remote community by leveraging nuclear icebreaker technology) and CNNC's HTR-PM (a demonstration plant initially that has been connected to the grid).

The NEA (Nuclear Energy Agency) was founded by the predecessor of the OECD and is an intergovernmental agency with 34 countries holding membership.

The NEA hosts an SMR dashboard that follows all but a few of the global SMR projects we are aware of. Currently the dashboard hosts details for 79 projects (Exhibit 4) totaling 30+ GWth of projects. If we use a typical rule of thumb of 3:1 GWth to GWe and imagine that SMR nuclear capex is \~\$10 bln/GW, the implication is \$100 bln of project capex to deliver the equivalent of 10 typical-scale power plants. We remind investors that commercial SMR doesn't currently exist (we discuss military SMRs in an appendix).

We highlight two projects which are not yet included in NEA: NuScale's RoPower and multiple BWRX-300 fleet projects beyond Darlington.

## COST MATTERS, OPPORTUNITY ALSO MATTERS

The purpose of SMRs can be two-fold: (1) a bespoke project suitable for a unique application (i.e., remote small community lacking other forms of electricity) or (2) a game-changing NOAK (Nth of a Kind) technology where the initial investments (i.e., high cost projects) pave the way to a competitive source of electricity. For the second case to hold, we would argue (1) a commitment to a significant number of projects (10+?), and (2) a modular repeatable proven design. ## SMR FUEL DEMAND CAN DRIVE URANIUM DEMAND UPWARDS

We highlighted the nuclear fuel cycle in Americas Energy & Transition: Top pushbacks to our Cameco initiation. Below is the recap of the enrichment step and how that implies to uranium.

Massive adoption of SMR will create more uranium demand. And here we re-introduce our favorite orange juice analogy.

We start with an analogy of making orange juice from oranges. If I want a glass of orange juice, there is a trade off how to fill it. I can gently squeeze a large number of oranges, or I can greedily and forcefully squeeze a smaller number of oranges. I can trade off the material into the process against the work or effort of squeezing.

The same thing happens in enrichment, causing a potential opportunity for Cameco, which we discuss.

Natural uranium consists of $\sim 0.7\%$ of the U-235 isotope, while the remaining $99.3\%$ is the U-238 isotope.

Isotope separation is a physical technique used to increase the concentration, or 'enrich' one isotope in relation to others. The enrichment plants primarily are centrifuge based (other techniques exist such as diffusion). Because U-235 is lighter than U-238, when the centrifuges are spun rapidly (50,000 - 70,000 rpm) during the enrichment process, it helps separate the heavier U-238 from the lighter U-235. The more centrifuges the gas passes through, the higher the U-235 content it has at the end and thus the higher assay.

Clearly it takes “spinning” or “work” or “effort” or “available equipment” to enrich.

The majority of reactors, which are light water reactors (either Pressurized Water Reactors - PWR or Boiling Water Reactors - BWR), require low-enriched uranium (LEU) as fuel with U-235 increased from the natural 0.7% to 3-5% product assay. 3% product assay corresponds to requirements from older reactors, while 4%-5% is the standard for modern light water reactors.

There is growing interest in enriching uranium to approximately 7% and, for certain special power reactor (Gen IV and SMR), even up to close to 20%, which is classified as high-assay LEU (HALEU). That would take more “spinning”.

To describe the work of enrichment, the industry uses the concept of Separative Work Units (SWU) to measure enrichment. We don't like the term SWU but bow to the industry use. SWU is proportional to energy but incorporates also a notion of capacity.

One other concept of SWU is separation. Enrichment takes an input mixture of U-235 and U-238 and separates them into two piles - one with more U-235 and one with less U-235. Clearly if I have two piles and one is richer in U-235, the other (the tailings) must be poorer.

The chart below shows the SWU (“work”) to move from natural uranium at 0.7% to various levels of U-235.

Let's say we start with 1,000 kg of input (the yellowcake that has been refined and converted). At 0.7% U-235, it contains 7 kg of U-235 and 993 kg of U-238. If I separate them perfectly, it would take 1,300 SWU (that much work/effort/energy).

If I was satisfied with 5% grade and wanted 1 kg of enriched uranium product produced from yellowcake (at 0.7%), I have a choice. I can be sloppy and discard a lot of my U-235 in the tailings (say 0.5%) [i.e., do a bad job squeezing the oranges and end up with a pile of poorly squeezed oranges with juice left in them]. That would require 45 kg of yellowcake but only consume 13 SWU of work. If I am diligent and discard ‘cleaner’ tailings (say only 0.1% U-235), I would need 16 kg of yellowcake (a third less!) but consume 28 SWU of work (twice the work). If I need a certain amount of enriched product for my reactors but I have limited enrichment facilities (limited SWU), then I have to consume more uranium to get there.

## APPENDIX - WHAT NUCLEAR VESSELS TEACH US ABOUT SMR

The first use of nuclear power was the Trinity nuclear test (July 1945).

The next month (August 6 $^{th}$ and 9 $^{th}$ 1945), two nuclear bombs would be used against Japan by the United States.

The Soviet Union would follow the US in weapons development and test their first weapon by 1949.

Peace-time uses of nuclear power would follow. By December 1951, EBR-1 (Idaho) would generate sufficient electricity for four light bulbs. 1954 would see the Obninsk power plant generate small scales of industrial electricity.

Calder Hall (United Kingdom) would become the first industrial-scale project in 1956 with 4 reactors and a total of 240 MW of electricity or MWe (roughly a quarter the size of today's typical design such as an AP1000). The world's first power plant was thus an SMR (<300 Mwe).

Also in 1951 was the authorization of the USS Nautilus (SSN-571). Authorized in 1951 and delivered in 1955, the Nautilus was the first nuclear-powered boat and submarine.

The Nautilus was powered by an S2W reactor (“S” for submarine, “2” for $2^{nd}$ generation, and “W” for Westinghouse). It delivered 60-70 MWt (MW of thermal energy).

1958 would see the Soviet's first nuclear submarine. 1961 would see the first nuclear aircraft carriers and cruisers.

On the civilian side, The Soviet Union would launch the first vessel – an icebreaker in 1959 – followed by other merchant vessels from the US (1962), Germany (1969) and Japan (1972).

Civilian ships were challenged not by technology nor economics but rather by litigation, specifically around liability/ indemnification around accidents and port entry.

As an aside, earlier nuclear submarines had poorer shielding (with seamen exposed to 173 mrem/year compared to today's seamen at 10 mrem/year). For context, a chest x-ray is 10 mrem while a full-body CT is 1,000 mrem. An hour flying is 1 mrem. A typical American annual exposure budget is 620 mrem (strongly driven by whether your home has radon or thoron). The point being that even early generation nuclear submarines were safe.

In terms of reactor design, we show innovation but highlight (a) a small number of ‘winners’, and (b) a focus on PWR (the first technology that worked was improved upon). We’d expect the SMR race to have a few winners with the best & simplest technology ultimately being adopted.

## RISKS

## Cameco Corp

Downside risks to our price target include:

## 1. Nuclear safety - related risks

Nuclear power safety concerns, often due to reactor mismanagement, can cause delays in permits and construction. This slowdown in the nuclear energy sector adoption directly reduces the demand for Cameco's uranium, hitting its sales and revenue. Public fear and regulatory tightening can further dampen the market.

## 2. High labor costs

Cameco faces high costs for skilled labor in the nuclear industry. The limited supply of specialized workers drives up wages, squeezing profit margins. Labor - market fluctuations like shortages or strikes can disrupt production and increase expenses.

3. The rise of plutonium & thorium use could reduce demand for uranium

The growing use of plutonium and thorium in Fast Breeder Reactor (FBRs) and Molten Salt Reactor (MSR) threatens Cameco's uranium market share. However, Plutonium's high radioactivity and potential for misuse lead to strict regulations and possible public opposition, and the development of FBR and MSR are still in the early stage with more exporus in Asia. Considering Cameco's small exposure in Asia (17% uranium & 5% of UF6 sold to Asia in 2024), the impact of this Gen IV reactor development will be limited on Cameco.

## 4. Inconsistent operation and transparency in Inkai with Kazatoprom

In the Inkai project with Kazatoprom, inconsistent operations can lead to production inefficiencies and unexpected costs. Lack of transparency may cause misunderstandings and disputes between the partners. Both issues can undermine the project's performance and Cameco's returns.

EN_B: In short, if the Terafab materializes as planned, it could match TSMC’s entire wafer fab equipment spending within four to five years. That would push the global semiconductor equipment market to a completely new level.
EN_A: Wait, SpaceX wants to build its own chips, and at TSMC’s scale? That sounds almost unbelievable. What is the core question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: The question is simple: what do you do when the existing chip supply chain cannot keep up with your demand for AI compute? Musk’s answer is to build it yourself. Think back to 2014, when Tesla couldn’t get enough batteries from suppliers, so they built the Gigafactory. This is the same logic, but for semiconductors.
EN_A: So what exactly is this Terafab going to make? How is it different from what we already have?
EN_B: The key difference is integration. It aims to put everything under one roof: mask making, logic and memory fabrication, packaging, and testing. That allows for incredibly fast design-build-test-redesign cycles. The report mentions an initial investment of 55 billion dollars in Grimes County, Texas, which could expand to 119 billion if all phases are completed.
EN_A: 55 to 119 billion dollars. That is a staggering number. How does the report justify that kind of spending?
EN_B: It uses UBS’s forecast for SpaceX’s AI business alone, which is expected to generate about 1.1 trillion dollars in capex over five years. Roughly 20 percent of that, or 225 billion, is allocated to the Terafab. Assuming the typical 60 percent of fab capex goes to equipment, that implies 135 billion in wafer fab equipment spending over five years. That is roughly the size of the entire global WFE market this year.
EN_A: So what does that mean for the existing players? Is this a threat or an opportunity?
EN_B: That is the million-dollar question. The report focuses on two key angles. First, Intel. It could act as the knowledge owner for the Terafab, licensing process technology and manufacturing know-how, similar to the old IBM-AMD model. There is even a scenario where Intel dedicates its Ohio site to a joint venture with SpaceX. Second, memory makers like Micron, SK Hynix, and Samsung. This is where the big mystery lies.
EN_A: Why is memory such a mystery?
EN_B: Because Musk explicitly said the Terafab will make its own memory chips. But where will they get the memory IP? Existing memory giants have very little incentive to license their core technology to a potential competitor. The report does not fully answer that question yet. It only speculates that if Terafab succeeds, it could force Korean companies to accelerate their own US fabrication plans.
EN_A: So Intel could be a winner, but memory suppliers face a potential disruptor. That is a huge asymmetry. What are some of the open questions the report leaves hanging?

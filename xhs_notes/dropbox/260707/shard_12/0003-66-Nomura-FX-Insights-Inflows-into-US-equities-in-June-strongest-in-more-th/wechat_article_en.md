## The Divergence Between US Equity and Bond Flows Exposes a Critical Risk-On, Risk-Off Fracture That the Market Is Underpricing

The most striking feature of the June flow data is not the magnitude of equity inflows but the simultaneous outflows from US bonds. The conventional narrative that foreign capital is simply "buying America" is too simplistic. Investors are buying US equities and selling US bonds. That is a specific bet on growth and a specific bet against duration.

The implication is that foreign investors are not seeking safety in US assets. They are seeking exposure to US corporate earnings and economic dynamism while hedging against the risk that the Federal Reserve will maintain higher-for-longer rates or that fiscal deficits will keep term premiums elevated. This is a risk-on trade, not a safe-haven trade. The outflows from bonds also suggest that foreign investors are not convinced that the US inflation problem is fully resolved. If they were, they would be locking in yields. Instead, they are rotating into equities, which implies a belief that nominal growth will remain strong enough to support earnings but not so strong as to trigger a policy error that would crush valuations.

For currency markets, this divergence is a powerful signal. The dollar has been supported by the notion that US assets are universally attractive. But the data shows that foreign demand for US bonds is weakening. If this trend continues, the dollar's interest rate advantage will be increasingly offset by reduced capital inflows into the fixed-income market. The dollar may remain strong against low-yielding currencies, but it could lose momentum against currencies where central banks are also maintaining tight policy. The fracture between equity and bond flows introduces a new source of volatility for FX markets that is not yet priced into options or forward curves.

## The Report's Data Raises an Unanswered Question That Is Critical for Asset Allocation: Is the US Equity Inflow Driven by Fundamental Reassessment or by Passive Rebalancing?

The report provides high-quality, high-frequency data on flows, but it does not answer the most important question for observers: what is driving these flows? The distinction between active and passive flows is crucial. If the inflows are primarily driven by passive rebalancing into US equity indices, then the trend is likely to persist regardless of valuation or fundamentals. Global investors who track the MSCI World or the S&P 500 have no choice but to allocate capital to US equities in proportion to their market capitalization. As US equities perform, their weight in global indices increases, forcing passive investors to allocate more. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle that can continue even as valuations become stretched.

If, on the other hand, the inflows are driven by active managers making a fundamental decision to favor the US, then the trend is more fragile. Active managers can change their minds. If US economic data softens or if earnings disappoint, they can rotate out. The report does not break down the flows into active versus passive components. It also does not identify the type of investor behind the flows. Are they sovereign wealth funds, pension funds, hedge funds, or retail investors? Each has a different time horizon and a different sensitivity to valuation.

This unanswered question is the key to determining whether the June surge is the beginning of a multi-year trend or the climax of a cycle. Observers who assume it is structural will favor US equities and reduce EM exposure. Those who assume it is cyclical will look for entry points in EM and prepare for a reversal. Until we have better data on the composition of these flows, the prudent approach is to treat the trend with respect but not with conviction.

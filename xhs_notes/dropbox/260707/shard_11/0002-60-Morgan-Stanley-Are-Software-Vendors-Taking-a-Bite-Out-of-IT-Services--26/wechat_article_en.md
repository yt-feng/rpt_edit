# Software Vendors Are Not Just Building AI — They Are Building the Services Layer, and That Changes Everything for IT Services

The most consequential strategic shift in enterprise technology this year is not a new model release or a cloud pricing change. It is the quiet but accelerating move by the largest software and cloud vendors to deploy their own forward-deployed engineering teams dedicated to AI transformation. Microsoft has committed $2.5 billion and 6,000 embedded experts to its new Frontier Company initiative. Amazon has launched a dedicated AWS Forward Deployed Engineering organization backed by $1 billion. SAP is creating its own FDE teams, reportedly starting in July 2026. These are not incremental investments. They represent a structural redefinition of the boundary between software vendor and services provider.

This matters now because the enterprise AI market is at a critical inflection point. After two years of experimentation, proof-of-concept projects, and pilot programs, the next phase requires deep integration into core business processes. That is precisely the kind of work that has historically belonged to IT services firms like Accenture, Capgemini, and TCS. If the software vendors themselves are now building the capacity to deliver that integration, the traditional IT services business model — selling labor at a margin — faces a challenge that is not cyclical but structural.

The argument of this article is straightforward: the software industry is quietly absorbing the services layer, and the implications for IT services valuations, business models, and competitive positioning are far more profound than the market currently prices in. Observers who treat the current pullback in IT services as a cyclical trough may be missing the early signs of a permanent shift.

## Big Tech's Forward Deployed Engineering Push Is Not a Services Partnership — It Is a Competitive Incursion into Services Territory

The language used by Microsoft, Amazon, and SAP is carefully chosen. They describe these initiatives as "forward deployed engineering," a term borrowed from defense and elite technology consulting. The framing is deliberate: these are not support teams or implementation consultants. They are engineers embedded with customers to build custom AI solutions. Microsoft's Frontier Company goes beyond traditional FDE, with a $2.5 billion investment and support from its SI partner ecosystem including Capgemini. Amazon's AWS FDE organization "focuses on business outcomes and leaves customers self-sufficient with AI."

The critical insight here is not the dollar amount. It is the strategic logic. Software vendors have historically avoided heavy services exposure because services are lower-margin, less scalable, and create conflicts with their partner ecosystems. So why are they moving into services now? The answer is that AI deployment is not a configuration exercise. It requires deep customization, data integration, workflow redesign, and change management. If the software vendor does not provide this capability, the customer may not successfully deploy the product at all. In other words, the services layer has become a necessary condition for product adoption.

This creates a fundamentally different competitive dynamic. In the past, software vendors and services firms were complementary. The software vendor built the platform; the services firm implemented it. Today, the software vendor needs to control the implementation to ensure the product works, to capture the data and feedback from deployment, and to prevent the services firm from building its own IP on top of the platform. The partnerships Microsoft announced with Capgemini suggest a collaborative outcome is possible, but the terms of that collaboration are now set by the vendor, not the services firm.

The pattern is still early. The number of FDE mentions in company documents and news has risen sharply since January 2025. But the trajectory is clear. If this trend continues, the question for IT services firms is not whether they will lose market share in AI implementation. It is whether they will be relegated to the role of capacity augmenters for vendor-led projects, with thinner margins and less strategic control.

## SAP's Cost Reallocation Signals That Software Vendors Will Sacrifice Everything Else to Fund the AI Transition

SAP's recent moves provide a concrete example of the intensity behind this shift. According to press reports, SAP is cutting hiring and travel costs unrelated to AI to free up resources for AI development. The company is reportedly focusing new hiring "on selected profiles only, mainly core AI roles." This is happening alongside a management reshuffle in which the CPO's product and engineering responsibilities are being split between the CEO and COO ahead of the CPO's expected departure.

The pattern is unmistakable. SAP is not simply adding AI investment on top of its existing cost structure. It is actively reallocating resources away from non-AI activities. The company is also looking externally for a new executive product lead, targeting candidates in the US. This suggests that SAP views the AI transition as requiring not just incremental investment but a fundamental reconfiguration of its talent base and leadership.

The implication for the broader software industry is that the AI transition will be expensive, and it will force trade-offs. Companies that succeed will be those willing to cut legacy activities and redirect resources. Companies that fail to make these trade-offs will fall behind. For IT services firms, this creates a double pressure. First, their software vendor partners are becoming more demanding and more focused on their own AI agendas. Second, the vendors' cost-cutting in non-AI areas may reduce the volume of traditional implementation and maintenance work that services firms rely on.

The question that remains unanswered is whether this reallocation is a temporary phase or a permanent shift in how software companies allocate capital. If AI becomes the dominant cost center for software vendors, the services firms that depend on those vendors for partnership and project flow will need to rethink their own resource allocation strategies.

## The IT Services Sector Is Priced for a Cyclical Recovery, but the Evidence Points to Structural Pressure

The performance of IT services stocks in 2026 tells a story of selective adjustment. The sector is down approximately 30% year-to-date, but within that decline, there are significant divergences. Nordic leader Tieto has been broadly flat year-to-date, trading at 11x forward adjusted P/E. That represents a 17% premium to Accenture and a 34% premium to Capgemini.

This premium is difficult to justify on fundamentals. Tieto's guidance calls for organic growth of -2% to 0% in 2026, with adjusted EBITA margins of 14.8% to 15.8%. The company's medium-term targets call for annual revenue growth of over 5% and EBITA margins above 16% by 2028. But the gap between current performance and those targets is wide, and the path to closing it is unclear in an environment where enterprise IT budgets remain constrained and AI is creating new competitive dynamics for the labor-based revenue model.

The broader IT services sector faces a similar challenge. The market seems to be pricing in a cyclical recovery in enterprise IT spending. But the evidence for that recovery is thin. Meanwhile, the structural developments from software vendor FDE initiatives, from AI-native platforms that reduce the need for systems integration, and from the potential for AI to automate portions of the services workflow itself, are not cyclical. They are permanent.

The Indian IT services vendors face their own version of this dynamic. One major research report recently adjusted its view on TCS, citing a lack of signs of growth acceleration, likely lower margins with accelerated investments, and low-single-digit earnings CAGR over the next two years. This is not a cyclical observation. It is a structural one.

The unanswered question is whether the current valuations adequately reflect the risk that the IT services business model is being permanently reshaped from multiple directions. If software vendors continue to absorb more of the services layer, if AI reduces the labor intensity of implementation work, and if enterprise buyers become more comfortable with direct vendor relationships, the earnings power of traditional IT services firms may be permanently different from historical levels.

## The Buy-Versus-Build Question for AI Platforms Is Becoming a Decision Every Software Company Must Face

The launch of Base44's proprietary LLM, following its acquisition by Wix, illustrates a strategic question that is becoming central to the software industry. Base44, an AI-centric software development platform, has launched its own model. The rationale, according to the company, is that "ownership of the model gives Base44 direct control over compute and inference spend, expected to result in a structurally stronger margin profile over time."

This is not an isolated case. OVH Group, a French cloud services provider, has also announced plans to train its own frontier AI models. While these examples are still anecdotal, they point to a broader pattern. As AI becomes embedded in software products, the decision to use third-party foundation models versus building proprietary ones becomes a strategic choice with significant implications for cost structure, differentiation, and competitive positioning.

The trade-offs are well-understood. Using third-party models reduces upfront investment and allows companies to benefit from rapid improvements in the underlying technology. But it also means ceding control over costs, latency, and the ability to differentiate. Building proprietary models requires significant capital and talent, but it offers the potential for better margins, deeper product integration, and a moat based on unique data or use-case optimization.

The improving capabilities of open-source LLMs are making this calculation more complex. As open-source models close the gap with proprietary frontier models, the cost advantage of using third-party APIs diminishes, and the strategic case for building or fine-tuning proprietary models strengthens. This is particularly relevant for software platforms that have access to unique, high-quality data that can be used to train specialized models.

For observers, this means that the software industry's cost structure and competitive dynamics are in flux. Companies that successfully build proprietary AI capabilities may emerge with stronger margins and deeper moats. Companies that remain dependent on third-party models may face margin compression as API costs rise or as their differentiation erodes. The decision is not purely technical. It is a strategic choice that will shape the competitive landscape for years to come.

## Cloudflare's Move to Block AI Crawlers Signals a New Era of Permissioned Data Access That Will Reshape Content Economics

Cloudflare's announcement that it will change default settings to block AI crawlers on ad-supported pages is a small technical change with large strategic implications. The company recognizes that "most site owners want to be discoverable in AI" but that "maximizing discovery shouldn't mean giving away intellectual property for free." Starting September 15, new customers and new sites for existing customers will have default settings that allow search but block training and agent use on pages that display ads.

This is part of a broader shift toward permissioned, attributable, and potentially monetized use of proprietary content in AI systems. For publishers and professional information providers such as RELX, Wolters Kluwer, and Informa, this is a critical development. Their competitive moats are built on proprietary data and trusted content. If they can control how that content is used by AI systems, they can potentially create new revenue streams or protect existing ones from being reshaped by AI-generated alternatives.

The legal and regulatory backdrop is also shifting. In May 2026, Elsevier, a part of RELX's STM business, joined a class-action complaint against Meta alleging the reproduction of copyrighted works in developing the Llama AI model. This is not an isolated case. The legal framework for AI training data is being contested on multiple fronts, and the outcomes will have significant implications for content owners.

The unanswered question is whether the industry will move toward a licensing model, a blocking model, or some hybrid approach. Cloudflare's move provides infrastructure for blocking, but the economic logic points toward licensing. If content owners can demonstrate that their data has value for AI training and inference, they have an incentive to monetize that value rather than simply block access. The challenge is that the market for AI training data is still immature, and the mechanisms for tracking and attributing usage are underdeveloped.

For observers of content and information services companies, this is a risk and an opportunity. The risk is that AI systems will increasingly bypass traditional content distribution channels, reducing the value of proprietary databases and publications. The opportunity is that content owners may be able to create new revenue streams by licensing their data for AI training and inference, potentially at higher margins than their traditional business models.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Magnitude of the Services Displacement

The research report provides compelling evidence that software vendors are moving into services territory, that IT services valuations face structural pressure, and that the content economy is being reshaped by AI. But it leaves several critical questions unanswered.

First, how fast will the displacement of traditional IT services by vendor-led FDE teams occur? The report notes that Microsoft's partnerships with Capgemini suggest a collaborative outcome, but it does not quantify the share of AI implementation work that will flow through vendors versus traditional services firms. The answer depends on whether enterprise buyers trust vendors to provide objective implementation advice, and whether vendors can scale their FDE teams fast enough to meet demand.

Second, will the IT services firms be able to adapt by building their own AI capabilities and repositioning themselves as AI-native services providers? The report does not explore the counter-strategy. Some IT services firms are investing heavily in AI tools, proprietary platforms, and specialized AI consulting practices. The question is whether these investments can offset the competitive pressure from vendor-led FDE teams.

Third, what is the revenue and margin impact for software vendors themselves? The FDE initiatives require significant investment, and services revenue is typically lower-margin than software revenue. If software vendors become more services-heavy, their margin profiles may shift, and their valuations may need to adjust accordingly.

## A Decision Framework for Observers Navigating the Software-Services Convergence

For those trying to make sense of these developments, the following framework may be useful. It is not a formula for selection, but a set of questions that can help distinguish between companies facing cyclical headwinds and those facing structural challenges.

First, assess the degree of vendor dependency. IT services firms that derive a significant portion of their revenue from implementing and maintaining third-party software platforms are more exposed to vendor FDE initiatives than firms that have their own proprietary platforms or deep domain expertise. The more a services firm acts as a bridge between the vendor and the customer, the more vulnerable it is to being bypassed.

Second, evaluate the AI readiness of the services firm's own business model. Firms that are investing in AI tools to automate their own delivery, that are building proprietary AI platforms, and that are developing AI-specific consulting practices are better positioned than firms that are treating AI as just another technology wave to be implemented for clients. The key question is whether the services firm is using AI to change its own cost structure or simply to generate new project revenue.

Third, examine the software vendor's strategy. Not all software vendors are moving into services with equal intensity. Some are using FDE teams as a temporary measure to accelerate AI adoption, with the intention of eventually productizing the solutions and reducing the services component. Others may view services as a permanent part of their business model. The distinction matters for assessing the long-term competitive landscape.

Fourth, consider the content and data assets. Companies that own proprietary, high-quality data that is valuable for AI training have a potential revenue stream that is independent of their core business. The key question is whether they can effectively monetize that data in a market that is still developing.

Finally, do not assume that current valuations reflect these dynamics. The IT services sector has been a consistent underperformer, but the market may still be pricing in a cyclical recovery that does not materialize. The software sector has been a strong performer, but the margin implications of moving into services are not fully reflected in current valuations.

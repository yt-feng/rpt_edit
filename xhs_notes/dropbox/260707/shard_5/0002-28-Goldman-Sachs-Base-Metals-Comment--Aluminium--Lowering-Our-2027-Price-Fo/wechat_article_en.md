## The Al Taweelah Restart Is a Canary in the Coal Mine for Global Supply Assumptions

The most immediate catalyst for the revised outlook is the faster-than-expected restoration of production at the Al Taweelah aluminium smelter. What matters is the trajectory. The company is now targeting full production by the end of the first quarter of 2027, pulling forward the previous end-2027 timeline by roughly nine months.

To be clear, significant operational hurdles remain. Only around 20% of the cells have had frozen metal removed, a necessary precondition for restart. The company itself has stated that returning to pre-incident levels could take up to a year. Yet market feedback and the operator's own accelerated efforts suggest a faster ramp is plausible. This is a classic situation where the official timeline is conservative, and the actual outcome is likely to beat it. Those who anchored to the prior guidance are now playing catch-up.

The so-what here extends beyond a single smelter. The Al Taweelah restart is a leading indicator for a broader Middle East supply recovery that the market had underestimated. Indonesia's rise as an aluminium powerhouse is not new, but its scale is accelerating. The country is leveraging its abundant coal reserves to power new smelters, creating a low-cost production base that is competitive with Chinese and Middle Eastern capacity. The 1.2 million tonne annual increment is equivalent to roughly two large smelters coming online in a single year. This is not a one-off. The strategic implication is that the aluminium market is moving from a world where supply is constrained by energy costs and geopolitical risk to one where supply is abundant and increasingly concentrated in low-cost jurisdictions. ## Two Unresolved Questions the Report Highlights but Does Not Fully Answer

Every rigorous analysis reveals its own gaps, and this report is no exception. Two questions stand out as critical for those who want to form their own view rather than simply adopt the analyst's conclusion.

The report assumes a relatively stable demand growth trajectory, with global primary consumption rising from 75.2 million tonnes in 2026 to 77.4 million tonnes in 2027, a 3% increase. But this assumes that lower aluminium prices do not themselves stimulate additional demand, or that any demand response is captured in the base case. In reality, lower prices could accelerate substitution away from other materials, particularly in the automotive and packaging sectors, and could also encourage inventory restocking by downstream consumers. The demand side of the balance is less certain than the supply side, and the report does not stress-test alternative demand scenarios.

Second, how will Chinese smelters respond to the surplus? China's production is forecast to rise modestly to 46.3 million tonnes in 2027 before plateauing. But China has a history of imposing production caps and capacity swaps to manage its domestic market. If the global surplus materialises as projected, Chinese authorities may choose to restrict output to support prices, particularly if the social and economic costs of a price collapse become politically salient. The report's balance assumes Chinese production remains on its current trajectory, but policy risk is inherently unpredictable. A Chinese output cut of even 1 million tonnes would significantly alter the surplus picture.

These are not flaws in the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of any forecast. But they are the questions that active observers must answer for themselves.

EN_A: So building materials companies are all talking about "solutions strategy" these days. Is this a real growth driver, or just a fancy way to say they're buying more companies?
EN_B: Great question. We just looked at a deep dive from a global investment bank. They found that over the past five years, the word "solutions" got mentioned three times more often on earnings calls than in the five years before that.
EN_A: Three times more? That's not just marketing fluff. So what problem is this strategy actually trying to solve?
EN_B: The core idea is to bundle complementary products to solve a specific construction problem, rather than selling individual components. Think about a roofing system. It needs to be waterproof, insulated, and durable. If one company can provide the whole package, they can charge more and capture more of the value chain.
EN_A: Sounds like any company could do that. What's the catch?
EN_B: The report argues not all companies are suited for this. The most effective strategies have three dimensions: first, products that can be sold as a system with warranties; second, a sales network that reaches the same decision-maker; and third, the ability to bundle in high-margin complementary products.
EN_A: So who's doing it well? Give us some names.
EN_B: The report highlights two standouts. Sika is strong in basement waterproofing. Their patented membrane product grew sales at a 27% CAGR from 2023 to 2025, and they offer a 20-year warranty. Then there's Kingspan, which is expanding its US non-residential roofing business, leveraging its strength in panels.
EN_A: So Sika and Kingspan are the winners? What about the others?
EN_B: The report sees them as outperformers. But companies like Saint-Gobain and Holcim are also pushing solutions strategies, and the jury is still out. For example, Saint-Gobain hasn't shown clear revenue outperformance in North America compared to local peers.
EN_A: So why the difference? What makes some strategies work and others not?
EN_B: It comes down to whether the product category is suitable for bundling. For technically complex areas like roofing and basements, where repairs are costly, customers will pay a premium for a full system. But for commoditized products like cement, customers are price-sensitive and the decision-makers are different, so the strategy is less effective.

## Commercial HVAC and Liquid Cooling Are Becoming the New Growth Engine

The most encouraging signal from Sanhua's recent management meetings is the accelerating momentum in commercial HVAC and liquid cooling. Sanhua built a dedicated liquid cooling team last year, and the ramp is accelerating. Management expects liquid cooling revenue to grow more than 50 percent year-over-year in 2026, reaching approximately 3 billion renminbi, up from 2 billion in 2025. The company is expanding from primary-side microchannel products—components that sit between the data center and the coolant distribution unit—into higher-value secondary-side components including pumps, cold plates, valves, and sensors. These are the components that sit between the CDU and the server or chip, and they command higher margins and deeper customer relationships.

Equally important is Sanhua's strategic positioning within the value chain. The company has explicitly stated that it does not plan to manufacture full CDU systems. Instead, it will supply components to system integrators like Vertiv and Envicool. This is a deliberate choice. By focusing on components rather than systems, Sanhua avoids direct competition with its own customers and maintains the flexibility to supply multiple integrators. The model is analogous to what the company has done in automotive thermal management: become the critical component supplier to a fragmented customer base.

Management has also highlighted potential project breakthroughs in self-designed ASIC server supply chains. Sanhua works directly with cloud customers on qualification, while shipments go primarily to server supply-chain manufacturers. Current products are mainly CDU internal valves and sensors, but future opportunities include cold plates on the server side. If these breakthroughs materialize in the second half of 2026, they could provide a significant catalyst for the stock.

The so-what here is clear: liquid cooling is not a side project for Sanhua. The 50 percent growth rate for 2026 is impressive, but the base is still small relative to the company's total revenue. The real test will be whether Sanhua can sustain that growth rate as the base expands and competition intensifies.

Sanhua's auto thermal management segment presents the most nuanced picture. Domestic new energy vehicle retail sales may decline, but overseas vehicle shipments remain strong, particularly from export-oriented Chinese OEMs like Leapmotor, Geely, and BYD. Tesla demand also showed some recovery in May and June compared with the first quarter.

The company's production schedule for the third quarter looks busy, and some new products—including oil pumps and expansion valves—are facing tight capacity. This is a good problem to have, but it also signals that Sanhua is pushing the limits of its current manufacturing footprint. The company continues to shift from individual components toward integrated modules, which supports content-per-vehicle growth. New opportunities include sensors, sensor-cleaning valves, oil pumps used in autonomous driving, and thermal management for in-car refrigerators.

The risk is external. Domestic demand uncertainty, overseas policy shifts, and export tariffs all hang over the segment. Sanhua management believes the company is internally prepared to achieve its 15 to 20 percent compound annual growth target, but external risks remain outside its control. The strategic question for observers is whether auto thermal management can remain a growth driver even as the global EV market matures. Sanhua's answer is that content-per-vehicle growth, driven by module integration and new product categories, can offset volume headwinds. That thesis is plausible, but it is not yet proven at scale.

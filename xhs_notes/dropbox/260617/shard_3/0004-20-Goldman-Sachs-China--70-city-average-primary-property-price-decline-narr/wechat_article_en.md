# China's Property Market Is Not Stabilizing — It Is Fracturing Along a New Fault Line

The headline numbers from China’s May housing data look like a modest improvement. The weighted average price decline in the primary market narrowed from 2.9 percent month-on-month annualized in April to 1.8 percent in May. Tier-1 cities posted sequential gains, led by Shenzhen at 3.9 percent. A casual observer might conclude that policy support is working and the worst of the correction is behind us. That conclusion would be dangerously premature.

The real story is not that prices are stabilizing. It is that a structural decoupling is accelerating between China’s primary and secondary housing markets, between its top-tier cities and the rest of the country, and between what official statistics show and what actual transaction data reveals. Interpreting the May data as a broad-based recovery conflates a narrow, policy-driven stabilization in new-home sales in a handful of cities with a genuine inflection in the overall property cycle.

This matters because the property sector has been the single largest driver of China’s economic cycles over the past two decades. Its trajectory shapes household wealth, local government finances, bank asset quality, and consumer confidence. Misreading the signal now — when the policy response is becoming more aggressive but also more targeted — could lead to serious misallocation of capital and strategic miscalculation.

What follows is not a summary of the May data. It is an analytical framework for understanding what the data actually reveals about the nature of the current correction and what it implies for investors, policymakers, and corporate strategists over the next twelve to eighteen months.

## The Primary Market Is Tightly Controlled, Not Naturally Recovering

The headline improvement in May’s 70-city weighted average price — from a 2.9 percent monthly annualized decline to a 1.8 percent decline — has been widely cited as evidence that the market is bottoming. This interpretation confuses administrative intervention with organic demand recovery.

Primary market prices in China are not free-market prices. They are heavily influenced by local government land sales strategies, developer pricing discipline, and the selective release of sales permits. When a city government wants to stabilize price indices, it can restrict the number of new projects launched in lower-priced districts, delay sales permits for projects that would drag down the average, or encourage state-backed developers to hold prices steady. The result is an index that reflects policy intent as much as market reality.

The narrowing of the sequential decline is therefore better understood as a policy-driven stabilization of the official price index rather than a genuine improvement in market clearing conditions. The fact that the number of cities experiencing sequential price increases edged up in the primary market while simultaneously edging down in the secondary market is the most telling indicator in the entire dataset. It suggests that policy efforts are succeeding in managing the official narrative of new-home prices, but the underlying market — where real transactions between willing buyers and willing sellers occur — continues to weaken.

This divergence is not sustainable. At some point, the gap between primary and secondary market prices becomes large enough to distort buyer behavior. Rational homebuyers will increasingly prefer the secondary market, where prices have already adjusted downward by 5 to 10 percent over the past year according to both NBS data and third-party platforms. The primary market will then face an even harder task of clearing inventory without further price cuts, which would defeat the purpose of the stabilization effort.

## Tier-1 Cities Are an Exception That Proves the Rule

The sequential price gains in Tier-1 cities — 1.4 percent month-on-month annualized in May, up from 0.7 percent in April — are real but misleading if extrapolated nationally. Shenzhen’s 3.9 percent gain is particularly striking, but it reflects a specific set of localized factors rather than a broad-based recovery in Tier-1 demand.

First, Tier-1 cities benefit from a concentration of policy attention. The recent easing measures — including Guangzhou’s buyback program and expanded use of housing provident funds — are disproportionately targeted at top-tier cities. These are the markets where local governments have the fiscal capacity to intervene directly and where state-owned enterprises are most active in purchasing unsold inventory. Second, Tier-1 cities have the deepest pools of high-net-worth buyers who are less sensitive to mortgage rates and more sensitive to long-term asset allocation considerations. A modest improvement in policy sentiment can trigger a disproportionate response in these markets.

Third, and most importantly, the Tier-1 gains are occurring against a backdrop of sharply declining transaction volumes in most other segments. The high-frequency tracker showing 30-city new home transaction volumes roughly in line with year-ago levels masks a composition effect: a smaller number of cities are accounting for a larger share of total transactions. The inventory months data — which improved from 28.9 to 28.5 months, driven mostly by Tier-2 cities — suggests that even in the better-performing segments, the fundamental overhang of unsold housing remains extreme.

The implication is clear: Tier-1 cities are not leading a national recovery. They are absorbing policy stimulus that would otherwise have been spread across a broader geographic base. The rest of the country, particularly Tier-3 and Tier-4 cities where the sequential decline also narrowed but from a much lower base, remains in a structural downtrend that policy has not yet arrested.

## The Secondary Market Is the Canary in the Coal Mine, and It Is Already Silent

One of the most striking features of the current data is the divergence between primary and secondary market price trends. While the primary market index shows a narrowing decline, secondary market data from multiple independent sources — including the NBS’s own 70-city secondary index, Centraline, Beike, and Zhuge — all show continued price declines of 5 to 10 percent over the past year.

This is not a minor discrepancy. The secondary market represents the overwhelming majority of housing transactions in China. It is where most households realize capital gains or losses. It is the market that directly affects household balance sheets and consumer confidence. And it is the market that is least susceptible to administrative price management.

The fact that Beike suspended the release of its secondary home price data in October 2023, Guoxinda suspended its series in December 2023, and Zhuge suspended its 100-city series in December 2025 — all while the official index continued to show a more benign picture — should raise serious questions about data reliability. When multiple independent data providers withdraw from price reporting, it is rarely because the data has become more favorable.

For investors and strategists, this means that relying on the official primary market index as a proxy for the overall housing market is a mistake. The secondary market data, imperfect as it is, provides a more accurate picture of the true state of demand and pricing power. And that picture remains one of ongoing decline, with no clear bottom in sight.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Transmission Mechanism from Price to Volume to Credit

The May data provides a snapshot of prices, but it leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important of these concerns the relationship between price stabilization and transaction volume recovery.

Historically, housing market recoveries in China have followed a predictable pattern: policy easing leads to increased transaction volumes, which in turn supports prices. The current cycle has broken this pattern. Despite significant policy easing — including mortgage rate cuts, down payment reductions, and purchase restriction relaxations — transaction volumes have remained depressed. The 30-city high-frequency data showing volumes in line with year-ago levels is not a sign of recovery; it is a sign that the market has stabilized at a very low level of activity.

This raises a fundamental question that the report does not address: what is the threshold of price decline required to clear the current inventory overhang? With inventory months at 28.5 nationally, and significantly higher in Tier-3 and Tier-4 cities, the market needs either a substantial increase in demand or a substantial decrease in prices to restore equilibrium. The current policy approach appears to be aimed at preventing the latter, but without addressing the former, the inventory overhang will continue to act as a drag on prices.

A second unresolved question concerns the credit channel. Housing is the primary collateral for household lending in China. Continued price declines in the secondary market erode collateral values, which in turn constrains bank lending capacity and household borrowing ability. The report documents the price decline but does not model its second-order effects on credit creation. If secondary market prices continue to fall at 5 to 10 percent annually, the impact on bank balance sheets and the broader credit cycle could be substantial.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Fracturing Market

For readers who need to translate this analysis into actionable strategic decisions, the following framework may be useful. It is not a recommendation to buy or sell any asset. It is a set of questions that should guide any investment or business decision involving China’s property market.

First, distinguish between policy-driven price stabilization and genuine demand recovery. If you are investing in or lending against primary market assets in Tier-1 cities, you are essentially making a bet on continued government intervention. That bet may pay off in the short term, but it carries significant tail risk if policy fatigue sets in or if the fiscal cost of intervention becomes politically unsustainable.

Second, use secondary market data as your primary signal. The official primary market index is a managed number. Independent secondary market data, even with gaps in coverage, provides a more reliable picture of true market conditions. Watch for inflection points in secondary market transaction volumes, not just prices. A recovery in volumes typically precedes a sustainable price recovery by six to twelve months.

Third, monitor the inventory months trend by city tier. The national average of 28.5 months is concerning, but the dispersion is more important. Cities where inventory months are declining rapidly — particularly Tier-2 cities — may be approaching a genuine bottom. Cities where inventory months are still rising, especially in Tier-3 and below, remain in a structural oversupply that no amount of policy easing can quickly resolve.

Fourth, pay attention to the behavior of independent data providers. When companies like Beike and Guoxinda stop publishing price data, it is usually because the data is too negative to be commercially viable. The resumption of independent price reporting would be a more reliable signal of market stabilization than any official index.

Fifth, prepare for the possibility that the primary-secondary market divergence will eventually resolve through a correction in the primary market rather than a recovery in the secondary market. If secondary market prices continue to fall, the gap will become too large for rational buyers to ignore. Developers will eventually be forced to cut primary market prices more aggressively, which will feed back into the official index and potentially trigger a new wave of policy response.

## The Questions That Remain Open

The May data tells us that the property market is not in freefall. That is the good news. But it also tells us that the market is becoming more fragmented, more policy-dependent, and more opaque. The divergence between primary and secondary markets, between Tier-1 cities and the rest, and between official statistics and independent data sources, is widening rather than narrowing.

What remains unresolved is whether this fragmentation represents a transitional phase toward a healthier, more differentiated market or the early stages of a more disorderly adjustment. The answer depends on factors that the May data alone cannot reveal: the willingness of local governments to absorb losses on land sales, the capacity of state-owned enterprises to continue purchasing unsold inventory, the trajectory of household income growth, and the broader macroeconomic environment.

These are not academic questions. They will determine the returns on billions of dollars of real estate exposure, the fiscal health of dozens of provincial governments, and the stability of China’s banking system. The May data provides a useful diagnostic, but the prognosis remains uncertain.

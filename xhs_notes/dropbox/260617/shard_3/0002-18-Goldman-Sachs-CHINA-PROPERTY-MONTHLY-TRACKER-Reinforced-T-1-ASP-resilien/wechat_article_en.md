# China’s Property Market Is No Longer a Single Story: The T1 Recovery Is Real, But It Is Not the Whole Market

The most important finding from May’s property data is not that national sales remain weak, or that construction continues to contract. It is that China’s housing market has structurally bifurcated. Tier-1 cities are now exhibiting a self-sustaining price recovery that has persisted for multiple months, while the rest of the market continues to grind lower. This divergence is not a temporary data anomaly. It reflects a fundamental shift in where demand, capital, and developer strategy are concentrating. For investors, the question is no longer whether the market will recover broadly, but whether the T1 recovery can pull other cities along, or whether the gap will widen.

May’s data from a global investment bank’s monthly tracker confirms that primary market average selling prices in 70 cities declined at roughly the same pace as April and March, at -0.2% month-on-month. Secondary market prices worsened slightly to -0.3% month-on-month. But within these averages, Tier-1 cities stood out. Their primary and secondary prices rose by +0.2% and +0.3% month-on-month respectively, marking the fourth and third consecutive months of positive momentum. Shenzhen and Shanghai recorded broad-based improvements across most product types in secondary transactions. Beijing’s high-end projects outperformed. This is not a flicker. It is a pattern.

The timing matters. This recovery is happening against a backdrop of continued weakness in national primary sales volume, which fell 13% year-on-year in May, and a construction sector where new starts and completions declined by 25% and 20% year-on-year respectively, both below expectations. The fact that T1 prices are rising while the broader market is still contracting forces a re-evaluation of what the recovery narrative actually means. It is not a recovery of the system. It is a recovery of the most concentrated, highest-income urban markets, driven by supply constraints, product mix upgrades, and selective buyer demand.

This article interprets the May data through a strategic lens, focusing on what the divergence implies for price trajectories, secondary market dynamics, developer behavior, and the policy questions that remain open. The goal is not to summarize the data, but to extract the actionable insights and the unresolved tensions that should guide investment thinking.

## The T1 Price Recovery Has Become Self-Reinforcing, But It Is Still Narrowly Based

The most consequential signal from May is the persistence of positive month-on-month price momentum in Tier-1 cities. In the primary market, T1 prices rose for the fourth consecutive month. In the secondary market, they rose for the third. This is not a one-off bounce. It is a sustained trend. The report notes that 10 of the 70 tracked cities have now recorded three consecutive months of positive or flat secondary price changes from March through May. Among them are Shanghai, Shenzhen, Tianjin, and Urumqi. The list is short, but it is meaningful.

What is driving this? The report points to product mix upgrades as a key support for primary prices. Developers in T1 cities are launching higher-quality projects with superior net-to-gross ratios and better ceiling heights, and these units command higher prices. At the same time, new home supply in core areas remains limited, reducing competitive pressure. The example of Hangzhou, where six recently launched ultra-luxury projects achieved strong prices and sell-through momentum, illustrates the point. This is not broad-based demand. It is concentrated demand for premium product in supply-constrained locations.

On the secondary side, the drivers are different. The report identifies easing listing supply pressure as a factor. Property owners in T1 cities are becoming more reluctant to sell at deeply discounted prices, particularly as rental yields show signs of improvement. The report notes that Tier-1 city rents have increased month-on-month for three consecutive months, outperforming the same periods in 2024 and 2025, which both saw negative month-on-month trends. Rising rental yields reduce the urgency to sell, which supports transaction prices.

The so-what for investors is clear: the T1 recovery is real, but it is not a signal that the national market has turned. It is a signal that the most liquid, highest-income urban markets are finding a floor, driven by supply-side discipline and selective demand. The risk is that this recovery remains confined to a handful of cities. The opportunity is that if T1 prices stabilize, it could eventually create a positive reference point for other cities. But the data so far does not confirm that transmission mechanism is working.

## Secondary Market Volume Is Growing, But the Quality of That Growth Deserves Scrutiny

One of the more positive surprises in May was secondary sales volume. In 15 tracked cities, secondary transaction volume grew 8% year-on-year, above expectations and accelerating from 4% growth in April. This is the third consecutive month of year-on-year decline in secondary listing volume across 100 cities, which has helped accelerate transaction turnover. On the surface, this looks like a healthy sign: more transactions, less supply overhang, faster turnover.

But the quality of this volume growth needs to be examined. First, the year-on-year comparison base matters. The report notes that June 2025 had a flat month-on-month base, which makes the year-on-year improvement easier to achieve. Second, the volume growth is happening alongside continued price weakness in the secondary market outside T1 cities. The 70-city secondary price index fell by -0.3% month-on-month in May, a sequential widening from April’s -0.2%. More transactions at lower prices is not necessarily a sign of health. It can also be a sign of distress selling being absorbed by bargain buyers.

The report also notes that rental yields are marginally improving, but that 50-city average rent levels continue to trend lower. The improvement is concentrated in T1 cities. This means that outside the top tier, the rental market is still soft, which undermines the investment case for buying secondary properties. If rental yields are not attractive, and prices are still falling, the incentive to buy is weak.

The strategic implication is that secondary volume growth should be interpreted as a normalization of transaction activity from an extremely low base, not as a demand recovery. The real test will be whether volume growth can sustain without further price declines. If secondary prices continue to fall even as volumes rise, it suggests that the market is still searching for a clearing price. If prices stabilize while volumes grow, that would be a more constructive signal.

## Developer Land Banking Is Becoming a Tale of Two Strategies, With Margins Holding but Volumes Shrinking

The report tracks land acquisition activity for six covered developers. The headline numbers show that average land investment as a share of contract sales fell to 20% in May from 21% in the first five months of the year. This is consistent with the broader sluggish land market. But within the average, there is significant dispersion. The report notes that Poly A and Greentown accelerated their land banking in May, while the rest of the tracked developers slowed down. This is not random. It reflects a strategic divergence between developers that have the balance sheet strength to invest counter-cyclically and those that are conserving cash.

The quality of the land being acquired is notable. 95% of tracked developers’ land acquisition value in May was in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities. 78% was in the top 10 cities. Project-level gross profit margins were estimated at around 23%, similar to the 24% average for the first five months. This tells us that the developers that are still buying land are being highly selective. They are concentrating on the highest-demand urban markets and maintaining margin discipline. They are not trying to grow volume. They are trying to grow quality.

The so-what for investors is that the consolidation theme in Chinese property is not just about weaker developers failing. It is also about stronger developers becoming more focused and more profitable on a per-project basis. The developers that survive this cycle will emerge with a smaller but higher-quality land bank, concentrated in the cities that are showing the earliest signs of price stabilization. The risk is that land banking volumes remain too low to support future revenue growth. The opportunity is that the margins on new projects are holding up, which could support profitability even if sales volumes remain subdued.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: Can the T1 Recovery Spread, and What Would It Take?

The report is thorough in its data and forecasts, but it leaves several important questions open. The first is whether the T1 price recovery can spread to other cities. The report notes that 13 out of 70 cities recorded positive or flat secondary price changes in May, down from 16 in April and 17 in March. The number of cities showing price improvement is actually shrinking. This suggests that the recovery is not broadening. It is concentrating.

The second open question is about the role of policy. The report lists several policy measures to watch, including full relaxation of housing purchase restrictions in T1 cities, housing provident fund reforms, mortgage interest subsidies, and government inventory buybacks. But it does not model the impact of any specific policy change. The implication is that the current recovery is happening without major new stimulus. If policy were to become more aggressive, it could accelerate the recovery. But if policy remains incremental, the recovery may remain narrow.

The third question is about rental yields. The report notes that T1 rents have risen for three consecutive months, which is positive. But it also notes that 50-city average rents continue to trend lower. The sustainability of the T1 rental recovery depends on employment and income growth, which are outside the property market’s control. If the broader economy weakens, the rental recovery could stall.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the report. They are the natural boundaries of what any single data release can tell us. The May data confirms that the T1 recovery is real and persistent. But it does not confirm that the recovery is self-sustaining across the whole market. That remains the central analytical tension for investors.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios for the Next Six Months

Based on the May data and the report’s forecasts for June, investors should consider three scenarios for the second half of 2026.

Scenario one: The T1 recovery broadens. In this scenario, T2 cities begin to follow T1 into positive month-on-month price territory. Secondary listing supply continues to ease. Rental yields stabilize in more cities. Policy provides incremental support, such as further mortgage rate cuts or relaxation of purchase restrictions in core districts. In this scenario, the national sales decline narrows, and developer margins remain supportive. The investment implication is to overweight developers with high exposure to T1 and key T2 cities and strong land banking discipline.

Scenario two: The T1 recovery stays narrow. In this scenario, T1 prices continue to rise, but T2 and lower-tier cities continue to decline. Secondary volume growth fades as the base effect wears off. Rental yields remain weak outside T1. Policy remains incremental and does not provide a catalyst. In this scenario, the national market remains in a slow grind lower, with periodic stabilization in the top tier but no broad recovery. The investment implication is to be highly selective, focusing only on developers with dominant positions in T1 markets.

Scenario three: The T1 recovery stalls. In this scenario, even T1 prices begin to soften as the supply of new premium projects runs ahead of demand. Rental yields stop improving. Secondary listing supply increases again as owners who held off selling decide to exit. Policy disappointment compounds the weakness. In this scenario, the entire market re-tests its lows. The investment implication is to reduce exposure to the sector entirely and wait for clearer signs of a floor.

The May data leans toward scenario two. The T1 recovery is real, but it is not yet broad. The narrowing of the number of cities with positive price momentum is a warning sign. Investors should watch the June data closely for signs of whether the T1 recovery is gaining traction in other cities or losing momentum.

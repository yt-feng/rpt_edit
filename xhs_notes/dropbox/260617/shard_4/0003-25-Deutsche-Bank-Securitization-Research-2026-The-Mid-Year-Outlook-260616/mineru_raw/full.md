## Big Pic: Demand surge pricing

DB Economics View.

■ Resilient economy: 2.2% real GDP growth forecast. Supported by fiscal policy, financial conditions, and AI investments. Fiscal deficit at 6.6% with risk from war, tariff refunds. Higher oil (\$150/bbl) a risk to consumer spending and could reduce real GDP to 1.75%.  
Labor: Firmer payroll gains, broader job growth. 4.3% unemployment through 2026.  
Inflation: Disinflation story less convincing, with core PCE expected to remain at 3.0%.  
Rates: Fed on hold “indefinitely”; risk skewed towards hikes. 10yr UST forecast: 4.7%.

■ Supply. Securitized full-year issuance now projected at \$1tn, net issuance is only \$270bn.

Demand. \$500bn of inflows into IG credit. \$300bn of projected annuity inflows plus \$200bn IG mutual and bond funds. Securitized likely to garner \~\$200bn.

Credit. We downgrade the Consumer slightly from B+ to a B. For securitized, a 'B' grade is still a positive backdrop and only impacts a handful of sectors and some deep subordinates.

Rates. Higher rates normally a headwind for issuance/credit, but strong economy over-riding this challenge. Higher rates will create refi stress for CLOs, CMBS.

Demographics. Demographics are playing a key role in credit. How? 1) Boomers have strong natural demand for fixed income and have the most assets, 2) Boomers are also home-owners and sitting on significant home equity.

\- Relative Value. Take the carry. Securitized will outperform Corporates via carry. Securitized sector forecasts are slightly wider. We see Non-QM as an outperformer; CLO carry is compelling. Subordinate securitized bonds will remain a food fight due to insurance demand and annuity inflows.

## Big Pic: Supportive technicals, accelerating credit resolution

Supply runs hot, but the market is rate-constrained. We forecast \$160bn of private-label CMBS issuance in 2026, with SASB and CRE CLO activity carrying the momentum while conduit remains capped by borrower refi economics and rate volatility.  
- Spreads. Technicals remain constructive. Limited net supply and higher rates will keep demand firm. We expect AAA spreads to remain range-bound at 75-80bp, with BBB spreads widening by roughly 50bp.  
■ Relative value. Prefer SASB mezz over conduit due to better collateral transparency and sponsor selection, even though risk is binary.  
Credit stress is moving from extension to realization

Conduit 60+ delinquency continue to rise. Legacy office and recent multi-family loans are the primary delinquency driver.  
Headline delinquency understates stress. SASB delinquencies remain contained, but proactive extensions, modifications, and denominator growth have helped keep delinquencies low. Office needs more A/B modifications to reflect the basis reset.  
Liquidation volume/severity are increasing as special servicers work through the distressed backlog. Loss severity is a key trend to watch over the next year.

CMBS governance is a structural weak link. Workout outcomes hinge on conflicted control rights, opaque modifications, and delayed loss recognition. We propose cleaner incentives, proper accountability, faster transparency, and clearer documentation that limits discretion in workout outcomes.

## Potential fixes for CMBS market?

Post-GFC CMBS structures improved, but governance remains the weak link. The market is still exposed to conflicted control rights, opaque loan modifications, delayed loss recognition, and documentation that leaves too much room for interpretation.

■ Reduce special-servicer conflicts. Separate B-piece economics from special-servicer appointment rights. Use an independent special servicer with no controlling-class affiliation to reduce conflicts in workout decisions.  
■ Make modifications accountable. Require ex-ante recovery estimates for every material modification (e.g., the expected recovery from a mod). Tie a portion of workout/liquidation fees to realized recovery versus prior estimate. After two modifications or 24 months of cumulative extension, further relief should require operating advisor consent or certificate-holder negative consent.  
■ Accelerate transparency and price discovery. Require independent appraisals within 60 days of delinquency, engaged by the trustee rather than the special servicer. Disclose all material modification terms within five business days.  
- Improve documentation clarity. Simplify waterfall, control-rights, appraisal-reduction, and workout-fee language, so key economic outcomes are mechanical, auditable, and less dependent on interpretation. Standardize disclosure templates for modifications, extensions, fee reimbursements, and realized-loss calculations.

CMBS does not need another full structural overhaul. It needs cleaner incentives, proper accountability, faster transparency, and clearer documentation that limits discretion in workout outcomes.

These are broad, directional ideas. Implementation would require comprehensive refinement and stakeholder alignment. Happy to hear your thoughts.

## Primary Market – room for catchup in H2

The first immediately noticeable data point related to the primary market is New Issue volume, which is tracking lower year-to-date – \$75B priced through last Friday compared to \$92B at this time a year ago, a nearly 20% gap.

The softer YoY comp is due to several reasons already mentioned – concerns over software (a large sector when it comes to exposure in CLOs and Lev Loans), fallout from the Iranian conflict and the higher energy prices its produced, and a challenged arbitrage environment due to what remains a strong bid for leveraged loan debt.

Although loan spreads have marginally widened over the last month compared to CLO primary spreads which have tightened, the current level of loan spreads for a modeled new issue CLO are still 40 bps tighter than the 5-year average, causing many CLO managers to prioritize repricings in the current environment rather than new deal creation.

However, the CLO repricing market has also been challenged in parts – like NI, Resets are also softer YoY, down by 21%, \$73B vs \$93B at this time a year ago.

Since the story of May, however, Reset deal activity has strengthened, averaging \$5.1B per week and narrowing the gap with last year's volume. And given where NI spreads were at this time in 2024, the vintage most in scope for a Reset option – 150 / 188 bps for AAAs / WADMs compared to Reset spreads today – 122 / 156 bps – Reset volume should continue to see a re-acceleration in H2, although likely remaining well off of last year's levels.

## Credit – growing dispersion in manager performance

CLO credit metrics remain firm at the headline across portfolios – WARF, CCCs, and current defaults show a marked improvement YoY.

However, like in the loan market, there is growing dispersion – when considering the Max vs Min, several credit metrics are showing a greater gap between the outperforming vs underperforming CLO portfolios. And when moving beyond credit and considering key market value metrics, such as Weighted Average Portfolio price and the number of assets priced sub-80, the gap in performance is also evident.

CLO equity performance at the manager level is also showing growing dispersion. Quarterly payouts are driven by several factors, such as vintage, initial pricing, and composition of the loan portfolio at the time of pricing. Gaps in performance tend to arise over time due to differing outcomes by Managers when it comes to avoiding loan losses and par erosion. When considering the trailing 2 year quarterly average for payouts, the range between the top and bottom for platforms with at least \$5B in AUM is quite stark – 7.6% at the high end compared to 2.3% at the low end.

So, at the headline, CLO credit metrics are showing relative stability. Yet, the average is masking the underlying dispersion between portfolios.

When looking at the loan market, default rates have been moderating – 3.1% in the LLI (nominal) compared to 4.4% at this time a year ago.

![](images/d72ed755d1e5f5007b45d656a7df41ffcfe583c8ce9ea96dfddcf361d508757e.jpg)

But the distressed ratio, an indicator of the percentage of loans of the LLI priced sub-80, remains at elevated levels from both a nominal and par value perspective. This is an important leading signal of credit distress – when the distressed ratio rises, it typically signals that defaults will rise in the 6-9 months following.

Given a few factors – (1) rates remaining elevated and on hold, (2) mounting pressure on Software borrowers with maturities in 2028, (3) likely signs of consumer weakening for the middle and bottom earning cohorts – we see the likelihood of this trend to hold and for loan defaults to once again begin to drift higher by year's end.

The knock-on effect will be greater loan losses, which will weigh on the Jr OC cushions for some portfolios and ultimately equity cash payouts. This divergence in outcomes reinforces the need for CLO investors to re-underwrite the platforms they are allocating to in the back half of this year.

Other parts of the world, including India and parts of Asia, have seen reductions in planned capacity in 2H2026 due to the increased cost of the jet fuel, as well as, in certain isolated cases, the availability of jet fuel. In more developed markets, there has been some trimming of marginal routes because of them becoming uneconomic due to higher fuel costs, but these changes have been very modest. The market for aircraft ABS has been remarkably resilient in 2026 in the face of the start of the Iran War on February 28, 2026. The subsequent rise in fuel prices and the resulting damage to airline profitability were clear negatives for the aircraft ABS sector, yet the market for both primary and secondary issues remained strong, even after the start of the war, with seemingly no pause at all in the pace of issuance except in the immediate aftermath of the conflict's start. Despite the start of the war, market participants were able to look beyond what most expected to be a temporary rise in fuel prices (seemingly proved correct with the sharp reduction in fuel prices we have seen since the post-war peak) to market fundamentals that would remain strong – airlines were likely to remain profitable in aggregate in 2026, even with higher fuel prices, traffic growth remained positive in 2026, even with the operational disruptions in the Middle East and the market for aircraft remained tight even with the demise of Spirit Airlines and manufacturers (slowly) increasing production. We believe, particularly with the announcement of a new Iran-US agreement to reopen the supply of oil from the Gulf, that fundamentals for aircraft ABS will be positive in the remainder of 2026. With spreads already at tight levels (+150-160bp for better seniors) and issuance likely at record levels, we believe that opportunities for significant aircraft ABS outperformance will be idiosyncratic rather than broad-based. Best opportunities for outperformance can most likely be found in the secondary market, we believe, particularly around sub-tranches of pre-Covid transactions trading at a discount that will likely see accelerating repayment from aircraft sales in 2026. For post-Covid transactions, we prefer relative value of senior tranches to mezz tranches as the cash flow structure introduced after Covid significantly favors the senior tranche relative to the mezz tranche in a downside scenario.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates – these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may – by construction – lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.

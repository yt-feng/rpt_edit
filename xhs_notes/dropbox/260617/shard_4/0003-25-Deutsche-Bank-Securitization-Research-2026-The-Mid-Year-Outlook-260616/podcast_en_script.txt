EN_A: I came across a report recently with a really interesting title: "Demand-Surge Pricing." What does that even mean? Is it saying that the securitized market is now a seller's market?
EN_B: That's exactly right. The core thesis of this report is that in the second half of 2026, the securitized credit market will see a form of demand-driven pricing. Simply put, there's too much money chasing too few good assets.
EN_A: Too much money? But the Fed is on hold, and rates are still high. Isn't that a headwind?
EN_B: That's the most counter-intuitive part of the report. Normally, high rates would suppress the market. But this time is different. There are two reasons. First, the U.S. economy is still resilient, with GDP growth forecast at 2.2%. Second, a new class of mega-buyers has entered the arena.
EN_A: Mega-buyers? Who are we talking about?
EN_B: Insurance companies and retirees. The report has a fascinating chart titled "Give Grandma and Grandpa a Call." The data shows that the U.S. population over 60 is 84 million, over 75% own their homes, and they control 60% to 70% of the nation's financial wealth.
EN_A: So what are these people doing right now?
EN_B: They are buying fixed income. Because today's rates are the highest we've seen in 20 years. The average 10-year Treasury yield over the past two decades was just 2.9%. Now it's near 4.5%. This generation has been waiting for this moment to lock in high yields. They are pouring money in through annuities and bond funds, creating an estimated 500 billion dollars of inflows into investment grade credit.
EN_A: 500 billion dollars! How much of that will flow into securitized products?
EN_B: The report estimates about 200 billion dollars. But the key is that the net supply of securitized products for the whole year is only 270 billion. So you have a lot of money chasing a limited amount of assets. That's the definition of demand-surge pricing.
EN_A: So what does that mean for investors? Is it a free-for-all?
EN_B: Not exactly. The report points out that most AAA-rated securitized spreads are already near their three-year lows. So the future returns will come from carry, not from spread compression.
EN_A: So it's about collecting the coupon, not betting on price appreciation?
EN_B: Exactly. The report is very clear that securitized will outperform corporate bonds, driven by carry. And it specifically calls out sectors like Non-QM RMBS and CLOs as having very compelling carry.
EN_A: But I noticed the report is cautious on consumer credit. It even downgraded the consumer sector from B+ to B.
EN_B: That's right. That's the second key insight. Even though the macro economy is strong, consumer credit stress is rising. The report mentions that delinquencies on credit cards and auto loans are trending higher. But here's the nuance: a "B" grade for securitized credit is still a positive backdrop.
EN_A: Why is a downgrade considered positive?
EN_B: Because of the structure. Securitized products have a capital stack. Even if the underlying credit deteriorates slightly, the senior AAA bonds remain very safe. The real impact is on the deep subordinated bonds. The report uses a great phrase: it says the subordinate bonds will remain a "food fight."
EN_A: A food fight? That sounds intense.
EN_B: It is. Because insurance companies and annuity funds are desperate for yield. They are aggressively buying these higher-yielding subordinated bonds. So even as credit weakens, demand remains incredibly strong.
EN_A: What about commercial real estate? I keep hearing that office space is a disaster.
EN_B: CRE is definitely the most stressed sector. The report shows that CMBS delinquencies are still rising, driven by legacy office loans and some multi-family loans. But here's the interesting part: the report says credit stress is moving from "extension to realization."
EN_A: What does that mean in plain English?
EN_B: It means that for a long time, troubled loans were just being extended and modified, kicking the can down the road. Now, special servicers are starting to actually work through the distressed backlog. Liquidation volumes and loss severities are increasing. It's a painful but necessary clean-up process.
EN_A: So how should an investor approach this sector?
EN_B: The report suggests preferring SASB deals over conduit CMBS. SASB deals are single-asset, single-borrower. The collateral is more transparent, so you know exactly which building you are exposed to.
EN_A: But the report also flags a structural issue with CMBS governance, right?
EN_B: Yes, and that's a very deep point. The report is blunt: it says the workout process is plagued by conflicted control rights, opaque modifications, and delayed loss recognition. It calls for cleaner incentives and faster transparency. This is a real systemic weakness.
EN_A: What about the new Basel III rules? Do they help banks buy more securitized products?
EN_B: This is good news. The 2026 re-proposal is much friendlier than the 2023 version. The risk weight floor for senior tranches was lowered from 20% to 15%, and the punitive "p-factor" was left unchanged. This should make banks better buyers of securitized products in the future.

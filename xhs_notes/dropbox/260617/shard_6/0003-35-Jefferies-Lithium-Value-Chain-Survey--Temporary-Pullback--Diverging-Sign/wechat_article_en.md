# The Lithium Value Chain Is Splitting in Two: Converters Are Braking While Battery Producers Hold Steady

The lithium market is not experiencing a uniform downturn. It is undergoing a structural divergence between two layers of the value chain that have fundamentally different outlooks on demand, pricing, and inventory risk. According to a proprietary survey of lithium converters and battery materials producers, converters expect prices to decline more than 10 percent in the third quarter, while battery producers anticipate only modest single-digit declines. This gap is not noise. It reflects a deeper asymmetry in how each segment reads the signals from end-market demand.

The survey, which covers approximately 77 percent of lithium converter sales in China and 73 percent of battery materials producers, reveals that new orders have decelerated and confidence in those orders has weakened. Converters are seeing volumes shrink year-over-year, while battery producers report relatively stable volumes. Inventory dynamics tell an even more telling story: converters believe battery producers are holding too little inventory, but battery producers believe their own customers have a modest overhang. Both cannot be right simultaneously, and that contradiction is the central tension in the lithium market today.

This is not a cyclical trough that will resolve neatly with a demand rebound. The data suggests the value chain is repricing risk in an environment where the relationship between upstream and downstream expectations has broken down. For investors, the critical question is not whether lithium prices will recover, but which layer of the chain will prove to have the more accurate read on reality.

The timing matters. The survey data runs through mid-2026 and shows that the divergence between converter and battery producer expectations has been persistent for over two years. It is not a short-term anomaly. It is a structural feature of a market where information asymmetry and inventory positioning have created a persistent gap between what upstream suppliers see and what downstream buyers feel.

## Converters See a Deeper Slowdown Than Battery Producers, and the Gap Has Not Closed

The most striking pattern in the survey is the persistent gap between converter and battery producer expectations across nearly every metric. Converters have consistently expected larger price declines, reported weaker volumes, and shown lower order backlog confidence than battery producers. From early 2024 through mid-2026, converter expectations for price changes over the next three months have ranged from negative 12 percent to negative 6 percent, with only a brief period in early 2026 showing a positive reading. Battery producers, by contrast, have consistently expected price declines of only 2 to 8 percent, with no positive expectations at any point.

This gap matters because it indicates that the two segments are operating with different assumptions about end-demand. Converters are closer to the raw material supply and have less visibility into final battery demand. They react to order flows from battery producers, which have been weak. Battery producers, in turn, are closer to automotive and energy storage customers, and they report that new orders have been flat to slightly positive year-over-year, even as converters report negative readings.

The volume data reinforces the divergence. Converter volumes have been negative year-over-year for most of the survey period, with readings as low as negative 15 percent. Battery producer volumes have been closer to flat, with occasional positive readings. This suggests that battery producers are not seeing the same demand weakness that converters are experiencing. The question is whether converters are overreacting to temporary destocking, or whether battery producers are underestimating the slowdown that is about to hit them.

## Inventory Signals Are Contradictory and Suggest Information Asymmetry Across the Chain

Inventory data from the survey reveals a critical disconnect. Converters believe that battery producer inventories are too low, with readings often below the 50 percent threshold that indicates excess inventory. Battery producers, however, believe that their own customers hold inventories that are too high, with readings frequently above 50 percent. This contradiction implies that each layer of the chain is interpreting the same end-market data differently, and neither has a complete picture.

The inventory-to-volume ratio tells a similar story. For converters, the gap between inventory growth and volume growth has been volatile, swinging from negative to positive and back. For battery producers, the ratio has been consistently negative or near zero, indicating that inventory growth has not outpaced volume growth. This suggests that battery producers have managed their inventory more cautiously, while converters have experienced more pronounced swings.

The implication for investors is that the risk of a sharp correction is asymmetric. If battery producers are correct that customer inventories are too high, then a destocking cycle could hit the entire chain, pulling battery producer volumes down to meet converter expectations. If converters are correct that battery producer inventories are too low, then any improvement in end-demand could trigger a rapid restocking cycle that drives prices higher. The market is pricing in neither scenario cleanly, which creates both risk and opportunity.

## Order Backlog Confidence Is Collapsing for Converters but Remains Elevated for Battery Producers

The order backlog data is perhaps the most forward-looking indicator in the survey. A reading above 50 percent indicates that order backlogs are expanding. For converters, this reading has been below 50 percent for most of the survey period, with readings as low as 20 percent. For battery producers, the reading has consistently been at or above 50 percent, with readings as high as 70 percent.

This divergence suggests that battery producers have a pipeline of committed orders that converters do not see. It could mean that battery producers are building inventory in anticipation of future demand, or it could mean that converters are being cut out of the supply chain as battery producers source more material directly or through alternative channels. The survey does not resolve this question, but it is a critical one for investors to monitor.

The sales expectations data tells a similar story. Converters expect sales to decline year-over-year in the coming month, with readings consistently negative. Battery producers expect sales to be flat to slightly positive. This gap has persisted for over two years and shows no sign of closing. If battery producers are right, then the converter segment is facing a structural decline in market share. If converters are right, then battery producers are about to experience a sharp downward revision in their own expectations.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Source of the Divergence and the Risk of a Convergence Event

The survey provides an excellent description of the divergence but does not fully explain its root cause. There are at least three possible explanations, and each has different implications for investors.

First, the divergence could be driven by technology shifts. If battery producers are moving toward new cathode chemistries that require less lithium or different lithium specifications, converters may be seeing reduced demand for their current product mix even as overall battery production grows. The survey does not break down demand by chemistry, so this remains an open question.

Second, the divergence could reflect a structural change in the supply chain. Battery producers may be integrating backward, sourcing lithium directly from mines or through long-term contracts that bypass traditional converters. If this is happening, the converter segment could be facing a permanent reduction in its addressable market, not a cyclical downturn.

Third, the divergence could be a timing issue. Converters may be reacting to a temporary destocking event that has already passed, while battery producers are looking through it to a demand recovery that has not yet materialized. The survey data shows that converter expectations have been consistently negative for two years, which argues against a simple timing explanation, but it remains possible.

The risk of a convergence event is the most important unresolved question. If the divergence resolves through a sharp downward revision in battery producer expectations, the entire chain could face a synchronized downturn. If it resolves through an upward revision in converter expectations, the chain could see a rapid price recovery. The survey data does not favor either outcome, which means investors need to monitor the same metrics the survey tracks and be prepared for either scenario.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Signals to Watch and Two Scenarios to Prepare For

Given the divergence in the survey data, investors need a framework for making decisions that does not depend on knowing which segment is correct. The following three signals provide a way to monitor the resolution of the current tension.

First, track the ratio of converter to battery producer expectations for new orders. If this ratio starts to converge toward one, it will indicate that information asymmetry is breaking down. If it remains wide, the divergence is likely structural and will persist.

Second, monitor the inventory-to-volume ratio for battery producers. If this ratio turns positive, it will indicate that battery producers are building inventory relative to sales, which would support the converter view that demand is weaker than battery producers believe. If it remains negative, the battery producer view of stable demand is more credible.

Third, watch for changes in the order backlog confidence for battery producers. A sustained drop below 50 percent would be a leading indicator that battery producer expectations are about to converge with converter expectations. A sustained rise above 70 percent would support the battery producer view that demand is robust.

Two scenarios should be on the table. In the first scenario, the divergence resolves through a downward revision in battery producer expectations. This would likely lead to a synchronized downturn in the entire lithium value chain, with prices falling further and volumes contracting across both segments. In this scenario, the safest position is to reduce exposure to the upstream segment and wait for a clear bottom in converter expectations.

In the second scenario, the divergence resolves through an upward revision in converter expectations. This would likely be driven by a restocking cycle that pulls converter volumes and prices higher. In this scenario, the best opportunities are in the converter segment, which would benefit from the largest upside surprise.

The survey data does not tell us which scenario is more likely. It tells us that the market is not pricing in either scenario cleanly, which means there is a potential mispricing that investors can exploit if they are willing to take a view.

## The Real Opportunity Lies in Understanding the Information Asymmetry, Not in Predicting Prices

The most valuable insight from this survey is not a price forecast. It is the recognition that the lithium value chain is structurally fragmented in its view of demand. This fragmentation creates opportunities for investors who can identify which layer of the chain has the more accurate read on reality and position accordingly.

The survey provides the data to make that judgment, but it does not make the judgment for you. The charts show the divergence clearly, but they do not explain why it exists or how it will resolve. That is the analytical challenge that investors must address themselves.

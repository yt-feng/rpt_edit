# The Hormuz Deal Reshapes Oil Markets, But the Real Strategic Question Is How Fast Supply Can Recover—and What That Means for 2027 Prices

The announcement of an interim deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz marks a pivotal moment for global oil markets. A major global investment bank has cut its price forecasts, reflecting the assumption that Persian Gulf exports normalize to pre-war levels by the end of July rather than August. This one-month acceleration in supply recovery reduces the fair value of crude by roughly $10 per barrel for late 2026 and $5 per barrel for 2027. The new base case now sees Brent at $80 in the fourth quarter of 2026 and averaging $75 in 2027, with WTI at $75 and $70 respectively.

Yet this headline revision masks a more complex and strategically important reality. The risks around this supply recovery are profoundly two-sided, and the net bias remains to the upside. The market is not simply pricing in a smooth normalization. It is wrestling with the aftermath of the sharpest oil production shock in history—a 14 million barrel per day disruption—that was managed with a far smaller-than-expected deficit and without sustained extreme prices. The implications for how investors should think about oil over the next eighteen months go far beyond a simple calendar adjustment.

The core argument here is that the deal changes the base case but not the fundamental strategic calculus. The market is entering a period where the speed and completeness of supply recovery will determine whether prices settle near long-term fair value or break significantly higher. The report's analysis reveals a market that is structurally tighter than headline surplus figures suggest, and where the most important variables are not fully captured by conventional supply-demand models.

## The Supply Recovery Is Not a Single Event But a Sequence of Uncertain Steps, and Each Step Carries Asymmetric Price Risk

The assumption that Gulf exports normalize by end-July appears straightforward, but the underlying mechanics are anything but. The report estimates that oil flows from Persian Gulf countries have already risen to roughly 11 million barrels per day, about 50 percent of pre-war normal levels. To reach full normalization, Hormuz flows need to increase by approximately 12 million barrels per day from current levels—a massive logistical and geopolitical undertaking.

The recovery could be stronger than expected. Producers such as Saudi Arabia and the UAE may respond more aggressively to low OECD commercial stocks, potentially pushing production above pre-war levels. Iran's production could rise on sanctions relief, adding further supply. In a stronger recovery scenario, the report suggests Brent could average just under $70 in late 2026 and under $60 in 2027.

But the recovery could also be weaker. A resumption of regional hostilities or strikes on ships could keep shippers risk-averse, prolonging the supply disruption. Clearing potential mines might require significant time. And critically, Iran might effectively close the Strait again even after re-opening, particularly if detailed nuclear talks do not succeed. In a weaker recovery scenario, Brent could rise above $130 in late 2026 and average $105 in 2027.

The asymmetry here is striking. The downside scenario delivers prices that are roughly in line with long-term fair value. The upside scenario delivers prices that are nearly double that. This is not a symmetric risk distribution. The market is pricing in a benign outcome, but the structural forces that could prevent that outcome are numerous and non-trivial.

## The Large 2027 Surplus Is Less Bearish Than It Appears Because Stock Levels and Structural Demand Are Changing the Rules of the Game

The report forecasts a 3.2 million barrel per day surplus in 2027, which on its face suggests significant downward pressure on prices. Yet the bank maintains resilient 2027 price forecasts in line with long-term fair values of $75 for Brent. This apparent contradiction reveals a deeper insight about how oil markets are evolving.

The reason is that OECD commercial oil stocks are unlikely to reach very high levels following large draws in the first half of 2026 and a structural trend of global strategic stockpiling. The report estimates that strategic stockpiling alone will absorb over 1 million barrels per day in 2027, with China accounting for roughly half of that total. This structural demand from government stockpiling is not captured in standard surplus-deficit calculations, and it fundamentally alters the relationship between physical balances and price.

Furthermore, the report identifies a security premium that is likely to keep a floor under prices. The experience of managing a 14 million barrel per day production shock with a smaller-than-expected deficit has demonstrated remarkable flexibility in the system, particularly from Chinese demand. But that same experience has also shown that disruption risk is real and costly. Markets are likely to demand a premium for bearing that risk going forward, even as supply normalizes.

The implication is that traditional surplus analysis may be misleading investors. A 3.2 million barrel per day surplus in a world where governments are actively stockpiling and where disruption premiums persist is not the same as a 3.2 million barrel per day surplus in a world of purely commercial inventory management. The effective buffer is smaller than the headline number suggests.

## The Demand Recovery Is Not Automatic, and Its Shape Will Determine Whether the Supply Recovery Creates Glut or Balance

The report notes a forecast for a somewhat firmer demand recovery in the second half of 2026 through 2027, driven by improved affordability as prices decline from crisis levels. But this is not a mechanical relationship. Historical patterns show that oil demand tends to recover swiftly following supply shocks but remains weak following recessions. The current environment combines elements of both.

The supply shock from the Hormuz disruption has created a demand response through price rationing. As prices fall back toward pre-crisis levels, demand should recover. But the report's data shows that the demand downgrade since the war began has been substantial—approximately 4.7 million barrels per day in the second quarter of 2026. The question is how much of that demand loss is permanent versus temporary.

The strategic question for investors is whether the demand recovery will be strong enough to absorb the returning supply. If demand snaps back quickly, the surplus could be smaller than forecast. If demand proves stickier—due to lingering uncertainty, changed consumer behavior, or economic weakness—the surplus could be larger. The report's base case assumes a recovery, but the data suggests this is one of the most uncertain variables in the outlook.

The report does not fully answer the question of what demand elasticity looks like in a post-shock environment. The remarkable flexibility shown by Chinese demand during the crisis suggests that the system has more capacity to absorb disruption than models predicted. But it also raises the question of whether that flexibility represents a structural change or a one-time response to extraordinary circumstances.

## The Report Leaves an Unresolved Question: What Happens If the Deal Fails or If the Recovery Stalls Midway?

The report's analysis is built on the assumption that the deal is implemented and that supply normalization proceeds on the stated timeline. But the deal itself is an interim agreement, and its full details remain unclear. The report acknowledges several scenarios where the recovery could be weaker, but it does not fully explore the implications of a partial or stalled recovery.

Consider a scenario where Hormuz reopens but at reduced capacity, or where flows return to 70 percent of pre-war levels but then plateau. The report's data shows that the recovery to date has brought flows to roughly 50 percent of normal. Getting from 50 percent to 100 percent is not a linear process. Each incremental increase faces diminishing returns and increasing logistical and political hurdles.

The report also does not fully address the question of what happens to the security premium if the recovery is partial. If markets perceive that the risk of future disruption remains high, the premium could persist even as physical supply improves. This would create a scenario where prices remain elevated despite apparent surplus, as the market prices in option value for future disruption.

The unresolved question is whether the market is correctly pricing the probability of a failed or partial recovery. The forward curve shows prices declining toward long-term fair value, suggesting the market is pricing in a high probability of full normalization. If that probability is overestimated, the current forward curve may be too low.

## A Decision Framework for Oil Investors: Three Scenarios and the Key Variables That Differentiate Them

For investors trying to navigate this environment, the report's analysis suggests a framework organized around three scenarios, each driven by a small set of critical variables.

The base case assumes full supply normalization by end-July, demand recovery of roughly 4-5 million barrels per day, and a security premium that fades gradually. In this scenario, Brent averages $80 in late 2026 and $75 in 2027. The key variables to watch are Hormuz flow data, OECD commercial stock levels, and demand indicators from China and emerging markets.

The upside scenario assumes that supply recovery stalls or reverses, with Hormuz flows remaining below pre-war levels through 2027. In this scenario, Gulf exports rise gradually by 10 million barrels per day by December 2027, but never fully normalize. Brent could rise above $130 in late 2026 and average $105 in 2027. The key variables to watch are geopolitical developments in the Gulf, nuclear negotiations with Iran, and shipping insurance rates.

The downside scenario assumes that supply normalizes by early July, that demand losses prove stickier than expected, and that non-OPEC supply proves stronger than forecast. In this scenario, Brent averages just under $70 in late 2026 and just under $60 in 2027. The key variables to watch are the pace of Iranian production recovery, demand data from Europe and the United States, and non-OPEC production growth.

The framework highlights that the most important variable is not the level of supply but the speed and completeness of its recovery. A one-month delay in normalization has a $10 per barrel impact on late 2026 prices and a $5 per barrel impact on 2027 prices. The market is pricing for the fastest plausible recovery, which means any delay creates upside risk.

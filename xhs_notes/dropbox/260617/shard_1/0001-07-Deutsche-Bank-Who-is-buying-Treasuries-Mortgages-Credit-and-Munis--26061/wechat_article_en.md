# The $61 Trillion Question: Who Will Absorb the Next Wave of US Government Debt, and at What Price?

The United States fixed income market now stands at $61 trillion in outstanding securities, with Treasury and agency debt alone accounting for roughly 70% of that total. Treasury debt as a percentage of GDP has reached 96%, up from 75% before the pandemic, and non-financial corporate debt has declined from a pandemic peak of 55% of GDP to 41% today. These numbers tell a clear story: the composition of American debt is shifting, and the burden is migrating inexorably toward the sovereign sector. The critical question for every institutional investor, portfolio strategist, and risk manager is no longer whether this debt will grow, but who will hold it, on what terms, and at what point the buying capacity of traditional marginal buyers becomes exhausted.

The report from DB's rates and credit strategy team, led by Matthew Luzzetti and colleagues, provides the most granular dissection available of who is buying Treasuries, mortgages, credit, and municipal securities. It is not a forecasting document in the traditional sense. It is a structural map of demand. And what that map reveals is both reassuring and deeply unsettling. The domestic private sector has stepped in to absorb much of the supply that the Federal Reserve and foreign central banks have withdrawn. But the composition of that demand is changing in ways that will matter enormously for the next phase of the cycle.

Foreign ownership of US Treasuries has fallen to 30% of outstanding debt in the first quarter of 2026, barely above the multi-decade low of 29% seen in the third quarter of 2023. The Federal Reserve still holds close to 30% of Treasuries with ten years or longer maturity, but its overall footprint is shrinking as quantitative tightening continues. The burden of absorbing new issuance has fallen on domestic banks, mutual funds, pension funds, insurance companies, and primary dealers. These buyers have different mandates, different duration preferences, and different sensitivity to price than the foreign official sector or the central bank. Understanding their behavior is not an academic exercise. It is the foundation for any serious view on interest rates, credit spreads, and portfolio construction over the next two to three years.

## The Domestic Private Sector Has Become the Marginal Buyer of Treasuries, but Its Appetite Is Not Infinite

The most important structural shift in the Treasury market over the past five years is the retreat of the two dominant buyers that sustained the market during the quantitative easing era. Foreign official holdings, which peaked at nearly 50% of outstanding Treasuries in the early 2010s, have fallen to 30%. The Federal Reserve's holdings, which exceeded 25% of the market at the peak of QE, are declining. The gap has been filled by domestic private investors, but the composition of that demand is fragile.

Net purchases by the domestic sector have risen sharply, reaching approximately $700 billion in 2024 before moderating to around $500 billion in 2026. This category includes households, banks, pension funds, insurance companies, government retirement funds, mutual funds, GSEs, and other entities. The breadth of this category is both a strength and a weakness. It means demand is diversified across many investor types with different motivations. But it also means that no single group has the balance sheet depth or the policy mandate to absorb supply in a crisis the way the Fed or foreign central banks once did.

The critical insight here is that domestic private buyers are price-sensitive in a way that official buyers are not. When the Fed was buying Treasuries as part of QE, it was largely indifferent to yield. When foreign central banks accumulate reserves, they are motivated by reserve management objectives, not relative value. But when a domestic pension fund or mutual fund buys a Treasury, it is making an active allocation decision against alternatives. If yields are not attractive enough relative to credit, mortgages, or cash, these buyers will step back. The market has already seen this dynamic play out in the volatility of auction demand over the past eighteen months, where tail coverage ratios have fluctuated wildly depending on the level of yields and the maturity being offered.

The implication is that the Treasury market is now more elastic on the demand side than it has been in decades. This is not necessarily a bearish observation, but it is a structural change that increases the probability of periodic dislocations. If supply surges faster than domestic demand can adjust, yields will need to rise to clear the market. The days of captive buyers absorbing unlimited supply at artificially low yields are over.

## Banks Are Returning as Buyers of Treasury and Agency Debt, but Their Capacity Is Constrained by Regulation and Deposit Dynamics

One of the most striking data points in the report is the trajectory of commercial bank holdings of Treasury and agency securities. After contracting from a peak of $4.7 trillion in 2022 to around $4.0 trillion in 2024, bank holdings have expanded again to approximately $4.8 trillion in 2026. This reversal deserves careful attention because banks are not traditional marginal buyers of duration. They are leveraged institutions with regulatory constraints on their balance sheets and a natural preference for shorter maturities.

The contraction in bank holdings during the tightening cycle was driven by two factors. First, the rapid increase in interest rates generated mark-to-market losses on existing bond portfolios, creating a powerful disincentive to add to those positions. Second, the outflow of deposits from the banking system during the early stages of the tightening cycle reduced the liability base that banks use to fund asset purchases. The subsequent recovery in bank buying reflects a stabilization of deposit bases and a normalization of the yield curve, which has made it more attractive for banks to extend duration again.

But the regulatory environment remains a binding constraint. The Basel III endgame proposals, whatever their final form, will require banks to hold more capital against their securities portfolios. This means that the return of banks as buyers of Treasuries and agency debt is likely to be more measured than in previous cycles. Banks will be selective about which maturities they buy and will be sensitive to the capital charges associated with different tenors. The report's data on the maturity composition of bank holdings would be particularly valuable here, as it would reveal whether banks are concentrating their purchases in the short end of the curve or extending further out.

The strategic implication is that banks can no longer be counted on as automatic stabilizers for the Treasury market. Their buying is now more discretionary, more regulated, and more dependent on the shape of the yield curve. This adds another layer of uncertainty to the demand outlook for government debt.

## Primary Dealers Have Become the Shock Absorbers of Last Resort, and Their Balance Sheets Are Stretched

The most dramatic change in the structure of the Treasury market is the explosion of primary dealer net holdings. According to the report, primary dealer positions in US government securities have risen from approximately $150 billion in 2022 to over $550 billion in 2026. This is not a cyclical fluctuation. It is a structural shift in how the market functions.

Primary dealers are the intermediaries that the Federal Reserve uses to conduct open market operations and that the Treasury relies on to distribute new debt. In normal market conditions, dealers hold inventory temporarily and distribute it to end investors. The current level of dealer holdings suggests that the distribution mechanism is not working as smoothly as it should. Dealers are holding larger and larger inventories for longer periods, which means they are effectively acting as investors rather than intermediaries. This is a sign of underlying demand weakness.

The risks associated with elevated dealer inventories are well understood but worth restating. Dealers are highly leveraged and finance their positions in the repo market. If a sudden spike in yields forces them to mark down their inventory, they can be forced to delever rapidly, which amplifies the selloff. This is precisely the mechanism that caused the Treasury market dysfunction in March 2020 and the LDI crisis in the UK gilt market in September 2022. The current level of dealer holdings represents a systemic vulnerability that has not been adequately addressed by regulatory reforms.

The report's data on the maturity composition of dealer inventories shows that the buildup is concentrated in shorter-dated securities, particularly bills and notes under three years. This is somewhat reassuring, as short-dated securities have lower duration risk and are easier to finance. But it also means that the market for longer-dated Treasuries is even more dependent on the willingness of real money investors to step in. If those investors become hesitant, the burden will fall on dealers to hold longer-dated inventory, which would increase their risk exposure significantly.

## The Foreign Buyer Base Is Shrinking and Shifting, Creating a Structural Tailwind for Higher Yields

Foreign ownership of US Treasuries has fallen from a peak of nearly 50% in the early 2010s to 30% today, not far from the multi-decade low of 29% seen in the third quarter of 2023. This decline is not a temporary phenomenon. It reflects a deliberate diversification away from US dollar assets by several major reserve holders, most notably China and Russia, but also a broader trend among emerging market central banks.

Global FX reserves have stabilized at around $13 trillion, but the dollar share of those reserves has been trending lower. The report notes that the dollar's share remains above 55%, but the trajectory is downward. This is a slow-moving but powerful force. Every percentage point decline in the dollar share of global reserves represents approximately $130 billion of reduced demand for US government debt. Over a decade, this can amount to more than a trillion dollars of foregone buying.

The decline in foreign demand is not uniform across all US fixed income sectors. Foreign investors remain significant buyers of corporate bonds, where they hold approximately 28% of outstanding securities alongside life insurance companies and mutual funds. They are also active in the agency mortgage market. But their retreat from Treasuries is the most consequential shift because Treasuries are the benchmark asset class that determines the cost of capital for the entire economy.

The strategic question that the report leaves open is whether foreign demand will stabilize at current levels or continue to decline. The answer depends on factors that are outside the scope of the report: the trajectory of US fiscal policy, the evolution of geopolitical tensions, and the development of alternative reserve assets. What is clear is that the era of ever-increasing foreign demand for US Treasuries is over. The market must adjust to a new equilibrium where domestic buyers play a larger role and yields are higher as a result.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Fiscal Supply and Private Demand Elasticity

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves one critical question unresolved. It provides a detailed snapshot of who is buying today, but it does not fully model how demand will respond to a sustained increase in supply. The Congressional Budget Office projects that the federal deficit will remain above 5% of GDP for the foreseeable future, which implies that Treasury issuance will continue at elevated levels. The report's data on buyer composition is a static picture. The dynamic question is how each category of buyer will adjust as supply accumulates.

The domestic private sector has absorbed the post-QE supply so far, but the elasticity of that demand is unknown. At what level of yields do pension funds become indifferent between Treasuries and credit? At what point do mutual funds decide that cash is more attractive than duration? How much capacity do banks have to add to their securities portfolios before regulatory constraints bind? These are the questions that will determine whether the market clears smoothly or experiences periodic dislocations.

The report's data on primary dealer inventories provides a hint. The fact that dealers are holding record amounts of Treasuries suggests that the distribution mechanism is already under strain. If supply continues to grow faster than end-investor demand, dealer inventories will rise further, increasing the risk of a disorderly adjustment. The report does not provide a framework for quantifying this risk, but it provides the raw material for investors to build their own.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Map Your Portfolio Against the Changing Demand Structure

The most practical use of this report is as a tool for portfolio construction. Investors should think about the US fixed income market not as a single homogeneous asset class but as a set of submarkets with different demand structures. The report provides the data to map those structures and to identify where the risks and opportunities lie.

For Treasury investors, the key insight is that the marginal buyer has shifted from price-insensitive official institutions to price-sensitive private investors. This means that the Treasury market is likely to be more volatile and more responsive to changes in supply expectations. Investors should pay close attention to auction data and dealer positioning as leading indicators of market stress.

For credit investors, the report's data on the buyer base for corporate bonds is reassuring. Foreign investors, life insurance companies, and mutual funds together hold the majority of outstanding corporate bonds, and these buyers have relatively stable demand profiles. The decline in non-financial corporate debt as a share of GDP from 55% to 41% also suggests that the supply side is improving, which should support credit spreads.

For mortgage investors, the report's data on agency MBS holdings is essential reading. The Fed's withdrawal from the agency MBS market has created a gap that private buyers have been slow to fill. The report provides a detailed breakdown of who is buying agency debt and how that has changed over time, which is critical for understanding the technical dynamics of the mortgage market.

For municipal investors, the report offers a similarly granular view of the buyer base for state and local government debt. The muni market has historically been dominated by retail investors and mutual funds, but the report's data shows how that is evolving.

## The Unanswered Questions That Make the Full Report Essential Reading

No single report can answer every question about the future of the US fixed income market. The value of this analysis is that it identifies the right questions. The data on foreign central bank behavior is detailed but stops short of projecting how reserve managers will respond to a potential shift in US fiscal credibility. The analysis of bank buying is thorough but does not fully incorporate the impact of regulatory changes that are still being finalized. The data on dealer positioning is alarming but does not provide a clear threshold for when inventory levels become dangerous.

These are not weaknesses of the report. They are the natural boundaries of any forward-looking analysis. The report provides the foundation. Investors must build the structure on top of it. The data on auction demand trends, on the maturity breakdown of private holdings, and on the asset allocation shifts across different investor categories are all available in the full report with accompanying charts. For anyone who manages fixed income exposure, these are not optional inputs. They are essential.

EN_A: Copper prices have been on a wild ride. They went from around eight thousand six hundred dollars a ton last January to over fourteen thousand in May. That’s an all-time high. What’s driving this?
EN_B: It’s a triple whammy. First, supply disruptions at major mines like Grasberg in Indonesia. Second, a lot of speculative money piled into the futures market. But the biggest factor is U.S. trade policy, which is fundamentally distorting the global copper trade.
EN_A: Distorting it how? What policy are we talking about?
EN_B: The Trump administration wants what they call “resource independence.” They want to make sure the U.S. has enough domestic refining capacity. Instead of the government buying and stockpiling copper, they’re threatening to put a tariff on imported refined copper. Fifteen percent starting in January 2027, and potentially thirty percent by 2028.
EN_A: So traders are rushing to get copper into the U.S. before those tariffs hit?
EN_B: Exactly. That’s why we’ve seen COMEX inventories in the U.S. build up by over five hundred thousand tons since last summer. They’ve been adding roughly ten thousand tons every single week. This artificial stockpiling is a huge part of why prices are so high.
EN_A: So what’s the big question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: The big question is: will these tariffs actually happen? The White House has a deadline of June 30th, less than three weeks from now. The report’s base case is that they will not impose the tariffs. They call this the “TACO” scenario.
EN_A: TACO? Like the food? That’s a memorable name.
EN_B: Right. The logic is that the administration promised to negotiate trade deals with countries like Chile to secure supply. But so far, no deals have been announced. Without those deals, slapping a tariff on copper just hurts American manufacturers.
EN_A: So if the report is right and they don’t impose tariffs, what happens to the copper price?
EN_B: They see it gradually declining back to around eleven thousand dollars a ton. If the tariff threat is gone, there’s no reason to keep all that copper locked up in U.S. warehouses. That inventory will flow back into the global market, increasing supply and putting downward pressure on prices.
EN_A: But what if they do impose tariffs? Is there a bull case here?
EN_B: Absolutely. They outline three bull case pathways. The mildest one is just a delay in the decision. The most aggressive one is announcing both the fifteen percent tariff in 2027 and the thirty percent tariff in 2028. In that scenario, copper prices could very well challenge the all-time high again.
EN_A: So for someone listening, what does this really mean in practical terms?
EN_B: It means copper is sitting at a huge policy crossroads. If you bet on no tariffs, the current high price looks fragile. If you bet on tariffs, there’s still upside. The report highlights that companies like Freeport-McMoRan and Antofagasta have a very high sensitivity to copper prices, over one times beta.
EN_A: Are there any questions the report doesn’t fully answer? Things that would make me want to read the full document?
EN_B: Two big ones. First, the report assumes that diverting copper to the U.S. will create a shortage elsewhere. But it doesn’t fully explore whether that shortage will be offset by a demand slowdown or substitution with other materials. Second, it mentions the lack of a deal with Chile, but it doesn’t give details on other potential negotiations, like with Indonesia. Those open questions are what will really determine the long-term price path.

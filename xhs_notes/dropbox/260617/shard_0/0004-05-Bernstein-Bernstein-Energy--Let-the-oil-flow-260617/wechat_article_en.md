# The Oil Market Has Already Absorbed a 1 Billion Barrel Shock. The Real Story Is What Comes Next.

The recent US-Iran Memorandum of Understanding represents a constructive diplomatic step, but the market is mispricing the timeline for normalization. While headlines focus on the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, the physical reality of restoring oil flows is far more complex and protracted than many investors assume. The operational details remain unclear: the timing of vessel returns, the availability and pricing of war-risk insurance, safety protocols for transiting ships, and the progress of minesweeping operations all introduce significant uncertainty. As a result, while flows are set to resume, physical trade is unlikely to normalize quickly given lingering logistical and security constraints.

The critical insight that many market participants are underestimating is that the oil market has already absorbed approximately one billion barrels of supply shock through inventory drawdowns. This is not a small adjustment. It represents a structural tightening that will not be resolved by a simple diplomatic handshake. The cumulative drawdown spans roughly 300 million barrels from strategic petroleum reserves, approximately 450 million barrels from Chinese inventories, around 140 million barrels from oil-on-water, and the balance from commercial stocks. This significant depletion reinforces the importance of restocking once supply flows stabilize.

We expect normalization to take around six months. This timeline reflects the time needed for credible minesweeping activities, vessel repositioning, insurance repricing, and the rebuilding of port and loading schedules. Tanker availability and backlog clearing could further slow the pace of recovery. Flows will increase gradually rather than snap back to normal. The market is pricing a quick resolution, but the data suggests a more measured trajectory.

By 2027, we expect the market to shift into a surplus of approximately 2.8 million barrels per day, equivalent to roughly one billion barrels annually. However, much of this surplus will be absorbed by inventory restocking. Following the recent drawdowns, buyers are likely to rebuild stocks, which should absorb part of the excess supply. We expect inventories will broadly return to pre-conflict levels by the end of 2027. Oil prices should remain supported near term in the USD 80 to 90 per barrel range for 2026 and 2027. Brent has averaged approximately USD 87 per barrel year-to-date, and we expect prices to stay close to USD 90 per barrel this year amid tight inventories and gradual supply recovery. Looking ahead, we forecast approximately USD 78 per barrel in 2027, with a stronger first half and softer second half profile. Longer term, we anchor at approximately USD 75 per barrel.

## The One Billion Barrel Drawdown Creates a Structural Floor Beneath Prices That Diplomacy Cannot Quickly Remove

The most important number in this entire analysis is one billion barrels. That is the volume of global inventory that has been consumed since the onset of the Strait of Hormuz disruption. To put this in perspective, this is equivalent to the entire annual oil consumption of Japan, the world's fourth-largest consumer. This drawdown has effectively tightened the market, leaving inventories at extremely low levels. This creates a strong floor for prices, because any incremental recovery in flows initially goes toward restocking rather than satisfying incremental demand, keeping near-term balances tight.

The composition of the drawdown tells a strategic story. The 300 million barrels drawn from strategic petroleum reserves represent a deliberate policy choice by consuming nations to bridge the supply gap. The 450 million barrels from Chinese inventories reflect Beijing's willingness to deploy its strategic stockpiles to manage domestic price stability. The 140 million barrels from oil-on-water indicate that even the floating storage cushion has been depleted. When every category of inventory is drawn down simultaneously, the system loses its shock absorbers. The market becomes structurally vulnerable to any supply disruption, no matter how small.

The implication for investors is clear: even as diplomatic progress is made, the physical market will remain tight until inventories are rebuilt. The process of restocking one billion barrels at a rate of, say, three million barrels per day would take nearly a year. This timeline extends well beyond the initial diplomatic breakthrough, meaning that price support from inventory dynamics will persist even as supply gradually recovers. The market is likely to experience a prolonged period of backwardation, where near-term prices remain elevated relative to forward curves, reflecting the urgency of current supply constraints.

## The Six-Month Normalization Timeline Reflects Hard Physical Constraints, Not Political Will

The expectation of a six-month normalization is not a conservative assumption; it is a realistic assessment of the logistical and operational challenges involved. The first constraint is minesweeping. The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway through which approximately 20 million barrels per day of oil transits under normal conditions. Any minesweeping operation must be thorough and credible to reassure shipping companies and insurers. This is not a task that can be rushed without risking catastrophic accidents.

The second constraint is insurance. War-risk insurance premiums for vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz have surged to levels that make many voyages economically unviable. Even after a diplomatic agreement, insurers will need time to reassess risk, set new premiums, and build confidence in the safety of the waterway. This process will be gradual and will depend on observable progress in minesweeping and security protocols.

The third constraint is vessel repositioning. Many tankers have been diverted to alternative routes or placed into floating storage during the disruption. Bringing these vessels back into service for Hormuz transits requires time for repositioning, crew changes, and port scheduling. The backlog of cargoes waiting to be loaded will also take time to clear. Tanker availability could become a bottleneck, as the global fleet has not expanded significantly during the disruption.

The fourth constraint is port and loading schedules. Export terminals on the Gulf coast have been operating at reduced capacity or have been shut entirely. Restarting these facilities requires inspections, maintenance, and coordination with downstream buyers. The ramp-up will be gradual, not instantaneous. The data on OPEC seaborne exports supports this view: exports fell by approximately 14 million barrels per day at the peak of the disruption, and the recovery trajectory shown in the data is clearly gradual rather than sharp.

The strategic implication is that investors should not expect a V-shaped recovery in oil flows. The normalization will be more like a U-shaped recovery, with a prolonged period of below-normal supply followed by a gradual return to equilibrium. This timeline creates opportunities for positioning in assets that benefit from sustained tightness, such as upstream producers with low-cost barrels and strong balance sheets.

## The 2027 Surplus Will Be Largely Absorbed by Restocking, Not by Weakening Demand

The forecast of a 2.8 million barrel per day surplus by 2027 might appear bearish at first glance. However, the critical nuance is that much of this surplus will be absorbed by inventory restocking, not by weakening demand. The distinction matters because restocking represents a structural demand for barrels that is separate from end-user consumption. When inventories are being rebuilt, the effective demand for crude oil is higher than the underlying consumption data would suggest.

The math is straightforward. To rebuild one billion barrels of inventory over the course of a year requires approximately 2.7 million barrels per day of additional supply. This means that the forecast surplus of 2.8 million barrels per day is almost exactly matched by the restocking requirement. The net effect on market balances is close to neutral. Inventories will return to pre-conflict levels by the end of 2027, but the process of getting there will absorb virtually all of the surplus supply.

This dynamic has important implications for the oil price trajectory. The forecast of USD 78 per barrel in 2027, with a stronger first half and softer second half, reflects the transition from restocking to equilibrium. During the first half of 2027, the market will still be in the process of rebuilding inventories, providing price support. In the second half, as inventories approach pre-conflict levels, the support from restocking will fade, and prices will drift toward the long-term marginal cost anchor of approximately USD 75 per barrel.

The longer-term anchor of USD 75 per barrel is based on the marginal cost of supply. This is the price level required to incentivize sufficient investment in new production to meet long-term demand growth. Below this level, investment would decline, setting the stage for future supply tightness. Above this level, excess supply would eventually emerge. The USD 75 per barrel anchor provides a reference point for assessing whether current prices are above or below equilibrium.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Risk of Strategic Stockpiling Behavior by Major Consumers

The report provides a comprehensive analysis of the supply-demand dynamics, but it leaves an important question unresolved: will major oil-consuming countries permanently increase their strategic inventory targets as a result of this experience? The answer to this question has significant implications for the long-term oil price outlook.

During the disruption, China drew down approximately 450 million barrels from its inventories. This represented a willingness to use strategic stocks to manage domestic price stability. However, the experience of being forced to deplete inventories to such an extent may lead Chinese policymakers to conclude that their strategic reserves are insufficient. If China decides to increase its target inventory level by, say, 200 million barrels, this would represent additional structural demand that would need to be absorbed by the market.

Similarly, OECD countries drew down approximately 300 million barrels from strategic petroleum reserves. The experience of relying on SPR releases to manage a supply disruption may lead to a reassessment of optimal inventory levels. Some countries may decide that the cost of holding larger inventories is justified by the insurance value they provide against future disruptions. If multiple countries simultaneously increase their target inventory levels, the cumulative impact on oil demand could be substantial.

The report does not model this scenario explicitly, but the data suggests it is a plausible outcome. The disruption has demonstrated that even a temporary closure of the Strait of Hormuz can cause significant economic damage. The logical response for risk-averse governments is to increase their buffer stocks. If this behavior materializes, it would create a structural bid for oil that would push prices above the marginal cost anchor for an extended period. Investors should monitor policy statements and inventory data from major consuming countries for signs of such behavior.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios and Their Implications

The analysis in the report can be translated into a decision framework for investors. The framework is based on three key variables: the timeline for supply normalization, the pace of inventory restocking, and the behavior of strategic stockpilers. By combining these variables, investors can assess the probability-weighted outlook for oil prices and identify the most attractive opportunities.

Scenario One is the base case, which aligns with the report's central forecast. Supply normalizes over approximately six months. Inventories are rebuilt to pre-conflict levels by the end of 2027. Strategic stockpilers maintain their current targets. In this scenario, oil prices remain in the USD 80 to 90 per barrel range through 2026 and decline to approximately USD 78 per barrel in 2027. The long-term anchor is USD 75 per barrel. Under this scenario, investors should favor upstream producers with strong balance sheets and low-cost barrels that can generate attractive free cash flow even as prices decline. The report highlights that Asia oil and gas companies are yielding a double-digit free cash flow yield at USD 80 per barrel, suggesting that any pullback in valuations could be an opportunity to buy rather than sell.

Scenario Two is the slow normalization case. Minesweeping, insurance, and logistics challenges take longer than expected, extending the normalization timeline to nine to twelve months. Inventory draws continue deeper than currently projected. In this scenario, oil prices remain above USD 90 per barrel through 2026 and decline more slowly in 2027. The long-term anchor may shift higher as the cost of maintaining supply security increases. Under this scenario, investors should overweight producers with direct exposure to the tight market, particularly those with spare capacity or the ability to ramp up production quickly.

Scenario Three is the strategic stockpiling case. Major consumers, particularly China and OECD countries, decide to permanently increase their strategic inventory targets. This creates additional structural demand of 1 to 2 million barrels per day for several years. In this scenario, the surplus projected for 2027 disappears entirely, and the market remains in deficit for an extended period. Oil prices would likely trade above the long-term anchor for several years, supporting higher valuations for the entire sector. Under this scenario, investors should focus on companies with long-duration reserves and the ability to grow production over time.

The key insight from this framework is that the risk is asymmetric. The downside scenario of a rapid normalization and sharp price decline is mitigated by the inventory restocking requirement. The upside scenarios are driven by structural changes in consumer behavior that are not fully priced into the market. This asymmetry suggests that the risk-reward profile for oil exposure is attractive at current valuations.

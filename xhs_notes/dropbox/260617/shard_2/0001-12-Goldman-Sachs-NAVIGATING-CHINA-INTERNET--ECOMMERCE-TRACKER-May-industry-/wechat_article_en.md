# China’s Internet Giants Are Pivoting from Price Wars to AI Infrastructure: The Strategic Shift That Will Define the Next Cycle

China’s online retail sector has entered a phase of measured stabilization rather than robust recovery, with May industry GMV growing just 3% year-on-year. This modest improvement from April’s flat performance masks a deeper structural transition: the competitive dynamics that once defined Chinese e-commerce—aggressive price competition, complex promotional mechanisms, and margin-sacrificing market share grabs—are giving way to a more disciplined operational environment. Simultaneously, the center of gravity for strategic investment is shifting decisively toward artificial intelligence infrastructure, where capital deployment measured in trillions of renminbi is reshaping the competitive landscape. For investors, the critical question is no longer which platform offers the deepest discounts, but which companies can successfully navigate the tension between near-term e-commerce headwinds and long-term AI-driven value creation.

The May data reveals a sector bifurcating along category lines. Food and apparel spending showed sequential improvement, while home appliances continued to decline 16% year-on-year, weighed down by the high base effect of last year’s trade-in program. Communication devices moderated to just 1% growth, down from 6% in April, as mobile phone trade-in subsidies that began in January 2025 have now lapped their own introduction. The broader retail sales picture was weaker than consensus expectations, declining 0.6% year-on-year in May against a Bloomberg consensus forecast of -0.2%. Jewelry, home appliances, and sports equipment were the primary drags, falling 8.9%, 15.6%, and 8.0% respectively. This is not a consumer spending crisis, but it is a clear signal that the post-pandemic pent-up demand has fully normalized, and that the trade-in stimulus programs are facing increasingly difficult year-over-year comparisons.

Against this backdrop, the 618 shopping festival—traditionally a bellwether for consumer sentiment and platform competitiveness—has undergone a fundamental redesign. The industry is shifting toward more disciplined operations and away from aggressive price competition, driven in part by tightening regulations that bar unfair competition and false promotions. Platforms have retired complex sales mechanisms in favor of straightforward discounting, and they are now reporting results over longer counting windows. Parcel volume during the May 13 to June 14 period grew 8% year-on-year, down from 16% during the same period last year. This deceleration is not necessarily a negative signal; it may reflect a healthier, less promotional environment where unit economics matter more than gross merchandise value growth.

## Alibaba’s Share Price Weakness Reflects Five Distinct Investor Concerns, Each of Which Has a Plausible Counter-Narrative

The recent weakness in Alibaba’s share price has concentrated investor attention on five specific debates, and understanding each is essential for assessing whether the current valuation discount is an opportunity or a value trap. The first concern centers on e-commerce and customer management revenue weakness. April and May retail sales were slow, and Alibaba’s merchant rebate policies could lead to a year-on-year decline in CMR for the June quarter. The counter-argument is that healthy parcel growth may narrow the gap between GMV and CMR, because merchant rebate policies are dependent on reaching GMV targets. Furthermore, benign competition should support underlying e-commerce profits even if top-line metrics appear soft.

The second debate involves potential competition between Alibaba Cloud and state-owned telecommunications companies in the AI infrastructure space. Media reports indicate that the Chinese government plans to spend 2 trillion renminbi on building data centers over the next five years, with SOEs such as telcos operating most of these facilities. The critical insight here is that internet hyperscalers—Alibaba, Baidu, ByteDance, and Tencent—are expected to invest approximately 1.5 trillion renminbi in capex over just the next two years. Given explosive AI token growth and tight computing supply in the near-to-medium term, first-mover advantages in capex spending, combined with AI model and chip capabilities, will differentiate the AI cloud landscape from the traditional CPU-based IaaS cloud market. The pricing and margin profile of AI cloud services is fundamentally different from that of commodity cloud infrastructure.

The third concern relates to the AI model landscape itself. Ongoing restructuring, investor focus on talent retention, and stronger competition from independent AI model players such as GLM and DeepSeek have created uncertainty. DeepSeek’s significant pricing cuts have prompted Xiaomi and MiniMax to follow suit. However, pricing power is sustaining for top-performing models—GLM and Qwen 3.7 Max continue to command premium pricing—while AI cloud computing prices have actually increased since April due to hardware cost pressures. Alibaba’s leadership positioning in the computing layer, combined with its leading MaaS and AI cloud scale, positions it as a key beneficiary of proliferating AI token computing demand, from coding to agentic use cases across both its in-house Qwen model and other AI models.

The fourth debate centers on how Alibaba will fund its AI capex. The company’s next three-year capex forecast is approximately 600 billion renminbi, compared to its FY27E EBITA of 92 billion renminbi and net cash of 260 billion renminbi. While US hyperscalers have recently turned to fundraising, Alibaba has more than sufficient net cash combined with operating cash flow to drive its AI investment build-out for at least the next two years, alongside improving group profitability over FY27-29E. The fifth concern is the widened sum-of-the-parts discount, currently at 15% in valuation frameworks, as investors gravitate toward direct plays on AI infrastructure, AI models, or pure e-commerce standalone companies.

## The Decisive Shift from Price Wars to AI Capital Expenditure Creates a New Competitive Hierarchy

The most important strategic development in China’s internet sector is the reallocation of capital from customer acquisition and price subsidies to AI infrastructure. This is not a marginal adjustment; it represents a fundamental change in how competitive advantage is built and sustained. In the previous cycle, the winners were platforms that could offer the lowest prices and the fastest delivery. In the next cycle, the winners will be those that can build the most efficient AI computing infrastructure, attract the best AI talent, and monetize AI capabilities across their ecosystems.

The implications for competitive dynamics are profound. Companies that are late to the AI capex cycle will find themselves at a structural disadvantage, unable to offer the computing power necessary for advanced AI applications or to achieve the cost efficiencies that come from scale. This is why the government’s 2 trillion renminbi data center plan, while potentially creating competition from state-owned enterprises, actually reinforces the importance of first-mover advantage for internet hyperscalers. The telcos may operate many of the new facilities, but they lack the AI model capabilities, the chip design expertise, and the MaaS platforms that generate higher-margin revenue streams.

This shift also changes the calculus for e-commerce profitability. As platforms reduce their reliance on price competition and complex promotional mechanisms, the path to margin improvement becomes clearer. The 618 festival’s move toward simpler discounting and longer measurement windows is consistent with a broader industry trend toward rational competition. For JD.com, which the analysis identifies as a key idea for the second half of 2026, the second quarter is expected to be the last tough quarter for year-over-year comparisons. A top-line and profit recovery in the second half of the year would validate the thesis that disciplined operations can yield better financial outcomes than market share battles.

## The Report Leaves Critical Questions Unanswered About the Sustainability of AI Monetization and Regulatory Risk

While the analysis provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the current inflection point, it leaves several meaningful questions open. The most significant concerns the monetization trajectory of AI investments. The report notes that MaaS ARR trends are positive and that Alibaba’s Token Foundry is consolidating model layer and computing resources. But it does not fully address the timeline required for AI cloud revenue to become material enough to offset the deceleration in e-commerce CMR. The capex numbers are enormous, and the payback period remains uncertain. How quickly can AI token demand translate into revenue growth that moves the needle for a company of Alibaba’s scale?

A second open question involves regulatory risk in the AI space. The government’s 2 trillion renminbi data center plan could evolve into a more directive industrial policy that favors state-owned enterprises over private hyperscalers. While the report argues that internet companies have advantages in AI model and chip capabilities, regulatory frameworks can shift quickly. If the government mandates that a certain percentage of AI computing capacity be reserved for state-owned operators, the economics of private AI cloud investments would change materially.

Third, the competitive dynamics in the AI model layer remain highly fluid. DeepSeek’s aggressive pricing cuts and the emergence of new independent players suggest that the model layer may become commoditized faster than expected. If pricing power erodes for even the top-performing models, the margin profile of AI cloud services could compress, reducing the return on the massive capex being deployed. The report notes that pricing power is sustaining for top models, but this is a snapshot, not a trend line.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the China Internet Inflection Point

For investors trying to position for the next cycle, three strategic questions should guide portfolio construction. First, which companies have the balance sheet strength to sustain multi-year AI capex without sacrificing their core business profitability? Alibaba’s net cash position and operating cash flow provide a buffer, but the scale of investment required means that every dollar spent on AI infrastructure is a dollar not returned to shareholders or deployed in e-commerce. The trade-off is real, and companies with weaker balance sheets will be forced to make difficult choices.

Second, which companies have genuine competitive advantages in the AI stack that extend beyond capital expenditure? The report highlights Alibaba’s leadership in the computing layer and its MaaS platform. But competitive advantage in AI is not static. It depends on talent retention, model performance, and the ability to integrate AI capabilities into existing products and services. Companies that can demonstrate a clear path from AI investment to revenue generation will deserve premium valuations; those that are simply spending to keep pace will not.

Third, how should investors think about the relationship between e-commerce profitability and AI investment? The conventional wisdom is that these are separate businesses with separate economics. But the reality is more complex. AI capabilities can enhance e-commerce operations—through better recommendation algorithms, more efficient logistics, and improved customer service. The companies that can create virtuous cycles between their AI and e-commerce businesses will have a structural advantage over pure-play competitors in either space.

## The Next Phase Requires Granular Understanding of Sub-Sector Dynamics

The report’s sub-sector ranking provides a useful starting point for portfolio construction. Cloud and data centers are ranked as the preferred sub-sector, with GDS, VNET, Alibaba, and Kingsoft Cloud as key ideas. The catalysts are clear: proliferation of AI token demand from agentic AI, improving cloud pricing and margin upside, and further hyperscaler capex ramp-up into the second half of 2026. Games and entertainment rank second, with Tencent and NetEase as key ideas, supported by attractive trough-cycle valuations and new game launches. E-commerce and mobility rank third, with JD as a key idea, driven by profit year-over-year recovery and top-line re-acceleration from the third quarter of 2026.

Within logistics, the analysis is more nuanced. SF-H shares are rated Buy for time-definite express, JD Logistics for supply chain, and ZTO for ground and e-commerce express. J&T, STO, Yunda, and Kerry Logistics are rated Neutral, while YTO and Sinotrans are rated Sell. This dispersion of ratings within a single sub-sector underscores the importance of company-specific analysis in the current environment. The tailwinds from e-commerce parcel growth are real, but they are not uniform across all logistics providers.

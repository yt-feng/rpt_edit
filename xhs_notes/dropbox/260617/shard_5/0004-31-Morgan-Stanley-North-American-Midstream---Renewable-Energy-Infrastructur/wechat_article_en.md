# The Next Capital Rotation Is Already Underway: Why Energy Infrastructure Will Outperform as Geopolitical Risk Reprices

The conventional view holds that a diplomatic resolution in the Strait of Hormuz would be a negative catalyst for energy infrastructure, removing the war premium that has supported commodity prices and midstream equities. This interpretation is dangerously incomplete. An agreement to reopen the strait would not simply deflate energy prices; it would trigger a capitulation event that rotates capital back into the Energy sector with a force that most investors are not positioned for. The timeframe for traffic normalization, the scale of global inventory replenishment required, and the structural demand from AI-driven power consumption create a setup where the next leg of outperformance belongs to midstream and renewable energy infrastructure, not to the broad market momentum trades that have dominated.

The evidence from the past week makes this tension explicit. While the S&P 500 managed only a modest gain, the Momentum factor recorded its sharpest drawdown of the year, with daily swings of plus or minus four percent. This is not a normal correction. It is a positioning unwind that has disproportionately hit AI and power stocks, dragging down natural gas infrastructure names that have no fundamental reason to decline. The relative weakness in companies like WMB, DTM, and KMI reflects market structure, not business deterioration. When momentum unwinds, it takes everything correlated with it, and in recent months, natural gas infrastructure has been swept into the AI trade by association. The disconnect between price action and project activity is now extreme.

This is precisely the moment when strategic investors should be leaning in, not pulling back. The report identifies multiple catalysts converging in the second half of 2026 that will reassert the fundamental case for energy infrastructure. But to understand why this matters now, one must first grasp the mechanism by which the market has mispriced the most important geopolitical variable of the year.

## A Strait of Hormuz Reopening Is Not a Deflationary Event; It Is a Capital Rotation Trigger

The market has been pricing a diplomatic resolution to the Hormuz situation as if it were a simple supply restoration. The logic appears straightforward: reopen the strait, increase crude availability, lower prices, and reduce the urgency for midstream investment. This reasoning misses two critical variables that the report highlights but does not fully synthesize.

First, the normalization of traffic through the Strait of Hormuz will not happen overnight. Even if an agreement is reached, the logistical process of resuming full flows, verifying compliance, and addressing insurance and security concerns will take months. During this period, global crude inventories will need to be replenished from levels that have been drawn down significantly. China alone has cut crude imports by nearly 5 million barrels per day from pre-conflict levels, and its observable inventories have been running well above historical norms. But this surplus is not infinite, and it is not evenly distributed. The report notes that Chinese refiners have been running at reduced rates, and underlying end-user oil demand is down only 1.2 million barrels per day year-over-year. The gap between import cuts and demand destruction is a deferred call on future supply.

Second, the capital rotation effect is not about crude prices. It is about investor positioning. The momentum unwind has created a vacuum in energy infrastructure that will be filled as capital rotates out of crowded AI and technology trades. The report observes that a 10 percent momentum drawdown occurs roughly four times as often as a 10 percent S&P drawdown. This is not a risk metric; it is a timing signal. When momentum breaks, the sectors that have been neglected or sold off become the natural recipients of rebalancing flows. Energy infrastructure has been neglected. The performance dispersion between midstream and the S&P 500 over the past year is stark, and the valuations remain attractive relative to the growth trajectory.

The question is not whether capital will rotate back into energy. The question is whether investors will be positioned before the rotation accelerates.

## The Permian Gas Takeaway Bottleneck Is Breaking, and the Winners Are Already Visible

The most concrete operational catalyst in the report is the entry into service of KMI's Gulf Coast Express expansion, adding 570 million cubic feet per day of compression capacity from the Permian Basin. This is not a marginal event. It is the primary reason Waha natural gas prices have rebounded sharply since late May. Without this capacity, Permian gas was trapped in a basin with insufficient egress, depressing prices and discouraging production. The expansion changes that calculus.

But the report makes clear that this is only the beginning. WhiteWater's 2.5 Bcf/d Blackcomb Pipeline and ET's 2.2 Bcf/d Hugh Brinson Pipeline are scheduled to come online in the coming months, adding a combined 4.7 Bcf/d of takeaway capacity. This is transformative. The Permian has been producing gas at levels that exceed available pipeline capacity, forcing producers to either shut in wells with high gas-to-oil ratios or flare excess gas. The new pipelines will relieve this pressure, but they will also create a new bottleneck downstream at Agua Dulce and Katy, where gas will accumulate while waiting for LNG export demand to materialize.

This creates a two-phase opportunity. In the near term, the relief of Permian constraints benefits gathering and processing companies such as TRGP, which will see higher volumes flowing through their systems. Producers such as Permian Resources and Devon will be able to restart curtailed wells and add oil-weighted activity that had been deferred. In the medium term, the accumulation of gas at Gulf Coast hubs sets the stage for a surge in LNG feedgas demand as new liquefaction capacity comes online. The report does not specify the timing of these LNG projects, but the implication is clear: the infrastructure buildout is happening now, and the companies that own the pipes connecting the Permian to the coast will capture the value.

The strategic question for investors is whether the market is pricing this correctly. The recent weakness in natural gas infrastructure stocks suggests it is not. The momentum unwind has created an entry point that may not last.

## Hyperscaler AI Investment Is Accelerating, and Natural Gas Is the Only Scalable Bridge

The report includes a chart showing hyperscaler AI capex rising from approximately $300 billion in 2026 to $400 billion in 2027, with the bulk of spending concentrated among Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle. This is not a forecast; it is a commitment. These companies have announced data center projects that require unprecedented amounts of reliable, 24/7 power. Renewable energy alone cannot meet this demand at the required scale and reliability. Nuclear is too slow to build. Battery storage is too expensive for baseload applications.

Natural gas is the bridge. The report notes that recent underperformance in natural gas infrastructure stocks is "overdone and unreflective of new project announcements likely to occur in 2H26." This is a direct statement from the analysts that the market is missing the connection between AI capex and midstream investment. Data centers need gas-fired power plants, and those plants need pipelines. The project announcements that the report expects in the second half of 2026 will make this connection explicit.

The implication for investors is that the current pullback in names like WMB, DTM, and KMI is a buying opportunity. These companies are not dependent on the momentum trade for their earnings growth. They are dependent on the physical movement of natural gas, which is set to increase materially as AI-driven power demand accelerates. The market will eventually recognize this. The question is whether the recognition comes before or after the next round of project announcements.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question That It Does Not Fully Answer: What Happens to Chinese Demand When Inventories Normalize?

The report provides extensive data on Chinese crude imports and inventories, showing that China has been cutting imports dramatically while running down its commercial and strategic stockpiles. The analysts estimate that underlying end-user oil demand is down only 1.2 million barrels per day year-over-year, with the rest of the import reduction coming from inventory destocking. This means that when Chinese inventories reach normal levels, imports will need to rebound sharply to meet actual demand.

The unanswered question is: what is the trigger for this rebound? The report notes that Chinese refiners have cut run rates and that independent refiners have shifted away from fuel oil as a feedstock. But it does not specify the conditions under which these trends reverse. If the Strait of Hormuz reopens and crude supply becomes more predictable, Chinese buyers may accelerate their import programs to rebuild inventories before global prices rise. This would create a demand shock that the market is not pricing.

The second-order implication is that midstream infrastructure is not just a domestic U.S. story. It is a global story. If Chinese demand rebounds while U.S. LNG exports are still ramping up, the competition for pipeline capacity and liquefaction services will intensify. Companies with assets that can serve both domestic and export markets will benefit disproportionately. The report does not quantify this scenario, but it is the logical extension of the data presented.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in Energy Infrastructure

For investors trying to navigate this environment, the report supports a three-part framework that prioritizes clarity over complexity.

First, identify the assets that benefit from Permian gas takeaway expansion. The companies that own gathering and processing systems in the Permian, or that operate pipelines connecting the Permian to Gulf Coast hubs, are the direct beneficiaries of the capacity additions now coming online. The volume growth is visible and measurable. The risk is not demand; it is execution. But with KMI's GCX already flowing gas, the execution risk is declining.

Second, focus on natural gas infrastructure with exposure to AI-driven power demand. The hyperscaler capex trajectory is the most reliable demand signal in the energy sector. Companies that can connect gas supply to data center load centers will see multi-year volume growth that is independent of commodity prices. The recent weakness in these names is a function of market structure, not fundamentals.

Third, monitor Chinese import data as a leading indicator for global crude and gas demand. The report's analysis of Chinese inventories suggests that the current period of import weakness is temporary. When the rebound comes, it will tighten global energy markets and support the case for additional infrastructure investment. Investors should watch for a sustained increase in Chinese crude loadings as a signal that the demand recovery has begun.

The report's final section on ratings and price targets shows that the analysts maintain Overweight ratings on the majority of midstream and renewable energy infrastructure names. This is not a tactical call. It is a strategic conviction that the structural drivers of energy infrastructure demand are strengthening, not weakening.

# China's Battery Consumption Tax Resumption Will Accelerate Sector Consolidation, Not Derail It

On July 17, 2026, China's Ministry of Finance, jointly with the Customs and State Taxation administrations, announced the end of a decade-long consumption tax exemption for mature battery products. Starting September 1, 2026, primary lithium and lithium-ion batteries will face a 2% consumption tax, rising to 4% from September 1, 2027. The market's immediate reaction may focus on the headline cost burden, but the strategic implications run far deeper than a simple margin squeeze.

The policy is not a blanket levy. Next-generation technologies—including sodium-ion, solid-state, and fuel cells—will remain exempt through December 31, 2028. This carve-out transforms what could be read as a fiscal measure into an industrial policy signal: Beijing is deliberately accelerating the transition away from commoditized lithium-ion production toward advanced chemistries. For observers and corporate strategists, the question is not whether the tax will hurt, but which companies it will help by hurting their competitors.

The answer, based on granular earnings sensitivity analysis, is unambiguous. The tax will disproportionately impact domestic-centric, low-margin manufacturers, while sector leaders with overseas revenue and high unit profitability will absorb the shock with minimal earnings erosion. This is not a headwind for the industry; it is a catalyst for the market share consolidation that many analysts have predicted but few have been able to point to a concrete trigger for. The consumption tax is that trigger.

## CATL's Overseas Exposure and High Unit Profitability Make It Nearly Immune to the Tax

The most striking data point in the analysis is the comparison between unit tax burden and unit net profit across covered companies. A 4% consumption tax translates to an estimated unit tax of RMB 11–24 per kWh. Against this, CATL's 2025 unit net profit of RMB 109 per kWh stands out as an outlier. The company's margin buffer is more than four times the upper end of the tax range at full implementation.

This resilience is reinforced by CATL's geographic diversification. With over 30% of sales coming from overseas markets—and that share projected to rise to 36% by 2028—a significant portion of its revenue is simply outside the domestic tax net. Under the most adverse scenario of zero tax pass-through to customers, CATL's net profit would decline by only 2% in 2026, 9% in 2027, and 13% in 2028. Under a more realistic 50% pass-through, those figures shrink to 1%, 5%, and 6% respectively. For a company with over RMB 72 billion in 2025 net profit, these are rounding errors, not strategic threats.

The strategic implication is that CATL can use this policy as a competitive lever. With ample margin to absorb the tax or selectively pass it through to customers, the company can maintain pricing discipline while smaller rivals are forced to choose between margin compression and volume loss. This is the classic dynamic of a dominant player using a regulatory shift to strengthen its position.

## Tier-2 and Tier-3 Manufacturers Face Earnings Impact That Could Push Some into Losses

The contrast with CATL is stark when examining the sensitivity of smaller players. For CALB and REPT, the analysis projects earnings downside of 8–41% under a 50% tax pass-through scenario in 2026–2028, widening to 15–82% with no pass-through. These are not marginal impacts; they represent significant pressure on business models already operating with thin margins.

Gotion presents the most extreme case. With domestic unit profitability already lower than peers, the analysis indicates that under a no-pass-through scenario, the company could turn loss-making by 2028. Even with 50% pass-through, net profit would decline by 68% from the base case. For a company that generated only RMB 2.4 billion in net profit in 2025, a swing to losses would trigger covenant reviews, working capital stress, and potentially force asset sales or equity dilution.

The pattern across these manufacturers reveals what the tax is really doing: it is exposing the structural weakness of companies that built their business models on domestic volume growth without developing either export channels or proprietary technology that commands pricing power. EVE and Zenergy fare somewhat better, supported by higher unit profitability, but even they face earnings reductions of 15–29% by 2028 under the no-pass-through scenario. The tax does not discriminate by size alone; it discriminates by business model robustness.

## Downstream Impact Is Highly Segmented, with BESS Projects Most at Risk

The tax's effect on downstream customers is not uniform, and understanding this segmentation is critical for forecasting how the competitive dynamics will play out. Under a full pass-through scenario, passenger vehicles face a 1–2% end-customer price impact. Economy models will feel this more acutely than premium vehicles, but the magnitude is small enough that demand changes are unlikely. Automakers have margin flexibility and can absorb or partially pass through such costs without disrupting sales trajectories.

Electric heavy-duty trucks (eHDTs) are even more insulated. The analysis calculates that a short-haul eHDT generates approximately RMB 8,000 more profit per month than an internal combustion engine equivalent under current fuel prices. Full pass-through of the 4% tax amounts to roughly RMB 8,000 per unit—meaning a driver could recover the entire incremental cost within one month of operation. The driver economics remain compelling, and fleet purchasing decisions will not be swayed.

Battery energy storage system (BESS) projects are the exception that demands close monitoring. For a 400 MWh project, full pass-through of the 4% tax would reduce the internal rate of return by 0.3 percentage points. This may sound small, but many BESS projects are already structured around hurdle rates that leave little margin for error. A 0.3 percentage point decline could push projects from viable to marginal, particularly in a rising interest rate environment where financing costs are also compressing returns. Developers may delay procurement, negotiate harder on battery pricing, or shift to tax-exempt next-generation storage technologies sooner than planned.

The downstream segmentation reinforces the core thesis: the tax will accelerate the shift toward higher-value applications and technologies. BESS projects, which are highly sensitive to upfront capital costs, will put pressure on battery suppliers to absorb the tax or offer innovative pricing structures. This favors manufacturers with both the balance sheet strength to offer flexible terms and the technology roadmap to supply exempted next-generation products.

## A Decision Framework for Observers and Corporate Planners

The consumption tax resumption creates a clear strategic choice for battery manufacturers and their customers. The following framework, derived from the earnings sensitivity analysis, can guide decision-making across three dimensions: exposure, resilience, and response.

First, assess domestic revenue concentration. Companies with over 70% domestic sales are directly exposed to the full tax burden on their core revenue base. Those with significant overseas exposure have a natural hedge. The data shows that domestic share ranges from 64% for CATL to 98% for CALB, and this single metric explains most of the variation in earnings sensitivity.

Second, evaluate unit profitability relative to the tax burden. The critical threshold is whether unit net profit exceeds the estimated unit tax of RMB 11–24 per kWh by a comfortable margin. Companies operating below or near this threshold face structural margin compression that cannot be offset by volume growth alone. The analysis shows that CATL's unit profit is 4.5 to 10 times the tax range, while Gotion's is perilously close.

Third, determine pass-through capability. This depends on customer concentration, contract structures, and competitive positioning. Manufacturers supplying premium passenger vehicle OEMs with long-term contracts have more pricing power than those selling commoditized cells to BESS developers. The scenario analysis provides a range, but the actual outcome will depend on negotiation leverage. Companies with proprietary technology, such as CATL's lithium iron phosphate (LFP) innovations or solid-state patents, can differentiate their offering and command better terms.

Fourth, consider technology transition timing. The tax exemption for next-generation batteries through 2028 creates a window for strategic pivots. Manufacturers that can accelerate sodium-ion or solid-state production will not only avoid the tax but also capture pricing premiums from customers seeking to future-proof their supply chains. This is particularly relevant for BESS developers, who face the highest downstream sensitivity and have the strongest incentive to switch.

The framework leads to a clear strategic observation: prioritize companies with low domestic exposure, high unit profitability, strong pass-through leverage, and credible next-generation technology roadmaps. CATL screens positively on all four dimensions. Most Tier-2 and Tier-3 players fail on at least two, making them candidates for market share loss, margin compression, or strategic restructuring.

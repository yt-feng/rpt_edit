# China's Consumer Recovery Is Real but Fragile: The Income Story Is Stronger Than the Spending One

China's consumer economy in the second quarter of 2026 presents a paradox that demands a more nuanced reading than headline retail sales data suggest. Household disposable income per capita grew 5.6% year-over-year in nominal terms during Q2, accelerating from 4.9% in Q1, while household consumption growth only inched up to 3.8% year-over-year from 3.6%. The gap between income growth and spending growth widened. This is not a consumer who is unable to spend. It is a consumer who is choosing not to spend at the same pace as their earnings improve. The sequential data sharpen the picture: on a seasonally adjusted annualized basis, nominal household consumption per capita decelerated to 4.0% in Q2 from 4.5% in Q1, even as income growth held relatively firm. The spending slowdown was concentrated in food, partially offset by education, culture, and entertainment, suggesting a structural rebalancing rather than a broad retreat.

The labor market provides the foundation for this interpretation. Official urban surveyed unemployment declined from 5.3% in March to 5.1% in June, and a weighted composite of PMI employment sub-indexes rose quarter-over-quarter. Wage indicators were mixed but improved on balance: official wage income growth picked up to 5.2% year-over-year, and the report's revamped wage tracker suggests urban wage growth reached 4.7% year-over-year in Q2, up from 4.3% in Q1. This improvement stands in direct tension with the more downbeat sentiment observed among onshore observers, who cite concerns over AI-driven job displacement. The data suggest the labor market is healing, but the healing is not yet felt in consumer confidence or spending behavior.

The central strategic question for business leaders and observers is not whether Chinese consumers have money. They do. The question is why they are holding onto it. The household savings rate ticked up to 32.6% in Q2 from 32.3% in Q1, slightly above its pre-COVID trend-implied level. Estimated excess household bank deposits reached RMB 61 trillion. The consumer is not broke. The consumer is cautious. This caution is rational, grounded in three structural forces: negative property wealth effects, ongoing household deleveraging, and a policy framework that remains largely supply-side oriented despite rhetorical commitments to boosting consumption.

## Household Balance Sheets Are Stabilizing, but the Rotation from Housing to Financial Assets Is Not Yet a Consumption Engine

Total household assets have plateaued at approximately RMB 730 trillion in recent quarters, according to the report's quarterly balance sheet tracker. The key structural shift is that asset growth is rotating away from housing and toward financial assets, a trend that accelerated after the September 2024 policy pivot. This rotation is positive for financial markets but does not directly translate into consumption. Financial asset wealth is less liquid than housing equity, and the distribution of financial asset gains is concentrated among higher-income households, who have a lower marginal propensity to consume. The implication is that while household net worth is no longer declining, the composition of that net worth is becoming less supportive of broad-based consumption.

On the liability side, household leverage has drifted lower since mid-2024, driven by declining outstanding mortgage balances and short-term consumer loans. Deleveraging is a rational response to a property market that has not yet found a durable floor. New property sales declined further in Q2, and the negative wealth effect from housing continues to weigh on household sentiment. The household is repairing its balance sheet. This is prudent microeconomic behavior, but it creates a headwind for consumption that will persist until property prices stabilize and households regain confidence in housing as a store of value.

The data on household bank loan flows reinforce this picture. Both short-term and medium- to long-term household loan flows remained weak in Q2. Consumers are not borrowing to spend. They are borrowing less across the board. This is consistent with a household sector that is reducing leverage rather than expanding consumption through credit. The cumulative increase in household bank deposits moderated from 10.0% of GDP in Q1 to 8.8% in Q2 on a four-quarter rolling basis, but the absolute level of deposits remains elevated. The consumer is saving more and borrowing less. This combination is deflationary for consumption in the near term, even as it improves household financial health over the medium term.

## The Labor Market Is Improving, but the Quality of That Improvement Matters More Than the Headline Rate

The official unemployment rate declined to 5.1% in June, and the weighted average of employment sub-indexes across PMI surveys rose in Q2 versus Q1. These are positive signals. However, working hours of enterprise employees were below last year's level in Q2, and migrant workers' average monthly income growth edged down to 3.0% year-over-year from 3.2% in Q1. The labor market is adding jobs, but those jobs may be paying less or offering fewer hours. The divergence between the official unemployment rate and the migrant worker income data suggests that the recovery is uneven across income segments.

The report's revamped wage tracker, which synthesizes multiple indicators, suggests urban wage growth rose to 4.7% year-over-year in Q2 from 4.3% in Q1. This is a meaningful acceleration, but it remains below pre-COVID trend levels and below the pace of nominal GDP growth. Wage growth is improving, but it is not yet strong enough to overcome the precautionary saving motive created by property wealth losses and uncertain income expectations. The labor cost sub-index in the CKGSB Business Condition Index survey pointed to faster wage growth, which is consistent with the wage tracker. But the migrant worker data and the decline in working hours suggest that the improvement is concentrated in higher-skilled, higher-income segments.

The tension between improving wage data and downbeat sentiment is critical. Onshore observers during recent marketing trips expressed increased concern over AI displacement of jobs. This concern may be amplifying precautionary behavior even among households that are not directly affected. The perception of labor market risk can be as powerful as the reality. If households believe their jobs are at risk from automation, they will save more regardless of current wage growth. The data suggest that the labor market is improving, but the narrative around labor market risk is deteriorating. This gap between data and perception is a headwind that policy cannot easily address.

## Consumer Confidence Is a Lagging Indicator That Is Now Diverging Across Income Groups

The official NBS consumer confidence index slipped modestly in April and May compared to Q1, consistent with the sequential softening in consumption. However, Morning Consult's daily consumer sentiment surveys, which are timelier and capture higher-income groups more heavily, point to an improvement in Q2 versus Q1, with the increase broad-based across age and income groups. The divergence between the two surveys may reflect the composition of the samples. The NBS survey captures a broader cross-section of the population, while Morning Consult's sample tilts toward higher-income households, who have benefited more from the equity market rally and the rotation toward financial assets.

Within the Morning Consult data, a more nuanced pattern emerges. Consumer sentiment improved across income groups in Q2 versus Q1, but in June, sentiment declined among the highest-income group while improving among other groups. This suggests that the equity market rally, which has disproportionately benefited higher-income households, may be losing its momentum as a driver of confidence. The tech index continued to outperform the consumer index in the onshore equity market during Q2, despite a recent weakening. The rotation from housing to financial assets has supported equity valuations, but the concentration of gains in the tech sector means that the wealth effect is narrow.

The policy backdrop adds another layer. In mid-July, China's State Council approved the 15th Five-Year Plan for Expanding Consumption, underscoring consumption as a medium-term policy priority. However, the measures are largely medium-term and supply-side oriented, focused on improving the quality and variety of goods and services rather than directly boosting household income or reducing precautionary saving. Given the current labor market conditions, subdued income expectations, and ongoing negative property wealth effects, the near-term impact of this plan is likely limited. The policy framework is oriented toward the long-term structural transformation of the consumption economy, not toward addressing the immediate cyclical weakness in consumer spending.

## A Decision Framework for Interpreting the Chinese Consumer in Mid-2026

For business leaders and observers, the Q2 data point to a consumer economy that is recovering but not rebounding. The distinction matters. A recovery implies gradual improvement from a low base. A rebound implies a sharp, broad-based acceleration. The data support the former, not the latter. The following framework can guide decision-making in this environment.

First, track the gap between income growth and spending growth. If the gap narrows, it signals that consumers are gaining confidence and beginning to deploy their accumulated savings. If the gap widens, as it did in Q2, it signals that precautionary motives remain dominant. The savings rate and the level of excess deposits are the key indicators. As long as the savings rate remains above its pre-COVID trend-implied level, consumption will lag income growth.

Second, monitor the composition of household asset growth. The rotation from housing to financial assets is positive for financial markets but negative for consumption in the near term. Housing wealth is more broadly distributed and has a higher marginal propensity to consume than financial asset wealth. Until property prices stabilize and housing regains its role as a store of value, the consumption headwind from negative wealth effects will persist. The plateau in total household assets at RMB 730 trillion is a stabilizing signal, but it is not yet a catalyst for spending.

Third, disaggregate the labor market data. The official unemployment rate is a lagging and incomplete indicator. Focus on wage growth by segment, working hours, and migrant worker income. The improvement in urban wage growth to 4.7% year-over-year is positive, but the decline in working hours and the slowdown in migrant worker income suggest that the recovery is uneven. Premium and mass-market consumption will diverge. Higher-income households, who benefit from wage growth and equity gains, will drive spending on education, culture, entertainment, and travel. Lower-income households, who face stagnant migrant wages and declining working hours, will remain cautious.

Fourth, recognize that policy is a medium-term tailwind, not a near-term catalyst. The 15th Five-Year Plan for Expanding Consumption is structurally positive, but it will take years to implement. Near-term consumption will be driven by labor market conditions, income expectations, and property market stability, not by policy announcements. The consumer goods trade-in program, which boosted certain product categories in previous quarters, showed diverging results across products in Q2, suggesting that the marginal impact of such programs is diminishing.

Fifth, prepare for a consumption environment that is selective and value-conscious. The sequential deceleration in consumption was driven by weaker spending on food, partially offset by stronger growth in education, culture, and entertainment. This is not a consumer who is cutting back across the board. It is a consumer who is reallocating spending from necessities to experiences and self-improvement. Brands and companies that can tap into this reallocation will outperform those that rely on broad-based consumption growth.

The Chinese consumer in mid-2026 has more income and more savings than a year ago. But that consumer is also more cautious, more selective, and more focused on balance sheet repair. The recovery is real, but it is fragile. The sequential softening in Q2 is a reminder that the path to normalized consumption will be gradual and uneven. For those who can read the data with precision and act on the structural shifts beneath the surface, the opportunity is significant. But it requires patience, granularity, and a clear-eyed assessment of the headwinds that remain.

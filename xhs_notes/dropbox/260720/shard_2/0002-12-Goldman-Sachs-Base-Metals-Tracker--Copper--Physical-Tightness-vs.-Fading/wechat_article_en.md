## The Physical Market Is Tight, But the Tightness Is Structural, Not Demand-Driven

China's visible copper cathode inventories have fallen to the bottom of their seasonal range, and the latest draw was amplified by typhoon-related stockpiling and delayed cathode arrivals. That disruption hit a market that already had little inventory cushion. The natural inference is that end demand is accelerating. The evidence does not support that conclusion.

The tightening is primarily a substitution effect. Tighter VAT enforcement on scrap in China has constrained domestic scrap circulation and kept secondary rod output unusually weak. Fabricators have shifted toward cathode-based rod as a result. Concentrated smelter maintenance has compounded the effect, with domestic cathode output falling month-on-month in June and remaining broadly flat year-on-year. Chinese apparent copper demand through May is up 2.7 percent year-to-date, but that figure masks the scrap-to-cathode substitution rather than reflecting genuine end-use acceleration.

This distinction matters for price forecasting. A demand-driven tightness is more durable and more likely to push prices higher over time. A substitution-driven tightness is inherently limited. Once scrap markets adjust—either through higher scrap imports, regulatory adaptation, or improved enforcement compliance—the cathode demand boost recedes. The current tightness is real, but its marginal driver is regulatory, not structural demand growth.

## The US Import Pull Is Draining an Already Thin ex-US Market, Creating a Two-Tier Copper Market

The US copper market is experiencing a build in inventories driven by tariff expectations. The market is pricing roughly a 30 percent probability that a 15 percent refined copper tariff will be implemented by January 2027. That expectation has created a powerful incentive to pull metal into the United States ahead of any potential tariff imposition. US copper inventories have continued to build, while ex-US visible inventories have fallen as metal flows toward American shores.

This is not a market rebalancing. The ex-US market was already operating with thin inventory buffers. The US import pull is draining those buffers further, leaving the rest of the world more exposed to any supply disruption. The result is a two-tier copper market: the US has a growing inventory cushion, while the rest of the world faces increasing physical tightness. For global price formation, the ex-US tightness is the more relevant factor, because LME copper prices are set in a global context, not a US domestic one.

The risk is asymmetric. If tariff expectations recede, the US inventory build unwinds, releasing metal back into global markets and depressing prices. If tariffs are imposed, the US market becomes more insulated, but the rest of the world faces even tighter conditions. In either scenario, the current price level embeds a tariff premium that may not be fully justified by underlying fundamentals.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Copper Market's Conflicting Signals

The current copper market presents a classic tension between micro and macro signals. Physical indicators point to tightening conditions, but macro sentiment is deteriorating. The question is not which signal will dominate, but under what conditions each signal becomes the marginal price driver.

Three scenarios should guide decision-making. First, if macro conditions stabilize and AI sentiment recovers, the physical tightness will reassert itself as the dominant price driver. In this scenario, copper prices are likely to move higher, supported by low inventories, scrap substitution, and the US import pull sustaining ex-US tightness. The key trigger to watch is a de-escalation in Middle East tensions and a reversal in Fed repricing.

Second, if macro conditions deteriorate further but remain within a moderate range, the physical tightness will provide a floor but not a catalyst. Prices will trade in a range, with downside limited by inventory draws and upside capped by risk aversion. In this scenario, the US tariff premium becomes the most uncertain variable. A decline in tariff probability would remove a key support for current price levels.

Third, if macro conditions deteriorate sharply—a significant escalation in Middle East conflict, a renewed Fed hiking cycle, or a broad tech sell-off—the physical tightness will not prevent a price correction. The ex-US market's thin inventory buffers would amplify the downside, as speculative length is unwound and liquidity dries up. The report notes that renewed Fed hikes are not the economists' base case, but the market is pricing a non-trivial probability of that outcome, and copper prices would be vulnerable to that tail risk.

The practical implication is that copper investors should not treat the current physical tightness as a sufficient condition for holding long positions. It is a necessary condition, but it must be paired with a stable or improving macro backdrop to generate sustained upside. The risk-reward balance has shifted toward a more defensive posture until the macro uncertainty resolves.

## The ex-US Copper Market Will Remain Tight Near Term, But the Price Impact Depends on Macro Stability

The near-term outlook for the ex-US copper market is one of continued tightness. The US import pull will continue to draw units away from an already thin ex-US market. Low Chinese inventories provide limited cushion. The scrap-to-cathode substitution will persist until scrap market conditions normalize. These factors all support a floor under prices.

But the price impact of that tightness depends entirely on the macro environment. In a stable macro environment, tight physical conditions drive prices higher as buyers compete for available metal. In a deteriorating macro environment, tight physical conditions prevent a crash but do not generate upward momentum. The difference is critical for positioning.

The report's price forecast of $13,400 per tonne for 2026 and $13,800 per tonne for 2027 suggests a modestly constructive view, but the forecast is only modestly above forwards. That is not a call for a major rally. It is a call for a market that is supported by fundamentals but constrained by macro headwinds.

For serious business audiences, the takeaway is not about the direction of copper prices over the next week. It is about the changing structure of price formation in the copper market. The old model of "inventories down equals prices up" is no longer sufficient. Physical tightness is a necessary condition for higher prices, but it is not a sufficient one. The AI narrative, tariff expectations, and macro sentiment now play a larger role than at any point in recent history. Investors who ignore those factors are making a bet that is increasingly disconnected from how the market actually prices copper.

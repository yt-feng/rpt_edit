# India’s Moment Is Not About Recovery — It Is About a Structural Opportunity Set That Has Widened

India’s equity market has fallen 10 percent in dollar terms in the first half of 2026, while Korea rose 112 percent and Taiwan 64 percent. That dramatic divergence is not a sign of weakness — it is the beginning of a strategic re-entry window. The MSCI India forward price-to-earnings multiple has compressed from 23.2x at the end of 2024 to 18.6x today, falling below its 10-year average for the first time in three years. Relative to emerging markets, India’s valuation premium has collapsed to levels last seen during the COVID-19 trough. The trigger for the selloff was the global rotation into AI and tech momentum, which pulled record foreign capital out of India — US$29 billion in net outflows in the first half alone. But that same rotation is now showing signs of exhaustion, and the capital that fled India is beginning to return. The question for institutional investors is not whether India is cheap. It is whether the opportunity set has become broad enough to warrant a structural increase in allocation. The evidence suggests it has.

The case for adding India exposure today rests on four intersecting developments that together create a more favorable risk-reward profile than at any point in the last three years. First, valuations have reset across the board, with Financials and Technology trading at COVID-era multiples. Second, earnings downgrades, while still broad, are showing early signs of bottoming in large-caps and in sectors such as Materials and Consumer. Third, foreign investor sentiment is improving after the record outflow, with global emerging market active funds already reducing their underweight from -2.7 percent to -2.1 percent. Fourth, the factor landscape now offers a clear barbell: momentum stocks are trading at historically cheap levels with improving earnings revisions, while quality defensive exposure remains prudent given unresolved geopolitical uncertainty. Each of these elements on its own would be noteworthy. Together, they signal that the window for active allocation to India has reopened — but only for those who are selective.

## Valuations Have Reset Across Sectors, with Financials and Technology at COVID-Era Multiples That Create a Rare Entry Point

The most concrete signal in the data is the breadth of the valuation reset. MSCI India now trades at 18.6x forward PE, which is 0.7 standard deviations below its 10-year average. On a price-to-book basis, the market is at 3.3x, still slightly above the long-term mean, but the relative PB versus emerging markets has fallen to COVID-19 lows. Sector-level analysis reveals an even sharper picture. Financials are trading at 1.9 standard deviations below their 10-year average PE, and Technology at 1.5 standard deviations below. Both sectors are at valuation levels not seen since the pandemic. Healthcare, Industrials, and Utilities remain above average but within one standard deviation, meaning they are not overextended. Staples, Financials, Technology, and Energy are all trading at COVID-era lows on price-to-book as well.

This is not a case of a single sector dragging the market lower. The compression is systematic. When a market that had been priced for perfection across most sectors suddenly trades at or below historical averages in the majority of its constituents, the risk of further multiple contraction diminishes. The implication is that the downside from valuation is now limited, while the upside from any normalization of sentiment or earnings is substantial. For global allocators who had been using India as a funding source for the AI trade, the question shifts from whether to reduce exposure further to whether the current valuation discount justifies re-entering.

## Earnings Support Is Emerging from Large-Caps and Select Sectors, Even as the Broader Downgrade Cycle Has Not Yet Bottomed

The earnings picture remains the most debated element of the India thesis. The average Indian stock continues to see a higher pace of downgrades than upgrades, and the downgrade cycle has not yet shown a clear bottom. That is the headline. But the nuance matters more than the aggregate. Large-caps are beginning to show early signs of returning to net upgrades, even as mid-caps and small-caps remain under pressure. Sectorally, Materials and Consumer ex-Autos are showing earnings recovery, while all other sectors remain in downgrade territory. This is not a uniform recovery — it is selective, and it is led by the parts of the market that are most liquid and most accessible to foreign investors.

The long-term earnings growth expectations for India have fallen to 10.6 percent, down from a peak of 12.9 percent in January 2026 and now below the long-term average. Historically, when long-term growth expectations have been at or below these levels, the subsequent 12-month forward returns for the market have been positive. The relationship is directional rather than precise in magnitude, but the signal is consistent. Low expectations create the conditions for positive surprises. The combination of a valuation reset and depressed earnings expectations is the classic setup for mean reversion. The risk is that the downgrade cycle persists longer than anticipated, but the data suggests that the rate of deterioration is slowing, and the base of expectations is now low enough that even modest improvements will register as positive revisions.

## Foreign Capital Is Returning, and the Structural Flow Dynamic Is Shifting Away from the AI Funding Trade

The record US$29 billion in foreign institutional outflows in the first half of 2026 was not a vote of no confidence in India’s fundamentals. It was a tactical rotation. Global investors used India as a funding source to allocate to the AI and tech momentum trade concentrated in Korea and Taiwan. That trade has now come under pressure due to record-high valuations, elevated earnings expectations, and extreme crowding. Since June, the outflow has slowed, and in July month-to-date, India has seen net foreign inflows of US$1.8 billion. Domestic institutional flows, which had been running at US$49 billion in the first half — 19 percent higher than the same period in 2025 — are now moderating. The SIP flow trend, which had shown some improvement in May, has slowed again, indicating that domestic retail enthusiasm is not yet reaccelerating.

The key structural shift is in the behavior of global emerging market active funds. These funds reduced their underweight to India from -2.7 percent in February to -2.1 percent in May. That is still an underweight, but the direction of change is significant. If the AI trade continues to face headwinds, the reallocation into India could accelerate. The opportunity set for foreign investors is also widening. Sectors such as Staples, Technology, Energy, and Real Estate are at record-low levels of crowding, meaning that positioning is light and the risk of crowded exits is minimal. At the same time, Utilities, Real Estate, Technology, and Materials are showing above-average dispersion, which creates active stock-picking opportunities for managers who can differentiate between winners and losers within sectors.

## Factor Positioning Should Be a Barbell: Add Momentum at Historically Cheap Levels While Maintaining Quality Defensive Exposure

The factor landscape in India has shifted dramatically in the first half of 2026. Quality and high-volatility stocks have been the most resilient, declining only 0.8 percent versus the market’s -4.5 percent. Low volatility has been the worst performer, down 7.2 percent. Since April, however, the factor dynamics have reversed sharply. Momentum stocks are up 15 percent, high-volatility and small- and mid-cap stocks are up 20 percent, and quality is up 17 percent, all outperforming the market’s 14 percent gain over the same period. The momentum rally, which began in April, appears to have further room to run.

The valuation support for momentum is compelling. Price momentum and large-caps are now the cheapest factors, trading at one standard deviation below their 10-year average on both absolute and relative bases. Quality and high free-cash-flow yield remain expensive, near one standard deviation above average. On earnings revisions, momentum is the only factor that remains in net upgrades. Quality is showing early signs of recovery, but all other factors — value, small- and mid-caps, high volatility — are still seeing increased downgrades. The combination of cheap valuations, improving earnings revisions, and an inflection in crowding makes momentum the most attractive factor for adding risk.

The prudent strategy is a barbell. Maintain a core of quality defensive exposure, given that geopolitical uncertainty and the uneven earnings recovery warrant caution. But add momentum exposure selectively, focusing on stocks where earnings momentum trends have been improving over the past six months. Large-caps should be preferred over small- and mid-caps, as large-caps offer both cheaper valuations and better earnings revision momentum. The opportunity is not to make a binary bet on India’s direction. It is to construct a portfolio that is positioned for the early stages of a rotation that has valuation support, improving fundamentals, and a favorable flow backdrop — without ignoring the risks that remain.

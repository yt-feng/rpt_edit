EN_A: So China just announced a new consumption tax on lithium-ion batteries. What is the big deal here?

EN_B: It is about how China chooses between its current, overcapacity-plagued battery industry and its future technologies.
EN_A: How does that choice show up in the policy?
EN_B: The core is that for mature technologies like LFP and NMC batteries, a 2% consumption tax will kick in from September 2026, and then double to 4% by September 2027.

EN_B: The impact is pretty modest. At the battery pack level, the price increase is only 1% to 3%. For energy storage systems, it is even less, just 1% to 2%. So it is unlikely to kill demand.
EN_A: So if it is not about killing demand, what is the real point?
EN_B: The real point is to send a clear signal to the industry, especially the second-tier battery makers who are struggling with very thin margins. This tax is meant to curb overcapacity.
EN_A: So it is an anti-involution policy?
EN_B: Exactly. The report says the industry is plagued by overcapacity at the tier-two level. This tax will force those players to rethink their expansion plans. It is a brake on excessive capacity.
EN_A: What about the market leader, CATL? Is this good or bad for them?
EN_B: In the short term, it is a slight negative sentiment. But in the long run, this will accelerate industry consolidation, which is actually good for the leader. And there is another key part of the policy.
EN_A: What is that?

EN_B: Exactly. The message is clear: if you stick with old lithium-ion, you pay a tax. If you move to sodium-ion or solid-state, you get a tax break. The report estimates that by 2030, 10% to 20% of China's battery output could be these new technologies.

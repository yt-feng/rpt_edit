EN_A: Have you ever wondered what happened to a brand like Howard Johnson? It was everywhere, and now it is almost gone.
EN_B: That is exactly the kind of question a recent report from a global investment bank tries to answer. Their core argument is that hotel brands have a lifecycle.
EN_A: A lifecycle? So brands are born, they grow, they peak, and then they decline?
EN_B: Exactly. The report’s key finding is that in the U.S., most mainstream hotel brands hit their peak number of hotels about 30 years after they are launched.
EN_A: 30 years? That is a very specific number. How did they figure that out?
EN_B: They looked at major brands like Holiday Inn, Crowne Plaza, and Comfort Inn. The data was remarkably consistent. The peak in total hotels and the development pipeline both fall within a 25 to 35 year window from the brand’s birth.
EN_A: So, when we talk about Crowne Plaza struggling, it might not be a management failure. It could just be that its lifecycle is running its course.
EN_B: That is the implication. Crowne Plaza launched in 1983. Its U.S. pipeline peaked around 2008, which is exactly 25 years later. The report suggests that simply rebranding or refreshing it might not be enough.
EN_A: Why do brands peak around the 30-year mark? Is it just about consumers getting bored?
EN_B: That is part of it. The report points to three main drivers. First, newer hotels always get better guest reviews. As a brand ages, some hotels get old and worn, which drags down the whole brand’s reputation.
EN_A: That makes sense. What is the second reason?
EN_B: Consumer preferences change. What people wanted 30 years ago, like a full-service hotel with a big restaurant and meeting rooms, is not what they want today. They prefer limited-service hotels that are more efficient and cheaper to run.
EN_A: So an old brand gets stuck with an outdated model.
EN_B: Exactly. And third, you eventually run out of good locations. You cannot keep building the same brand in the same city without hurting existing owners. Once the best spots are taken, growth naturally slows down.
EN_A: Are there any brands that break this 30-year rule? I can think of InterContinental, which is still growing and is almost 80 years old.
EN_B: The report identifies three exceptions. The first is international expansion. When a brand goes global, it enters new markets with a clean slate, free from the aging hotels and tired image it might have back home.
EN_A: So that is how InterContinental keeps growing. They are big in new markets.
EN_B: The second exception is the owner-operated model. Think of Premier Inn in the UK. Whitbread owns and operates its hotels, so they have total control over quality. They do not rely on third-party franchisees who might let standards slip.
EN_A: And the third exception?
EN_B: Luxury brands. The report found that for luxury hotels, age does not hurt guest scores. In fact, older luxury brands like InterContinental or Grand Hyatt are seen as more prestigious. Guests pay for that heritage and history.
EN_A: So from an investment perspective, what is the takeaway?
EN_B: It is straightforward. Hotel groups with younger brand portfolios have a built-in growth advantage. The report shows Hilton and Hyatt have the youngest brands, which gives them more runway before hitting that 30-year ceiling.
EN_A: So the smart money is on who has the most runway left.
EN_B: Right. But the report leaves a couple of things open. For example, Comfort Inn managed to defy the cycle by splitting its brand in the 1990s. Can that trick be repeated? The report does not fully answer this yet.
EN_A: And it mainly focuses on the U.S. market. We still need to verify if this lifecycle model works the same way globally.

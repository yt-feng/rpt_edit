# Hotel Brands Have a Built-In Expiration Date: The 30-Year Peak Is the Industry's Most Underappreciated Structural Constraint

Most investors treat hotel brand portfolios as permanent assets. They are wrong. A rigorous analysis of the lifecycle of major US hotel brands reveals a pattern that is both remarkably consistent and strategically inconvenient: mainstream hotel brands peak approximately 30 years after launch, then enter a long, often irreversible decline. This is not a story about Howard Johnson's mismanagement or Crowne Plaza's failed turnaround. It is a structural law of the hospitality industry that applies across brands, ownership structures, and market cycles. The implication is uncomfortable but unavoidable: the hotel groups that will outperform over the next decade are not necessarily those with the strongest current brands, but those with the youngest brand portfolios and the most disciplined approach to replacing aging brands before they decay.

The timing of this insight matters more than usual. The US hotel industry is entering a period where a disproportionate share of its major brand inventory is crossing the 30-year threshold. Holiday Inn was launched in 1952. Comfort Inn launched in 1981. Rodeway Inn dates to 1962. Each of these brands is now in or past its peak pipeline phase. The industry's growth engine is therefore shifting, quietly but decisively, from the legacy brands that built modern hospitality to newer brands that are still in their rapid adoption phase. Investors who fail to understand this lifecycle will misallocate capital and misjudge competitive dynamics.

The mechanism behind the 30-year peak is not a single cause but a convergence of four forces, each reinforcing the others. Guest preferences shift over time, and brand standards are inherently sticky. Newer hotels earn higher review scores, which drives better revenue per available room, which attracts more developers, which creates a virtuous cycle for young brands and a vicious one for old brands. White space runs out: brands cluster in high-demand locations, and brand owners must protect incumbent franchisees from excessive cannibalization. And franchise contract lengths of 10 to 20 years create a natural buffer: the first two decades of a brand's life see minimal churn, which means the decline phase is delayed but also compressed when it arrives. The result is a remarkably predictable pattern across brands that otherwise differ in scale, positioning, and ownership.

## The 30-Year Peak Is Not a Statistical Artifact; It Is a Recurring Structural Pattern Across Dozens of Major US Hotel Brands

The evidence for this lifecycle is not based on a single brand or a single decade. When the analysis examines the total hotel count over time for major US brands, the pattern is striking. Holiday Inn peaked in total hotels approximately 30 years after its post-war relaunch. Crowne Plaza peaked in pipeline 25 years after launch and in total hotels 28 years after launch. Comfort Inn, Rodeway Inn, and a range of other mainstream brands all show similar inflection points. The peak in pipeline tends to arrive slightly before the peak in total rooms, which makes intuitive sense: developers stop committing to new builds before the existing stock begins to shrink. The pipeline peak typically occurs around 25 to 30 years, and the room count peak follows at roughly 30 to 35 years.

This pattern holds even when accounting for the distorting effects of the global financial crisis. One might argue that the pre-2008 pipeline boom and post-2008 contraction created an artificial peak for brands launched in the late 1970s and early 1980s. But the data does not support that interpretation. The timing of the peak is consistent across brands that experienced very different macroeconomic environments. The lifecycle appears to be driven by brand-specific forces, not by aggregate economic cycles.

The stabilized post-peak range is not homogeneous. Comfort Inn has maintained roughly 80 percent of its peak hotel count, while Holiday Inn has settled at approximately 40 percent. This variation is itself instructive. The brands that have managed to stabilize at a higher percentage of their peak tend to be those that underwent significant brand reinvention or repositioning. Comfort Inn benefited from a brand split and reinvigoration in the 1990s that few other brands have successfully replicated. Holiday Inn, by contrast, has struggled with its full-service legacy at a time when guest preferences have shifted decisively toward limited-service offerings.

## Guest Preferences Shift Against Older Brands in a Predictable Way, and This Is Not a Problem That Marketing Can Solve

The most fundamental driver of the lifecycle is the relationship between hotel age and guest satisfaction. In the mainstream segments, newer hotels consistently earn higher review scores. This is not a minor effect. The correlation between the average year a hotel was built and its average review score is visible across midscale and upscale brands. Older hotels show wear and tear. Owners face a choice: invest in renovations or accept declining ratings. But renovations are expensive, and the return on that investment is uncertain when the brand itself is perceived as outdated.

This creates a structural disadvantage for older brands that is difficult to overcome through marketing or brand investment. A hotel brand is ultimately an aggregation of physical assets owned by third-party franchisees. The brand owner can set standards, but it cannot force every owner to renovate simultaneously. As the brand ages, the proportion of hotels that are past their prime renovation cycle increases. Even new hotels built under an old brand face a headwind: travelers may avoid the brand based on their experience at older properties, depressing the revenue per available room that new owners can achieve. This reduces the incentive for developers to build new hotels under the brand, which accelerates the decline.

The shift in guest preferences is not limited to physical quality. Consumer expectations about what a hotel should offer have changed dramatically. The full-service model, with its on-site restaurants, meeting spaces, and manned front desks, was once the standard. Today, many travelers prefer limited-service properties that offer lower rates and greater efficiency. Holiday Inn, as a full-service brand, has faced a persistent headwind from this shift. The brand's operating costs are higher than those of limited-service competitors, but guests are increasingly unwilling to pay a premium for amenities they do not value.

## International Expansion and Owner-Operation Can Defy the Lifecycle, but These Exceptions Prove the Rule

There are two notable exceptions to the 30-year peak pattern, and both are instructive. The first is international expansion. When a brand enters a new geographic market, it effectively resets the lifecycle clock in that market. The brand benefits from a blank canvas: there are no aging incumbent hotels to drag down perception, and the brand can be adapted to local preferences. On a global basis, the 30-year peak largely disappears. Brands that have reached maturity in the US can continue to grow in Asia, Europe, and the Middle East. This is not a loophole; it is a strategic requirement for any brand that aspires to long-term relevance.

The second exception is owner-operated brands. When the brand owner also owns the hotels, it can enforce maintenance standards far more rigorously than an asset-light franchisor can. Whitbread's Premier Inn brand in the UK is the clearest example. Premier Inn is nearly 40 years old and still growing, although the pace has slowed as white space becomes a constraint. The owner-operator model gives Whitbread the ability to invest in renovations across its entire portfolio and to make coordinated decisions about brand positioning. But this model has its own limitations: it is capital-intensive and limits the speed of expansion.

Luxury brands represent a third category that does not follow the lifecycle pattern. In luxury, older hotels are often more highly valued by guests. The correlation between hotel age and review scores is flat or even slightly positive. Luxury travelers prize heritage, craftsmanship, and established reputations. Brands like InterContinental, which turned 80 years old in 2026, continue to open new properties and deliver growth. The mechanism is different: luxury brands invest heavily in maintaining standards, and their pricing power allows them to fund those investments. But luxury is a small segment of the total hotel market. The lifecycle pattern applies overwhelmingly to the mainstream brands that constitute the bulk of the industry's room count.

## The Portfolio Age Profile Is Now a Competitive Differentiator, and the Gap Between Winners and Losers Will Widen

The strategic implication for investors is clear: the age profile of a hotel group's brand portfolio is a leading indicator of its future unit growth. The groups with the youngest brands are best positioned for the next decade, while those with older portfolios face a structural headwind that no amount of operational improvement can fully offset.

Hilton and Hyatt stand out as having the youngest brand portfolios among the major asset-light hotel groups. Hilton has invested heavily in launching new brands such as Tru and Spark, both of which are still in their rapid adoption phase. Hyatt has similarly refreshed its brand lineup. In the US, approximately 64 percent of Hyatt's hotels and 30 percent of Hilton's hotels are in brands that are more than 30 years old. For comparison, the equivalent figures for Marriott and IHG are significantly higher, meaning a larger share of their US hotel count is in brands that have already passed or are approaching the lifecycle peak.

This is not a prediction that Hilton and Hyatt will inevitably outperform on every metric. Brand age is one factor among many, and execution matters. But the lifecycle analysis suggests that the growth advantage enjoyed by younger brand portfolios is structural and persistent. The pipeline data supports this: the brands that are still adding hotels at a rapid pace are overwhelmingly those launched in the last 15 to 20 years. The older brands are, in aggregate, seeing their pipelines shrink.

The counterargument is that hotel groups can simply launch new brands to replace aging ones. This is true in theory, but it is difficult in practice. Launching a new brand requires convincing third-party owners to invest capital with limited evidence of brand performance. The early years are slow. The brands that succeed tend to show their trajectory within the first three years, but many do not succeed. The industry is littered with brand launches that never achieved critical mass. The groups that have demonstrated the ability to consistently launch and scale new brands are the exceptions, not the rule.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Brand Lifecycle and Industry Consolidation

The lifecycle framework is powerful, but it leaves several important questions unresolved. The most significant is how the lifecycle interacts with industry consolidation. As legacy brands decline, the hotel groups that own them face a choice: invest in brand revitalization, allow the brand to shrink gracefully, or acquire younger brands to replenish the portfolio. The acquisition route is expensive and carries integration risk. The revitalization route has a poor track record. Crowne Plaza's attempted turnaround, which the report's authors examined in 2019, has not reversed the brand's decline. The implication is that many legacy brands will simply continue to shrink, and the industry's growth will be concentrated in a small number of newer brands.

A second unresolved question is whether the lifecycle can be altered by changes in the franchise model. The current model, with 10- to 20-year contracts, creates the buffer that delays the decline but also makes it more abrupt when it arrives. Shorter contract terms or more flexible brand standards could theoretically extend the lifecycle. But such changes would also reduce the stability that franchisees value. The trade-off is not obvious.

A third question concerns the role of technology. The lifecycle analysis is based on physical hotel assets and guest preferences that have evolved slowly. But technology platforms, distribution systems, and loyalty programs are becoming increasingly important differentiators. It is possible that a brand with a superior technology platform can extend its lifecycle by offering owners and guests benefits that are independent of the physical asset. The data to test this hypothesis is not yet available.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask About Every Hotel Group

For investors evaluating hotel groups, the lifecycle framework suggests three diagnostic questions that go beyond the standard metrics of revenue per available room growth and net unit growth.

First, what is the age profile of the group's brand portfolio, and how much of its US hotel count is in brands that are more than 30 years old? This is a straightforward calculation, but it is rarely disclosed in investor materials. The groups with the highest exposure to post-peak brands face a structural headwind that will show up in slower unit growth and higher churn rates. The groups with the youngest portfolios have a structural tailwind.

Second, does the group have a demonstrated track record of launching and scaling new brands? The lifecycle framework implies that every hotel group must eventually replace its aging brands. The groups that have done this successfully, such as Hilton with Tru and Spark, have a competitive advantage that is difficult to replicate. The groups that have not, or that have launched brands that failed to gain traction, face a more difficult path.

Third, is the group's growth strategy dependent on international expansion for its legacy brands? International expansion can reset the lifecycle clock, but it requires local market expertise, regulatory navigation, and the ability to adapt brand standards to different cultural expectations. Not all groups have the organizational capability to execute this strategy at scale. The groups that do may be able to extend the productive life of their legacy brands beyond what the US data would suggest.

These questions do not replace traditional financial analysis. But they add a dimension that is often overlooked. The hotel industry's brand structure is not static. It is evolving according to a predictable pattern that rewards disciplined portfolio management and punishes complacency.

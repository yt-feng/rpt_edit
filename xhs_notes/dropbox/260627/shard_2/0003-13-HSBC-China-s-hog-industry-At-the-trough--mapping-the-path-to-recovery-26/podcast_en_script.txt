EN_A: So today we are diving into a deep-dive report on China’s hog industry, and the headline is pretty brutal: hog prices just hit their lowest level since 2014. B, can you paint the picture for us? How bad is it on the ground?
EN_B: It is genuinely one of the worst cycles on record. Self-breeding farmers are losing over 300 yuan per hog. And this deep loss has been going on for more than nine months. According to the report, this is the second-worst loss cycle since 2014, both in terms of depth and duration.
EN_A: Second worst? So what was the worst?
EN_B: That was back in 2021, when losses peaked at 318 yuan per head. This cycle is slightly less extreme, but still brutal. In April 2026, the national average hog price hit 9 yuan per kilogram — an all-time low since 2014. It has since recovered a bit to around 9.5 yuan, but it is still deeply depressed.
EN_A: So why are prices so low? Is it because people are eating less pork?
EN_B: Actually, no. Demand is surprisingly resilient. Pork is now the cheapest animal protein compared to chicken, beef, eggs, and fish. And cold-storage inventory hit a three-year high earlier this year, which means processors are buying aggressively. So the problem is not weak demand — it is oversupply.
EN_A: Oversupply? But I thought the government was trying to reduce supply?
EN_B: That is exactly the twist. In May 2025, the Ministry of Agriculture held meetings urging large corporate farms to cut their sow herds. And the big players did comply — Muyuan, for example, started reducing sows. But the non-corporate farmers, the smaller players, they actually expanded. They saw the policy as a signal that prices would go up, so they added capacity.
EN_A: So the policy backfired in a way?
EN_B: In a sense, yes. The report has a great calculation: if a farmer added 10,000 sows in May 2025 against the policy tide, by March 2026 those hogs would have cost them about 23 million yuan in losses just from selling finishing hogs. That is a huge hit.
EN_A: So now those farmers are under pressure. What happens next?
EN_B: The report argues that sow inventory reduction will accelerate through the second quarter of 2026. There are three forces at play. First, the non-corporate farmers who expanded are now financially strained and will be forced to cut. Second, corporate farms have already reduced and will stabilize. Third, if the price of 7-kilogram piglets falls below 200 yuan per head, the piglet-specialist farms will also start cutting capacity.
EN_A: So supply is shrinking. Does that mean prices will rebound soon?
EN_B: The report expects year-on-year supply contraction to begin in the fourth quarter of 2026. But it warns that this will be a moderate recovery, not a sharp spike. Because there is a lot of pork sitting in cold storage, which will absorb some of the supply reduction. So the inflection point is coming, but it will be gradual.
EN_A: For investors, does this mean it is time to buy?
EN_B: The report maintains Buy ratings on Muyuan, Wens, and Haid Group. The logic is that the stock prices already reflect the worst of this cycle. In fact, the valuations imply a permanent impairment of earnings power, which the report thinks is overly pessimistic. The hog cycle has a self-correction mechanism.
EN_A: But there are still some open questions, right?
EN_B: Absolutely. One big one is efficiency gains. The report estimates that production efficiency could improve by 4 to 5 percent year-on-year in the second half of 2026. That would partially offset the reduction in sow numbers. So even if there are fewer sows, each sow might produce more hogs. The report does not fully resolve this tension — it is one of those things you need to read the full report to understand.
EN_A: So the core story is supply contraction is coming, but the pace and scale are still uncertain. For the full picture, you will want to check out the complete report. Thanks for breaking it down, B.
EN_B: Thanks for having me.

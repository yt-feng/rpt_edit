# The Container Shipping Upcycle Is Not What You Think It Is: Why This Cycle Is Structural, Not Cyclical

The prevailing narrative among many market participants is that the current container shipping upcycle is a familiar creature—a temporary spike driven by frontloading, tariff noise, and Red Sea disruption. According to this view, rates will roll over by autumn, and the sector will revert to its structurally challenged equilibrium. That narrative is increasingly difficult to defend.

A detailed webinar hosted by Linerlytica in late June 2026, discussed in a recent global investment bank report, presents evidence that the current cycle is fundamentally different. Spot rates to the US West Coast have reached approximately USD 6,000 per 40-foot container, and US East Coast rates have climbed to USD 7,000-8,000. These are not panic-driven peaks; they are levels supported by a demand base that is shifting in composition and geography.

The critical insight, and the one that should command the attention of serious investors, is that this upcycle is being driven by structural demand—specifically technology-led investment in AI, data centres, clean energy, and the power infrastructure that supports them. Traditional China export categories such as garments, footwear, and toys have actually declined year-to-date. The strength is coming from batteries, electric vehicles, solar panels, and the heavy equipment required to build data centre capacity. This is not a restocking event.

The implications for earnings, valuation, and strategic positioning are substantial. Sector EBIT margins, which sat at roughly 5% in the first quarter of 2026, are on track to rise to approximately 10% in the second quarter, with further upside in the third quarter if rates hold. Companies like Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd may need to raise their full-year 2026 guidance. Yet the market remains sceptical, and valuations across the sector do not reflect this inflection.

This article unpacks the evidence behind the structural demand thesis, examines the capacity dynamics that will determine how long the cycle lasts, identifies the risks that could unwind the current tightness, and presents a decision framework for investors navigating this environment. The argument is not that the sector is permanently transformed, but that the current cycle has deeper foundations than most investors assume—and that the market may be mispricing the near-term earnings trajectory as a result.

## Structural Demand from Technology and Clean Energy Is Replacing the Traditional Trade Cycle

The most important data point in the entire report is not the spot rate level. It is the composition of China's exports. In the first five months of 2026, traditional consumer goods exports from China were flat to negative: garments declined 1.6%, footwear fell 10.3%, and toys dropped 12.6%. If this cycle were driven by frontloading in anticipation of tariffs, these categories should have been surging. They were not.

Instead, the demand strength is coming from sectors tied to long-term capital expenditure. Batteries, electric vehicles, and solar equipment are being shipped in volume. More significantly, the buildout of data centres and the power infrastructure required to support them is generating demand for heavy equipment and specialised containers that did not exist in previous cycles. This is not a consumer-led phenomenon. It is an industrial and technology-led phenomenon.

The report notes that trans-Pacific volumes are up roughly 4-5% year-on-year, with May strong and June even firmer. European demand is running above last year's peak. But the most striking data point is the absorption of capacity by emerging markets. African demand is up 50% year-to-date, and Latin American demand is up 12%. These regions are taking capacity that might otherwise have been deployed on the main east-west trades, which is a structural tightening mechanism that has little to do with the Red Sea.

The implication is straightforward: even if the Red Sea were to normalise tomorrow—which it will not in the near term—there is a floor under demand that did not exist in previous cycles. The composition of trade is shifting toward categories that are less sensitive to consumer sentiment and more tied to multi-year investment programmes. This does not eliminate cyclicality, but it does change the shape of the cycle.

## Capacity Discipline Is Holding, But the Real Risk Is Not Red Sea—It Is 2027-2028

The conventional bear case for container shipping has two pillars: the return of Red Sea capacity and the delivery of new vessels. Both are valid concerns, but the timing and magnitude matter more than the direction.

On the Red Sea, the report estimates that normalisation of Gulf routes could absorb more than 2% of the global fleet, but a full return to Red Sea transits could release approximately 6% of effective capacity. That is a meaningful swing factor. However, the report also notes that congestion is currently supporting tightness, and the timing of any resolution remains highly uncertain. The market is pricing in a faster resolution than the evidence supports.

The larger structural risk is the 2027-2028 supply wave. Newbuild deliveries are expected to ramp up in the second half of 2027 and peak in 2028, with supply growth of approximately 14%. Even allowing for demolition capacity of at most 1 million TEU per year, net supply growth in 2028 could be 11-12% or higher. Demand growth, by contrast, is projected at 6-7% for 2026 and is unlikely to sustain that pace into 2028.

This is the real time bomb in the sector. The current tightness is a function of disruption and demand composition, not of fundamental supply-demand equilibrium. When the supply wave hits, the structural overcapacity that has plagued the industry for decades will re-emerge. The question is not whether it will happen, but whether the industry's consolidation and alliance structures can mitigate the damage.

The report highlights that alliance shifts are changing market share but not competition or pricing power. The Gemini Cooperation, for example, is underperforming due to contract exposure, spot discounting, and the cost structure of hub-and-spoke networks. Schedule reliability premiums, which some operators hoped would justify higher rates, appear limited. This suggests that when the supply wave arrives, pricing discipline may break down faster than many expect.

## Earnings Leverage Is Underappreciated: Margins Are Inflecting Even as Costs Rise

One of the most counterintuitive findings in the report is that earnings leverage is improving despite higher fuel costs. Bunker fuel is approximately 30% above pre-conflict levels, but operating expenses are expected to rise only 2-3%. The reason is that revenue is growing much faster than costs.

The China Containerized Freight Index (CCFI) is up roughly 40-42% year-on-year. With operating costs rising only modestly, the incremental revenue flows almost entirely to the bottom line. The report estimates that sector EBIT margins could rise from approximately 5% in the first quarter of 2026 to roughly 10% in the second quarter, with further upside in the third quarter if current spot rates hold.

This margin expansion is not yet reflected in consensus estimates for many operators. The report argues that Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd could potentially raise their full-year 2026 guidance. If they do, the market will be forced to re-evaluate its earnings assumptions for the entire sector.

The valuation picture is mixed but supportive for selective names. Maersk trades at 0.6 times 2026 estimated book value with a return on equity of just over 2%. Evergreen Marine trades at 0.7 times book with a return on equity of 6.4%. COSCO Shipping H-shares trade at 0.7 times book with a return on equity of nearly 9%. These are not expensive multiples for companies that are about to report accelerating earnings.

The exception is Hapag-Lloyd, which trades at 1.2 times book with a negative return on equity. The report maintains a Reduce rating on the stock, reflecting concerns about fuel cost exposure and margin pressure. This divergence within the sector underscores the importance of stock selection.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Demand Composition Puzzle and the Tariff Endgame

For all its analytical depth, the report leaves several important questions unresolved. The first is the sustainability of technology-led demand. Data centre buildout and clean energy investment are multi-year trends, but they are also lumpy and subject to policy shifts. If AI investment cycles slow or if clean energy subsidies are reduced, the demand floor could erode faster than expected.

The second unresolved question is the tariff endgame. The report notes that the removal of the IEEPA and Section 122 likely boosted exports and the trans-Pacific rebound, and that proposed Section 301 tariffs look similar. But it does not model the impact of a full-blown trade war scenario. If tariffs escalate significantly, the demand composition could shift again, and the emerging market absorption that is currently supporting the market could reverse as global trade volumes contract.

The third question is the behaviour of the alliances during the supply wave. The report notes that alliance shifts are not changing pricing power, but it does not explain what would happen if the 2027-2028 supply wave triggers a price war. The industry is more consolidated than it was a decade ago, but fixed costs are high, and the temptation to fill vessels with low-margin cargo will be strong.

These are not weaknesses in the report. They are honest acknowledgements of uncertainty. Investors who want to position for the current cycle must also prepare for the structural risks that lie beyond it.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Your Positioning

For investors trying to navigate this environment, the report points toward a simple but powerful decision framework built around three questions.

First, what is your time horizon? If your horizon is six to twelve months, the case for overweighting select container shipping names is strong. Margins are inflecting, guidance is likely to be raised, and valuations are not pricing in the earnings momentum. The report's Buy ratings on Maersk, Evergreen Marine, and COSCO Shipping reflect this near-term opportunity.

If your horizon is two to three years, the calculus changes. The 2027-2028 supply wave is a real and material risk. The sector's structural overcapacity has not been solved; it has merely been postponed by disruption. Investors with longer horizons need to be more selective, focusing on operators with strong balance sheets, diversified business models, and the ability to maintain dividends through a downturn.

Second, which operators have pricing power that will survive the next downturn? The report suggests that scale matters, but so does contract structure. Operators with heavy exposure to spot markets will benefit more in the current upcycle but will suffer more in the next downturn. Operators with long-term contracts and diversified revenue streams—such as Maersk's logistics and terminals businesses—may offer better downside protection.

Third, how do you hedge the Red Sea risk? A rapid normalisation would release significant capacity and could trigger a sharp rate correction. The report does not recommend a specific hedge, but the implication is clear: if you are overweight the sector, you should have a view on the Red Sea timeline, and you should be prepared to adjust positions if that timeline changes.

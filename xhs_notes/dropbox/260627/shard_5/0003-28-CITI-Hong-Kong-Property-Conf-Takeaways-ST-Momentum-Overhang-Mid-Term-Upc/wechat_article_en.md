# Hong Kong Property’s Mid-Term Upcycle Is Intact, But Short-Term Sentiment Overhang Creates a Tactical Entry Window

The Hong Kong property market is caught in a rare moment of analytical tension. On one hand, the structural case for a multi-year upcycle in residential prices has never been clearer: a looming supply deficit in 2026-2028, development margins that have decisively bottomed, and a land market where developers are aggressively replenishing inventory. On the other hand, near-term sentiment is fragile, weighed down by China’s ODI regulation headlines, a correction in Hong Kong equities, and a wait-and-see mood among buyers that has temporarily chilled transaction volumes. The market is not confused about the direction; it is confused about the timing.

A global investment bank’s recent property and financials conference, which gathered 14 property corporates and global investors, provided the clearest signal yet that the sector is at an inflection point. The dominant question from investors was not whether the market would recover, but how quickly the policy overhang would fade and whether the current share price weakness represents a buying opportunity or a value trap. The answer, based on the evidence, is that the mid-term upcycle is on track, but the next two months will likely remain volatile. For investors with a 12-month horizon, this creates a tactical entry window. For those with shorter timeframes, the risk of further downside before the next catalyst—likely the strong first-half 2026 results in August—is real.

The conference revealed a subtle but important shift in investor behavior. Attendees were less focused on macro beta and more on company-specific features: dividend per share growth, buyback programs, capital recycling strategies, low-gearing balance sheets, and new intellectual property or land acquisitions. This is a market that has stopped waiting for the macro to turn decisively and has started picking individual names on their own merits. That is a sign of maturity, not weakness.

## The ODI Regulation Overhang Is Mostly Noise, But It Will Take Time for Sentiment to Recover

The single biggest source of uncertainty for Hong Kong property investors in recent months has been China’s outward direct investment regulation and its potential impact on residential and office demand. The conference provided a critical reality check: none of the property firms in attendance observed any measurable impact from the policy. Over the past month, there were one or two cases of transaction cancellations or extended completion periods, but these are considered normal and consistent with prior experience rather than directly linked to the new regulation.

The data on non-Hong Kong ID buyers is instructive. Across developers, the share of non-HKID buyers remains below 10 percent, which aligns with the bank’s own analysis of Inland Revenue Department data. Even in Kai Tak, an area popular among mainland buyers, the share of non-HKID holders is approximately 15 percent, despite 77 percent of buyers carrying mainland surnames at one major project. This suggests that the headline narrative of a mainland buyer exodus is overblown. Many of these buyers are likely Hong Kong residents with mainland surnames, not cross-border capital flows that would be affected by ODI rules.

There is a deeper structural point here that investors should not overlook. Hong Kong property sits outside the scope of China’s Common Reporting Standard tax enforcement. According to developers, KYC responsibilities rest with financial institutions or agents, while the developers themselves neither hold direct access to nor maintain confidential personal data. This makes Hong Kong property an attractive asset allocation not only for living needs or rental yield but also for capital that values discretion. The regulatory overhang is a sentiment issue, not a fundamental one. However, sentiment takes time to heal, and the next one to two months will likely see continued noise as the market digests this reality.

## The Residential Market Is Experiencing a Temporary Volume Dip, Not a Price Trend Reversal

The primary market is on track for approximately 12,500 units in the first half of 2026, which would be a 21-year high. Secondary market volumes are up 35 percent year-on-year. These are not the numbers of a market in trouble. Yet in June alone, transaction activity has been quieter. The reasons are familiar: a recent correction in Hong Kong equities, buyer hesitation amid volatile news flow, bad weather, slower project launches, and narrower room for price negotiation in the secondary market after the recent price recovery.

This cyclical headwind is unlikely to alter the mid-term home price upcycle. The anchoring logic is simple and powerful: Hong Kong faces a structural under-supply of new homes in 2026 through 2028, with completions estimated at only 15,000 to 18,000 units per year. Against a backdrop of steady demand from local upgraders, expatriate relocations, and long-term mainland buyers, this supply deficit will eventually force prices higher. The question is not whether prices will rise, but when the market will regain the confidence to price in that reality.

The bank’s analysis suggests that sector share prices could remain volatile in June and July, before the likely strong first-half 2026 results serve as the next potential catalyst in August. This creates a clear tactical challenge: investors who wait for clarity may miss the entry point, while those who buy too early may face further short-term pain. The resolution lies in company-specific selection. Developers with strong pre-sales, visible margin recovery, and active land replenishment—such as Sun Hung Kai Properties, which is tracking toward HK$37 billion in FY26E sales against a target of HK$30 billion—are better positioned to weather the noise.

## Development Margins Have Bottomed, and Land Replenishment Signals Confidence

One of the most important findings from the conference is that the bottoming of development margins is now visible and will crystallize in the first-half 2026 results. This is not a forecast; it is a statement of fact based on higher-margin inventory sales already in the pipeline. Even in Kai Tak, where competition is fierce, developers are achieving mid-teens operating profit margins after 10 to 15 percent price hikes year-to-date. Sales strategies have been adjusted to seek profit through premium units while balancing sell-through for mass projects.

The implications for capital allocation are significant. Strong property sales drive cash flow and lower gearing, which in turn gives developers the flexibility to pursue land acquisitions. Henderson Land, for example, is expected to report development margins recovering to high-teens in the first half of 2026, excluding the Legacy project, compared to just 8 percent in FY25. Kerry Properties has already acquired three land sites year-to-date with EBITDA margins above 20 percent, and is targeting gearing below 30 percent by end-2026.

This is a market where the smart money is betting on itself. Developers are not just selling into the current cycle; they are positioning for the next one. The land market is active, and the companies that are buying now are signaling that they see the current sentiment weakness as an opportunity, not a threat. For investors, the key question is which developers have the balance sheet strength and project pipeline to execute on this strategy without over-leveraging.

## Central Office Rent Is Recovering, But Other Districts Face a Longer Digestive Period

The office market presents a more nuanced picture than residential, but one that is equally instructive for sector strategy. Spot rent for Central super Grade-A offices has been picking up progressively, underpinned by no new supply and rising enquiries amid a re-centralization trend. This clearly favors the bargaining power of landlords. The Henderson is now 95 percent let. Central Yards has received such strong interest that it can afford to select tenants at its own pace without rush, and is on track to be fully committed by the first quarter of 2027. International Commerce Centre is showing decent leasing progress, riding on strong transportation connectivity and the largest floor plate in Hong Kong at 78,000 square feet.

Other districts are still digesting supply with high competition, with two notable exceptions. Admiralty is benefiting from spill-over demand from Central, and West Kowloon is gaining market share on the back of high-quality new supply. Reversion remains negative but is narrowing. Hongkong Land, for example, is estimated to face negative reversion of about 5 percent in 2026E, moving to flat in 2027E.

The strategic takeaway for investors is that Central office is a landlord’s market, while the rest of Hong Kong remains a tenant’s market. This divergence has implications for portfolio construction. Landlords with concentrated exposure to Central super Grade-A assets are better positioned to deliver earnings growth and dividend stability than those with diversified office portfolios in weaker districts. The bank’s picks include Swire Properties and Hongkong Land, both of which have significant Central exposure and are actively optimizing their portfolios.

## Retail Recovery Is Real, But Reversion Dynamics Vary Widely by Sub-Sector

Retail tenant sales are improving across both luxury and non-discretionary categories, but the pace of recovery is uneven. Ranking from high to low: mainland China luxury is growing fastest, followed by Hong Kong luxury at around 10 percent year-on-year, with Hong Kong non-discretionary just above zero. Luxury retail faces a fade-out of favorable base effects in the second half of the year, but leasing alpha by key landlords should continue to drive outperformance relative to the broader market.

The reversion picture is mixed and requires careful navigation. Some luxury landlords are expecting negative reversion in 2026E despite good tenant sales, because the expiring rents are high. Harbour City is a case in point. This negative reversion should turn positive in 2027E as the high-base leases roll off. Typical non-luxury malls are seeing less negative retail reversion, while neighborhood malls have spot rent stabilizing but reversion likely staying at negative high-single-digit magnitude throughout 2026E.

For investors, the implication is that retail recovery is not a uniform trade. Landlords with the ability to reconfigure assets, attract new brands, and manage lease expiries will outperform. Swire Properties, for example, has upside potential from asset reconfiguration at Beijing Sanlitun and Shanghai HKRI Taikoo Hui. Hongkong Land is seeing brand re-openings at Central Landmark. These are not passive holdings; they are active management stories.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Path from Sentiment Overhang to Price Discovery

Despite the wealth of data and analysis, the conference report leaves several open questions that investors need to resolve for themselves. First, how long will it take for the ODI regulation overhang to fully dissipate? The evidence suggests no material impact to date, but sentiment is not driven by evidence alone. The market needs to see a sustained period of stable transaction volumes and rising prices before it fully re-embraces the sector.

Second, what is the likely trajectory of interest rates and how will it affect developer financing costs and buyer affordability? The report notes that higher interest rate expectations are one reason investors have trimmed exposure, but it does not model the sensitivity of developer margins or REIT valuations to different rate scenarios.

Third, how will the rotation to other sectors or regions evolve? The conference noted that many investors have rotated out of Hong Kong property into other markets. If global risk appetite improves further, this rotation could accelerate, delaying the sector’s recovery.

These are not weaknesses in the analysis; they are the natural boundaries of a single report. The full picture requires ongoing monitoring of transaction data, policy announcements, and corporate earnings. For serious investors, the report provides a framework, not a conclusion.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Next Six Months

For investors who want to act on the insights from this report, a structured decision framework is essential. The first question is time horizon. If you are investing for the next three months, the risk of further volatility is high, and the best approach is to wait for the August catalyst before adding exposure. If your horizon is 12 months or longer, the current weakness represents a tactical entry point, particularly for developers with visible margin recovery and active land replenishment.

The second question is sub-sector preference. Residential developers with strong pre-sales and margin recovery are the most direct play on the mid-term upcycle. Central office landlords offer stability and re-rating potential as reversion narrows. Retail landlords require more careful selection, favoring those with active asset management and lease expiry profiles that will turn positive in 2027E.

The third question is balance sheet strength. In a market where sentiment is fragile, companies with low gearing, strong cash flow, and a track record of dividend growth or buybacks will be rewarded. The bank’s picks—Swire Properties, Sun Hung Kai Properties, CK Asset Holdings, and Link REIT—all share these characteristics.

The fourth question is optionality. Developers that are actively buying land now are signaling confidence in the cycle. Investors should favor those that are replenishing their pipelines without over-leveraging. The companies that are sitting on the sidelines may be safer in the short term, but they risk missing the next upcycle.

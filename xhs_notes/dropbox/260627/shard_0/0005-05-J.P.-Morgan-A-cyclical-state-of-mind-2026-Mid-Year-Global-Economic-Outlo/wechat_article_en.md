# The 2026 Global Economy Is Not Breaking—It Is Recalibrating, and Central Banks Are the Wild Card

The global economy entered 2026 with a clear narrative: the post-trade-war rebound in business confidence would finally lift non-tech spending and hiring, sustaining above-potential growth. Then came the Strait of Hormuz closure, an energy price shock that rewrote the near-term script. Headline inflation spiked, consumer purchasing power took a hit, and central banks tilted hawkish. Yet the core thesis of a cyclical upturn remains intact. The energy shock has not derailed the expansion; it has compressed it into a midyear slowdown that sets the stage for a second-half reacceleration. The real question is not whether growth can hold—it can—but whether the price stability trade-off will force monetary policy into a more aggressive posture than markets currently price. That is the tension that defines the second half of 2026 and the outlook for 2027.

The conventional macroeconomic narrative has been that last year's job stall represented a structural shift—weak labor supply and technology diffusion promoting productivity-led growth and policy normalization. That view was always contestable. The evidence now points the other way: business sentiment is normalizing, tech spending is broadening beyond hyperscalers, and labor demand is firming. The energy shock interrupted this lift but did not reverse it. The Strait agreement has reduced the risk of a disruptive oil spike. Global GDP growth will downshift into midyear as the CPI spike softens consumption, but the inventory cycle is turning positive, fiscal measures are cushioning households, and falling oil prices should boost purchasing power in the second half. The cyclical upturn is delayed, not denied.

But there is a cost. Core inflation is set to remain above target for a sixth straight year. The pass-through from energy to core goods prices is building. Services inflation has rebounded broadly. Labor markets are tightening again. The combination of above-potential growth and sticky inflation creates a dilemma for central banks. They have tolerated elevated inflation so far, keeping real rates low by historical standards. That tolerance is the foundation of supportive financial conditions. But if growth reaccelerates as expected, and labor markets tighten further, the tolerance threshold may be tested. The risk is not a recession. It is an unexpected reset of monetary policy expectations that generates financial stress in an otherwise healthy expansion.

This is not a forecast of doom. It is a strategic recognition that the expansion's resilience depends on central banks' willingness to accept above-target inflation. That willingness is not infinite. The next six months will reveal whether the cyclical lift can be sustained without forcing a policy mistake.

## The Energy Shock Compressed the Cycle but Did Not Break It, and the Business Sector Lift Is Broadening

The Strait of Hormuz closure was a genuine shock. It raised our 2026 headline inflation projection by one full percentage point and trimmed global GDP growth by 0.2 percentage points. But the impact is best understood as a compression, not a reversal. The underlying drivers of the cyclical upturn—fading business caution after last year's trade war, a rebound in non-tech investment, and a broadening tech boom—remain in place. Incoming data through the first half of 2026 shows tech spending expanding beyond hyperscalers, supporting demand and contributing to goods price pressures. Business sentiment, which had fallen sharply during the trade war, began normalizing at the start of the year. The Strait closure interrupted that normalization, but with tensions receding and oil prices falling, a resumption is likely.

The inventory cycle is also turning. Global retail sales volume growth is expected to stall into midyear as consumers digest higher prices, but industrial production gains should hold up, supported by stockbuilding. This divergence—soft consumption, solid production—is a classic mid-cycle pattern. It reflects a shift from consumer-led growth to business-led growth. The question is whether the consumer side recovers quickly enough to sustain the expansion into 2027. The answer depends on two factors: job growth and energy prices.

## Job Growth Is the Transmission Belt from Business Confidence to Household Income, and It Is Already Firming

The most important development for the second-half outlook is the recent pickup in US job growth. We see this as linked to fading business caution, not a temporary bounce. If sustained, US job gains of 100,000 per month or faster are achievable. That would provide a direct cushion to household purchasing power at a time when real income is squeezed by higher energy costs. Globally, employment gains are rebounding toward 1% annualized, reversing last year's deceleration. This is consistent with a labor market that is tightening, not loosening.

The implications for inflation are straightforward. Tighter labor markets put upward pressure on wages and services inflation. Global wage inflation, while moderated from its peak, remains above the pace seen at the peak of the 2000s expansion. That is not a recipe for a return to 2% core inflation. It is a recipe for persistent above-target inflation, which central banks will have to address.

The counterargument is that last year's rise in the global unemployment rate—back to pre-pandemic levels—indicates slack. But that rise was concentrated in the trade-war period and has already begun to reverse. Output gap estimates point to steadily reducing slack. If our growth forecast is correct, the unemployment rate will continue to fall. That puts the Fed and other central banks in a position where they must choose between accepting above-target inflation or tightening further.

## Central Bank Tolerance for Above-Target Inflation Is the Expansion's Hidden Support, but It Is Not a Free Lunch

Financial conditions remain remarkably supportive. The Fed's own model of the financial conditions impulse for US growth is sending its strongest signal in two decades, excluding recession exits. Global credit stress measures are low. Household savings rates have moved lower, but wealth gains have been material and debt servicing costs remain manageable. This is not an economy under financial strain. It is an economy that is growing strongly enough to generate balanced profit and labor income growth.

But this favorable backdrop is contingent on central banks maintaining their current posture. Global policy rates have fallen over the past two years even as core inflation stayed near 3%. Real rates are low relative to historical episodes of demand-restraining policy. The baseline forecast from a major global investment bank anticipates that the Fed, ECB, and most other central banks will raise rates by less than 30 basis points over the coming year. That is a shallow tightening cycle, reflecting a tolerance for inflation that would have been unthinkable a decade ago.

The risk is that this tolerance is tested by the data. If growth reaccelerates in the second half, and core inflation remains above 3%, the case for more aggressive action will strengthen. Past experience shows that an unexpected reset of monetary policy expectations tends to generate financial stress, even in an otherwise healthy expansion. The market is not pricing that risk. That is precisely why it matters.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: How Much Core Inflation Can Central Banks Tolerate Before They Act?

The report provides a clear framework: core inflation surprises are sufficient to deliver hikes, and the Fed will hike because of the interaction of elevated core inflation with a tightening labor market. But it does not specify the threshold at which tolerance breaks. Is it 3.5% core PCE? 4%? Or is it the pace of labor market tightening that matters more than the level of inflation? The report notes that global policy rates will rise less than 30 basis points over the coming year, but that is a baseline forecast, not a commitment. The range of outcomes is wide.

A related question is whether the productivity boost from AI can offset the inflationary pressure from tighter labor markets. The report acknowledges AI as a contributor to demand this year, not just supply. But it does not quantify the net effect on inflation. If AI-driven productivity gains are large enough, they could allow above-potential growth without overheating. If they are not, the trade-off between growth and inflation becomes sharper.

These are not gaps in the analysis. They are open questions that any serious investor must monitor. The next three to six months will provide answers.

## A Decision Framework for the Second Half of 2026

For readers who need to act on this outlook, the key is to distinguish between the cyclical and the structural. The cyclical case is straightforward: business confidence is normalizing, tech spending is broadening, the inventory cycle is turning, and job growth is firming. The energy shock is a headwind, not a game-changer. The second half should see a reacceleration.

The structural question is about inflation and policy. Core inflation is not coming down to 2% this year or next. Central banks have a choice: accept it and keep rates low, or fight it and risk financial stress. The baseline is acceptance, but the risk is a hawkish surprise.

The framework for decision-making is therefore:

1. Watch US job growth. Sustained gains above 100,000 per month confirm the cyclical lift and tighten labor markets. That increases the probability of Fed action.

2. Watch core goods inflation. The pass-through from energy to goods prices is building. If it accelerates, it will add to the pressure on central banks.

3. Watch European sentiment. The rebound in Euro area business confidence is a leading indicator for the global cycle. If it materializes as expected, it confirms the breadth of the upturn.

4. Watch central bank communication. The tolerance for above-target inflation is the key variable. Any shift in language toward a more hawkish stance is a signal to adjust positioning.

The most important insight from this report is that the global economy is not breaking. It is recalibrating. The energy shock was a test, not a failure. But the cost of above-potential growth is persistent inflation, and that cost will eventually have to be paid. The question is when, and how much.

# China’s Credit Rating System Has Become a Dangerous Distortion That Regulators Can No Longer Ignore

The Chinese bond market is facing a credibility crisis that has been years in the making, and regulators have finally acknowledged the problem. In early 2026, the People's Bank of China and other regulatory bodies formally requested that domestic rating agencies overhaul their methodologies to better reflect actual credit risk. This intervention is long overdue. According to data compiled by Bloomberg, an astonishing 59 percent of all rated issuers in China currently hold AAA or AA+ ratings. Among non-financial enterprises specifically, the share of debt financing instruments carrying these top-tier grades has risen to 74.6 percent. To put this in perspective, even in the most developed bond markets in the world, major international rating agencies assign such high grades to only a small fraction of issuers. The gap is not merely a matter of methodological difference; it is a structural distortion that undermines the entire information function of the credit market.

The fundamental problem is that this inflation of ratings is occurring precisely when corporate financial health in China is deteriorating at an alarming rate. According to the National Bureau of Statistics, the share of loss-making industrial companies climbed to 23.8 percent in 2025, the highest level since 2000. This represents a doubling from the 11.8 percent recorded in 2017. The situation has worsened further in 2026: in the first four months of the year, the share of loss-making firms reached 32.4 percent, up from 31.7 percent in the same period a year earlier. The divergence between the rating distribution and the underlying financial reality is no longer a subtle inconsistency; it is a chasm that threatens to mislead investors, distort capital allocation, and blind policymakers to emerging systemic risks.

This is not merely a technical issue for credit analysts. It is a strategic concern for anyone with exposure to Chinese assets, whether through direct bond holdings, equity positions in companies that rely on debt markets, or broader macroeconomic exposure to the world's second-largest economy. The property bust, which remains the dominant force shaping China's economic trajectory, continues to drive up credit risks for both corporations and households. In this fragile environment, a rating system that systematically understates risk does not merely provide poor information; it actively encourages mispricing of risk, delays necessary adjustments, and increases the probability of a disorderly correction when the gap between perception and reality finally closes.

## The Proliferation of Top-Tier Ratings Directly Contradicts the Deteriorating Financial Health of Chinese Corporates

The data from the National Bureau of Statistics tells a stark story that every investor should internalize. The share of loss-making industrial companies has more than doubled in less than a decade, rising from 11.8 percent in 2017 to 23.8 percent in 2025. The trend has accelerated into 2026, with the first four months showing a further increase to 32.4 percent. This means that nearly one in three industrial companies in China is currently operating at a loss. In a properly functioning credit market, such a deterioration would trigger a significant widening of credit spreads and a re-rating of risk profiles across the corporate sector. Instead, the domestic rating system has moved in the opposite direction, with the share of top-tier ratings actually increasing over the same period.

The disconnect is not just about the level of ratings; it is about the direction of change. As more companies have fallen into loss-making territory, the rating agencies have responded by awarding more AAA and AA+ ratings. This suggests that the rating process is not merely failing to capture deteriorating fundamentals but is actively moving in the wrong direction. For an investor trying to make sense of the Chinese bond market, the signal-to-noise ratio has become dangerously low. The ratings no longer differentiate between companies that are genuinely creditworthy and those that are struggling. In effect, the market has lost one of its most important information channels for assessing risk.

The implications extend beyond the bond market itself. Equity investors who rely on debt market signals to assess the health of companies they own or are considering purchasing are also operating with distorted information. Similarly, international investors who use credit ratings as a first screen for market entry may be making fundamentally flawed decisions about portfolio allocation. The rating inflation creates a false sense of security that can persist only until a default event forces a sudden and painful reassessment.

## The Property Bust Has Created a Fragile Economic Environment That Exposes the Rating System's Weaknesses

The timing of the rating system's failure could hardly be worse. China's economy is still being battered by an unprecedented property bust that has reshaped the landscape of corporate and household credit risk. The property sector has historically been a major source of collateral and credit demand in China, and its contraction has sent shockwaves through the entire financial system. Developers that were once considered blue-chip borrowers have defaulted, and the ripple effects have spread to suppliers, local governments, and financial institutions that hold property-related assets.

In this environment, a flawed rating system does more than just provide inaccurate information; it actively masks the buildup of default vulnerabilities. When a system tells investors that nearly three-quarters of non-financial enterprise debt is top-tier quality, it creates an implicit assumption that defaults will be rare and contained. This assumption is directly contradicted by the rising share of loss-making companies, but because the rating system has not adjusted, market participants have no easy way to reconcile the two signals. The result is a market that is pricing risk based on outdated or misleading information, which increases the likelihood that when a correction comes, it will be sharper and more disruptive than if the adjustment had been gradual and transparent.

Policymakers are also affected. Central bank officials and financial regulators rely on credit ratings as one input into their assessment of systemic risk. If the rating system is systematically understating risk, then macroprudential policy may be calibrated too loosely. The fact that the PBoC has now explicitly requested a revamp of the rating system suggests that regulators have recognized this danger. But the recognition comes after years of divergence between ratings and fundamentals, and the process of rebuilding credibility will take time.

## A Reformed Rating System Would Reshape Capital Allocation and Force a Repricing of Risk Across Chinese Credit Markets

If the regulatory push leads to meaningful reform, the consequences for Chinese credit markets will be profound. A rating system that more accurately reflects credit risk would inevitably result in a significant downgrade of many currently highly rated issuers. This would trigger a cascading series of adjustments. Fund managers who are constrained by mandates that require minimum rating thresholds would be forced to sell bonds that no longer qualify. Insurance companies and pension funds, which are among the largest institutional investors in bond markets, would need to reassess their portfolios. The demand for lower-rated bonds would decline, pushing up yields and increasing borrowing costs for the companies that are downgraded.

This repricing would have real economic effects. Companies that have been able to borrow at artificially low rates because of inflated ratings would face higher financing costs. Some would be forced to reduce investment or restructure their balance sheets. Others might find themselves unable to refinance maturing debt, leading to a wave of defaults that the current rating system has been masking. In the short term, this adjustment would be painful. But in the medium to long term, it would be healthy. Capital would flow more efficiently to the companies that are genuinely creditworthy, and the market would be able to perform its function of allocating capital to its most productive uses.

The reform would also change the dynamics of the primary market. Companies issuing new bonds would face more realistic pricing from the outset, reducing the temptation for issuers to push for inflated ratings. Over time, this would reduce the moral hazard that has been embedded in the system, where companies and rating agencies have had incentives to maintain the fiction that credit risk is lower than it actually is. A more honest rating system would also make China's bond market more attractive to international investors, who have been wary of relying on domestic ratings that they do not trust.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question That It Does Not Fully Answer: What Mechanism Will Drive the Transition to a More Accurate System?

While the report makes a compelling case that the rating system is broken and that reform is necessary, it leaves open the question of how the transition will actually work. The regulatory request for a revamp is a necessary first step, but it is far from sufficient. Rating agencies face significant conflicts of interest that have contributed to the current situation. They are paid by the issuers they rate, which creates a natural incentive to provide favorable assessments. In a system where top-tier ratings have become the norm, any agency that unilaterally starts issuing lower ratings risks losing market share to competitors that maintain the status quo.

Collective action problems of this kind are notoriously difficult to resolve through voluntary reform. The regulators may need to impose binding standards, such as requiring that the distribution of ratings in China converge toward the distribution observed in international markets. Alternatively, they could mandate that rating agencies disclose the statistical basis for their ratings, including the historical default rates associated with each rating grade. Such transparency would make it much harder for agencies to maintain inflated ratings without being exposed.

Another unresolved question is the role of international rating agencies. If domestic agencies are forced to become more accurate, will international agencies expand their coverage of Chinese issuers, or will they remain focused on the largest and most transparent companies? The answer matters because international ratings provide an external benchmark that can help discipline the domestic market. But international agencies face their own challenges in China, including limited access to information and the political sensitivity of downgrading state-owned enterprises.

The report also does not fully explore the implications for the broader financial system. If a significant number of bonds are downgraded, banks that hold these bonds as collateral for lending may need to reassess their collateral valuations. This could have knock-on effects on the availability of credit, particularly for smaller companies that rely on bank lending rather than bond issuance. The interaction between the bond market and the banking system is complex, and a sudden repricing of credit risk in one channel could amplify stress in the other.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Chinese Credit Market During the Rating Transition

For investors who need to make decisions today, while the rating system is still in flux, a pragmatic framework is essential. The first step is to treat domestic credit ratings as a starting point, not a conclusion. Any bond that carries a AAA or AA+ rating from a Chinese domestic agency should be subjected to the same level of scrutiny that an international investor would apply to a much lower-rated bond. The rating should be seen as a signal that the issuer has passed a certain regulatory or procedural hurdle, not as an independent assessment of creditworthiness.

The second step is to focus on fundamental indicators that are harder to manipulate than ratings. The most important of these is the trend in profitability. If a company is rated AAA but its share of loss-making operations is rising, the rating is likely stale. Similarly, investors should examine cash flow generation, leverage ratios, and the quality of collateral. In the current environment, companies with strong cash flows and low leverage are likely to be genuinely creditworthy, regardless of their rating. Companies that are highly leveraged and dependent on refinancing are vulnerable, even if they carry a top rating.

The third step is to monitor regulatory developments closely. The PBoC's request for a revamp is a signal that change is coming, but the pace and direction of that change are uncertain. Investors should consider scenarios: a rapid and comprehensive reform that forces a sharp repricing of risk, a slow and incremental reform that allows the market to adjust gradually, or a reform that is announced but not effectively implemented. Each scenario has different implications for portfolio positioning. In the rapid reform scenario, holding highly rated bonds that are likely to be downgraded would be risky. In the slow reform scenario, the current distortions could persist for longer, and investors might be able to earn a premium by holding bonds that the market still treats as safe.

Finally, investors should prepare for the possibility that the reform process itself could trigger a crisis of confidence. If the market loses faith in the rating system before a new system is in place, the result could be a sudden and disorderly repricing of risk. This is the scenario that regulators are trying to avoid, but it cannot be ruled out. The safest position in such an environment is to favor the highest quality issuers, those with strong fundamentals that would survive even a severe downgrade cycle, and to maintain ample liquidity to take advantage of opportunities that may arise if the market overcorrects.

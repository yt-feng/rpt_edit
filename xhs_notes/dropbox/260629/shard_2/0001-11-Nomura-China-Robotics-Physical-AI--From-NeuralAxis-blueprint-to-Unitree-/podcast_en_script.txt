EN_A: So everyone is talking about physical AI, but a lot of people think it is still far from reality. Is that true?
EN_B: Actually, not really. A recent research report makes a very interesting point. It says the real bottleneck for physical AI is not scaling language model reasoning, but engineering a low-latency reflex layer.
EN_A: A reflex layer? That sounds like robots need to have instinctive reactions.
EN_B: Exactly. The report references a framework called NeuralAxis. It divides humanoid robot control into three layers: a reasoning layer, a coordination layer, and a reflex layer. The reflex layer can have latency as low as 40 milliseconds.
EN_A: 40 milliseconds? That means a robot can adjust its actions before we even notice what is happening?
EN_B: Right. Things like grip force control and ankle balancing can be handled locally by reflex processors distributed in the joints, hands, and feet, without going through a central brain.
EN_A: That sounds pretty groundbreaking. Where does this framework come from?
EN_B: It was proposed by a company called NXP, unveiled by their CEO at COMPUTEX 2026. But the report focuses on a company that has actually turned this blueprint into a product.
EN_A: Which company?
EN_B: Unitree. They released the WVLA2.0 model, which is the first commercially deployable version. It fuses the predictive power of a world model with the end-to-end action generation of VLA, rather than betting solely on VLA.
EN_A: Fusion? What does that mean in practice?
EN_B: It means it can both predict and generate actions. Its perception system uses four parallel visual streams, including a depth camera, a LiDAR, and side cameras, and it can update position within 10 milliseconds under interference.
EN_A: How does it perform in real tests?
EN_B: In one demo, a G1 robot equipped with WVLA2.0 autonomously completed six tasks in a disturbed meeting room without teleoperation. Each inference cycle took about 90 milliseconds, so roughly ten iterations per second.
EN_A: Ten iterations per second? That is getting close to human reflexes. But are there any limitations?
EN_B: The report points out several. There are blind spots, higher noise levels, imprecise fine manipulation, and a lack of quantified continuous success rate benchmarks.
EN_A: So how does Unitree plan to commercialize this?
EN_B: They are targeting industrial manufacturing as the earliest use case, things like joint motor assembly and loading. Then logistics sorting and flexible 3C assembly, and finally home and healthcare scenarios.
EN_A: Why is home last?
EN_B: Because open, unstructured environments are much harder to handle. The report also notes that Unitree's edge comes from full-stack in-house integration and real-machine data from its global fleet, which cloud-only model vendors cannot replicate.
EN_A: Are there any questions the report does not fully answer?
EN_B: One big one is how to define safety standards for the reflex layer. NeuralAxis provides a blueprint, but the actual failure modes and redundancy in deployment still need more validation.

The company has raised its data centre revenue outlook to more than $1 billion in 2026 and well above $2 billion in 2027. These figures are not aspirational. For a company that has historically been viewed as a lagging indicator of end-market demand, this forward visibility is unprecedented.

Yet the market remains sceptical. The stock trades at roughly 11x forward EV/EBITDA, a discount that reflects residual concern about gross margin headwinds from manufacturing reshaping and the pace of the industrial recovery. The tension between improving demand visibility and near-term margin compression defines the investment case today. The question is not whether STMicroelectronics is executing well on AI. The question is whether the structural uplift from data centres is large enough to offset the transitional costs of repositioning the manufacturing base.

The answer, based on the evidence from the second-quarter report, is that the data centre opportunity is not just large enough — it is accelerating faster than the company’s own prior guidance. And it is doing so at a moment when supply is tightening across the semiconductor value chain.

STMicroelectronics highlighted a clear acceleration in demand across multiple end markets, supported by distribution inventories that have now fallen below normal target levels. The book-to-bill ratio of nearly 2x overall indicates that order intake is running well ahead of shipments, a dynamic that typically precedes revenue acceleration.

The recovery is most evident in general-purpose microcontrollers and analog products, where point-of-sale trends have strengthened materially. Silicon carbide also continued to improve, with low-teens year-on-year growth, mid-30s sequential growth, and a book-to-bill well above one. This is notable because silicon carbide had been a source of investor concern earlier in the cycle, as competition intensified and pricing pressure emerged. Automotive is tracking ahead of prior expectations, with low-double-digit percentage growth.

The outlier is personal electronics, where management expects a negative second half year-on-year following a positive first half. The strategic takeaway is that the demand recovery is broadening. AI data centres are the primary growth driver, but the recovery in general-purpose microcontrollers, analog, and silicon carbide suggests that the inventory correction that weighed on the semiconductor industry through much of 2025 is now largely complete. ## Manufacturing Reshaping Is a Near-Term Headwind but a Long-Term Competitive Necessity

The most significant near-term risk to the earnings trajectory is the cost of the manufacturing reshaping programme. STMicroelectronics is transferring silicon production from 200mm to 300mm wafers and silicon carbide production from 150mm to 200mm wafers. The programme is not expected to be complete before the end of 2027. During the transition, gross margin will continue to be affected by costs related to technology transfers, product qualifications, and mask redesigns.

In the second quarter, gross margin included a roughly 60 basis point headwind from non-recurring costs associated with the reshaping programme, and management expects a similar impact through the remainder of the year. The company guided to a third-quarter non-GAAP gross margin of 37 percent at the midpoint, which represents a sequential improvement, and expects further progress in the fourth quarter as revenue scales. However, the pace of recovery will be moderated by continued underloading charges, including start-up costs at new fabs, particularly in China, as well as ongoing expenses linked to the manufacturing transfer programme.

The tension is clear. The reshaping programme is necessary to support future microcontroller and AI growth against a tightening supply backdrop. The Agrate 300mm fab is expected to reach full build-out before 2028 and begin supporting microcontroller growth once 90nm and 40nm technologies are qualified. The Crolles fab is set to ramp towards 15,000 wafers per week and expand further in line with AI data centre demand. In China, qualification of 40nm technology with a local manufacturing partner should increase available capacity for domestic industrial and optical transceiver customers.

These investments are long-dated and capital-intensive. They depress near-term gross margin and free cash flow. But they are also the foundation for the revenue growth that the company has guided to in 2026 and 2027. The question for investors is whether the market is adequately discounting the transitional costs or whether it is overestimating the duration and magnitude of the headwind.

The evidence from the second-quarter report suggests that the headwind is manageable. Management remains confident that gross margin will improve sequentially in the fourth quarter, even with the ongoing reshaping costs. The data centre revenue trajectory provides a clear line of sight to revenue growth that should absorb the underloading charges as utilisation rates increase. The risk is not that the reshaping programme fails. The risk is that the pace of gross margin recovery disappoints in the near term, creating a window of vulnerability for the stock.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine the Risk-Reward Profile

The STMicroelectronics investment case can be distilled into three questions that investors must answer to determine their conviction level.

First, how durable is the data centre revenue visibility? The company has stated that fiscal year 2026 data centre revenues are fully covered by backlog and fiscal year 2027 expectations are supported by existing customer engagements. This is a high level of forward coverage, but it is not a guarantee. The risk is that data centre operators could push out deployments or reallocate spending if macroeconomic conditions deteriorate. However, the current evidence points in the opposite direction. The book-to-bill ratio in optical interconnect is above 2x, indicating that demand is accelerating, not decelerating. The structural drivers of AI infrastructure spending — hyperscaler capital expenditure, the shift to 800G and 1.6T optics, and the scaling of silicon photonics — are multi-year trends that are unlikely to reverse in the near term.

Second, how quickly can gross margin recover to pre-reshaping levels? The reshaping programme is a multi-year process, and the headwinds will persist through 2027. However, the sequential improvement in third-quarter gross margin guidance suggests that the worst of the margin compression may be behind the company. The key variable is revenue growth. As data centre and industrial revenues scale, underloading charges will decline as a percentage of revenue, and the fixed cost base of the new fabs will be spread over a larger revenue base. If revenue growth meets or exceeds the company’s guidance, gross margin should recover steadily. If revenue growth disappoints, the margin recovery will be slower and the stock will remain range-bound.

Third, is the valuation discount justified? STMicroelectronics trades at roughly 11x forward EV/EBITDA, a discount to historical averages and to some semiconductor peers. The discount reflects the market’s scepticism about the pace of margin recovery and the sustainability of data centre demand. But the discount also creates an asymmetric risk-reward profile. If the data centre revenue trajectory materialises as guided and gross margin improves sequentially, the earnings power of the company in 2027 and 2028 is significantly higher than current consensus estimates imply. The risk is that the reshaping programme takes longer or costs more than expected, compressing margins for an extended period.

For investors with a 12- to 18-month time horizon, the risk-reward is balanced. The data centre opportunity is real and well-supported by backlog. The industrial and automotive recovery is broadening. The manufacturing reshaping is a necessary investment that will enable future growth. The valuation is not demanding. But the near-term margin headwinds and the execution risk associated with the fab transitions mean that the stock is unlikely to re-rate aggressively until there is clear evidence that gross margin is on a sustainable upward trajectory.

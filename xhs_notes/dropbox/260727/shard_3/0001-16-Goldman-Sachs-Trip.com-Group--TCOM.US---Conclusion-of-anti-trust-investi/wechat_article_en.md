# The Trip.com Antitrust Settlement Removes a Valuation Overhang, but the Real Story Is How Its Business Model Must Now Evolve

On July 25, China's State Administration for Market Regulation concluded a six-month investigation into Trip.com Group by imposing a RMB 5.179 billion penalty for what it deemed an abuse of dominant position in the country's online hotel booking market. The fine, calculated at 7.5% of the company's 2025 domestic revenue, is notably steeper than the 4% and 3% levied on Alibaba and Meituan in their respective antitrust cases. At first glance, the headline number looks punitive. But the stock has been trading at 11x forward earnings, a 30% discount to global peer Booking.com, and the company holds RMB 104 billion in liquidity. The penalty is absorbable. The more consequential question is what happens next to the operating model that generated those profits.

The settlement closes a regulatory chapter, but it opens a strategic one. Trip.com must now dismantle the two specific practices that the regulator identified as anti-competitive: exclusive partnerships with its "Premium Category" hotels and the enforcement of lowest-price guarantees across all platforms through technical tools like the Price Adjustment Assistant. These were not peripheral features of the business. They were central mechanisms through which Trip.com maintained its roughly 50% share of China's online travel booking gross merchandise value. Removing them forces a fundamental rethinking of how the company competes, how it monetizes its platform, and how it retains the hotel supply that made it indispensable to travelers.

Observers who view this as a one-time fine followed by a return to normalcy are missing the structural shift. The penalty is backward-looking. The business model adjustments are forward-looking, and they carry real implications for the domestic hotel segment that contributed approximately 41% of group EBIT in 2025.

## The Penalty Magnitude Signals Tougher Regulatory Standards for Platform Dominance, Not Just a One-Off Cost

The 7.5% fine rate is the most revealing number in the entire ruling. Alibaba paid 4% of its domestic revenue in 2021. Meituan paid 3% later that same year. Trip.com is paying nearly double the highest previous rate. This is not an accident or a rounding error. It reflects a regulatory judgment that the abuse was not incidental but structural, and that the practices had been in place since 2020, spanning a period when Chinese regulators were already signaling heightened scrutiny of platform companies.

The penalty also includes confiscation of RMB 1.658 billion in gains deemed to have been generated by the anti-competitive practices, plus a full refund of RMB 122 million in deposits withheld from hotel operators. The total package of roughly RMB 5.3 billion is manageable against Trip.com's balance sheet, but it sets a precedent. Future platform companies operating at similar levels of market concentration should expect fines calibrated to the upper end of the legal range, not the middle. The cost of regulatory risk for dominant Chinese internet platforms has permanently increased.

For Trip.com specifically, the penalty removes the single largest overhang on the stock. The investigation was announced in January, and the market has been pricing in uncertainty ever since. The stock is at trough valuation. With the fine quantified and the investigation concluded, the uncertainty discount can begin to unwind. But the fine is only the price of admission to the next phase. The real work lies in reconfiguring the platform.

## The Two Banned Practices Were the Engine of Trip.com's Domestic Hotel Dominance, and Their Removal Reshapes the Competitive Landscape

The regulator identified two distinct but interrelated practices. First, Trip.com required hotels in its "Premium Category" to enter exclusive partnerships, offering maximum traffic allocation and benefit support in exchange for not listing on competing platforms. Second, it forced hotels in its "Golden Category" and unbranded tiers to offer the lowest prices on Trip.com, using automated tools to adjust prices without hotel consent and enforcing compliance through traffic restrictions, delisting, and deposit withholding.

These practices worked in tandem. The exclusivity agreements locked up supply. The price parity clauses ensured that Trip.com always had the best available rate. Together, they created a self-reinforcing moat. Hotels that wanted access to Trip.com's massive user base had to accept both conditions. Competing platforms could not match the inventory depth or the pricing, which in turn made Trip.com even more indispensable to hotels. The network effects were challenging for competitors and beneficial for Trip.com.

Trip.com has stated it will phase out the "Premium Category" entirely, cancel all related cooperative agreements and traffic arrangements, and design a new merchant tiered cooperation model. It will also terminate the "Golden Category" program, deactivate the Price Adjustment Assistant and the GuaPaiTong feature, and stop adjusting prices without hotel consent. These are not cosmetic changes. They strike at the core of how Trip.com managed its hotel supply chain.

The immediate implication is that competing platforms, particularly Tongcheng Travel and Meituan's hotel booking service, will gain access to supply that was previously locked up. Hotels that were restricted to Trip.com can now multi-home. The question is how many will choose to do so. Trip.com still has the largest user base, the best search and booking experience, and the most sophisticated data infrastructure. But it will now have to earn hotel loyalty through service quality and value rather than contractual exclusivity. That is a fundamentally different competitive posture.

## The Domestic Hotel EBIT Contribution Faces Structural Pressure, Even If the Revenue Impact Is Delayed

Trip.com's domestic hotel segment generated RMB 7.4 billion in EBIT in 2025, representing a 40.9% margin. That margin is the highest among its domestic business lines, well above transportation at 25.9% and packaged tours at 31.1%. The margin reflects the pricing power that came with exclusivity and price parity. Hotels had limited alternatives, so Trip.com could extract higher take rates.

That margin is now at risk. Not overnight, but over the next 12 to 18 months. The removal of exclusivity means hotels can negotiate with multiple platforms, which will put downward pressure on take rates. The elimination of price parity clauses means hotels can offer lower rates on other channels, which will reduce Trip.com's conversion advantage. The company will need to invest more in traffic acquisition, merchant services, and technology to retain supply, all of which will compress margins.

The report's own estimates show domestic hotel EBIT growing only 2% in 2026, to RMB 7.55 billion, compared to 12% growth in 2027. That flat trajectory in the near term reflects the anticipated disruption. The margin is expected to decline from 40.9% to 38.7% in 2026 before recovering to 40.8% in 2027. That recovery assumes the new model stabilizes, but it is not guaranteed. If competition intensifies more than expected, the margin compression could be deeper and longer.

Observers should watch three leading indicators in the coming quarters: take rates on domestic hotel bookings, hotel churn rates to competing platforms, and the pace of new merchant sign-ups under the revised tiered model. If take rates remain steady and churn remains low, the transition will be orderly. If either metric moves in the wrong direction, the margin risk is real.

## The Settlement Creates a Decision Framework for Observers Evaluating Chinese Platform Companies Post-Investigation

The Trip.com case, combined with the Alibaba and Meituan precedents, now provides a reasonably clear playbook for how Chinese regulatory investigations conclude and what they mean for observers. The framework has four stages.

Stage one is the investigation announcement. The stock sells off on uncertainty, and the valuation multiple contracts. In Trip.com's case, the stock went to 11x forward earnings, a 30% discount to its global peer. The duration of this stage varies, but six months appears to be the typical investigation period based on these three cases.

Stage two is the settlement announcement. The fine is quantified, the practices are identified, and the remediation plan is specified. The stock typically rallies. Alibaba rose 8% on the day of its settlement. Meituan rose 9%. The magnitude of the rally depends on how much uncertainty was priced in and how manageable the fine appears relative to the company's cash position.

Stage three is the business model transition. This is the longest and most uncertain phase. The company must implement the remediation measures, which often involve dismantling the very practices that generated its competitive advantage. Revenue growth may slow, margins may compress, and market share may shift. This phase can last 12 to 24 months. Alibaba's commerce business faced increased competition from PDD and Douyin after its antitrust settlement. Meituan's local services margins came under pressure as it adjusted merchant terms.

Stage four is the new equilibrium. The company stabilizes at a lower but sustainable growth rate, with a new valuation multiple that reflects the reduced regulatory risk and the altered competitive dynamics. In Alibaba's case, the stock never returned to its pre-investigation highs, but it found a floor. In Meituan's case, the stock recovered more fully because its core business model proved more resilient.

Trip.com is entering stage three. The key variable is how quickly and effectively it can transition from a model based on exclusivity and price control to one based on platform quality, merchant services, and user experience. The company has already taken the first step by ceasing the banned practices in January, before the settlement was announced. That proactive move suggests management understands the direction of travel. But implementation is everything.

Trip.com's domestic hotel business faces headwinds, but the company has two other engines that can partially offset the drag. The international segment, while still loss-making at the EBIT level, is narrowing its losses. Trip.com, the company's international brand, is projected to reduce its EBIT loss from RMB 1.26 billion in 2025 to RMB 945 million in 2026, with margins improving from negative 14% to negative 7%.

The outbound business is particularly important because it is less exposed to the regulatory dynamics that govern the domestic market. Chinese travelers booking international hotels are not subject to the same exclusivity or price parity concerns. The competitive landscape is more fragmented, and Trip.com's advantage comes from its user base, payment integration, and customer service rather than contractual lock-in.

The pure international business, including Skyscanner and Trip.com, is still small relative to the domestic operation. Combined, they contributed negative 2% of group EBIT in 2025. But their trajectory is improving. Skyscanner generates stable 20% margins. Trip.com is on track to reach breakeven by 2027. If these segments scale as projected, they will provide a meaningful diversification benefit and reduce the company's dependence on the domestic regulatory environment.

The valuation case for Trip.com ultimately rests on whether the growth in outbound and international can compensate for the margin compression in domestic hotels. At 11x forward earnings, the market is pricing in significant disruption. If the transition is managed well, the multiple should expand toward the 16x that the report uses in its sum-of-the-parts valuation. If the transition is messy, the stock could remain range-bound until the new equilibrium becomes visible.

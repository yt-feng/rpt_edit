# Germany Is the Only Force Driving Eurozone Fiscal Expansion in 2026, and That Concentration Is a Strategic Risk

The Eurozone is about to experience its first coordinated fiscal expansion since 2021. But this is not a story of collective European resolve. The newly submitted annual progress reports to the European Commission reveal a stark reality: the entire 2026 fiscal easing of 0.2% of GDP in cyclically adjusted terms is attributable to Germany alone. Without Berlin's ambitious spending package, the Eurozone would face a mildly contractionary fiscal stance. France, Italy, and Spain are all tightening. This concentration of fiscal firepower in one economy creates a fragile macro backdrop where the entire region's growth outlook hinges on German budget execution, political commitment, and administrative capacity. For investors and corporate strategists, the question is not whether Europe is stimulating—it is whether Germany can deliver.

## Why This Matters Now

The timing of this fiscal shift is critical. The Eurozone headline deficit is set to rise from 2.9% of GDP in 2025 to 3.5% in 2026, the first increase in three years. This reverses a period of gradual consolidation that followed the pandemic-era spending surge. The expenditure-to-GDP ratio is projected to reach 50.6%, the highest since 2021. Yet revenues are only expected to rise by 0.3 percentage points to 47.1% of GDP. The gap is widening, and the composition of spending matters more than the aggregate.

The macro context adds urgency. The Eurozone is navigating the tail end of an energy shock, elevated inflation in services, and a manufacturing sector that has struggled to regain momentum. Fiscal policy now becomes the primary lever for demand management, given that the European Central Bank is likely to remain cautious on further rate moves. But if that fiscal lever is concentrated in one country, the transmission mechanism becomes uneven, and the region's resilience depends on assumptions that may not hold.

## The 2026 Budget Plans Reveal a Two-Speed Eurozone in Fiscal Intent

Eleven of the twenty-one Eurozone countries project rising deficits in 2026. Nine expect lower deficits. One expects no change. But this binary split masks the real story: the magnitude of divergence is extreme. Germany's deficit is set to rise by 1.6 percentage points to 4.3% of GDP. Estonia projects a 2.3 percentage point increase to 4.3%. Meanwhile, France is planning a 0.1 percentage point reduction to 5%, Italy a 0.2 point cut to 2.9%, and Spain a 0.3 point decline to 2.1%.

The implication is clear. Countries under Excessive Deficit Procedures are tightening. Countries with fiscal space or strategic imperatives are spending. But the Eurozone aggregate is being pulled upward almost entirely by Germany. If one removes Germany from the calculation, the Eurozone deficit would remain unchanged at 2.9% of GDP. This is not a region-wide stimulus. It is a German stimulus with a European label.

The government debt ratio adds another layer of concern. According to the annual progress reports, Eurozone debt is set to rise from 87.8% of GDP in 2025 to 91.3% in 2026. That is a sharp increase in a single year, and it reflects both higher deficits and the compounding effect of debt dynamics when nominal GDP growth moderates. The debt trajectory is now upward, not stabilizing.

## The Fiscal Impulse Is Entirely Dependent on German Execution, and Execution Is Not Assured

The cyclically adjusted primary balance is the cleanest measure of discretionary fiscal policy. It strips out the effects of the economic cycle and interest payments. The annual progress reports show a Eurozone fiscal easing of 0.2% of GDP in 2026, the first easing since 2021. But the contribution breakdown is revealing. Germany contributes 0.5 percentage points to the Eurozone impulse. France, Italy, and Spain each deduct 0.1 percentage points. The rest of the Eurozone is broadly neutral.

This means that if Germany fails to execute its planned spending—whether due to political gridlock, administrative bottlenecks, or a shift in priorities—the Eurozone fiscal impulse turns negative. The report's own analysis confirms this: if the German cyclically adjusted primary balance remained unchanged, the overall Eurozone impulse would be slightly contractionary.

The report references a German fiscal stimulus tracker that shows a sharp rise in federal spending in the first quarter, driven by defense. That is encouraging, but it is early. The full-year budget execution requires sustained commitment across multiple spending categories, including infrastructure. The German fiscal package is large, but large packages are also complex to implement. Procurement delays, legal challenges, and capacity constraints in the construction sector are all real risks.

Assuming a fiscal multiplier of 0.9, the budget plans imply that fiscal policy will lift Eurozone GDP growth by approximately 20 basis points in 2026. The report's own forecast is 30 basis points, based on slightly different assumptions about discretionary tightening in France, Italy, and Spain. That 10-basis-point gap is small in absolute terms but meaningful for an economy growing near potential. The difference between 20 and 30 basis points could determine whether the Eurozone grows above or below trend.

## Recovery and Resilience Facility Grants Add Modest Support, but Timing and Absorption Are Uncertain

The RRF grants from the NextGenerationEU program do not affect member state budget balances, so they must be analyzed separately. As of the first quarter of 2026, 69% of all scheduled disbursements through end-2026 have occurred. The report expects grant disbursements to accelerate slightly from 0.2% of EU GDP in 2025 to 0.3% in 2026, adding approximately 0.1 percentage points of stimulus on top of the national budget effect.

That is helpful but not transformative. Moreover, the RRF is a finite program with a hard end date. Some disbursements are likely to slip into 2027, either because of administrative delays or because payments made late in 2026 will effectively function as 2027 fiscal support. The report acknowledges this possibility. The implication is that the fiscal boost from RRF grants is both modest and uncertain in its timing.

For a region that needs sustained investment in digital transformation, energy security, and defense, the RRF is a one-time instrument. It cannot substitute for a durable fiscal framework that allows counter-cyclical spending without triggering debt sustainability concerns. The reformed EU fiscal framework is supposed to provide that, but the early evidence suggests it is not yet functioning as intended.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of German Fiscal Expansion and the Political Economy of Adjustment

The report is meticulous in its aggregation and decomposition of fiscal plans. But it leaves several critical questions unanswered, and these are the questions that matter most for strategic decision-making.

First, the report does not address the political sustainability of German fiscal expansion. The current government has committed to a large package, but German coalition politics are fragile. A change in government or a shift in public opinion could alter spending priorities. Defense spending may be politically sacrosanct, but infrastructure spending is more vulnerable to delays and reallocations. The report provides a tracker for federal spending but does not model political risk.

Second, the report does not analyze the second-order effects of asymmetric fiscal expansion within a monetary union. If Germany stimulates while France and Italy tighten, the result is not just a difference in growth rates. It creates divergent inflation dynamics, divergent bond yields, and divergent credit conditions. The European Central Bank cannot set policy to suit both a booming Germany and a consolidating Italy. The report quantifies the fiscal impulse but does not explore the monetary-fiscal interaction.

Third, the report does not assess the quality of spending. A fiscal expansion driven by defense procurement has different multiplier effects than one driven by infrastructure or social transfers. Defense spending tends to have a lower multiplier and a higher import content. Infrastructure spending has a higher multiplier but longer implementation lags. The report uses an average multiplier of 0.9 but acknowledges a range of 0.7 for defense and 1.0 for infrastructure. The composition of German spending will determine whether the actual growth impact is closer to 20 or 30 basis points.

Fourth, the report does not address the risk of fiscal dominance. With Eurozone debt rising to 91.3% of GDP, and with several countries still running deficits above 3%, the room for further discretionary easing is limited. If a negative shock materializes—a new energy crisis, a trade disruption, a financial accident—the fiscal space to respond is narrower than it appears. The report documents the deficit trajectory but does not stress-test it against plausible adverse scenarios.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Corporate Strategists

The report provides the raw material for a structured decision framework. Based on the analysis, here are the key questions that any serious investor or corporate planner should ask before making Eurozone-related decisions.

First, assess German execution risk. The entire Eurozone fiscal outlook depends on Germany delivering its planned spending increase. Track the monthly federal spending data. Monitor procurement announcements. Watch for political signals from the coalition. If German spending falls short by even 0.2% of GDP, the Eurozone fiscal impulse disappears. This is the single most important variable.

Second, evaluate the tightening plans in France, Italy, and Spain. The report assumes these countries will tighten by 0.1 percentage points each. But the report also notes that its own forecast assumes less discretionary tightening than the published plans. The gap between announced plans and actual outcomes is where surprises occur. If France or Italy tighten more than expected, the drag on Eurozone growth increases. If they tighten less, the growth outlook improves. Monitor the European Commission's Spring Package in June for updated country-specific recommendations, including decisions on Excessive Deficit Procedures.

Third, calculate your exposure to the energy-shock-related fiscal measures. The report includes a detailed table showing that the fiscal response to the energy shock in 2026 is modest, averaging only 0.09% of Eurozone GDP, compared to 3.1% in 2022/23. The support is concentrated in a few countries: Croatia, Greece, Latvia, Slovenia, and Spain. If your business or investment portfolio is exposed to energy-intensive sectors in those countries, the withdrawal of support could create headwinds. If you are in Germany or France, the energy support is negligible and already priced in.

Fourth, consider the RRF grant trajectory. The report shows that 69% of scheduled disbursements have occurred. The remaining 31% will be distributed over the next nine months. Companies that are positioned to benefit from RRF-funded projects—digital infrastructure, green energy, transportation—should monitor the disbursement pace. A slowdown in RRF spending would offset some of the national budget stimulus.

Fifth, watch the debt dynamics. The Eurozone debt ratio is rising, and that has implications for bond markets. Countries with high debt and rising deficits, such as France and Belgium, may face renewed scrutiny from rating agencies and investors. The report does not model spread risk, but the data on debt and deficits is clear. The number of Eurozone countries with budget deficits above 3% of GDP is set to rise from eight in 2025 to nine in 2026. That is the highest since 2020. The era of easy fiscal conditions is ending, and the adjustment will not be painless.

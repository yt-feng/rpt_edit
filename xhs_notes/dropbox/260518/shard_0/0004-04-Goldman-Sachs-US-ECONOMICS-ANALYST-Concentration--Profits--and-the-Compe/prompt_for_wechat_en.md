# The Long-Run Rise in Corporate Concentration

New data spanning nearly a century of tax and administrative records document a long-run rise in corporate concentration across the US and other advanced economies. Drawing on the IRS Statistics of Income data for the US and on VAT and sales tax records, corporate tax filings, and company registries for other countries, economist Yueran Ma and co-authors have constructed sales, asset, equity capital, and net income shares of top firms as standardized cross-country measures of concentration. $^{1}$

As shown in Exhibit 2, the sales share of the top 1% of US firms rose from roughly 60% in the 1960s-70s to around 80% in recent years. Across Austria, Denmark, France, Germany, and Switzerland, the comparable figure increased from a similar starting point to roughly 70% over the same period. Alternative concentration measures from the same dataset—including top firms’ equity capital and net income shares—show a similar upward trend in a broader group of advanced economies, including Australia, Canada, Singapore, South Korea, and the UK.

Three main hypotheses have been proposed to explain rising corporate concentration.

The first points to globalization and trade. As globalization has allowed firms to access more efficient supply chains, larger pools of capital and labor, and broader product markets, these benefits have likely accrued disproportionately to a small share of larger, more productive firms. At the same time, greater trade openness has intensified competition through the entry of foreign firms into domestic markets, complicating the net effect on concentration. $^{2}$ Using a panel of advanced economies in 1960–2022, we find little evidence that increases in trade openness—proxied by the ratio of total exports and imports to GDP—have been associated with larger increases in concentration.

The second hypothesis points to weaker antitrust enforcement and higher regulatory barriers to entry. For example, economists Germán Gutiérrez and Thomas Philippon have argued that the rise in occupational licensing requirements has raised the fixed costs of starting new businesses, deterring entry by competitors. $^{3}$ While this channel has plausibly played some role, the evidence on its quantitative importance remains inconclusive. Using the Department of Justice Antitrust Division's budget as a proxy for enforcement intensity, we find that concentration has risen at a broadly similar pace across different US antitrust regimes since the 1960s (Exhibit 4). Evidence from Europe also suggests that while stricter merger enforcement is associated with lower concentration, this relationship has weakened over time. $^{4}$

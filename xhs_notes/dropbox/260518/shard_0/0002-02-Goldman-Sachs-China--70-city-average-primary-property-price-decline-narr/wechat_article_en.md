# China's Property Market Is Not Stabilizing: The Primary Market Data Mask a Deeper, More Fragmented Correction

The April 2024 housing data from China's National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) has been widely interpreted as a sign that the worst of the property downturn is over. The headline number appears supportive: the weighted average price decline in the primary market narrowed from a 4.0% month-on-month annualized drop in March to a 3.0% decline in April. Tier-1 cities, led by Shanghai, even posted sequential price gains. For investors watching for a floor, this looks like the first credible signal of stabilization in months.

That interpretation is dangerously incomplete. The NBS 70-city data, which tracks only new home transactions in the primary market, tells a story that diverges sharply from what is actually happening in the broader housing ecosystem. Secondary market data from the NBS and third-party platforms indicate price declines of 5% to 15% over the past year, with the gap between primary and secondary pricing continuing to widen. The primary market is not the real market. It is a controlled, policy-managed segment where developers and local governments can influence transaction prices through project selection, sales restrictions, and preferential financing. The secondary market, where genuine price discovery occurs, is telling a far more sobering story.

This divergence matters now more than ever because it reveals something structural about the nature of China's property correction. The market is not experiencing a uniform downturn that will resolve with a single inflection point. Instead, it is fragmenting along three axes: by city tier, by market segment (primary versus secondary), and by the quality of individual assets. Investors who focus only on the improving primary average will miss the fact that the correction is deepening in the segments that matter most for long-term portfolio exposure.

The argument of this article is straightforward: the narrowing of the primary market decline is a misleading signal of stabilization. The real story is one of increasing fragmentation, where a small number of premium assets in top-tier cities are masking widespread weakness elsewhere, and where policy intervention is becoming the primary driver of pricing rather than genuine demand recovery. Understanding this fragmentation is the only way to build a defensible investment posture in Chinese real estate today.

## The Primary Market Improvement Is a Policy Artifact, Not a Demand Signal

The improvement in the 70-city weighted average from a 4.0% to a 3.0% month-on-month annualized decline is real in the data, but its composition reveals that it is not a broad-based recovery. Tier-1 cities posted a 0.4% sequential increase, with Shanghai surging to a 2.0% month-on-month annualized gain after a 0.9% decline in March. Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities continued to decline, though at a slightly slower pace: 2.8% annualized for Tier-2 and 4.6% for Tier-3, compared with 4.6% and 5.0% respectively in March.

The critical question is why Shanghai's primary market is strengthening while the rest of the country remains under pressure. The answer lies in policy concentration. Shanghai has implemented targeted easing measures that include relaxed purchase restrictions in specific districts and higher Housing Provident Fund loan caps. These measures are designed to channel demand into the primary market for new developments, where local governments have the most direct interest in supporting land sale revenues. The result is a controlled price environment where transaction volumes and prices are being managed upward through administrative means, not through organic demand recovery.

This is not a sustainable foundation for a market turn. When price improvements are driven by policy rather than by income growth, population inflows, or credit expansion, they are vulnerable to reversal the moment policy support is withdrawn or exhausted. The sequential improvement in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities is even more tenuous. A narrowing from a 4.6% decline to a 2.8% decline in Tier-2 cities is statistically meaningful but economically marginal. It does not signal a change in trend; it signals a moderation in the rate of decline, which is a very different thing.

Investors should also note that the primary market data excludes the vast majority of housing transactions in China. The secondary market, which accounts for a growing share of total turnover as new supply slows, is where real pricing power is tested. And the secondary market is not improving.

## The Secondary Market Is the Canary, and It Is Still Singing a Warning

The NBS data on secondary market prices, alongside third-party indices from platforms such as Centaline, Beike, and Zhuge, paint a picture of continuing and in some cases accelerating weakness. The report notes that secondary market price declines range from 5% to 15% over the past year, a range that is both wide and alarming. The width of the range itself is a signal: price discovery in the secondary market is fragmented, with significant variation by city, district, and even within individual neighborhoods.

What makes the secondary market data more concerning than the primary market data is that secondary transactions are largely free of the administrative distortions that affect new home sales. Sellers in the secondary market are individuals, not developers with balance sheet pressures and government relationships. Their pricing decisions reflect genuine supply-demand dynamics: willingness to sell at a given price, urgency to liquidate, and competition from other sellers in the same complex. When secondary prices are falling at 5% to 15% annually, it means that homeowners are absorbing losses that developers have been able to avoid, at least temporarily, through controlled primary market pricing.

The divergence between primary and secondary pricing creates a structural problem for the market. Rational buyers will compare the price of a new development with the price of an existing unit in the same area. When secondary prices are significantly lower, as they are now, the incentive shifts toward the secondary market, which in turn puts additional downward pressure on primary market sales volumes. Developers are forced to choose between maintaining nominal prices and watching inventory accumulate, or cutting prices and acknowledging the losses that secondary market data already reflect. The longer this divergence persists, the more likely it is that primary prices will eventually have to adjust downward to align with secondary market reality.

The fact that the share of cities with sequentially higher property prices edged down in the secondary market in April, even as primary market data improved, is the most telling indicator in the entire report. It confirms that the two markets are moving in opposite directions. Any stabilization narrative that relies on primary market data alone is fundamentally incomplete.

## City-Tier Fragmentation Is Becoming a Permanent Structural Feature, Not a Cyclical Phase

The performance gap between Tier-1 cities and the rest of the country has been a consistent feature of China's property market for years, but the current cycle is deepening this divide in ways that may prove irreversible. In April, Tier-1 cities posted a sequential price increase while Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities continued to decline. This is not a temporary divergence that will close as the cycle matures. It reflects a fundamental reallocation of economic activity, population, and policy resources toward the largest cities.

Shanghai's 2.0% month-on-month annualized gain is particularly instructive. Shanghai benefits from a unique combination of factors: it is the financial center of China, it has the most restrictive household registration (hukou) system, which limits supply of new entrants while concentrating demand, and it has the deepest pool of high-net-worth buyers who are less sensitive to credit conditions. These factors make Shanghai's property market structurally different from even other Tier-1 cities like Guangzhou and Shenzhen, which also extended easing measures in April but did not see comparable price gains.

The implication for investors is that China's property market can no longer be analyzed as a single asset class. The risk-return profile of real estate exposure in Shanghai is fundamentally different from exposure in a Tier-2 city like Wuhan or a Tier-3 city like Wenzhou. This is not a new insight, but the current cycle is making it more extreme. The report notes that inventory months decreased to 28.9 in May from 29.3 in April, with the decrease mostly led by Tier-2 cities. That improvement in inventory is concentrated in a specific segment and does not reflect a national trend.

What the report does not fully address, and what remains an open analytical question, is whether this city-tier fragmentation is a self-reinforcing process. If Tier-1 cities continue to attract the best jobs, the best infrastructure investment, and the most favorable policy treatment, then their property markets may sustain a premium that is not merely cyclical but structural. Conversely, Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities may face a permanent discount that reflects their lower growth trajectory and higher supply relative to demand. This would represent a fundamental shift from the previous cycle, when rising tides lifted all cities, to a new regime where only the top tier retains pricing power.

## The Report's Unanswered Question: Can Policy Intervention Prevent a Secondary Market Contagion?

The most significant gap in the analysis presented in the report is the question of whether policy intervention can prevent the weakness in the secondary market from spreading back into the primary market. The report documents the divergence between the two markets but does not fully explore the mechanisms through which they interact.

There are three channels through which secondary market weakness could contaminate the primary market. First, the wealth effect: when homeowners see the value of their existing properties declining, they feel poorer and are less willing to make new purchases, particularly of higher-priced new developments. Second, the inventory channel: if secondary market prices are lower, buyers will gravitate toward existing homes, reducing demand for new homes and increasing developer inventory. Third, the financing channel: banks that hold mortgage portfolios backed by declining secondary market collateral may tighten lending standards, reducing the availability of credit for all housing transactions.

The policy response to date has focused on the primary market: relaxing purchase restrictions, raising loan caps, and encouraging developer sales. These measures have had some success in stabilizing primary market prices in select cities. But they have done little to address the underlying weakness in the secondary market, which is driven by broader economic factors: slowing income growth, high household leverage, and a shift in consumer preferences away from real estate as an investment asset.

The open question is whether secondary market prices have further to fall before they reach a level that attracts genuine demand, or whether the current declines are already pricing in a lower equilibrium. The report's data show that secondary prices in some third-party indices have declined by 15% or more over the past year. At some point, lower prices should attract buyers, particularly if mortgage rates remain favorable and alternative investment returns are low. But the timing of that inflection point is uncertain, and it could be delayed if sellers continue to hold out for higher prices or if buyers expect further declines.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Navigate the Fragmented Correction

For investors trying to build a position in Chinese real estate or in assets exposed to the sector, the report's findings suggest a framework that prioritizes granularity over aggregation. The days of making a single bet on "China housing" are over. The market has bifurcated to the point where a national average is not just unhelpful but actively misleading.

The first decision is city tier. Tier-1 cities, particularly Shanghai, offer a different risk profile from the rest of the country. Exposure to Tier-1 primary market assets may still generate positive returns, but only if the investor has conviction that policy support will remain in place and that demand from high-net-worth buyers will sustain pricing. For Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities, the risk of further price declines remains significant, and any investment thesis must be built on a specific project-level analysis rather than a city-level assumption.

The second decision is market segment. The primary market is more susceptible to policy intervention but also more opaque. Secondary market pricing is more transparent and more reflective of genuine demand, but it is also more volatile. An investor who wants to understand the true state of the market should focus on secondary market data, even though it is more fragmented and harder to aggregate. The primary market data should be treated as a policy signal rather than a market signal.

The third decision is time horizon. The current divergence between primary and secondary markets cannot persist indefinitely. At some point, either secondary prices will recover as buyers step in, or primary prices will fall to align with secondary levels. The direction of that convergence will determine whether the current period is a buying opportunity or a pause before further declines. Investors should watch secondary market transaction volumes as a leading indicator. If volumes pick up at lower prices, it suggests that a floor is being established. If volumes remain low despite price declines, it suggests that the correction still has further to run.

The report provides the data to ask these questions. It does not provide the answers, and no single report can. The answers will emerge from the interaction of policy, economic growth, and household behavior over the coming quarters. The framework above is designed to help investors identify which signals matter and which can be safely ignored.

## Why This Fragmentation Creates the Most Important Analytical Gap in the Report

The report's most valuable contribution is also its most frustrating limitation. It documents the divergence between primary and secondary markets with precision, but it does not fully explore the implications of that divergence for the financial system. The report notes that secondary market data from various platforms show price declines of 5% to 15%, but it does not analyze what those declines mean for bank balance sheets, for developer solvency, or for household wealth.

This is not a criticism of the report's authors, who are constrained by the data available. The NBS 70-city data are the standard benchmark, and the report does an excellent job of interpreting them. But the gap between the data that are widely available and the data that are most relevant for investment decisions is growing. The secondary market is where the real action is, and it is the least well understood.

Investors who want to build a defensible view of China's property market need access to more granular data: transaction-level pricing from secondary platforms, inventory data by district rather than by city, and delinquency data on developer bonds and bank mortgages. The report points in the direction of these data sources but does not provide them. That is the natural limit of a research report based on official statistics.

For readers who want to go beyond the headline numbers and examine the original charts, the transaction-level data, and the full analytical framework, the full report is essential reading. The charts alone tell a story that the text can only summarize. The divergence between the NBS 70-city index and the third-party indices is visible in the data, and it is the single most important visual in the entire analysis.

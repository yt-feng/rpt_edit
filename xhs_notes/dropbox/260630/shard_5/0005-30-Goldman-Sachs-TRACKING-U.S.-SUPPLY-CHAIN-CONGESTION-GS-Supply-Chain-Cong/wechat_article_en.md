# The U.S. Supply Chain Is No Longer in Crisis, but the Risk of a New Bottleneck Regime Is Rising

The headline numbers are reassuring. A global investment bank’s weekly supply chain congestion index declined seven percent week over week in late June 2026, and its bottleneck scale remains at two on a ten-point scale—a level consistent with pre-COVID fluidity. Container ship backlogs on the West Coast stayed flat at one vessel waiting to dock. Rail intermodal traffic on the West Coast accelerated to fourteen percent year-over-year growth. On the surface, the logistics network appears to have normalized after years of disruption.

But the data beneath the surface tells a more complex story. East Coast container ship backlogs ticked up from three to four. Chassis dwell times increased slightly across multiple container sizes. Ocean shipping rates from China to the U.S. West Coast jumped nineteen percent week over week. These are not crisis-level readings, but they are signals that the system is not yet in equilibrium. The supply chain is calm, not stable.

The critical question for executives, investors, and policymakers is no longer whether congestion will return to pandemic-era peaks. It is whether the current state of fluidity is durable or whether we are entering a new regime of chronic, lower-level friction that will reshape logistics strategy permanently. The evidence from this latest data release suggests that the answer depends less on the absolute level of congestion and more on the structural forces that are beginning to reassert themselves.

The post-pandemic normalization was driven by a collapse in demand for goods and a rapid unwinding of inventory hoarding. That phase is largely complete. What comes next is a period in which tariffs, geopolitical fragmentation, and shifting manufacturing footprints create persistent, localized stress points that a two-point index may not fully capture. The report’s own data hints at this transition. The challenge for decision-makers is to distinguish between noise and signal in a system that has become structurally more volatile.

## The Composite Index Masks a Divergence Between Coasts and Modes That Demands a More Granular View

The aggregate congestion index is useful for a macro read, but it obscures a pattern that has strategic implications. The West Coast is operating at near-normal fluidity. The number of container ships waiting to dock at Los Angeles and Long Beach has been stuck at one for weeks. Rail intermodal traffic is growing at double-digit rates, and velocity is improving. The West Coast has effectively healed.

The East Coast and Gulf Coast are not healing at the same pace. The backlog of container ships in those regions rose from three to four in the latest week—a small absolute number, but a meaningful directional shift. This divergence matters because it reflects a structural re-routing of trade flows. Shippers have been diversifying away from the West Coast for years, driven by labor disputes, drought-related canal disruptions, and a desire to reduce single-point-of-failure risk. The data now suggests that this re-routing is creating its own congestion patterns on the alternative routes.

The rail data reinforces this picture. West Coast intermodal traffic is accelerating, but rail service metrics are mixed. Union Pacific terminal dwell increased slightly. BNSF dwell also rose. Intermodal train speed on both carriers remains negative year over year. This is not a sign of a system under severe stress, but it is a sign that the rail network is absorbing increased volume with less slack than it had during the trough of the goods recession. The combination of rising volume and mixed service quality is a classic precursor to localized bottlenecks.

The implication is clear: a single national index is insufficient for operational or investment decisions. The relevant question is not whether the U.S. supply chain is congested, but where, in which mode, and at what velocity the friction is building. The West Coast may be approaching a new baseline. The East Coast may be entering a period of chronic, moderate congestion driven by its own success as an alternative gateway.

## Ocean Shipping Rates Are Signaling a Tightening Market That Could Precede a Broader Inflection

The most striking single data point in this report is the nineteen percent week-over-week increase in ocean container shipping rates from East Asia to the U.S. West Coast. On a year-over-year basis, rates are now up three percent—a sharp reversal from the negative nineteen percent reading in the prior week. This is not yet a trend, but it is a signal that the market for container shipping is tightening.

The report notes that rates remain well below pandemic peaks, and that is true. But the rate cycle in ocean shipping is notoriously volatile and tends to overshoot in both directions. The current level of approximately $5,740 per forty-foot equivalent unit is not alarming in absolute terms. The velocity of the change, however, is worth watching closely. A nineteen percent weekly increase in a market that had been in deflation suggests that capacity is being absorbed faster than the market had anticipated.

The mechanism is not hard to identify. The West Coast rail intermodal network is moving more volume, and the East Coast is seeing higher ship backlogs. When both coasts are under pressure simultaneously, the supply of available vessel capacity shrinks, and rates respond. The report’s own data on door-to-door shipping times from China to the U.S. shows a stable reading of 47 days, which is close to pre-pandemic norms. But stable transit times combined with rising rates is an unusual combination. It typically indicates that the market is moving from a period of excess capacity to one of balanced supply and demand, and possibly toward tightness.

For importers and logistics managers, this is the moment to reassess contracting strategies. The era of abundant, cheap ocean capacity may be ending. The question is whether this is a temporary spike driven by seasonal restocking or the beginning of a more sustained repricing. The report does not answer this question definitively, but the data suggests that the risk is skewed toward the latter.

## The Report’s Framework Is Powerful but Cannot Yet Answer Whether the Current Fluidity Is Structural or Cyclical

The investment bank’s supply chain congestion scale is a sophisticated tool. It aggregates weekly and monthly metrics across vessel backlogs, rail performance, chassis utilization, shipping rates, and labor market data to produce a single, comparable measure of fluidity. The methodology is transparent, the data sources are credible, and the historical series allows for meaningful comparisons to the pandemic era.

But the framework has a limitation that is becoming more important as the system normalizes. The scale is calibrated to measure distance from a pre-pandemic baseline. That baseline was established in February 2020, a period that itself was not necessarily representative of equilibrium. The pre-pandemic supply chain was characterized by just-in-time inventory management, low warehousing utilization, and relatively stable trade flows. That world no longer exists.

The current reading of two on the ten-point scale tells us that the system is as fluid as it was before the pandemic. But it does not tell us whether the system is as resilient. The structural changes that have occurred since 2020—near-shoring, diversification of port gateways, increased warehousing capacity, and the shift toward just-in-case inventory strategies—have altered the relationship between congestion and economic activity. A congestion index reading of two in 2026 may correspond to a different level of operational risk than the same reading in 2019.

The report’s own data illustrates this point. The Logistics Managers’ Index for transportation capacity contracted in May at a slower rate than in April, but it remains well below the expansion threshold. Warehousing capacity is expanding, but utilization remains high. Trucking employment is still below pre-pandemic levels. These are not crisis indicators, but they are signs that the logistics network is operating with less slack than the congestion index alone would suggest.

The unanswered question is whether the current fluidity is sustainable or whether it depends on a fragile combination of moderate demand, adequate capacity, and favorable geopolitics. The report acknowledges that tariffs and geopolitical conflicts are key variables, but it does not model their impact on the index. That is a gap that readers should recognize.

## Decision-Makers Should Adopt a Three-Part Framework for Interpreting Supply Chain Risk in the Current Environment

The data in this report can be translated into a practical decision framework for executives and investors. The framework has three components: baseline assessment, trend identification, and scenario planning.

First, baseline assessment. The current state of the U.S. supply chain is fluid but not loose. The congestion index is low, but individual metrics are showing signs of tightening. The appropriate baseline assumption should be that the system will remain in a range of one to three on the congestion scale for the remainder of 2026, with occasional spikes driven by seasonal demand or external shocks. This is not a prediction of crisis, but it is a rejection of the view that the supply chain has permanently returned to pre-pandemic norms.

Second, trend identification. The most important trends to monitor are the divergence between coasts, the trajectory of ocean rates, and the relationship between rail volume and service quality. Each of these trends has a lead time of several weeks to several months. If East Coast backlogs continue to rise, if ocean rates sustain their upward momentum, or if rail dwell times begin to increase consistently, the baseline assumption of fluidity will need to be revised.

Third, scenario planning. Decision-makers should prepare for two scenarios that the report’s data makes plausible but not certain. The first scenario is a gradual tightening in which the congestion index moves to three or four by late 2026, driven by sustained demand and capacity constraints. The second scenario is a more abrupt disruption triggered by a geopolitical event, a labor action, or a natural disaster that exposes the system’s reduced resilience. Neither scenario is the base case, but both have higher probability than the historical average would suggest.

The framework is not a substitute for the full report, but it provides a structure for interpreting the weekly data releases that will follow. The value of the congestion scale is not in any single reading. It is in the ability to detect shifts early and to act before those shifts become obvious to everyone.

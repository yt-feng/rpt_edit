EN_A: Which names came up the most in those meetings?
EN_B: LVMH, Richemont, Hermes, Kering, and then adidas and Inditex. LVMH was often described as the default long among the big luxury groups.
EN_A: Why LVMH? Is there a catalyst coming?
EN_B: Yes. For the first time in months, estimates for LVMH’s fashion and leather goods division have been revised up for the second quarter. It is a small move, from flat to around two percent organic growth. But it is symbolic. Bulls see this as a turning point.
EN_A: What does that mean for the stock? Does it signal the bottom is in?
EN_B: Bulls think so. They argue that if you strip out the Middle East headwind, the real run rate could be much higher. But bears say the two percent growth is only because the comparison base was so easy. The real test comes in the third quarter.
EN_A: What about Hermes? That stock has been a safe haven forever.
EN_B: Hermes is getting more complicated. Bulls love the valuation. It has derated significantly. But bears are asking a tougher question. Is there a structural problem emerging?
EN_A: Structural? Hermes bags are still impossible to buy.
EN_B: That is exactly the point. The pre-spend ratio, which is how much you need to buy before you get a quota bag, is dropping in key markets like China. If Hermes slows production, the stock could drop. If it keeps producing, the brand equity gets diluted. The CEO is in a tough spot.
EN_A: That sounds like a real dilemma. What about Richemont? Cartier and Van Cleef seem unstoppable.
EN_B: Richemont was the most loved name in the room. Almost nobody is shorting it. Investors love the new management structure and the jewelry division’s momentum, especially in Korea.
EN_A: But there must be risks.
EN_B: There are. Gold prices coming down is seen as a positive, but some investors question how sustainable Cartier watches and Van Cleef growth really is. The Alhambra line is now a two-billion-euro business, and the debate about whether it is becoming too ubiquitous is getting louder.
EN_A: Interesting. And what about Kering and Gucci? Are they still struggling?
EN_B: There is some good news. Gucci’s recovery in the US in the second quarter was surprisingly strong. Some hedge funds even closed their short positions. But bears argue that recovery is mostly driven by lower-priced, aspirational customers.
EN_A: So the quality of that recovery is questionable.
EN_B: Exactly. If Gucci can only grow by chasing entry-level buyers, the valuation multiple will stay capped. And the brand’s performance in China remains mediocre.
EN_A: Let’s talk about adidas. Is the World Cup a real catalyst?
EN_B: Bulls think so. They see strong momentum in the US from the World Cup campaign. It could be a multi-year tailwind. But bears worry about what happens after the tournament. The growth bar for the second quarter is already very high.

EN_A: So you just got back from New York after meeting with over 20 investors. What is the big takeaway on European luxury?
EN_B: The main signal is that US investors are starting to get interested again. They mentioned that portfolios are too concentrated in tech, so there is a willingness to deploy new money into non-tech sectors. And luxury is a natural beneficiary because of the wealth effect from tech.
EN_A: So the logic is that when tech stocks go up, people get richer and buy luxury goods. Is there any concrete evidence for that?
EN_B: Yes, Korea is the clearest example. The report points out that Korea shows the most direct correlation between AI-driven wealth and spending on personal luxury goods. Demand there is booming. Also, the reopening of the Middle East, improving FX trends, and the fact that expectations for LVMH’s second quarter sales have been revised up for the first time in months are all positive.
EN_A: Which stocks were discussed the most? Is there a clear consensus favorite?
EN_B: The most discussed names were LVMH, Hermes, Richemont, and Ferrari. Among them, LVMH was often described as the default long for investors who want to step into the sector. The reasoning is that Richemont is seen as too expensive and consensus, Hermes might be facing structural issues for the first time, and Kering is too much of a turnaround story.
EN_A: What is the specific catalyst for LVMH? Is it the second quarter results?
EN_B: Exactly. The report highlights that estimates for the Fashion and Leather Goods division have inflected positively over the past four weeks. It is only a modest improvement, from around zero to about two percent growth, but it is symbolic. Also, Dior is expected to turn positive for the first time in about three years, and management is very focused on this because Dior is the second biggest profit contributor to the group.
EN_A: That sounds bullish. But what about the bears? What are they saying?
EN_B: The bears have three main pushbacks. First, can the sector work without China? Channel checks there remain disappointing. Second, can leading brands like Vuitton and Gucci work without the middle-income consumer? Third, in a low-growth environment, how disruptive is Chanel? Is its strong growth additive to the sector, or is it just taking market share?
EN_A: What about Hermes? It has always been the safe haven. Is it under pressure now?
EN_B: The debate on Hermes is very split. The bulls argue that the stock has derated significantly, from an average forward PE of 44.5 times over the past ten years to 33.8 times now, which offers an interesting entry point. They see the weakness in non-leather goods as cyclical, not structural.
EN_A: And the bears? What is their main concern?
EN_B: The bears think Hermes might be facing a structural issue. They argue that the brand is becoming less desirable, which is reflected in a lower willingness to spend on non-leather goods to qualify for quota bags. The report mentions a key metric called the pre-spend ratio, which is declining in key markets like China. This puts CEO Dumas in a classic catch-22 situation.
EN_A: A catch-22? What do you mean?
EN_B: If he announces that handbag production growth will slow down, the stock will likely react negatively, similar to what happened with Ferrari. But if production continues to grow, it risks diluting brand equity in the long term, leading to further de-rating. The report does not provide a clear answer on this.
EN_A: So Richemont seems to be the most popular name. Why is that?
EN_B: Richemont is almost universally loved right now. The bulls have multiple arguments. Long term, the appointment of Nicolas Bos as CEO is seen as a game changer for governance. Medium term, Cartier and Van Cleef are gaining market share in jewelry. Short term, the decline in gold prices is viewed positively, as the rapid increase in gold prices was the main reason for earnings downgrades.
EN_A: But there must be some skeptics on Richemont too, right?
EN_B: There are very few bears, but one key concern is that the stock has done well while consensus EPS has been coming down. If the July 15th sales report does not trigger earnings upgrades, it will be hard for the stock to go much higher. Also, some investors question the sustainability of Cartier and Van Cleef’s growth, especially with the Alhambra line becoming so ubiquitous.
EN_A: What about Kering and Gucci? That story seems more complicated.
EN_B: It is very polarized. The bulls point to surprisingly strong recovery in the US, based on credit card data, which has led some hedge funds to cover their shorts. But the bears argue that the recovery is only in one geography, the US, while China remains mediocre. Also, the US recovery seems driven by aspirational customers buying cheaper bags, which raises questions about sustainability.
EN_A: And what about Burberry and EssilorLuxottica? Any interesting takes?
EN_B: For Burberry, the bulls see a turnaround in early stages with significant margin recovery potential. The bears worry that the top-line momentum is not sustainable and that there is a lack of catalysts. For EssilorLuxottica, the bulls think the derating is overdone. The bears are concerned about competition from smart glasses and a tough comparison base in the fourth quarter.
EN_A: And finally, what about Adidas and Inditex, since they are also part of the consumer sector?
EN_B: Adidas has very strong near-term momentum, especially in the US, driven by World Cup marketing. But the bears worry about growth drivers beyond the World Cup. For Inditex, the bulls see it as a high-quality compounding story, but the report does not fully elaborate on that.
EN_A: So overall, sentiment is improving, but there is still a lot of debate. What should investors focus on right now?
EN_B: The report leaves a key question unanswered: Is Chanel’s strong growth additive or is it taking market share? Also, how serious is the structural issue at Hermes? These are questions that require further verification and are explored in more detail in the full report.

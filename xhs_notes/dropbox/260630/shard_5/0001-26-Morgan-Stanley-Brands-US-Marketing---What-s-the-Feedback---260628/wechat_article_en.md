# The Luxury Sector Is No Longer a China Story: Why the Wealth Effect from Tech Is the New Engine, and Why Investors Must Distinguish Between Cyclical Recovery and Structural Disruption

The most important signal from a week of intensive investor meetings in New York is not that sentiment toward European luxury goods is improving. It is that the argument for owning luxury stocks has fundamentally shifted. For years, the bull case rested on Chinese demand. Today, that pillar is cracked, and the new foundation is the wealth effect generated by technology, a phenomenon most visible in Korea but increasingly relevant across developed markets. Investors who continue to frame luxury as a China-driven cyclical trade risk missing the deeper structural rotation underway.

The meetings, which included a mix of long-only and hedge fund investors, revealed a market in transition. The most discussed stocks—LVMH, Richemont, Hermes, Ferrari, Kering, Inditex, and Adidas—were debated not on whether the sector would recover, but on what kind of recovery it would be. The bulls see a slow, tech-wealth-driven expansion that favors established leaders. The bears see a sector that cannot grow without the Chinese middle-income consumer and faces a new disruptive threat from Chanel. Both sides have evidence. The question is which narrative will dominate the next twelve months.

The meetings also surfaced a critical tension: the willingness to deploy capital into non-tech sectors, particularly those benefiting from AI-generated wealth, versus the fear that luxury's growth trajectory remains too pedestrian to justify current valuations. This is not a market that has made up its mind. It is a market waiting for a catalyst, and that catalyst may come from an unexpected source.

## The Tech Wealth Effect Is Real and Concentrated, but It Is Not a Replacement for Chinese Demand

The clearest evidence of the tech-wealth effect on luxury spending comes from Korea, where the correlation between AI-driven equity gains and personal luxury goods purchases is strongest. Investors noted that Korean demand has been booming, and this is not a one-off. It reflects a structural shift: as tech portfolios become increasingly concentrated, a new class of wealthy consumers is emerging who are not dependent on Chinese economic momentum. This is a genuinely new dynamic, and it has implications for which brands benefit most.

The logic is straightforward. Technology has created massive wealth concentration in a small number of markets and among a narrow demographic. These consumers are not price-sensitive in the same way as the aspirational middle class. They buy at the top of the pyramid. For brands like Hermes and Richemont, which have long cultivated this customer base, the tech wealth effect is a tailwind. For brands that depend on volume and breadth, it is less relevant.

However, investors must be careful not to overstate this effect. The tech-wealth cohort is small. It can support strong growth in Korea and, to a lesser extent, in the United States. But it cannot replace the scale of Chinese demand, which has historically been the engine of the global luxury market. The bear case on this point is compelling: without China, can the sector grow at a rate that justifies current multiples? The answer, at least for now, is probably not at the aggregate level. The growth will be selective and brand-specific.

This is why the debate around LVMH is so important. As the industry bellwether, LVMH's second-quarter sales for its Fashion and Leather Goods division have become a proxy for the entire sector. The consensus expectation has crept up from flat to roughly plus two percent organic sales growth, a symbolic improvement. But as bears correctly point out, this is against an easy comparison of minus nine percent in the prior year. On a two-year stack, there is no improvement. The question is whether the market will reward the sequential improvement or punish the lack of underlying acceleration.

## Chanel's Ascent Is the Most Underappreciated Risk to the Established Order

The most disruptive force in luxury today is not a technology platform or a macroeconomic shock. It is a privately held, $19 billion turnover company with no public shareholders to satisfy and no quarterly earnings calls to manage. Chanel's very strong growth in 2026 is reshaping competitive dynamics in ways that the public market has not fully priced in.

The report makes this point indirectly, but the implications are far-reaching. Chanel is taking market share from Hermes and Dior, and it is doing so at a time when the overall industry is growing at only around two percent in constant currency. In a low-growth environment, market share shifts matter enormously. Every percentage point Chanel gains is a percentage point lost by a publicly traded competitor.

The strategic question for investors is whether Chanel's growth is additive to the sector or purely cannibalistic. If Chanel is expanding the total addressable market by attracting new consumers to luxury, then its success benefits everyone. But if it is simply winning share from established players, then the winners and losers become much more defined. The evidence from the investor meetings suggests the latter. Chanel is seen as a donor from Hermes and Dior, particularly in non-leather goods categories.

This has profound implications for valuation. Hermes has long traded at a premium based on its scarcity and brand equity. If Chanel is eroding that scarcity, the premium should compress. The bear case on Hermes is not about a bad quarter. It is about a structural change in consumer preference. The pre-spend ratio in China is declining, meaning consumers are less willing to buy non-leather goods to qualify for quota bags. If that trend continues, Hermes' model of using scarcity to drive cross-category spending is under threat.

## Richemont's Jewellery Dominance Is Real, but the Valuation Debate Has Shifted

Richemont was the most uniformly bullish name in the meetings, and for good reason. The appointment of Nicolas Bos as group CEO has resolved the corporate governance overhang that had kept institutional investors on the sidelines. The jewellery division, led by Cartier and Van Cleef, is gaining market share in an attractive category. The gold price decline is a near-term tailwind for margins. And the geographic mix is favorable, with overexposure to the United States and underexposure to China.

But the valuation debate has shifted. Richemont now trades at 27.8 times forward earnings, while Hermes trades at 33.8 times. The argument that Cartier and Van Cleef deserve a Hermes-like multiple is less powerful when Hermes itself is de-rating. The question is whether Richemont can sustain its growth trajectory without a catalyst for earnings upgrades.

The bears have a point: Richemont's stock has performed well over the past two years despite downward earnings revisions. If the July sales print does not trigger consensus upgrades, the stock may struggle to move higher. More importantly, the sustainability of the jewellery division's growth is not guaranteed. Cartier Watches and Van Cleef have driven most of the recent momentum, but Cartier Jewellery's growth has been more modest. And Van Cleef's Alhambra line, now a roughly two billion euro franchise, is facing questions about ubiquity and desirability.

This is a classic luxury sector dilemma. Success breeds scale, and scale can erode exclusivity. Richemont's management team is strong, but they are not immune to this dynamic. Investors who own the stock must ask themselves whether the current growth rate is structural or whether it reflects a temporary product cycle.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How the Sector Will Grow Without the Middle-Income Consumer

This is the most important open question in the report, and it deserves explicit attention. The luxury sector has historically grown by expanding its customer base, drawing in middle-income consumers who aspire to own a piece of the dream. That model is under severe strain. In China, the middle-income consumer has been under pressure for two years. In the United States, aspirational spending is concentrated in specific brands like Gucci, which has played with its product mix to attract this cohort.

The report provides data points but no clear answer. Hermes is exposed to the middle-income consumer through its non-leather goods categories, and this is a headwind. Gucci's recovery in the United States is driven by aspirational customers, which raises questions about sustainability. Dior's recovery is more pedestrian than initially hoped. The pattern is clear: the middle-income consumer is not coming back in force, and the sector needs to find a new growth model.

One possibility is that the sector will bifurcate. The top end, serving ultra-high-net-worth individuals, will continue to grow, driven by the tech wealth effect and geographic diversification into the Middle East and Korea. The accessible luxury segment will struggle, facing pressure from both value-oriented competitors and the disruption of new entrants like Chanel. This would imply a barbell strategy for investors: own the strongest brands at the top and the most agile brands at the bottom, but avoid the middle.

Another possibility is that the sector will consolidate. Strong brands will acquire weaker ones, and the industry will shrink to a smaller number of dominant players. This is already happening to some extent, but the pace could accelerate if growth remains pedestrian.

## A Decision Framework for Luxury Investors: Four Questions to Determine Which Narrative Wins

Investors do not need to predict the future. They need a framework to interpret incoming data and adjust their positions accordingly. Based on the report's insights, four questions will determine which luxury narrative prevails over the next twelve months.

First, is LVMH's second-quarter inflection real or illusory? If the Fashion and Leather Goods division delivers plus two to three percent organic sales growth, and if that growth is driven by improving fundamentals rather than easy comparisons, the bull case gains credibility. If the two-year stack shows no improvement, the bear case strengthens.

Second, is Chanel's market share gain accelerating or stabilizing? This is difficult to measure because Chanel is private, but investors can track it through competitor commentary and channel checks. If Chanel continues to take share from Hermes and Dior, the structural disruption thesis becomes more convincing.

Third, can Richemont sustain its jewellery division's growth without a catalyst for earnings upgrades? The stock is already pricing in a lot of good news. If the July sales print fails to trigger consensus upgrades, the valuation argument weakens, and the stock becomes vulnerable.

Fourth, is the tech wealth effect expanding beyond Korea? If it spreads to other markets, particularly the United States, it could provide a meaningful tailwind for the top end of the market. If it remains concentrated, its impact will be limited.

These four questions form a simple but powerful framework. Investors who can answer them correctly will be well positioned, regardless of which narrative ultimately wins.

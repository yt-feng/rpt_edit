# The Power Bottleneck Is the Real AI Constraint — And It Is Reshaping Global Energy Infrastructure

The dominant narrative around artificial intelligence has focused on compute capacity, model architecture, and chip supply. But a more fundamental constraint is emerging: the physical infrastructure required to deliver electricity to data centers is breaking down under the weight of demand. A global investment bank report published in June 2026 makes the case that power grid limitations, not semiconductor shortages, will determine the pace of AI deployment over the next decade.

This is not a marginal observation. The report documents that AI-driven data center demand has surged from 4.3 gigawatts in 2023 to an estimated 110 gigawatts by 2030, representing a shift from roughly 12 percent of tracked data center demand to 70 percent. Meanwhile, more than 2,500 gigawatts of renewable, storage, and large-load projects are stalled in grid interconnection queues worldwide. The gap between power demand and grid capacity is no longer a future risk — it is a present-day bottleneck that is reshaping investment decisions, technology choices, and regional energy strategies.

The report argues that the solution is not simply to build more grid infrastructure, though that is necessary. The more immediate response is a structural shift toward distributed generation, particularly in the United States, where "bring your own generation" models are gaining traction. Natural gas-fired power plants, reciprocating engines, aeroderivative turbines, and behind-the-meter solutions are being deployed to bypass grid connection delays that can stretch three to five years. This is not a temporary workaround. It represents a fundamental reconfiguration of how power is sourced for large-scale computing.

What makes this moment different from previous power demand cycles is the combination of AI-driven growth with renewed energy security priorities. The Middle East conflict has sharpened focus on import dependence. Countries are accelerating renewable adoption not just for climate reasons but to reduce exposure to price spikes and supply disruptions. This dual pressure — from AI and from energy security — is creating demand for power infrastructure equipment that the supply chain cannot currently meet. Transformer lead times have extended to 24 to 48 months for large units. Generator step-up transformer demand has increased more than 270 percent since 2019. Prices for substation transformers are roughly 80 percent higher over the last five years.

The report does not predict this situation will resolve quickly. It identifies structural constraints in manufacturing capacity, permitting frameworks, and workforce availability that will keep equipment markets tight through at least 2030. For investors and strategists, the implication is clear: the companies and regions that can secure power equipment and generation capacity will have a structural advantage in the AI economy.

## The Market Has Not Fully Grasped the Duration of Equipment Supply-Chain Constraints

The report draws on conversations with Citi's global banking professionals who work directly with power and energy clients. Their assessment is sobering: the market has not understood the real issues with the supply chain, and there is general ambiguity around the actual backlog and delivery timelines for critical equipment.

Gas turbines are costing two to three times what they used to cost. There is a shortage of substations, transformers, and switchgear equipment. Original equipment manufacturers are cautious about adding capacity because they must take a longer view to ensure they meet hurdle rates. This caution means decision-making is slow, and buyers are being forced to seek alternative sources of supply.

The constraints are not uniform across all equipment types, but the pattern is consistent. Large power transformers, used in utility substations and transmission networks, have lead times of 24 to 48 months. Medium and high-voltage switchgear and breakers, used in data center electrical rooms and grid interconnects, require 12 to 30-plus months. Generator step-up transformers, essential for natural gas-fired power plants and renewable energy facilities, now require up to three to four years for delivery.

These timelines matter because they directly affect the pace of data center construction. A typical data center build-out takes about two years, but the power infrastructure needed to support it can take three to five years if new generation is required. This leaves a period of gap that developers are trying to bridge with behind-the-meter generation, but even those solutions face equipment shortages.

The report notes that some data center developments have already been delayed or canceled due to power constraints. Developers are prioritizing locations where interconnect and power supply have more visibility. This is creating a geographic concentration of data center activity in regions with existing gas infrastructure, particularly Texas near the Permian Basin and the Marcellus-Utica shale region in Pennsylvania and Ohio.

## Speed-to-Power Is Driving a Structural Shift from Centralized Grids to Distributed Generation

The report's central analytical contribution is its framing of "speed-to-power" as the decisive variable in current energy markets. Because grid expansion cannot keep pace with demand, project developers are opting for generation that can be deployed faster, even if it is less efficient or more expensive on a levelized cost basis.

This is not a marginal phenomenon. The report documents that order books for gas turbines are very strong, driven by newer-generation ideas for data center power. But the more interesting development is the repurposing of equipment from other applications. Reciprocating engines originally used in oil fields, aeroderivative turbines from aviation, and fuel cells are being adapted to provide behind-the-meter solutions for data centers. The use-case value is strong, the report notes, but supply is limited and the solution is more nuanced and complicated than simply connecting to the grid.

The implications for natural gas infrastructure are significant. The report's banking professionals observe that the AI data center boom has become one of the biggest drivers of new electricity demand forecasts, and natural gas is expected to be a key part of the power supply to meet this demand. Energy companies are directly working with developers and hyperscalers to locate data centers next to existing natural gas resources and infrastructure.

This is leading to a strategic concentration of projects in Texas, which benefits from proximity to the Permian Basin. The report also notes growing demand in the Marcellus-Utica shale region of Pennsylvania and Ohio. If growth and corresponding demand rise to levels projected, the report argues, it is hard to imagine a world where we will not need development of gigawatts of new gas-fired power plants and corresponding natural gas infrastructure.

The report raises an important question that it does not fully answer: how much of this gas demand is a short-term bridge versus a long-term baseload opportunity? The banking professionals interviewed suggest it is all of the above. Natural gas plays a multifaceted role — as bridge fuel for projects awaiting grid connection, as baseload power alongside nuclear and renewables, and as backup and flexibility capacity. The report leaves open the question of how these roles will evolve as grid infrastructure catches up and as battery storage costs continue to decline.

## The Equipment Supply Chain Creates a Second-Order Bottleneck That the Report Only Begins to Explore

One of the most valuable contributions of the report is its detailed analysis of power infrastructure equipment markets, but it also reveals an analytical gap that deserves attention. The report documents the demand surge and extended lead times for transformers, switchgear, and generation equipment, and it identifies the OEMs' caution about adding capacity. But it does not fully explore the second-order implications of these constraints for the broader energy transition.

Consider the following: the same equipment that is needed for AI data center power is also needed for grid modernization, renewable energy integration, and electric vehicle charging infrastructure. The report notes that global EV sales surpassed 20 million units in 2025, rising roughly 20 percent year-over-year, and that energy security concerns are accelerating investment in renewables and energy storage. This means competition for equipment is not just between data center developers and utilities — it is between multiple sectors of the economy, all of which need similar electrical hardware.

The report estimates that meeting electricity demand through 2030 requires annual grid investment to rise to roughly $400 billion. But it does not fully address how this investment will be prioritized across competing demands. Will transformer allocations favor data center projects over renewable energy farms? Will switchgear supplies be directed to grid interconnection projects or to behind-the-meter generation? These allocation decisions will have significant implications for which technologies and regions advance fastest.

The report also does not fully explore the potential for technological substitution to ease equipment constraints. Solid-state transformers, advanced grid control systems, and modular generation solutions could reduce dependence on traditional large power transformers, but the report does not assess their readiness or scalability. This is a meaningful open question for readers who want to understand whether the current bottlenecks represent a structural constraint or a transitional phase.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating Power-Constrained AI Investment

For investors and strategists, the report provides a useful framework for evaluating opportunities and risks in the power-AI nexus. The framework has four dimensions: power access, equipment availability, regulatory speed, and fuel supply.

Power access is the most immediate constraint. The report makes clear that data center development is being driven by locations where power interconnection has more visibility. This means regions with existing grid capacity, colocation near gas infrastructure, or access to behind-the-generation solutions will attract disproportionate investment. Texas, Virginia, Georgia, and Ohio are highlighted as key regions in the United States.

Equipment availability is the second dimension. Companies that have secured transformer and switchgear orders will have a significant timing advantage over competitors. The report notes that OEMs are cautious about adding capacity, which means equipment shortages will persist. Investors should assess which companies in the power equipment supply chain have the most secure access to components and the most credible delivery timelines.

Regulatory speed is the third dimension. The report identifies permitting frameworks as a key constraint on grid expansion. Regions with streamlined permitting for generation and transmission projects will be able to add capacity faster. The report does not provide a detailed comparison of regulatory regimes, but it implies that jurisdictions with "well-meaning" but slow permitting processes will lose data center investment to faster-moving alternatives.

Fuel supply is the fourth dimension, particularly relevant for the United States. The report's banking professionals observe that natural gas will remain a cornerstone of the baseload power mix alongside nuclear and renewables. Regions with access to shale gas production and existing midstream infrastructure will have a structural advantage. The report also notes that hybrid systems pairing gas with battery storage provide a mitigating measure for peak demand events.

This framework suggests that the most attractive investment opportunities are not necessarily in the largest technology companies or hyperscalers. They may be in the power equipment manufacturers, natural gas midstream companies, and engineering firms that enable the physical infrastructure of AI computing.

## The Report Raises Questions That Deserve a Deeper Investigation

The report is comprehensive in its scope, but it leaves several meaningful analytical questions open. These are not weaknesses — they are invitations for further analysis.

First, the report does not fully address the risk that AI-driven power demand could slow. It acknowledges that some caution certain aspects of AI look like a bubble, but it argues that power is still very much needed as the Jevons Paradox takes hold. The report's stance is that related capital expenditure will be utilized in the future. But it does not stress-test this assumption. What would happen to power equipment markets if AI investment growth slowed from 70 percent annual rates to 20 percent? Would the equipment supply chain adjust, or would overcapacity create a different set of problems?

Second, the report does not provide a detailed comparison of how different regions are managing the speed-to-power challenge. It covers the United States, Europe, China, and Australia at a high level, but it does not offer a systematic framework for comparing regulatory regimes, equipment availability, and fuel supply across these markets. For global investors, this comparative analysis would be valuable.

Third, the report does not fully explore the implications of distributed generation for grid reliability and system stability. If a significant share of data center power comes from behind-the-meter gas generation, what does this mean for grid operators who need to balance supply and demand across the system? The report notes that developers are increasingly agreeing to curtail usage during peak hours to secure faster grid connections, but it does not analyze the system-level implications of this trend.

These questions point toward the need for ongoing analysis as the situation evolves. The report's value is not that it provides definitive answers — it is that it identifies the right questions and provides a rigorous analytical framework for addressing them.

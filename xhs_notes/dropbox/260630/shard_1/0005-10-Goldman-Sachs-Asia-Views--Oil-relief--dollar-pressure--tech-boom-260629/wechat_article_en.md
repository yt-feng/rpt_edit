# Asia's Divergence Is Deepening: The Oil Shock Reversal Exposes Which Economies Built Real Resilience and Which Were Simply Bailed Out

The dramatic collapse in Brent crude from nearly $120 per barrel to $72 has provided more than just relief for Asia's largest oil importers. It has revealed a fundamental divergence that will define the region's investment landscape for the remainder of the decade. The plunge in oil prices—driven by renewed hopes for a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz following intensive US-Iran negotiations—has not simply normalized conditions. It has exposed which Asian economies built genuine structural buffers against external shocks and which were merely masked by temporary fiscal palliatives and favorable export tailwinds.

This is not a story of uniform recovery. The oil price decline interacts with three other powerful forces—a resurgent US dollar approaching its 2026 highs, an AI-driven tech export boom of historic proportions, and a hawkish pivot from central banks across the region—to create a complex mosaic of winners and laggards. The strategic question for investors is no longer whether Asia will benefit from lower energy costs. It is which economies will translate that benefit into sustainable domestic demand, and which will find themselves trapped by currency weakness, monetary tightening, and structural imbalances that lower oil prices alone cannot fix.

The market has already begun to price this divergence. Asian equity strategists are doubling down on a concentrated set of winners: Japan, Korea, Taiwan, and China A-shares. They are underweight most of Southeast Asia. This is not a tactical call. It is a structural thesis about which economies have the policy flexibility, the export composition, and the domestic demand dynamics to turn the oil relief into a genuine growth catalyst. The next six months will determine whether that thesis holds or whether the current consensus has underestimated the second-order effects of a stronger dollar and stickier core inflation.

## The Oil Price Collapse Is a Stress Test, Not a Free Lunch, for Asia's Inflation Management

The most immediate implication of the oil price decline is that Asia's inflation trajectory is now a story of second-round effects rather than first-round shocks. This is a critical distinction. During the 2021-22 energy crisis, Asia experienced a broad-based inflation surge driven by direct passthrough from crude to retail fuel, transportation, and electricity costs. The current episode is different in three important ways, and that difference matters for how investors should position.

First, the shock was narrower in its sectoral impact and shorter in duration. While headline CPI spiked in several economies—most notably the Philippines, where inflation reached 7.2% year-on-year—the overall regional inflation picture remained manageable. Most Asian economies saw inflation move up but remain within or near target bands. This reflects the liberal use of strategic petroleum reserves and targeted fiscal subsidies to cushion the blow. Japan and China deployed price cap mechanisms that absorbed the initial shock. Indonesia and Malaysia maintained fixed retail prices that insulated consumers at the cost of mounting fiscal burdens.

Second, the collapse in oil prices means these fiscal interventions are no longer binding. The cost of maintaining price caps and subsidies has fallen dramatically, which creates fiscal room that was not anticipated even three months ago. This is a genuine positive, but it is not evenly distributed. Economies that had the fiscal space to intervene aggressively—Japan, China, Korea—now have additional capacity for discretionary easing. Economies that were already stretched—Indonesia, the Philippines—face a more complicated calculus.

Third, and most importantly, the inflation story is not over. Core inflation is likely to move higher in coming months as lagged passthrough from higher energy costs works through industrial supply chains. Chemicals, plastics, and transportation services will see delayed price adjustments. Food inflation is an even greater concern. The combination of the energy shock's aftereffects and a potential strong El Niño event means food prices are likely to rise later this year and into early 2027. The oil price decline prevents a renewed sharp spike in headline inflation, but it does not prevent a slow, grinding normalization of core prices.

The strategic implication is straightforward: central banks across Asia will remain cautious. The Bank of Japan hiked as planned and saw two board members advocate for an accelerated pace. The Reserve Bank of Australia paused but retained a hawkish bias. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand has teed up a likely hike. The Philippines and Indonesia hiked again, with Indonesia acting on an inter-meeting basis. The Bank of Korea is clearly hawkish, with three hikes now forecast beginning in July. India is the outlier, pushing back against market expectations of rate hikes while implementing measures to incentivize capital inflows.

This monetary tightening bias is the key constraint on the growth story. Lower oil prices ease the inflation pressure, but they do not eliminate the need for central banks to demonstrate credibility. In economies where currencies have weakened significantly against the dollar—and the dollar has strengthened back toward its March highs—the pressure to hike is even greater. The trade-off between supporting growth and defending the currency is becoming starker by the week.

## The Tech Export Boom Is Reshaping Asian Economies in Ways That Domestic Demand Cannot Yet Match

The most powerful force in Asian macroeconomics today is not oil prices or central bank policy. It is the AI-related capital expenditure boom, and it is transforming the region's export landscape in ways that have few historical precedents.

Consensus forecasts now estimate that the five large US hyperscalers alone will invest nearly $1 trillion in AI-related capex by 2027. This is not a forecast that has been trimmed. It has been revised higher repeatedly as the competitive dynamics of the AI arms race intensify. The implications for Asia's technology supply chain are extraordinary. Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, and Singapore have each run goods trade surpluses well in excess of 10% of GDP over the past three months. These are not normal trade numbers. They are structural shifts in the region's export composition that reflect Asia's deep integration into the AI equipment supply chain.

The question that will define the second half of 2026 is whether this export boom spills over into stronger domestic activity. The evidence so far is mixed. Korea's private consumption is expected to recover somewhat on wealth effects from booming equity markets, but the recovery is likely to be gradual. Taiwan's economy is a different story. It is growing at approximately 10%—the strongest pace in four decades—driven by extraordinary strength in tech production. This is not a cyclical upswing. It is a structural transformation powered by the single most important investment theme in global markets.

But the spillover effects are not automatic. Booming equity markets have eased financial conditions significantly, but volatility has increased recently. The wealth effect on consumption depends on the breadth of equity market participation, which varies significantly across the region. In Taiwan, where tech dominates both the economy and the equity market, the spillover is more direct. In Korea, where the chaebol structure means that corporate profits do not automatically translate into household income, the transmission mechanism is weaker.

The deeper strategic question is whether the tech export boom is creating a two-speed Asia. The economies that are deeply embedded in the AI supply chain—Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, Singapore—are experiencing export-led growth that is historically exceptional. The economies that are not—Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam—are being left behind, and lower oil prices alone will not close that gap. This divergence has implications for currency positioning, equity allocation, and fixed income strategy that extend well beyond the current quarter.

## China's Domestic Demand Weakness Is Structural, and the Export-Driven Growth Model Is Reaching Its Limits

China's economic data presents a puzzle that lower oil prices do not solve. In May, both retail sales and fixed asset investment contracted year-on-year—only the second time on record, with the first being during the COVID lockdowns of 2020. Retail sales fell 0.6% year-on-year, reflecting the fade-out of the trade-in program and pressure from higher oil prices. Fixed asset investment declined 4.1% year-on-year, suppressed by the property downturn and tight local government finances.

The contrast with the export sector could not be starker. Exports were up over 19% year-on-year in May, fueling industrial production growth of 4.5% and a monthly trade surplus of over $100 billion. Exports are by far the largest driver of China's recent growth. Domestic demand is growing at only a 1-2% annual rate. This is not a balanced recovery. It is a recovery that is entirely dependent on external demand, and that dependence creates vulnerabilities.

The oil price decline helps China in two ways. It reduces the cost of imported energy, which improves the trade surplus further. It also means that the price cap mechanisms that China deployed are no longer binding, creating fiscal room for potential stimulus. But the structural challenges remain. The property downturn is not a cyclical correction. It is a secular shift in the most important asset class for Chinese households, and it has destroyed wealth, suppressed confidence, and constrained local government finances in ways that will take years to resolve.

Policymakers seem willing to be patient. Recent policy communications have emphasized a more price-based monetary policy framework, reduced emphasis on aggregate credit extension, and rules-based capital account opening. There is a clear pickup in efforts to accelerate RMB internationalization, building out the financial plumbing to facilitate holdings by foreign central banks and institutional investors. The currency has strengthened against both the dollar and regional trade partners, which is a vote of confidence in the policy direction but also a headwind for export competitiveness.

The unanswered question is whether China's policymakers are being too patient. The logic of waiting for the property downturn to bottom naturally is understandable, but the social and political costs of prolonged domestic demand weakness are mounting. The oil price decline provides a window of opportunity for stimulus, but the form of that stimulus matters. Infrastructure spending through local government special bonds is the most likely channel, but its effectiveness depends on the quality of project selection and the willingness of local governments to spend. The risk is that China's export-driven growth model reaches its limits before domestic demand recovers, creating a growth vacuum that lower oil prices cannot fill.

## The Currency and Rate Dynamics Create a Decision Framework That Rewards Differentiation, Not Regional Uniformity

The combination of lower oil prices, a stronger dollar, and divergent growth trajectories creates a complex set of cross-currents for Asian currencies and fixed income markets. The strategic approach is not to make a single directional call on Asia as a whole, but to identify the relative value trades that exploit the divergence.

The strongest relative value trade is short Thai baht versus Indian rupee. This trade reflects three reinforcing factors. First, the RBI's recent measures to incentivize capital inflows are supporting the rupee at a time when many other Asian central banks are struggling to defend their currencies. Second, the current account dynamics favor India over Thailand. India's growth momentum has been better than expected, and the upgrade of the full-year GDP growth forecast to 6.8% reflects both the oil price benefit and stronger underlying activity. Thailand's economy faces more headwinds from the high oil price hangover and weaker export demand. Third, the valuation of the rupee is relatively attractive compared to other high-carry emerging market currencies, while the baht faces headwinds from different policymaker preferences with respect to exchange rates.

In fixed income, the easing in price pressures has made a constructive case for selective positions. The most attractive opportunity is in 30-year Indian government bonds. The improving macro backdrop, the recent RBI FX measures, and the potential for index inclusion create a favorable tailwind for long-duration Indian debt. The oil price decline improves India's fiscal position and reduces the pressure on the current account, which should support bond prices. The RBI's willingness to push back against market expectations of rate hikes—even though hikes are still expected later in the year—suggests that the central bank is prioritizing growth support over currency defense, which is positive for bonds.

The Malaysian ringgit presents a more nuanced case. Upcoming state-level contests have weighed on the currency, but unless a snap general election is called, the underlying macro fundamentals remain positive. The oil price decline reduces the cost of maintaining fixed retail prices, which improves the fiscal outlook. The recommendation to short the Singapore dollar against the ringgit reflects the view that the political risk is temporary and that the macro fundamentals will reassert themselves.

The broader framework for Asian markets is that three global factors will remain the principal drivers: Federal Reserve policy, the AI investment theme, and progress on reopening the Strait of Hormuz. These are not factors that Asian policymakers can control. They are external forces that will determine the direction of capital flows, currency movements, and risk appetite across the region. The strategic response is not to try to predict these factors, but to position for the divergence that they will create.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Tech Export Boom and the Risk of Overconcentration

The most important analytical gap in the current outlook is the sustainability of the AI-related tech export boom. The consensus forecasts for hyperscaler capex have been revised higher repeatedly, but the underlying assumptions about the pace of AI adoption, the return on investment for AI infrastructure, and the competitive dynamics among the major players are not fully tested. The $1 trillion figure for 2027 is staggering, but it is not based on a long track record of capital allocation in this specific technology cycle.

There are two risks that the current analysis does not fully address. The first is that the capex boom reaches a point of diminishing returns. If the major hyperscalers begin to question the ROI on their AI investments, the pace of spending could slow more quickly than the consensus expects. The second risk is that the supply chain concentration itself becomes a vulnerability. The current export surge is driven by a small number of economies that are deeply embedded in the AI equipment supply chain. If geopolitical tensions or supply chain disruptions affect any of these nodes, the impact on the broader region could be severe.

The report also leaves open the question of how the oil price decline interacts with the El Niño risk for food inflation. The aftereffects of the energy shock and the potential for a strong El Niño event mean that food prices are likely to rise later this year and into early 2027. The oil price decline prevents a renewed sharp spike in headline inflation, but it does not prevent a slow, grinding normalization of core prices. The impact on different economies will depend on their food import dependence, the structure of their food supply chains, and their fiscal capacity to provide subsidies. This is a second-order effect that deserves more attention than it has received.

EN_A: So we just went through a pretty detailed report on China’s June and Q2 data. What’s the one big takeaway you want listeners to remember?
EN_B: The key message is that the Q1 rebound was a flash in the pan. Q2 growth is set to slow significantly. We’re looking at real GDP growth dropping from 5% in Q1 to 4.1% in Q2.
EN_A: 4.1%? That’s lower than a lot of market expectations. How did the report get to that number?
EN_B: They break it down from both the supply and demand sides. On supply, industrial output and services growth both slowed sharply in April and May. Industrial production averaged 4.3% year on year, services at 4.4%. Since these two sectors make up nearly 90% of GDP, that alone pulls down the headline number.
EN_A: And what about demand? Is it even worse?
EN_B: Yes, demand-side data is actually weaker. Retail sales averaged negative 0.2% in April and May. Fixed asset investment fell to negative 9.5%. Exports looked strong at 16.7%, but the report points out that about half of that is driven by surging chip prices. Real export growth is much lower.
EN_A: So the export boom is kind of a mirage?
EN_B: Exactly. It doesn’t provide enough support to offset the domestic weakness. But here’s the twist: the report says official GDP will probably align more with the supply-side data, because China calculates GDP mainly from the production side.
EN_A: Interesting. What about inflation? I saw PPI is rising, but it doesn’t sound like good news.
EN_B: Right. CPI and PPI are both going up, but the report stresses this is mostly external—oil and chip prices—not a real recovery in domestic demand. The GDP deflator is expected to turn positive in Q2, breaking a streak of negative readings for 13 out of the last 14 quarters. But it’s really imported inflation.
EN_A: So it’s cost-push, not demand-pull. What about the June forecasts for specific indicators? Like the PMI?
EN_B: The official manufacturing PMI is expected to dip to 49.8, below the 50 threshold. The non-manufacturing PMI also falls to 49.8. The report notes Dragon Boat holiday tourism was lackluster, suggesting consumer spending is still weak.
EN_A: And industrial production? Any improvement in June?
EN_B: They expect it to stay flat at 4.5% year on year, same as May. But there are structural issues. The Middle East conflict is still disrupting supply chains for oil and chemicals. For example, operating rates at Shandong teapot refineries dropped from over 60% in March to below 49% by end of June.
EN_A: That’s a big drop. What about exports in June?
EN_B: Export growth is expected to moderate slightly from 19.4% in May to 16.2%, still elevated. But again, it’s mostly price effects from semiconductors. Import growth is forecast at 26.2%, driven by high oil and chip prices.
EN_A: So the trade surplus will widen?
EN_B: Yes, from 105.4 billion to 111.3 billion. But that’s not a sign of strong domestic demand. It’s more of a price distortion.
EN_A: Retail sales were negative in May. Will they turn positive in June?
EN_B: The report expects a slight improvement to 0.9%, but still very weak. Auto sales are the main drag, because the trade-in program was scaled back and the EV purchase tax went from 0% to 5%. Also, households are cutting spending on petroleum products and electronics because prices are rising.
EN_A: And real estate? Everyone’s watching that.
EN_B: Property investment is expected to contract even further, to negative 25% year on year in June. New home sales in 20 major cities also turned negative. But there’s a twist: existing home prices in tier-1 cities have risen for three consecutive months, with Shanghai and Shenzhen leading.
EN_A: So first-tier cities are stabilizing? But the national picture is still bad?
EN_B: Exactly. That’s the “double K-shape” divergence the report talks about. Top-tier and a few second-tier cities are improving, but most lower-tier cities are seeing deeper price declines. The national average home price fell again in May by 0.26%.
EN_A: What about credit data? How are June’s aggregate financing and loans looking?
EN_B: The report expects aggregate financing growth to slow from 7.7% to 7.4%. Loan growth also eases from 5.5% to 5.3%. Corporate bond issuance is strong, but it’s offset by weak new RMB loans and soft government bond issuance. M2 growth is forecast to drop from 8.6% to 8.4%.
EN_A: So overall, Q2 looks like a tough quarter. Does the report say anything about policy response?
EN_B: It mentions that given the sharp decline in investment and Beijing’s commitment to stabilize it, we might see faster bond issuance and more fiscal spending in coming months. But the report doesn’t fully lay out the details. That’s one reason to read the full version.
EN_A: So policy will likely step up, but the impact is still uncertain?
EN_B: Right. And another open question is whether the inflation spike is temporary or more persistent. The report says it’s externally driven, but the full report has a deeper analysis on the inflation path.

# China's Q2 Slowdown Reveals a Deeper Structural Divide: Exports Mask What Domestic Demand Cannot Hide

China's economic rebound in the first quarter of this year was always fragile. The data from April and May now confirm that the recovery has already peaked. Real GDP growth is set to decelerate sharply to 4.1 percent year-on-year in the second quarter, down from 5.0 percent in the first quarter. This is not merely a cyclical dip. It is the manifestation of a structural tension that is becoming impossible to ignore: an export engine powered by artificial intelligence and chip prices is running hot, while domestic demand—consumption, investment, and property—is sliding into negative territory.

The headline numbers are stark. Retail sales growth turned negative in April and May, averaging minus 0.2 percent year-on-year. Fixed asset investment fell by 9.5 percent over the same period. The only bright spot was exports, which surged 16.7 percent in nominal terms. But that strength is deceptive. Roughly half of the export surge is driven by surging chip and electronics prices tied to the global AI supercycle, not by a broad-based expansion in real industrial output. When adjusted for price effects, real export growth is far lower, and the net contribution to GDP is insufficient to offset the collapse in domestic spending.

The result is a widening "double K-shape" within the Chinese economy. On one side, export-oriented sectors tied to AI and semiconductors are thriving. On the other, domestic-facing industries—property, construction, chemicals, automobiles, and consumer services—are deteriorating. This divergence is not temporary. It reflects a fundamental misalignment between the external drivers of growth and the internal conditions required for sustainable expansion.

Why does this matter now? Because the second quarter data forces a reassessment of the policy outlook. Beijing has committed to stabilizing investment, but bond issuance has fallen short of expectations, and fiscal spending has been sluggish. With growth slowing and domestic demand weakening, the pressure to accelerate fiscal stimulus is mounting. Yet the nature of the slowdown—driven by structural factors like the prolonged property slump and the payback effects from the trade-in program—means that conventional stimulus may have diminishing returns. The real question is whether policymakers can address the root causes of the domestic demand weakness before the export tailwind fades.

## The Production Side Tells a Consistent Story of Deceleration, But the Demand Side Reveals a Much Deeper Contraction

When analyzing Chinese GDP, it is essential to understand how the official figure is constructed. China calculates GDP primarily from the production side, using industrial and services output data. This methodology tends to smooth out the volatility seen in demand-side indicators. In the second quarter, industrial production growth is expected to average 4.5 percent year-on-year, down from 6.1 percent in the first quarter. Services output growth is projected at 4.5 percent, down from 5.1 percent. Together, these imply a real GDP slowdown to 4.1 percent.

But the demand-side data tell a far more alarming story. Retail sales are contracting in nominal terms. Fixed asset investment is declining at nearly double-digit rates. The only support comes from exports, and even that is inflated by price effects. When you strip out the chip price surge, real export growth is much weaker. The implication is clear: the official GDP figure, while accurate by its own methodology, understates the severity of the domestic demand collapse.

This disconnect matters for investors and policymakers alike. If you rely solely on the production-side GDP number, you might conclude that the economy is merely slowing, not contracting. But the demand-side data suggest that large swaths of the economy are already in recession. The property sector is the most obvious example, with investment falling by 24.3 percent in May and expected to worsen to 25.0 percent in June. But the weakness extends to autos, catering, and merchandise sales excluding autos, all of which are being hit by the payback effect from the trade-in program and rising imported inflation.

The key insight is that the production-side and demand-side indicators are diverging in a way that has not been seen in recent cycles. This divergence is itself a signal. It suggests that the supply side is being propped up by external demand and price effects, while the domestic demand side is being hollowed out. Over time, this is unsustainable. If domestic demand continues to weaken, production will eventually have to adjust downward, dragging the official GDP figure lower.

## The GDP Deflator Is Turning Positive, But for All the Wrong Reasons

One of the few positive headline developments in the second quarter is that the GDP deflator is expected to turn positive for the first time in 14 quarters, rising to about 1.0 percent year-on-year. This might appear to be a sign of reflation or a recovery in pricing power. It is not.

The increase in the deflator is being driven almost entirely by external factors: surging oil prices due to the Middle East conflict and rising chip prices due to the AI supercycle. Consumer price inflation is running at 1.2 percent, supported by energy prices. Producer price inflation has climbed to nearly 4.0 percent, driven by non-ferrous metals, semiconductors, and new energy products. But core inflation—which excludes food and energy—is actually declining, falling to an estimated 1.0 percent in June from 1.1 percent in May.

This is the opposite of a healthy reflation. A genuine recovery in domestic demand would be accompanied by rising core inflation and broad-based price increases across services and consumer goods. Instead, what we are seeing is imported inflation that squeezes household purchasing power and corporate margins. Domestic fuel prices are rising, consumer electronics prices are rising due to chip costs, but wages and household incomes are not keeping pace. The result is a drag on real consumption, even as nominal spending holds up.

For investors, the implication is that the positive deflator is a misleading signal. It does not indicate that the economy is healing. It indicates that external shocks are passing through to domestic prices, creating a headwind for consumption and investment. The policy response should not be to tighten, but to support domestic demand. Yet the risk is that policymakers misinterpret the deflator as a sign of overheating and delay the necessary fiscal stimulus.

## The Property Market Is Stabilizing in Tier-1 Cities, But the National Picture Is Worsening

The property sector remains the single largest drag on the Chinese economy, and the data show no sign of a turnaround. Property investment is expected to contract by 25.0 percent year-on-year in June, worse than the 24.3 percent decline in May. On a year-to-date basis, property investment is down 18.2 percent.

There is, however, a subtle but important divergence within the property market. In tier-1 cities, existing home prices have shown signs of stabilization. Shanghai has recorded three consecutive months of month-on-month price gains, with a cumulative increase of 1.9 percent from the January lows. Shenzhen posted a 0.6 percent gain in May, supported by the easing of home purchase restrictions. Beijing and Guangzhou have also seen mild price upticks.

But this is a narrow, localized phenomenon. In most lower-tier cities, housing price declines have worsened. The national average home price fell by 0.26 percent month-on-month in May, after a period of narrowing declines. The Iceberg index, which tracks the lowest listing prices, recorded a 0.3 percent decline in May and a 0.4 percent decline in the first two weeks of June, suggesting that the rebound in tier-1 cities is not spreading.

What does this mean for the broader economy? The property downturn is not just a real estate problem. It is a wealth effect problem, a local government finance problem, and a household confidence problem. The stabilization in tier-1 cities is welcome, but it is insufficient to reverse the national trend. Until property prices stabilize in the lower-tier cities that account for the bulk of the housing stock, the drag on consumption and investment will persist.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Export Boom and the Risk of a Sudden Reversal

The report provides a detailed forecast for exports, projecting growth of 16.2 percent in June, only a modest slowdown from 19.4 percent in May. The drivers are clear: the AI supercycle, surging chip prices, and strong demand from developed markets. The manufacturing PMIs for the US and Japan have strengthened, and the RatingDog PMI, which surveys exporters in coastal regions, remains elevated at 51.5.

But the report does not fully address the sustainability of this export boom. Several open questions remain.

First, how much of the export strength is volume-driven versus price-driven? The report notes that roughly half of the export surge is due to chip and electronics prices. If chip prices stabilize or decline, the nominal export growth rate could fall sharply, even if volumes hold up. Second, what happens if the AI supercycle peaks? The semiconductor cycle is notoriously volatile, and a correction in chip demand would have an outsized impact on China's export numbers. Third, the Middle East conflict has disrupted factory operations in the oil and chemical sectors. If tensions escalate further, the supply-side disruptions could spread to other industries, reducing export capacity.

The report also does not fully explore the feedback loop between exports and domestic demand. The export boom is creating a two-speed economy, where resources and capital flow to export-oriented sectors while domestic-facing industries starve. This is not just a cyclical phenomenon. It is a structural distortion that could persist even after the export cycle turns. The risk is that when exports eventually slow, the domestic economy will be too weak to absorb the shock, leading to a sharper downturn than the current forecasts imply.

## A Decision Framework for Readers: How to Interpret the Conflicting Signals

For investors and business leaders trying to navigate the current environment, the conflicting signals from the Chinese economy can be paralyzing. The production-side data suggest a moderate slowdown. The demand-side data suggest a contraction. The export data suggest strength. The property data suggest a bifurcated market. The inflation data suggest imported price pressures. How do you make sense of this?

The framework I recommend is to focus on three leading indicators that will determine the trajectory over the next two quarters.

First, monitor the pace of government bond issuance. The report notes that bond issuance has been lower than last year, constraining fiscal spending. If Beijing accelerates bond issuance in the coming months, it would signal a commitment to supporting domestic demand. If issuance remains sluggish, the fiscal drag will continue.

Second, watch the property market in lower-tier cities. The stabilization in tier-1 cities is a positive signal, but it is not enough. The national average home price and the Iceberg index are better indicators of the broader trend. If price declines continue to widen, the property drag on the economy will intensify.

Third, track the real export volume, not just the nominal value. The chip price effect is inflating the headline export numbers. If you strip out the price effect, the real export growth is much lower. When chip prices eventually normalize, the nominal export growth rate will drop, and the true state of external demand will be revealed.

The central question for investors is whether the policy response will be sufficient to bridge the gap between the export-driven and domestic-driven parts of the economy. The report suggests that Beijing will likely step up fiscal spending. But the scale and timing remain uncertain. Until there is clear evidence of a coordinated fiscal push, the cautious stance is to assume that the domestic demand weakness will persist, and that the export tailwind is fragile.

# China’s Property Market Is Not Recovering — It Is Restructuring, and That Changes Which Stocks You Should Own

The widespread interpretation of China’s property sector as either in crisis or on the verge of a V-shaped recovery misses the central strategic reality of 2026. The market is not healing; it is undergoing a structural reallocation of capital, land, and demand that will permanently separate winners from losers. Based on conference takeaways from 19 major property firms and detailed first-half earnings signals, the evidence points to a market where aggregate sales remain under pressure — down 15% year-on-year in the first five months of 2026 — yet a select group of developers is posting double-digit growth. This divergence is not cyclical noise. It is the early contour of a new equilibrium in which land scarcity, product segmentation, and recurring income streams determine corporate survival and shareholder returns.

The sell-off in property stocks during late June — triggered by a 10% month-on-month decline in secondary market volumes and tighter liquidity — creates a tactical entry point, but only for those who understand that the old playbook of broad-based sector exposure is obsolete. The data from the first half of 2026 forces a difficult question: are you betting on the sector, or are you betting on the specific companies that have already adapted to a world of permanently lower land supply, compressed margins, and a K-shaped demand curve?

This article translates the key findings from a recent investment conference into a decision framework. It does not merely summarize the data. It argues that the most important insight is not about sales volumes or policy expectations — it is about the fundamental shift in how value is created in Chinese real estate. The companies that will outperform are those that have already moved beyond the traditional development model into a hybrid structure combining selective land acquisition, recurring income from commercial assets, and disciplined capital allocation. The rest will trade at persistent discounts to net asset value, trapped by legacy inventory and margin erosion.

## Sales Divergence Is Not a Blip — It Reflects a Permanent Shift in Market Structure

The headline figure that sector sales fell 15% year-on-year in the first five months of 2026 obscures a far more important pattern. Five major developers — including China Overseas Land & Investment, Jinmao, China Resources Land, COGO, and CMSK — posted sales growth exceeding 10% during the same period. This is not because they launched more projects. In fact, new launches across the sector were reduced. These companies achieved growth through better execution on existing project sales in core cities, where demand is more resilient and pricing power is stronger.

This divergence matters because it is not temporary. The underlying driver is a structural reduction in land supply. In the first five months of 2026, land acquisition by listed developers fell 48% year-on-year in value terms. Across 300 cities, land supply by gross floor area declined 32%, reaching a 20-year low. When land is scarce, the ability to acquire quality plots at reasonable prices becomes the single most important competitive advantage. Developers that paused land acquisition in the first half — such as COLI, Jinmao, C&D International, and Greentown — are not being conservative. They are waiting for the second half of 2026, when land supply is expected to increase and prices may cool. This is a deliberate, strategic sequencing of capital deployment.

The implication for investors is clear. The companies that can time their land purchases to coincide with lower prices, while maintaining sales momentum through better product execution, will emerge from this cycle with stronger balance sheets and higher market share. The companies that are forced to buy land at elevated prices — or that cannot sell existing inventory without deep discounts — will face a multi-year drag on returns. The sector is not recovering uniformly. It is bifurcating.

## The Earnings Shock in First-Half 2026 Will Separate Companies That Can Absorb It from Those That Cannot

The conference data reveals a looming earnings disappointment that the market has not fully priced. For several major developers, first-half 2026 earnings are expected to decline 35-40% year-on-year. The reason is a high base effect: in first-half 2025, COLI booked 68% of its full-year profit, Jinmao booked 77%, and similar concentrations occurred at Yuexiu, Seazen, and Poly. Combined with ongoing margin pressure from inventory sales, some developers — including Longfor and Poly Property — are expected to report losses for the period.

The temptation is to treat this as a one-time accounting distortion. That would be a mistake. The high base in first-half 2025 was itself a product of an unusual profit recognition pattern that is unlikely to repeat. More importantly, the margin pressure from inventory sales is not cyclical; it reflects the structural challenge of selling projects that were designed and costed under a different regime of land prices and regulatory expectations. The “old standard” projects — those approved before the second half of 2024 — are now the weakest segment of the market, as a conference speaker noted. They suffer from lower gross floor area efficiency compared to new-standard homes, making them harder to sell without price cuts.

The key question for investors is not whether earnings will be weak — they will be. The question is which companies have the balance sheet strength and alternative income streams to absorb the shock without cutting dividends or raising distressed capital. China Resources Land stands out in this regard. Its recurring income grew 8% year-on-year in the first five months of 2026, and it is expected to book more than 2 billion renminbi in gains from the REIT spin-off of a Chengdu mall. Its first-half earnings decline is estimated at only 10%, far less severe than peers. Beike, the housing platform, is also an outlier, with estimated earnings growth of 26% year-on-year driven by strong second-quarter gross transaction value.

The earnings season will be a stress test. Companies that can report single-digit earnings declines or outright growth will be rewarded. Those that report losses or steep drops without a credible recovery path will see their valuation discounts widen further.

## Land Scarcity Is Reshaping the Competitive Landscape Faster than Policy Can Respond

The most underappreciated structural shift in China’s property market is the dramatic reduction in land supply. In the first five months of 2026, land acquisition by the 12 major developers tracked in the conference data totaled 134 billion renminbi, down from 257 billion in the same period of 2025 — a decline of nearly half. This is not a temporary pullback driven by developer caution. It is a supply-side constraint imposed by local governments that are themselves reducing land sales in response to weaker demand and inventory overhangs.

The consequence is that land prices in the most desirable locations have been pushed up, even as overall transaction volumes fall. Developers that want to maintain their market position must either pay premium prices for scarce quality plots or wait for the second half of the year when supply is expected to increase. The strategic divergence between developers is already visible. Yuexiu and Poly China have been active in first-half land acquisition. CR Land has been consistently strong, particularly in Shenzhen. C&D International accelerated its land purchases in June, focusing on Shenzhen and Hangzhou. Meanwhile, COLI, Jinmao, Greentown, and others have deliberately slowed, conserving capital for the second-half window.

This creates a fascinating analytical puzzle for investors. Which strategy is correct? The developers that acquire land early risk overpaying in a market where prices are elevated. The developers that wait risk missing the best locations if supply does not materialize as expected. The answer likely depends on the specific city market. In Shenzhen, where secondary market inventory is not as distressed as headline numbers suggest — 50% of listed units have been on the market for more than a year, meaning they are not real sellers — demand for quality new homes remains strong. In Changsha, by contrast, with 22 months of inventory and a 20-year low in new land supply, home prices may stay at the bottom for three years or more.

The bottom line is that land strategy is now the single most important determinant of future returns. Investors need to assess not just a developer’s sales growth, but its land acquisition timing, geography, and cost discipline.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Policy Dependency and the REIT Exit

The conference data and analysis provide a rich picture of developer behavior and market dynamics, but they leave two critical questions unresolved.

First, the report assumes a supportive policy tone from the July Politburo meeting, citing a recent article in a state-affiliated publication that called for stabilizing the property market. But what form would such support take? The conference speaker explicitly stated that he does not expect any nationwide policy stimulus in 2026. If that is correct, then the supportive tone may be limited to incremental measures — further relaxation of home purchase restrictions in tier-two cities, or faster approval of urban renewal projects. The market may be pricing in more aggressive intervention than is realistic.

Second, the report highlights the growing importance of REITs as a source of recurring income and capital recycling. CR Land’s REIT spin-off of a Chengdu mall generated significant gains. Longfor reported 9% year-on-year same-store sales growth in its commercial portfolio. But the REIT market in China remains relatively small and illiquid compared to mature markets. Can it scale quickly enough to provide a meaningful exit for developers that need to monetize their commercial assets? And will the valuation multiples in the REIT market be attractive enough to incentivize more spin-offs? These questions are not answered in the report, but they will determine whether the recurring income story is a genuine transformation or a temporary accounting benefit.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Filters to Separate Winners from Laggards

Based on the patterns in the data, investors should evaluate property stocks through three sequential filters.

Filter one: Recurring income coverage. Companies that have a meaningful and growing stream of rental income, property management fees, and REIT gains will be far better positioned to absorb the earnings shock from development margins. CR Land and Longfor are the clearest examples, but investors should verify the quality and growth trajectory of each company’s recurring portfolio. A developer with less than 10% of earnings from recurring sources should be treated as a pure development play, with all the cyclical risk that entails.

Filter two: Land acquisition discipline and timing. The developers that paused land buying in the first half of 2026 are making a calculated bet that second-half supply will be more favorable. Investors need to assess whether that bet is justified by the company’s balance sheet strength and its track record of executing in competitive land auctions. Companies that overpaid for land in 2024 or early 2025 are now carrying inventory that is difficult to sell without margin compression. Companies that have maintained discipline will have the flexibility to acquire when prices are more attractive.

Filter three: City concentration and product positioning. The market is K-shaped. High-end luxury and low-end older units are outperforming mid-tier projects. Developers that are concentrated in core cities — Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Hangzhou, Chengdu — have a structural advantage. Developers with significant exposure to tier-two cities with weak demand, such as Changsha, face a prolonged period of price stagnation. Within each city, the ability to offer new-standard homes with higher gross floor area efficiency is a competitive differentiator.

Applying these filters will narrow the universe of investable property stocks significantly. The sector as a whole may trade at a 60-70% discount to net asset value, but that discount is not uniform. The companies that pass all three filters deserve a premium. The ones that fail on multiple filters may be value traps, regardless of how cheap they appear.

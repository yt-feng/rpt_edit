# China's Property Market Is Not Recovering—It Is Restructuring, and Only Prepared Investors Will Benefit

The dominant narrative around China's property sector has oscillated between panic and premature optimism for nearly five years. After the recent sell-off triggered by a 10% month-on-month decline in weekly secondary volume and tighter liquidity, many investors are once again questioning whether the bottom has fallen out. The answer is more nuanced than a simple yes or no. Based on a comprehensive analysis of 19 property firms that recently participated in a major industry conference, the evidence points to a market that is not broadly recovering but is undergoing a fundamental restructuring. The thesis is clear: the aggregate numbers will remain weak, but a narrow set of well-capitalized, operationally agile developers will emerge stronger, while the rest continue to deteriorate. The next six months will be the proving ground for this divergence, and investors must shift from a sector-wide bet to a precision stock-picking exercise.

Why now matters more than at any point in the last 18 months is a matter of converging signals. First, the first half of 2026 earnings season is shaping up to be a brutal reality check, with several major names facing profit declines of 35-40% year-on-year due to a high base effect from the first half of 2025 and ongoing margin compression. Second, the land market has entered a paradoxical phase: reduced government supply has pushed up prices for premium plots, creating a window for disciplined buyers in the second half of 2026. Third, the policy backdrop is shifting again. An authoritative article published in mid-June called for stabilizing the property market, signaling that the upcoming Politburo meeting in July will likely adopt a supportive tone. For investors, this is not a moment to flee the sector but to recalibrate expectations and identify the survivors that will lead the next cycle.

## Five Developers Already Proving the "K-Shaped" Thesis with Double-Digit Sales Growth Despite a Weak Market

The most important data point from the recent conference is not the sector average but the outliers. In the first five months of 2026, the overall sector contracted sales were down 15% year-on-year, a figure that would normally justify bearish sentiment. However, five specific names—including China Overseas Land & Investment (COLI), Jinmao, China Resources Land (CRL), COGO, and CMSK—posted more than 10% year-on-year sales growth. This is not a statistical fluke. These companies achieved this growth despite having fewer new project launches during the period, which means their success came from better execution on existing project sales in core cities.

The strategic implication is profound. In a market where total demand is shrinking, these developers are capturing market share by outperforming on product quality, location selection, and brand trust. They are not waiting for a rising tide to lift all boats; they are actively taking share from weaker competitors. For investors, this means the traditional approach of buying the sector on valuation alone is obsolete. The dispersion between winners and losers will only widen as the second half of 2026 unfolds, when these same five names, plus at least one more (C&D International), are expected to accelerate new sales launches from September. The combination of a low base from July 2025 and a pipeline of fresh supply gives them a clear growth trajectory that the rest of the sector cannot replicate.

## The Land Market Contradiction Creates a Once-in-a-Cycle Entry Point for Disciplined Developers

One of the most counterintuitive dynamics in the current market is the behavior of land acquisition. In the first five months of 2026, land acquisition by listed developers fell by 48% year-on-year in value terms. This is not because developers are unwilling to buy; it is because land supply in key cities has been deliberately constrained by local governments. Across 300 cities, the gross floor area of land supplied fell by 32% year-on-year, reaching a 20-year low. This artificial scarcity has pushed up prices for the limited quality plots that have been released.

The strategic question for developers is when to re-enter. The data shows a clear divide. Names like Yuexiu and Poly China were aggressive in the first half of 2026, while COLI, Jinmao, C&D, and Greentown deliberately slowed their acquisition pace, waiting for the second half when land supply is expected to increase and prices to moderate. This is a calculated bet on market timing. These companies have strong balance sheets from their 2025 performance and are positioning themselves to acquire land at more attractive valuations when the window opens. CRL, by contrast, has been consistently strong in land acquisition, particularly in Shenzhen, demonstrating that a steady, disciplined approach can work even in a constrained market.

For investors, this divergence in land strategy is a leading indicator of future performance. Developers that overpay for land in the current tight market risk compressing their future margins, while those that wait for the second-half supply wave could secure better economics. The winners will be those that combine patience with the financial firepower to act decisively when the opportunity arises. This is not a market for the faint-hearted or the over-leveraged.

## First-Half 2026 Earnings Will Be a Cleansing Event That Separates Structural Winners from Cyclical Losers

The upcoming earnings season for the first half of 2026 is widely expected to be painful, but the pain will not be evenly distributed. The report projects an average earnings decline of 35-40% year-on-year for a group of major names, driven by a high base in the first half of 2025 and ongoing margin pressure from inventory sales. COLI, for example, realized 68% of its full-year 2025 profits in the first half of that year, creating an almost impossible comparison. Jinmao faces a similar challenge at 77%. For Longfor and Poly Property, the situation is more severe, with expected losses due to margin erosion.

However, this earnings season should not be read as a confirmation of sector-wide distress. It is a cleansing event that will reset expectations and expose the structural differences between companies. China Resources Land stands out as a relative safe haven, with estimated first-half earnings down only 10% year-on-year. This resilience comes from two sources: a stable recurring income stream from its property management and rental businesses, which grew 8% year-on-year in the first five months, and a significant one-time gain of over RMB 2 billion from the REIT spin-off of a Chengdu mall. Beike, the housing platform, is another bright spot, with estimated earnings growth of 26% year-on-year driven by better-than-expected second-quarter gross transaction value.

The so-what for investors is clear. The earnings season will create a buying opportunity in stocks that are sold off indiscriminately. The key is to distinguish between companies whose earnings weakness is structural—driven by poor asset quality, high leverage, or weak market positioning—and those whose weakness is purely cyclical or base-effect-driven. The latter group, which includes CRL and Beike, and potentially COLI and Jinmao after the land market re-entry, will offer attractive entry points for long-term investors.

## The Physical Market Is Bottoming, but the Shape of the Recovery Is "K-Shaped" and Spatially Concentrated

A critical perspective from a leading industry expert at the conference provides the most realistic framework for understanding the physical market. The assessment is that the market has improved year-on-year in 2026, but a rapid turnaround is unlikely. The bottoming cycle is expected to extend through 2026 to 2030, led by core cities including Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Hangzhou, and Chengdu. The recent "little spring" rally, which peaked in late May, has already moderated due to equity market weakness and demand pull-forward from the March-to-May period.

The most important structural observation is the "K-shaped" divergence in the market. High-end luxury properties and low-end older or smaller units are outperforming, while mid-tier projects are struggling. This is a direct consequence of buyer behavior: high-net-worth individuals are still willing to pay for quality and scarcity, while first-time buyers and down-traders are seeking affordability in the secondary market for smaller units. The weakest segment is the "old standard" projects built before the second half of 2024, which now suffer from lower gross floor area efficiency compared to newer "new standard" homes.

For investors, this means that not all real estate assets are created equal. Developers with exposure to high-end luxury in core cities and those that have pivoted to the "new standard" product specifications are better positioned. The secondary market, which is now stable, provides a floor for prices, but new home sales growth will remain constrained by reduced land supply. The policy outlook, according to this expert, does not include nationwide stimulus in 2026, but significant developments are expected in the REIT market and urban renewal projects, which could provide alternative exit routes for developers with quality assets.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer Yet: The Liquidity Feedback Loop and Policy Implementation Risk

While the conference takeaways provide a comprehensive snapshot, they leave several critical questions unresolved. The first is the nature of the liquidity feedback loop between the equity market and the property market. The report notes that equity market weakness has contributed to the moderation in property visits and subscriptions since late May. If the stock market sell-off deepens or becomes prolonged, it could further dampen buyer sentiment and delay the recovery. Conversely, a stabilization in equities could provide a tailwind for property sales. The report does not model this interdependence, leaving investors to assess the macro environment independently.

The second unresolved question is the implementation risk around the expected supportive tone from the July Politburo meeting. While the article from mid-June signals a desire to stabilize the market, the specific policy measures remain unclear. Will the support come in the form of further interest rate cuts, relaxation of purchase restrictions in tier-1 cities, or direct government purchases of unsold inventory? The market has been disappointed by vague policy statements before, and the gap between rhetoric and execution remains a significant risk. Investors should watch for concrete actions rather than words.

Finally, the report does not fully address the implications of the management changes expected in the second half of 2026, including a new CEO for Greentown and a chairman change for COLI. Leadership transitions at major developers can create strategic uncertainty or, alternatively, signal a renewal of corporate strategy. The outcome will depend on the specific individuals appointed and their strategic priorities, which remain unknowable at this stage.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Next 12 Months in China Property

Given the complexity and divergence in the market, investors need a clear framework for decision-making. Based on the analysis, the following three-step approach is recommended:

Step one: Assess balance sheet strength and land acquisition discipline. The primary differentiator in this market is financial resilience. Developers with low leverage, strong recurring income streams, and a track record of disciplined land acquisition—such as CRL, COLI, and C&D—are better positioned to weather the earnings downturn and capitalize on the second-half land window. Companies that are over-leveraged or that overpaid for land in the first half of 2026 should be avoided.

Step two: Evaluate product and geographic positioning. The "K-shaped" recovery means that exposure to high-end luxury in core cities is a significant advantage. Developers with a strong presence in Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen, and Hangzhou, and those that have upgraded to "new standard" product specifications, are more likely to maintain sales momentum. Conversely, developers with heavy exposure to tier-2 and tier-3 cities, or with large inventories of "old standard" projects, face a prolonged destocking challenge.

Step three: Monitor the policy and macro environment for timing. The second half of 2026 offers a potential entry point, with more new sales launches from September, a supportive policy tone from the July Politburo, and the land supply window opening. However, investors should be prepared for volatility around the earnings season in August and should use any indiscriminate sell-offs to accumulate positions in the structural winners identified in steps one and two. The key is to be selective, patient, and disciplined.

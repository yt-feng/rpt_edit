# Delhi's EV Mandate Is Not Just a Local Policy—It Marks the Beginning of CNG's Terminal Value Reassessment

India's City Gas Distribution (CGD) sector has long enjoyed a comfortable narrative: CNG is the clean bridge fuel, government policy supports it, and urban auto-fuel demand grows predictably. That narrative just broke. On 29 June, the Delhi Cabinet approved its EV Policy 2026-30, a four-year, INR150 billion program that shifts the paradigm from incentives to hard mandates. From 1 January 2027, no new CNG or petrol auto-rickshaws or small goods carriers can be registered in Delhi. From April 2028, the same applies to two-wheelers. This is not a gentle nudge toward electrification; it is a regulatory door slamming shut on internal combustion—including CNG—for new vehicle purchases in India's capital.

The strategic significance extends far beyond Delhi's municipal boundaries. This policy introduces what a global investment bank report calls "terminal value risk" for CNG, particularly for companies like Indraprastha Gas Limited (IGL), where auto-CNG accounts for roughly 65% of volumes. The immediate earnings impact is limited because the existing fleet will continue to operate. But the forward-looking implication is stark: the growth engine of the CGD sector's most valuable segment is being legislated into decline. For investors, the question is no longer whether EV adoption will pressure CNG, but how quickly the mandate model will spread to other states and how CGD companies can reinvent themselves before their core business erodes.

This article argues that the Delhi EV Policy 2026 represents a structural inflection point for the Indian CGD sector. It is not a one-off event. It is a template that other state governments will likely follow, and it forces a fundamental reassessment of how to value CGD stocks—particularly those with high auto-CNG exposure. The report provides the data; this article provides the strategic lens.

## The Shift from Incentive-Led to Mandate-Led Policy Changes the Risk Calculus for CGD Investors

The difference between the 2020 Delhi EV Policy and the 2026 version is not a matter of degree; it is a matter of kind. The 2020 policy was incentive-led: it offered subsidies and tax waivers and hoped that consumers would choose electric. The result was a miss—Delhi targeted 25% EV penetration in new vehicle registrations by 2024 but achieved only about 13%. The 2026 policy abandons hope for compulsion. It sets hard registration bans with specific dates, and it backs those bans with subsidies that are now supplementary rather than primary.

For a CGD company like IGL, this change in policy architecture is existential. Under the incentive-led model, the company could reasonably assume that CNG would retain its competitive advantage on cost and convenience for years. The new mandate-led model removes consumer choice entirely for new vehicle purchases. Even if a consumer prefers CNG on economics, they will not be able to register a new CNG auto-rickshaw after 1 January 2027. The demand funnel for new CNG vehicles in Delhi is legislated to zero for key segments.

The "so what" for investors is that the valuation models for CGD stocks must now incorporate a step-change in auto-CNG volume risk. Previously, the risk was gradual substitution. Now, for IGL, it is a phased but deterministic decline in new vehicle additions. The existing fleet provides a volume buffer for several years, but the growth trajectory has a ceiling that is now visible and shrinking. The bank report's phrase "terminal value risk" is precisely correct: the long-term value of IGL's auto-CNG business is being discounted by a regulatory decision, not by market dynamics.

## IGL Faces the Most Direct Exposure, and Its Mitigation Options Are All Lower-Margin Businesses

The bank report's analysis of IGL is clear and sobering. Auto-CNG constitutes approximately 65% of IGL's volumes. Within that, three-wheelers have already been declining structurally—less than 20% of new three-wheeler sales in Delhi are CNG, as EV adoption has already taken hold. Two-wheelers are not material. The core risk is the car and taxi segment, which forms the bulk of the auto-CNG volume. The registration ban on CNG three-wheelers and small goods carriers from January 2027 will not immediately crater IGL's volumes, but it will stop the growth of the highest-value segment of its business.

The strategic question for IGL is what replaces that growth. The bank report identifies three avenues: geographic expansion into Uttar Pradesh and Haryana through its joint ventures, PNG household connections, and LNG diversification. All three are valid, but all three are structurally lower-margin than the Delhi auto-CNG business. PNG residential connections have lower margins per unit of gas sold and require long payback periods. Geographic expansion into UP and Haryana gas faces its own regulatory and competitive dynamics. LNG diversification is capital-intensive and exposes the company to global commodity price risk that its core CGD business does not have.

The implication is that IGL's return on capital employed is likely to compress over time as the mix shifts away from high-margin auto-CNG toward lower-margin alternatives. The bank report maintains a Buy rating on IGL with a target price of INR200, but the narrative has shifted from growth to defense. Investors need to assess whether the current valuation adequately discounts this structural headwind, or whether the market is still pricing IGL as if the old growth trajectory remains intact.

## The Contagion Risk for Other CGDs Is Real but Differentiated—Gujarat Gas Is Least Exposed, MGL Has Time

The Delhi policy's most important second-order implication is whether other states will follow. The bank report addresses this directly for Mahanagar Gas (MGL) and Gujarat Gas. MGL operates predominantly in Maharashtra, and the report notes that if Maharashtra follows Delhi's precedent, MGL could face similar terminal risk. However, the report also points out that Mumbai and Thane have not even banned diesel vehicles, which Delhi banned more than a decade ago. This suggests that Maharashtra's regulatory timeline may be slower, and that the first regulatory hits may fall on diesel rather than CNG.

Furthermore, Mumbai's coastal geography gives it better pollution dispersion than landlocked Delhi, which may reduce the political urgency for EV mandates. The report prefers MGL among CGDs under coverage due to faster volume growth and lower EV-mandate risk in the near to medium term. This is a relative call, not an absolute one. The question is not whether MGL will eventually face similar pressure, but how much time it has to adapt.

Gujarat Gas is in the strongest position. Its volume mix is dominated by industrial and commercial PNG, with auto-CNG representing only a small share. Even if Gujarat implements a similar policy, the impact on Gujarat Gas would be minimal. The company's growth story is tied to industrial expansion and gas penetration in the industrial sector, not to urban auto-fuel demand. For investors looking for CGD exposure without auto-CNG risk, Gujarat Gas is the clear structural winner.

The strategic insight here is that the CGD sector is not monolithic. The auto-CNG risk is company-specific and geography-specific. A decision framework that treats all CGD stocks as equally exposed to EV mandates will misallocate capital. The differentiation lies in the proportion of auto-CNG in the volume mix, the regulatory posture of the state government, and the company's ability to pivot to industrial and residential segments.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Speed of Fleet Turnover, the Role of Scrappage, and the Political Economy of Mandate Spread

The bank report provides excellent data on the policy's structure and its immediate implications for covered companies. But it leaves several important questions open—questions that a thoughtful investor needs to answer before making a final decision.

First, the speed of fleet turnover. The policy bans new registrations of CNG vehicles, but the existing fleet will continue to operate. How quickly will that fleet turn over? Auto-rickshaws and taxis in Delhi have average lifespans of 10-15 years. If the ban only affects new vehicles, the volume decline for IGL could be gradual—perhaps 5-7% per year from the auto-CNG segment, rather than a cliff. The report does not model this decay rate explicitly, and it matters enormously for near-term earnings.

Second, the scrappage incentive. The policy offers INR 100,000 for scrapping a BS-IV or older Delhi-registered car and purchasing an EV under INR 3 million. This could accelerate fleet turnover. If a significant number of existing CNG taxi owners take the scrappage incentive and switch to EVs, the volume decline could be faster than the natural fleet decay rate. The report mentions the scrappage incentive but does not estimate its uptake. This is a key variable.

Third, the political economy of mandate spread. Delhi is a Union Territory with a unique governance structure. Will other states like Maharashtra, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, or Karnataka adopt similar policies? The report suggests it is possible but does not provide a framework for assessing the probability. Factors such as state fiscal capacity, the strength of the auto and fuel lobbies, local pollution levels, and the political cycle all matter. An investor needs a view on this to assess the tail risk for MGL and other CGDs.

These open questions do not invalidate the report's thesis. They define the boundaries of what is known and what requires further analysis. The report's core insight—that Delhi's shift to mandates creates terminal value risk for auto-CNG—is robust. But the magnitude and timing of that risk depend on variables that the report does not fully resolve.

## A Decision Framework for CGD Investors: Three Questions to Determine Exposure and Response

For investors trying to translate this report into actionable decisions, I propose a three-question framework that is mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.

**Question 1: What proportion of the CGD company's volume is auto-CNG, and in which city?** This is the primary screen. IGL at 65% auto-CNG in Delhi is high risk. Gujarat Gas with minimal auto-CNG is low risk. MGL falls in between, with significant auto-CNG but in a regulatory environment that is likely slower to move. The answer to this question determines the magnitude of the potential volume impact.

**Question 2: How long is the regulatory runway?** For IGL, the runway is short: the first registration ban hits in January 2027, less than seven months from the policy's announcement. For MGL, the runway is longer but uncertain. The investor must assess the probability of Maharashtra adopting a similar policy within 2-3 years versus 5-7 years. This is a judgment call, but it can be informed by tracking state-level pollution data, EV adoption rates, and political statements.

**Question 3: Can the company replace auto-CNG margins with other businesses?** IGL's mitigation options—geographic expansion, PNG, LNG—are all lower margin. This means that even if volume is maintained, profitability per unit will decline. Gujarat Gas does not need replacement because its auto-CNG exposure is small. MGL may have more time to build alternative revenue streams, but the report does not detail what those would be. The investor must assess whether the company's strategic plan adequately addresses this margin compression.

This framework does not provide mechanical answers. It forces the investor to make explicit assumptions about regulatory timing, fleet turnover, and margin dynamics. That is the point. The report provides the data; the framework provides the structure for interpretation.

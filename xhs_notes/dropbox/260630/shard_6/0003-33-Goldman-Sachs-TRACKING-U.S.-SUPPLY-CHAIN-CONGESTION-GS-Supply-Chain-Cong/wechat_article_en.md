# Supply Chain Normalization Is Real, But the Next Disruption Is Already Being Priced In

The most dangerous assumption in logistics today is that the post-pandemic supply chain normalization represents a stable equilibrium. It does not. According to the latest data from a global investment bank's supply chain congestion tracking, the weekly bottleneck scale has remained at a reading of 2 through late June 2026, a level that is effectively indistinguishable from the pre-COVID baseline of February 2020. The composite index itself declined 7% week-over-week, and the absolute level of congestion is now a fraction of what it was during the peak of the 2021-2022 crisis, when the scale registered a 10. On the surface, this is a story of recovery. Below the surface, it is a story of fragile equilibrium and latent risk.

The report's data shows that the system has healed in almost every dimension. Container ship backlogs on the West Coast remain at one vessel. Rail intermodal traffic growth has accelerated to 14% year-over-year, driven by strong performance from both major Class 1 railroads. Chassis dwell times, while ticking up slightly in the most recent week, are dramatically lower than during the peak. Ocean shipping rates from East Asia to the U.S. West Coast have risen 19% week-over-week, but they are only 3% above year-ago levels—a far cry from the multiples of normal pricing seen in 2021. Door-to-door shipping times from China to the U.S. have stabilized at 47 days, down from over 80 days at the worst of the crisis.

Yet the report's own framing contains a critical tension. It notes that the key question remains the impact of tariffs and geopolitical conflicts on demand and the timing of freight flows. This is not a backward-looking question. It is a forward-looking one, and the data does not yet answer it. The supply chain has normalized into a system that is more transparent, more monitored, and more reactive than ever before. But it has not normalized into a system that is resilient to the specific shocks that are now gathering on the horizon. The next disruption will not look like the last one. It will be different in kind, not just in degree, and the tools that worked to measure the last crisis may not capture the next one until it is already here.

## The Composite Index Masks a Divergence Between the West Coast and the East Coast That Has Strategic Implications

One of the most revealing patterns in the report's weekly data is the growing divergence between West Coast and East Coast port conditions. The West Coast container ship backlog held steady at one vessel, a level that signals near-perfect fluidity. The East Coast and Gulf Coast backlog, however, ticked up from three to four vessels. This may seem like a minor movement, but in the context of a system that has been hovering near its pre-pandemic baseline, any directional change on the East Coast warrants attention.

The divergence is not random. It reflects structural shifts in import routing that began during the pandemic and have not fully reversed. Shippers diversified away from the West Coast during the worst of the congestion, routing more cargo through East Coast and Gulf Coast ports to avoid bottlenecks in Los Angeles and Long Beach. That shift has partly persisted, even as West Coast conditions have normalized. The East Coast ports now handle a larger share of total container volume than they did before 2020, and their infrastructure has not fully caught up. The result is a system where the West Coast is fluid but the East Coast is showing early signs of pressure.

The strategic implication is straightforward: companies that have optimized their supply chains around West Coast fluidity may be underestimating the risk of East Coast congestion. The report's data suggests that the East Coast backlog is still low in absolute terms, but the direction of travel matters. If tariffs or geopolitical disruptions further shift trade flows toward the East Coast, the current backlog of four vessels could become five, then six, then ten. The system is not yet under stress, but the conditions for stress are quietly building in a different geography than the one most executives are watching.

## Rail Intermodal Volume Is Strong, But Speed and Dwell Tell a Different Story About System Efficiency

The report highlights that West Coast Class 1 rail intermodal traffic growth accelerated to 14% year-over-year in the most recent week, up from 11% the prior week. This is a headline that suggests robust demand and smooth operations. But the supporting metrics complicate that narrative. Union Pacific's intermodal velocity declined 1.6% year-over-year, and BNSF's velocity declined 6.1%. Terminal dwell times increased for both railroads, with Union Pacific rising from 19.4 hours to 19.9 hours and BNSF rising from 22.1 hours to 22.4 hours.

This pattern—rising volume combined with declining speed and rising dwell—is a classic early indicator of congestion. It does not mean the system is broken. The absolute levels of dwell time are well below the crisis peaks of 2022, when rail container dwell at San Pedro's Bay reached approximately 16 days. But the direction is more important than the level. Volume is growing faster than the system can process it, and the gap is being absorbed by slower train speeds and longer waiting times at terminals.

The so-what here is that rail efficiency is a leading indicator for broader supply chain strain. When trains slow down, containers sit longer at rail ramps. When containers sit longer, chassis utilization drops because equipment is tied up. When chassis are scarce, truckers wait longer, and the cost of drayage rises. The report's data shows that chassis street dwell times for 20-foot containers increased from 4.4 days to 5.1 days in the most recent week, and for 40/45-foot containers from 6.3 days to 6.5 days. These are small movements, but they are consistent with the rail velocity story. The system is not yet congested, but it is becoming less fluid at the margins, and those margins are where cost and risk accumulate.

## Ocean Shipping Rates Are Signaling Something the Volume Data Does Not Yet Confirm

The report notes that ocean container shipping rates from East Asia to the U.S. West Coast rose 19% week-over-week and are now 3% above year-ago levels. This is a notable shift. For much of the past year, rates have been either flat or declining, reflecting the post-pandemic normalization of capacity. A 19% weekly jump is not normal, and it warrants scrutiny.

The report does not provide a definitive explanation for the rate increase, and that is precisely the point. Rate spikes in ocean shipping can be driven by demand surges, capacity reductions, or speculative behavior by carriers. The data on inbound container volumes is mixed. The "Big Three" West Coast ports—Los Angeles, Long Beach, and Oakland—saw inbound loaded containers surge 30% year-over-year in April, the most recent month for which data is available. But the year-over-year comparisons are distorted by the timing of tariff announcements and pre-buying behavior. The May data on inbound containers is not yet available, and the weekly volume data in the report does not provide a clear signal.

The strategic question for readers is whether the rate increase is a temporary blip or the beginning of a new cycle. If it is driven by genuine demand growth, then the current level of congestion may be underestimating the pressure that will build in the second half of 2026. If it is driven by carrier capacity management or short-term disruptions, then the rate spike may reverse as quickly as it appeared. The report's data is ambiguous on this point, and that ambiguity is itself a risk factor. Executives who rely on current rate levels to plan their logistics budgets may be caught off guard if rates continue to rise.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Tariff Cycles and Inventory Behavior

The report acknowledges that tariffs and geopolitical conflicts are the key variables that could disrupt the current normalization, but it does not—and cannot—resolve the uncertainty around how these factors will play out. This is not a criticism of the report. It is a recognition that the data is inherently backward-looking, and the most important questions about the future are not answerable from historical trends.

The specific gap is in understanding how companies are adjusting their inventory strategies in response to tariff uncertainty. The report shows that inbound container volumes at West Coast ports surged 30% year-over-year in April. This is consistent with a pre-tariff buying surge, where importers accelerated shipments to avoid higher costs. But the report does not have data on whether those inventories are being consumed or whether they are piling up in warehouses. The LMI warehousing utilization index for May was 62.9, down from 64.4 in April but still indicating expansion. This suggests that warehouses are still filling up, but at a slower pace.

The open question is what happens when the pre-buying surge ends. If companies have built inventories to hedge against tariffs, they may reduce orders in the second half of 2026, leading to a sudden drop in demand that would reverse the current rate increases and leave carriers with excess capacity. Alternatively, if tariffs are imposed and sustained, companies may restructure their supply chains permanently, shifting sourcing away from China and toward other Asian or nearshore locations. That would create a different pattern of congestion, focused on different ports and different trade lanes.

The report's data cannot distinguish between these scenarios. What it can do is provide a framework for monitoring the early signals. If inbound volumes decline sharply in the coming months, that would support the pre-buying-inventory-correction thesis. If volumes remain elevated and rates continue to rise, that would support the structural-demand thesis. The next few months of data will be decisive, and the report's weekly updates are the best tool available for tracking the inflection point.

## A Decision Framework for Readers: How to Use the Weekly Index as a Strategic Signal, Not Just a Scorecard

The report's weekly bottleneck index is a powerful tool, but it is only useful if it is interpreted correctly. Many executives treat the index as a simple scorecard: if the number is low, the supply chain is fine; if it is high, there is a problem. This is a mistake. The index is most valuable not for its absolute level but for its direction and for the divergence among its subcomponents.

Here is a practical decision framework based on the report's methodology. First, track the index level relative to its pre-pandemic baseline. The current reading of 2 is near that baseline, which means the system is not under broad stress. Second, monitor the subcomponents that are moving in the wrong direction. In the current data, those are East Coast port backlogs, rail dwell times, and chassis street dwell. These are not yet alarming, but they are the canaries. Third, compare the index to ocean rate movements. When the index is stable but rates are rising, as they are now, it suggests that rate increases are being driven by factors outside the physical congestion of the system—likely tariff expectations or carrier pricing power. When the index and rates rise together, that is a genuine congestion signal.

The most important application of this framework is for inventory planning. When the index is low and stable, companies should feel comfortable running lean inventories and relying on just-in-time delivery. When the index begins to rise, or when its subcomponents show consistent deterioration, the prudent response is to build buffer inventory. The current data is ambiguous enough that a moderate increase in safety stock is defensible, but a full-scale inventory build is not yet warranted. The report's weekly updates will provide the signal for when to shift from watchful waiting to active preparation.

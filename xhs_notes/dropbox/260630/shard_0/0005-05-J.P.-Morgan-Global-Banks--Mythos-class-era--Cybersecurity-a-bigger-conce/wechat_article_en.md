# Frontier Models Have Rewritten the Cybersecurity Risk Equation for Banks, and the Market Has Not Priced It Yet

The most dangerous risk facing global banks today is no longer credit defaults or capital inadequacy. It is the silent, compounding exposure to AI-driven cyber attacks that can now discover and exploit vulnerabilities in hours that once took human professionals months or years. A global investment bank report has laid out a stark case: the arrival of frontier models such as Mythos and GPT-5.5 has compressed the timeline for discovering previously unknown "zero-day" vulnerabilities from months and years to hours. This is not a theoretical risk. It is a structural shift in the threat landscape that the market has largely ignored because banks do not disclose enough about their cybersecurity preparedness.

The report argues that liquidity, not credit risk, could be the main trigger in a potential banking crisis triggered by a cyber event. Social media can now amplify deposit runs with unprecedented speed, as witnessed during the CS crisis. The capital framework that regulators obsess over is the wrong lens. What matters is infrastructure resilience, deposit-run liquidity haircut testing, and the ability to patch vulnerabilities faster than attackers can exploit them. The market continues to value banks on earnings and capital ratios, but the next crisis may not respect those metrics.

This article synthesizes the report's core argument, extends its logic into second-order implications for investors and bank management, and identifies the critical questions that remain unanswered. The goal is not to summarize the report but to interpret what its patterns mean for strategic decision-making.

## The Step Change in AI Cyber Capability Has Made a Previously Manageable Risk Uninsurable Without Preparation

The report documents a dramatic acceleration in AI cyber capabilities. The UK AI Security Institute evaluated Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview and found it could autonomously discover and exploit vulnerabilities on exposed networks, executing multi-stage attack chains end to end. It achieved a 73 percent success rate on expert-level hacking tasks that no model could complete before April 2025. GPT-5.5 reached a similar performance level, confirming this is a broader trend, not an isolated model achievement.

What makes this a step change is not just raw capability but speed. The AI Security Institute estimated that the length of cyber tasks AI models could complete had doubled every 4.7 months since late 2024, an acceleration from their previous estimate of 8 months. Mythos and GPT-5.5 substantially exceeded even that faster trend. The report notes that Anthropic has used Mythos Preview to scan more than 1,000 open-source projects and found what it estimates are 6,202 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities. One example: a vulnerability in wolfSSL, an open-source cryptography library used by over 5 billion devices, could allow an attacker to forge certificates and host fake bank websites that look perfectly legitimate to end users.

The implication is clear. The speed of vulnerability discovery now exceeds the speed of patching. Banks with legacy infrastructure, multiple platforms from historical M&A, or vendor-dependent core banking systems are particularly exposed. The report identifies four categories of vulnerable banks: those with old legacy code that cannot be patched quickly, those whose vendors are slow to release patches, those with multiple legacy platforms creating a larger attack surface, and those without access to the latest frontier models for defense.

## Cybersecurity Risk Is a Liquidity Risk, Not a Capital Risk, and Requires a Different Testing Framework

The report makes a crucial distinction that many investors overlook. Cybersecurity risk manifests not as a capital impairment but as a liquidity event. A successful cyber attack that compromises customer data or disrupts payment systems can trigger a deposit run amplified by social media. The CS crisis demonstrated how quickly deposits can flee in the digital age. A cyber-induced run could be even faster because the trigger is not a loss of confidence in the bank's solvency but a loss of confidence in its operational integrity.

The report argues that regulators should focus on infrastructure resilience testing and deposit-run liquidity haircut tests rather than capital requirements. This is a fundamentally different approach. Capital absorbs credit losses. Liquidity absorbs deposit outflows. A bank can be well-capitalized and still fail if it cannot meet a sudden surge in withdrawal requests. The report suggests that banks should be required to back-test their infrastructure and processes for a cyber attack event, similar to how they stress-test their capital positions.

The second-order implication is that bank valuation models need to incorporate a liquidity risk premium for cyber exposure. The report explicitly questions whether investors should assign a 1x higher multiple for banks with "sticky" client deposits and material excess deposits, based on their ability to manage through a cyber crisis. This is not a small adjustment. A 1x multiple difference on a 12x P/E bank represents roughly 8 percent of market capitalization. For European banks trading at 9x P/E, the potential re-rating is even more significant.

## Geographic and Size Asymmetries Create a Diverging Competitive Landscape

The report identifies two key differentiators that will separate winners from losers in this new environment: geography and size. US banks have early access to the most sophisticated AI models because the US is the epicenter of AI development. The report cites Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Amazon Web Services, Anthropic, Apple, Broadcom, Cisco, CrowdStrike, Google, JPM Chase, the Linux Foundation, Microsoft, NVIDIA, and Palo Alto Networks, as an example of collaborative defense. European banks were notably absent from the April announcement. China also appears to be ahead of most peers, while the rest of the world is at a disadvantage.

Size matters because cybersecurity is scalable. The report notes that technology costs average approximately 17 percent of global banks' operating expenses, but absolute spend is what matters for infrastructure resilience. US large-cap banks have higher absolute tech budgets than their global peers. European banks generally operate with lower absolute tech budgets and face a delay in access to the latest developments. This asymmetry will likely widen over time as frontier models become more powerful and expensive to deploy.

The market is already pricing some of this divergence. US global systemically important banks trade at an average of 12.5x P/E for 2028, while European banks trade at 9x and Japanese megabanks at 12x. The report suggests a premium for US banks may be justified due to lower cost of equity as the market factors in better cyber risk preparedness. But the question is whether the current premium is sufficient given the accelerating threat landscape.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered Questions About the Intersection of Cyber Risk and Digital Money

One of the most intriguing sections of the report addresses the vulnerability of decentralized finance and digital money to cyber attacks. The report notes that while cold wallets offer limited protection, the DeFi market remains vulnerable, especially with rapid developments in quantum computing. The Ethereum Foundation has a dedicated post-quantum research team and a structured roadmap targeting 2029 for full post-quantum protection, but that timeline may be too slow.

The report raises but does not fully resolve a critical question: will the increased use of agentic AI in financial services expand the attack surface faster than defenses can be built? Agentic AI systems that can autonomously execute transactions, manage portfolios, or interact with third-party platforms create new vectors for exploitation. The report suggests that the pace of roll-out of agentic AI for external use by banks may slow down as the growing attack surface leads to caution. But it does not quantify how much slower or what the economic impact might be.

Another unresolved question is whether banks will re-emphasize physical possession factors such as hard tokens and security keys as cyber attacks become more sophisticated. The report notes a potential tension between convenience and safety. If banks move back to physical authentication, customer experience will suffer, potentially driving users to less secure alternatives. The trade-off is real, and the report does not provide a clear answer on how banks should navigate it.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Bank Management

For investors, the report implies a need to reassess how they evaluate bank risk. The traditional focus on capital ratios, net interest margins, and loan loss provisions is insufficient. Cybersecurity preparedness should become a core part of the investment thesis. Here are the key questions to ask:

First, what is the bank's absolute technology spend and how does it compare to peers? The report uses tech spend as a barometer of resources to stay ahead of the curve. Banks with higher absolute spend are better positioned to patch vulnerabilities quickly and invest in frontier model defenses. Look for banks that are increasing tech spend faster than the industry average.

Second, how old is the bank's core infrastructure? Banks running on legacy COBOL code or multiple platforms from historical M&A have a larger attack surface. The report notes that frontier models can comprehend old code that current coders may not fully understand, making previously hidden vulnerabilities exploitable. Banks that have invested in modernizing their core systems are at an advantage.

Third, does the bank have access to the latest frontier models for defense? Geographic location matters. US banks have early access. European and Asian banks may face delays. The report suggests that being absent from collaborative initiatives like Project Glasswing is a red flag.

For bank management, the report implies several strategic imperatives. Increase absolute tech spend, especially on cybersecurity infrastructure and patch management. Invest in modernizing legacy core systems. Build relationships with frontier model providers and participate in collaborative defense initiatives. Develop deposit-run liquidity contingency plans that account for a cyber trigger. And consider purchasing cyber insurance, which the report notes is becoming increasingly relevant. Munich Re estimates the global cyber insurance market will grow from $15 billion in 2025 to $28 billion by 2030, with financial services among the biggest payers of premiums.

## The Market Is Underestimating the Speed of Change, Creating Opportunity for Prepared Investors

The report's most powerful insight is that the speed of AI cyber capability improvement is accelerating faster than the speed of defense adaptation. The AI Security Institute's estimate that cyber task completion length doubled every 4.7 months, and that Mythos and GPT-5.5 exceeded even that trend, suggests we are in a period of exponential threat growth. Banks that cannot keep up will face existential risk.

Yet the market continues to value banks based on backward-looking metrics. The report argues that cybersecurity is one of the biggest undiscounted risks not reflected in bank valuations. For investors who can identify banks with strong cybersecurity postures, sticky deposits, and access to frontier models, there is an opportunity to capture a premium that the market has not yet priced. For banks that are behind, the risk is not just a potential cyber attack but a structural valuation discount that will persist as the threat landscape worsens.

The report leaves one final open question: where will the curve of AI-led defense improvements intersect with the curve of AI-led attack capabilities? If defense can keep pace, the risk may be manageable. If attack capability outruns defense, we may face a systemic crisis. The answer is not yet clear, but the direction of travel is. Investors and bank management should act now, not wait for the intersection to become obvious.

# Cybersecurity, Not Credit, Is the New Systemic Risk That Bank Valuations Have Not Priced In

The next banking crisis will not begin with a loan book. It will begin with a breach. A global investment bank report argues that cybersecurity, driven by the rapid advancement of frontier AI models such as Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview and GPT-5.5, now represents a larger undiscounted risk to bank valuations than credit risk. This is not a hypothetical concern. The AI Security Institute in the UK has confirmed that these models can autonomously discover and exploit software vulnerabilities in hours, a task that previously took human professionals months or years. The implications for global banks are profound, yet the market continues to focus on capital ratios and earnings growth as the primary metrics of institutional strength. This analytical gap is the central tension that investors must now confront.

The argument is not merely about the probability of a cyberattack. It is about the mechanism through which such an attack could trigger a systemic crisis. The report contends that liquidity, not credit, would be the primary vector of failure in a cyber-induced banking event. The CS episode demonstrated how social media can amplify deposit runs with unprecedented speed. A sophisticated cyberattack that compromises customer data or payment systems could ignite a similar, or far worse, digital bank run. The speed at which deposits can flee a compromised institution in a fully digital banking environment is a risk that existing capital frameworks are not designed to measure. The market is therefore vulnerable to a sudden repricing of bank equity based on a risk factor for which there is currently no transparent or standardized disclosure.

## The Step Change in AI Cyber Capability Has Compressed the Timeline for Vulnerability Discovery and Exploitation to a Point That Outpaces Traditional Defense Mechanisms

The report's most critical data point is the performance leap in frontier AI models. Anthropic's Mythos Preview achieved a 73% success rate on expert-level hacking tasks that no model could complete before April 2025. GPT-5.5 reached a similar level of performance. This is not an incremental improvement; it is a discontinuous jump. The AI Security Institute's internal estimates show that the length of cyber tasks AI models could complete had been doubling every eight months as of November 2025. By February 2026, that doubling time had accelerated to 4.7 months. With the arrival of Mythos and GPT-5.5, the trend has substantially exceeded even that accelerated rate.

The practical consequence is that vulnerabilities that were once considered safe because they were difficult to find are now discoverable at machine speed. The report highlights that Mythos can reverse-engineer exploits on closed-source software, turning known but not yet widely patched vulnerabilities into active threats. It found over 6,200 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities in a scan of more than 1,000 open-source projects that underpin much of the internet. For banks, this means that the window between a vulnerability being identified and it being exploited is narrowing to the point where manual patching cycles are no longer sufficient. The traditional approach of periodic security audits and reactive patching is structurally obsolete.

## Legacy Infrastructure, Not Size Alone, Determines Which Banks Are Most Exposed, and Current Disclosure Is Inadequate for Investors to Differentiate

The report provides a specific framework for identifying the most vulnerable institutions. Banks with legacy core banking systems running on decades-old code, particularly COBOL, are at the highest risk. Frontier models can comprehend this older code, exposing vulnerabilities that were previously undetectable due to a lack of human expertise. Banks with multiple legacy platforms resulting from historical mergers and acquisitions have a larger attack surface. Institutions that rely on vendor-provided core banking systems are exposed if their vendors lack the agility to release timely patches. Finally, banks without access to the latest frontier models for defensive purposes may remain exposed to "known threats" for longer than peers who can use AI to identify and patch vulnerabilities proactively.

The report uses technology spending as a proxy for preparedness, but this is an imperfect measure. Tech costs averaged approximately 17% of global banks' operating expenses in 2025, but definitions vary, making like-for-like comparisons difficult. US large-cap banks have higher absolute tech budgets and earlier access to advanced AI models, as evidenced by their participation in initiatives like Anthropic's Project Glasswing, from which European banks were notably absent. However, the report is candid that, as an external observer, it is difficult to differentiate between banks on a bottom-up basis. Investors lack sufficient information about individual banks' IT mainframe infrastructure, back-office systems, middle-office processes, and external vendor reliance. This information asymmetry is itself a risk. The market cannot price what it cannot see.

## The Regulatory Framework Is Misaligned: Capital Ratios Are the Wrong Tool for a Cyber-Driven Liquidity Crisis

The report makes a pointed critique of the current regulatory approach. Regulators have focused heavily on capital ratios as the primary safeguard against bank failure. But capital is designed to absorb credit losses over time, not to withstand a sudden, digitally-driven liquidity drain. The report argues for a shift in focus toward cybersecurity-driven liquidity risk. It proposes two specific new tests: infrastructure resilience testing and a deposit-run liquidity haircut test. These would simulate how quickly a bank's deposit base could evaporate following a major cyber incident and whether the bank's liquidity buffers are adequate to survive such a scenario.

This is a fundamentally different lens. It suggests that the next banking crisis will not be a slow-motion credit event but a fast-moving confidence crisis triggered by a cyberattack. The report also advocates for regulators to incorporate cyber risk insurance into the operational capital profile for banks, incentivizing the purchase of appropriate protection. The global cyber insurance market is estimated to grow from approximately $15 billion in 2025 to around $28 billion by 2030, and financial services is among the largest payers of cyber premiums. However, the report notes that banks like Jaguar Land Rover, which lacked cyber insurance, had to shoulder the full cost of a damaging attack. Regulators should ensure that banks are not similarly exposed.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Intersection of Quantum Computing, DeFi, and the Cost Curve of AI Attacks

The report acknowledges several unresolved questions that investors should monitor. First, it raises the issue of quantum computing and its potential to eventually break the cryptography that underpins current digital security. The Ethereum Foundation has a roadmap targeting 2029 for post-quantum protection, but the timeline for quantum threats remains uncertain. Second, the report notes that DeFi markets remain vulnerable to cyber attacks, and the increased use of agentic AI in financial services is expanding the attack surface. Third, and most critically, the report poses a structural question: where will the two curves of AI-led defense and AI-led attack intersect? AI lowers the cost for bad actors to carry out sophisticated attacks, while also enabling faster patching and vulnerability discovery. The report does not and cannot predict which side gains the upper hand. This is the central analytical tension that will determine the severity of future cyber risk.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Four Questions to Assess Bank Resilience

Based on the report's analysis, investors can construct a practical framework for evaluating banks. The first question is about infrastructure: How old is the bank's core code, and how many legacy platforms does it operate? Banks with fewer, more modern systems are structurally more defensible. The second question is about spending: What is the bank's absolute and relative technology spend, and how much is specifically allocated to cybersecurity? The report suggests that US large-cap banks are better positioned due to higher absolute spending, but investors should verify this with management. The third question is about access: Is the bank participating in industry initiatives like Project Glasswing, or does it have partnerships with leading AI firms for defensive purposes? Access to frontier models for defense is becoming a competitive advantage. The fourth question is about liquidity: How would the bank's deposit base behave under a cyber-induced stress scenario? Banks with sticky, excess deposits and strong digital authentication protocols are likely to be more resilient. The report suggests that such banks may deserve a 1x higher multiple, implying a lower cost of equity, because of their ability to manage through the next crisis.

This framework is not a substitute for detailed due diligence, but it provides a starting point for engagement. The report's most valuable contribution is to frame cybersecurity not as a technical issue for IT departments but as a core strategic and valuation issue for bank investors. The banks that invest proactively, modernize their infrastructure, and secure access to the latest defensive AI tools will be the ones that survive the coming wave. The ones that do not will face a risk that no capital buffer can fully absorb.

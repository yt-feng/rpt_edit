EN_A: So we have this report on macro credit views, and the title itself is interesting: "Dispersion, not disruption." What does that actually mean for investors?
EN_B: It means the credit market is not heading for a crash, but there is a lot of divergence happening under the surface. The main takeaway is that spreads may widen a bit, but the sell-off will be contained because there is a strong "yield-based bid" from big institutional buyers.
EN_A: Yield-based bid? That sounds like a technical term. Who are these buyers?
EN_B: Think pension funds and insurance companies. They don't really care if spreads are tight or wide. They just want that all-in yield. And right now, with risk-free rates so high, they are buying a lot of investment-grade bonds. The report estimates that in the US market, these investors own at least 40% of the outstanding notional.
EN_A: So that is the anchor. But what about the role of interest rates? The report seems to say rates are the main driver now.
EN_B: Exactly. In IG credit, rates have taken over from spreads as the dominant factor in relative value. The report has a striking number: from 2010 to 2021, IG spreads made up 43% of the all-in yield. Today, it is just 15%. So almost all of the return is coming from the risk-free rate, not from credit risk.
EN_A: That is a huge shift. But what if rates become more volatile? Would that change things?
EN_B: That is exactly the risk the report flags. If rate volatility spikes, credit investors will demand a higher premium, which means wider spreads. So rates are still the key variable to watch. The report also notes that a flatter Treasury curve is causing credit curves to steepen a bit.
EN_A: And what about the regional picture? The report compares the US and Europe.
EN_B: The view is that US credit will continue to outperform Europe, but the lines are blurring. Cross-border issuance is increasing. For example, US firms now account for more than 17% of euro IG issuance, the highest since 2017. So the market is not as siloed as it used to be.

EN_B: In the US, the report prefers to move down in quality, favoring BBBs over higher-rated AA and A. The logic is that higher-rated companies are actually more likely to increase leverage and issue more debt, which could hurt their valuations. BBBs offer a better spread pickup.

EN_B: Not exactly. Given the weaker macro backdrop in Europe, the report slightly prefers A-rated over BBBs. But it still warns against moving too far up the quality spectrum because AA supply is becoming a headwind.

EN_B: In US HY, it is overweight BBs, underweight Bs, and neutral on CCCs. In Europe, it is more cautious: overweight BBs, neutral on Bs, and underweight CCCs. But the report does not give specific stock picks, which means investors need to do their own homework.

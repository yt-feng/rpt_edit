# US Equity Inflows Are Slowing, But the Real Story Is the Rotational Shift Beneath the Surface

The pace of foreign capital entering US equity markets has decelerated sharply. In the week ending June 25, foreign inflows into US-focused equity funds totaled just USD 1.4 billion, a dramatic slowdown from the USD 3.9 billion recorded the prior week. This is not a temporary lull. It is a signal that the global investment narrative is undergoing a structural recalibration. The technology and AI-driven exuberance that propelled US markets for much of the first half of the year is encountering resistance, and capital is beginning to reposition in ways that have profound implications for Asia ex-Japan currencies, emerging market asset allocations, and the broader risk appetite cycle.

The headline deceleration matters, but the real analytical insight lies in the composition of flows. While US equities are losing momentum, emerging market-focused ETFs continue to attract steady capital, recording USD 1.8 billion in inflows during the same period, nearly unchanged from the prior week's USD 1.9 billion. This stability in EM flows, juxtaposed against the volatility in US equity flows, suggests that global investors are not retreating from risk entirely. Rather, they are rebalancing their geographic exposures and reassessing the relative value proposition of different markets.

For Asia ex-Japan, the implications are twofold. First, the slowdown in US equity inflows reduces the tailwind for the US dollar, which has benefited from capital repatriation and equity-related demand. Second, the resilience of EM flows provides a potential floor for Asian currencies, particularly those tied to export-driven economies with strong tech supply chain linkages. However, the data also reveals a more nuanced picture at the country level, with Korean retail investors drastically reducing their US portfolio purchases and Taiwanese investors showing only tentative interest in USD-denominated bonds. These micro-level shifts contain the most actionable signals for currency and fixed income strategists.

The fundamental question facing investors is whether this deceleration in US equity inflows is a pause within an ongoing bull cycle or the beginning of a more sustained rotation away from US-centric risk assets. The answer depends on how one interprets the interplay between tech sector volatility, monetary policy expectations, and the evolving growth differential between the US and the rest of the world. This report provides the high-frequency flow data necessary to track these shifts in real time, but the strategic interpretation requires a framework that goes beyond the weekly numbers.

## The Deceleration in US Equity Inflows Is Not a Panic, But It Reflects a Loss of Conviction at the Margin

The most striking feature of the weekly flow data is the magnitude of the slowdown. Foreign inflows into US-focused equity funds fell from USD 3.9 billion to USD 1.4 billion, a decline of over 60 percent in a single week. This is not a catastrophic reversal, but it represents a meaningful loss of momentum. To contextualize this, the monthly inflow for June through the 25th stood at USD 11.5 billion, which is still robust by historical standards and exceeds the USD 8.8 billion recorded in May. However, the weekly trajectory matters more than the monthly aggregate for near-term positioning.

The weekly data captures the market's reaction to the volatility in global technology and AI-related stocks. When the narrative around AI-driven productivity gains and earnings growth becomes contested, the marginal buyer of US equities hesitates. The foreign investor base, which includes both institutional allocators and retail participants, is particularly sensitive to these narrative shifts because their investment decisions are often influenced by relative performance comparisons and currency-adjusted returns.

The so-what of this deceleration is that it reduces the exogenous demand for US dollars that accompanies equity inflows. When foreign investors buy US equities, they must first acquire dollars, which supports the currency. A slowdown in this flow removes a source of dollar strength, which in turn has implications for Asia ex-Japan currencies. For countries like South Korea and Taiwan, where export competitiveness is closely tied to exchange rate dynamics, a weaker dollar environment could provide relief. However, the relationship is not mechanical; it depends on whether the capital that is not flowing into US equities is being redirected into EM assets or simply held as cash.

## Emerging Market Flows Are Holding Steady, Suggesting a Search for Diversification Rather Than a Risk-Off Shift

While US equity inflows decelerated, EM-focused ETFs continued to attract capital at a steady pace. The USD 1.8 billion in inflows during the latest week was nearly identical to the USD 1.9 billion recorded in the prior week. This stability is analytically significant because it indicates that the slowdown in US flows is not a broad-based risk-off move. Investors are not fleeing equities entirely; they are reallocating across geographies.

The composition of EM flows is also instructive. The vast majority of inflows went into EM equity funds, which attracted USD 1.5 billion, while bond funds saw a more modest USD 357 million. This preference for equities over bonds in the EM space suggests that investors are positioning for a growth narrative, not a defensive one. They are betting that emerging markets, particularly those in Asia with strong manufacturing and export sectors, will benefit from a stabilization in global demand and potential monetary easing cycles.

For Asia ex-Japan, this steady EM flow provides a critical buffer. If capital were flowing out of both US equities and EM assets simultaneously, the pressure on Asian currencies would be intense. Instead, the resilience of EM flows suggests that Asian central banks may have more room to manage their exchange rate policies without facing destabilizing capital flight. However, this resilience is not uniform across the region. The country-level data from Korea and Taiwan reveals significant divergence in investor behavior, which is where the most granular insights reside.

## Korean Retail Investors Are Pulling Back Sharply, Signaling a Shift in Risk Appetite That Bears Watching

The most dramatic shift at the country level comes from Korea. Korean retail investors net purchased only USD 49 million of US portfolio assets between June 22 and 26, a collapse from the USD 990 million recorded in the prior week. This is a 95 percent decline. The composition is equally telling: the purchases were evenly split between US equities and US bonds, with approximately USD 25 million in each. In the prior week, equity purchases dominated at USD 836 million.

This abrupt pullback by Korean retail investors is a significant data point for several reasons. First, Korean retail investors have been a meaningful source of demand for US equities, often acting as a marginal buyer during periods of strong global risk appetite. Their retreat suggests that the recent volatility in tech stocks has directly impacted their confidence. Second, the shift toward a more balanced allocation between equities and bonds indicates a defensive posture, even if the absolute amounts are small.

The implication for the Korean won is nuanced. A reduction in Korean retail outflows to US assets reduces the demand for US dollars from this specific channel, which could be mildly supportive for the won. However, the broader context matters. If Korean retail investors are pulling back because they are concerned about global growth or tech sector fundamentals, that sentiment could spill over into domestic equity markets and reduce overall risk appetite. The won's trajectory will depend on whether this retail behavior is an isolated phenomenon or the leading edge of a broader shift in Korean investor sentiment.

## Taiwanese Investors Show Tentative Interest in USD Bonds, But the Monthly Trend Remains Bearish

Taiwanese investors presented a contrasting picture. In the latest week, they net purchased USD 61 million of USD-denominated bond ETFs, a reversal from net sales of USD 95 million in the prior week. This weekly improvement is notable, but it must be viewed against the monthly backdrop. For June through the 25th, Taiwanese investors have net sold USD 1.1 billion of USD-denominated bonds, reversing a small net purchase of USD 176 million in May.

This pattern suggests that Taiwanese investors are not yet convinced that US bonds offer an attractive risk-reward profile. The weekly uptick could be a tactical adjustment rather than a strategic shift. Taiwanese investors, who are among the most active participants in the USD bond ETF market in Asia, are likely weighing the implications of US interest rate expectations, currency hedging costs, and the relative attractiveness of domestic alternatives.

The so-what for Asia ex-Japan is that Taiwanese capital flows are a leading indicator for regional fixed income demand. If Taiwanese investors remain net sellers of USD bonds, it signals that the carry trade dynamics that have supported demand for US fixed income are under pressure. This could have spillover effects for other Asian investors who look to Taiwanese behavior as a benchmark. For the Taiwanese dollar, reduced outflows into USD bonds could provide some support, but the effect is likely to be modest given the scale of the overall capital account.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Missing Link Between Flow Data and Currency Positioning

The high-frequency flow data presented in this report is invaluable for tracking investor sentiment in real time. However, it leaves several critical questions unanswered for the Asia ex-Japan currency strategist. First, the data captures flows into US-focused and EM-focused funds, but it does not distinguish between hedged and unhedged exposures. This distinction is crucial for understanding the actual currency impact. A foreign investor buying a US equity fund without hedging the currency exposure creates direct dollar demand. An investor who hedges the currency risk neutralizes the flow's impact on the exchange rate.

Second, the data does not provide a breakdown by investor type beyond the retail versus aggregate distinction for Korea and Taiwan. Institutional investors, such as pension funds and sovereign wealth funds, may have very different reaction functions than retail investors. If institutional flows are moving in the opposite direction of retail flows, the net currency impact could be muted or even reversed.

Third, the relationship between equity flows and currency movements is not stable over time. During periods of high market stress, the correlation between flows and exchange rates can break down as other factors, such as carry trades, hedging activity, and central bank intervention, dominate. The report provides the raw flow data, but it does not offer a model for translating these flows into currency forecasts. This is where the reader must apply their own analytical framework, and it is precisely the kind of question that a deeper engagement with the full report and its accompanying charts can help answer.

## A Decision Framework for Asia Ex-Japan Currency Positioning Based on Flow Regimes

For practitioners, the weekly flow data can be organized into a simple decision framework based on three regimes. The first regime is "US-led risk-on," characterized by strong and accelerating US equity inflows alongside steady or rising EM flows. In this regime, Asian currencies tend to face headwinds as capital is drawn to US assets, and the dollar strengthens. The current data suggests we are moving out of this regime.

The second regime is "EM-led rotation," characterized by decelerating US equity inflows but stable or rising EM flows. This is the regime we appear to be entering. In this environment, Asian currencies have the potential to stabilize or appreciate, particularly those of economies with strong export linkages and current account surpluses. The Korean won and Taiwanese dollar could benefit, but only if the rotation is sustained and not accompanied by a broader risk-off move.

The third regime is "global risk-off," characterized by simultaneous outflows from both US and EM assets. This regime would be triggered by a significant negative shock, such as a sharp deterioration in global growth expectations or a financial stability event. In this regime, the US dollar typically strengthens as a safe haven, and Asian currencies come under significant pressure. The current data does not point to this regime, but the vulnerability is real if the tech sector volatility deepens.

The key question for investors is whether the current deceleration in US equity inflows represents a transition from regime one to regime two, or whether it is a precursor to regime three. The answer will depend on incoming data on global growth, corporate earnings, and central bank policy. The weekly flow data is the canary in the coal mine, and it is currently signaling caution without panic.

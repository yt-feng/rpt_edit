# The Global Economy Has Passed Peak Energy Shock, But Three Structural Crosscurrents Will Determine Who Wins and Who Loses in 2027

The sharp decline in Brent crude to approximately USD80 per barrel following the US-Iran ceasefire extension is a genuine reprieve for global policymakers, but it is dangerously misleading to interpret this as a return to normalcy. The energy supply shock that began in late February 2026 may have peaked in intensity, yet the economic landscape it has reshaped is fundamentally different from the one it interrupted. The real question facing investors and corporate strategists is not whether oil prices will stay down, but whether the global economy can navigate the three powerful and simultaneous forces that will now define the next 18 months: the relentless AI-driven investment boom, the emerging food price crisis from severe El Niño conditions, and the deeply uneven fiscal and monetary policy responses that will amplify divergence between economies.

Under the central scenario outlined in a major global economic research report from Q3 2026, global GDP growth is forecast at just 2.5% for 2026, the weakest pace since the pandemic, with only a modest recovery to 2.7% in 2027. These headline figures, however, conceal a stark divergence beneath the surface. The United States is experiencing an upward revision to its growth outlook, while India and Brazil face some of the largest downgrades. This is not a cyclical story. It is a structural realignment driven by which economies are positioned to benefit from the AI capex wave, which are most exposed to the agricultural shock from El Niño, and which have the fiscal space to absorb the lingering cost pressures that will not fade as quickly as the oil price fell.

The report makes clear that the ceasefire extension and the commitment to reopen the Strait of Hormuz are welcome, but they are not a solution. Iran has already signaled there will be no return to the pre-war status quo. The new normal in the Strait remains undefined, and the risk of a partial reopening scenario—where less than half of pre-conflict crude oil passes through—is real enough that the report models an "uglier" scenario with oil prices potentially reaching USD140-150 per barrel. In that world, growth would be much lower, inflation much higher, and several economies would tip into outright recession.

For now, markets are pricing the benign scenario. The analytical task is to understand what happens next.

## The AI Investment Boom Has Become the Dominant Force in Global Trade and Will Overwhelm the Energy Shock in Some Economies

The most consequential insight from this analysis is that the AI-driven export and investment cycle has become structurally more important for certain economies than the energy supply shock itself. The report documents that the enormous investment in the entire AI ecosystem has continued to drive world trade growth even against a backdrop of tariffs, bilateral trade frictions, and an unprecedented supply disruption in the Middle East. This is not a niche technology story. It is a macroeconomic force that is reshaping GDP trajectories, trade balances, and inflation dynamics in ways that are only partially understood.

Taiwan stands out as the most extreme example. The report forecasts GDP growth of 10.2% in 2026, driven overwhelmingly by AI-related exports and capital expenditure. Even in the ugly scenario, Taiwan's growth is still projected at 7.8%. This is not merely a semiconductor story, though that is the core. The entire ecosystem of AI infrastructure—from copper wiring to memory chips to cooling systems—has created a demand shock that barely paused for breath even at the height of the Strait of Hormuz disruption in April. The report cites a statement from Apple CEO Tim Cook in mid-June that soaring component costs have made price increases unavoidable. This is a signal that the AI boom is now generating its own cost-push inflation pressures that are independent of energy markets.

The implications for portfolio construction and country allocation are profound. The US also benefits disproportionately from the AI boom, with the report forecasting 2.3% growth in both 2026 and 2027, an upward revision from earlier projections. The US energy surplus and safe-haven status have already supported the dollar vis-a-vis all energy importers except China. The report's FX team forecasts that US growth exceptionalism and a greater likelihood of a Federal Reserve rate rise than a cut this year means the dollar should firm further against most currencies. This is a direct consequence of the AI boom concentrating capital flows, corporate profits, and wealth effects in the US economy.

The unevenness of the AI effect extends to consumer spending as well. The report notes that the impact on consumption varies enormously across age and income cohorts. Younger and higher-income consumers in tech-intensive economies benefit from wealth effects driven by surging tech equity prices, while older and lower-income households face the squeeze from higher input costs that have not yet fully passed through to final goods prices. This is creating a two-speed consumption dynamic within economies that complicates the outlook for central banks.

## El Niño Will Generate a Food Price Shock That Central Banks Are Not Prepared to Address

While the energy shock is receding, the report identifies a second powerful force that is only now beginning to materialize: the severe El Niño weather phenomenon. This is not a forecast risk. It is already affecting agricultural output in parts of Asia, and the report warns that it has the potential to push up inflation materially in the second half of 2026 and into 2027 in many emerging economies, including India and Brazil.

The mechanism is worth understanding in detail. The energy shock of March and April 2026 had already disrupted agricultural production through higher diesel and fertilizer costs, which hit the planting season in several Asian economies. Those lagged effects are still working through the system. But El Niño represents a separate and potentially larger shock. Reduced rainfall, temperature extremes, and altered growing seasons will reduce yields for key crops such as rice, wheat, and vegetable oils. The report's inflation forecasts for 2026 have been revised upward to 5.1% for India and 4.8% for Brazil, driven almost entirely by the emerging world and notably across Asia.

The critical analytical point is that food price inflation is structurally different from energy price inflation in its economic and political effects. Energy prices can be partially offset through subsidies, tax adjustments, and strategic reserves. Food prices hit household budgets directly and disproportionately affect lower-income households, which spend a much larger share of their income on food. This creates a political economy problem for governments already facing large budget deficits and rising demands for defense, age-related spending, and energy transition investments. The report notes that every region of the world starts from a position where budget deficits and debts are larger than before the pandemic, and structural spending demands are set to rise more sharply. Any relief from lower energy prices will be welcome, but it may be consumed by the food price shock.

Even advanced economies are not immune. The report warns that less-exposed economies could still be affected by higher imported food prices as well as higher packaging costs given plastic prices, which are linked to energy markets but have their own dynamics. The food price channel is also more persistent than the energy channel. Energy prices can fall rapidly, as they did in June, but food prices tend to be stickier due to the length of agricultural cycles and the time required for new supply to come to market.

## Monetary Policy Divergence Will Deepen as the ECB and BoE Pause While Asian Central Banks Are Forced to Hike

The combination of fading energy shocks, persistent AI-driven investment, and building food price pressures creates a uniquely challenging environment for central banks. The report's analysis suggests that the implications of the fall in oil prices for inflation mean the European Central Bank will not raise rates again and the Bank of England will not hike at all. Both could cut in 2027, according to the report, which is more dovish than current market pricing.

But this is not a uniform easing cycle. The report explicitly states that several Asian economies will likely be under pressure to raise rates further in the second half of 2026. The logic is clear: Asian economies are more exposed to food price inflation from El Niño, they are more dependent on energy imports (which means the passthrough from higher oil prices was more severe even if it is now reversing), and they face tighter financial conditions due to dollar strength. The report's forecasts show India's policy rate at 5.75% through 2027, but with inflation at 5.1% in 2026 and 4.6% in 2027, real rates remain negative. Indonesia is forecast to cut rates from 6.00% to 5.50% by 2027, but only if the El Niño shock does not force a delay.

The divergence between the Atlantic and Pacific monetary policy stances has significant implications for currency markets. The report's FX team forecasts that the dollar should firm further against most currencies, driven by US growth exceptionalism and the greater likelihood of a Fed rate rise. This creates a tightening feedback loop for emerging market economies: dollar strength forces them to keep rates higher to defend their currencies, which depresses domestic demand, which makes them more vulnerable to the food price shock, which forces further tightening. The report's ugly scenario explicitly models this dynamic, with policy rates rising in Canada, Japan, India, Australia, South Korea, Indonesia, Thailand, Singapore, Hong Kong, and the Philippines under the partial reopening scenario.

The key strategic question for investors is whether the current market pricing of central bank policy is correctly discounting the food price risk. The report implies that markets may be too complacent about the persistence of inflation in emerging economies, especially if El Niño proves more severe than currently anticipated.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between AI-Driven Demand and Food Supply Constraints

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved, and these gaps are where the most important investment insights may lie. The most significant unanswered question is how the AI boom's enormous demand for physical resources will interact with the agricultural supply constraints created by El Niño.

The report documents that AI construction and capex are driving up costs for a wide range of inputs, from copper wiring to memory chips. But the list of affected inputs is likely much longer. Data centers require enormous amounts of water for cooling, which competes directly with agricultural water use in regions facing drought from El Niño. The energy demands of AI infrastructure are massive, which means that even as oil prices fall, electricity prices in regions with high AI investment may remain elevated, further squeezing agricultural margins. The report does not model this interaction explicitly, but the logic suggests that the AI boom could exacerbate the food price shock in unexpected ways.

A second unresolved question concerns the duration of the AI capex cycle. The report assumes it continues, but does not explore the conditions under which it might slow. If the energy shock were to re-escalate under the ugly scenario, would AI investment be deferred? The report states that the global AI investment story would not be derailed even under the worst energy scenario, but this is an assertion rather than a modeled outcome. The historical evidence suggests that large-scale capital expenditure is rarely immune to macroeconomic shocks.

Third, the report does not fully address the political economy of the US-Iran negotiations. The ceasefire extension is a positive development, but the report acknowledges that Iran has indicated there will be no return to the pre-war status quo. What does the new normal look like? Higher transit costs? More frequent disruptions? A permanent risk premium in oil prices? The report's central scenario assumes a gradual return to near-normal production and flows by the end of Q3 2026, but this is an assumption, not a forecast. The ugly scenario is explicitly modeled as a possibility, not a tail risk.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are invitations for readers to apply their own judgment and to recognize that the range of possible outcomes is wider than the central forecast suggests.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Next 18 Months

For investors and corporate strategists, the report's analysis can be distilled into a decision framework with three dimensions: energy exposure, AI exposure, and food exposure. Every economy, sector, and portfolio can be mapped along these three axes, and the optimal strategy depends on the combination.

Economies with high AI exposure and low food exposure, such as Taiwan and the United States, are positioned to outperform regardless of the energy scenario, because the AI boom provides a structural growth buffer that offsets energy and food headwinds. The report's forecasts confirm this: the US receives upward revisions while India and Brazil face downgrades.

Economies with high food exposure and low AI exposure, such as India and Brazil, face the most challenging outlook. They are vulnerable to the El Niño shock, they face pressure to raise interest rates, and they do not benefit from the AI capex wave to the same extent as Taiwan or the US. The report's inflation forecasts for India at 5.1% in 2026 and 4.6% in 2027, combined with growth downgrades, suggest a stagflationary bias that is not fully priced by markets.

Economies with high energy import dependence and limited AI exposure, such as much of Europe and parts of Asia ex-Japan, face a more ambiguous outlook. The fall in oil prices is clearly positive, but the lingering effects of higher input costs, combined with weak domestic demand and fiscal constraints, mean the recovery is likely to be slow and uneven. The report forecasts Eurozone growth at just 0.3% in 2026, with inflation at 2.9%.

For portfolio construction, the framework suggests overweighting US and Taiwan exposure, underweighting India and Brazil, and maintaining a cautious stance on Europe. Currency positioning should favor the dollar, which benefits from both the AI boom and safe-haven flows. Commodity exposure should be differentiated: oil is likely to remain under pressure in the central scenario, but agricultural commodities could surprise to the upside if El Niño proves severe.

The most important strategic insight, however, is that the current environment rewards granularity. Aggregate global forecasts are less useful than country-specific and sector-specific analysis. The crosscurrents are too powerful and too uneven for a single global strategy to work.

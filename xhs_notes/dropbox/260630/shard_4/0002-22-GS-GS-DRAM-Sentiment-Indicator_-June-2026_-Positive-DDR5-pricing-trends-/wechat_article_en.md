# The DRAM Cycle Is Entering a New Phase: Spot Pricing Is No Longer the Only Signal Investors Should Watch

The conventional DRAM cycle has historically been driven by a simple, predictable mechanism: oversupply crushes prices, underinvestment triggers shortages, and the pendulum swings back. Investors have trained themselves to watch spot pricing as the canary in the coal mine, and for good reason—it has been the most reliable leading indicator of inflection points. That framework is becoming dangerously incomplete.

As of June 2026, the DRAM sentiment indicator compiled by a leading global investment bank points in a moderately positive direction, unchanged from April. On the surface, this suggests continuation of the existing trend. But the composition of that signal has shifted in ways that demand a more sophisticated reading. DDR5 spot pricing has risen 20% since the beginning of May, and DDR4 has followed with an 11% gain. Nanya Tech's revenue has posted ten consecutive months of triple-digit year-over-year growth, with May alone showing a staggering 730% increase. Korea's DRAM export revenue hit a record high in May, surpassing the previous peak by 21%. These numbers are undeniably strong.

Yet the most consequential development in this report is not the strength of current demand. It is the structural shift in how pricing expectations are being formed, particularly for HBM. The report notes that investor expectations for 2027 HBM pricing continue to rise, driven by the possibility that strong conventional DRAM pricing will be taken into account when negotiating next year's HBM contracts. This is not a marginal detail. It signals a potential re-anchoring of the entire pricing architecture for memory, where the traditional separation between commodity DRAM and high-bandwidth memory begins to blur.

The strategic question for investors is no longer simply whether the cycle is peaking or troughing. It is whether the cycle itself is being redefined by the coexistence of two fundamentally different demand drivers: the mature but resilient smartphone and server markets, and the explosive, structurally under-supplied AI-driven demand for HBM. The indicators that matter are changing, and the clockwork rhythm of past cycles may no longer apply.

## The Conventional DRAM Uptrend Remains Intact, But the Second Derivative Demands Attention

All the headline numbers point to a market that is still accelerating. Korea's DRAM exports rose 370% year-over-year in May, driven by rising memory prices and solid demand from Big Tech customers in the United States and China. Taiwan's server ODM revenues climbed 53% year-over-year, supported by rack-level AI server shipments and strong growth in ASIC-based AI servers. Aspeed, the dominant supplier of baseboard management controllers for servers, posted 69% year-over-year revenue growth, even against a high base of 75% growth in the prior year.

But the most analytically revealing data point in the report is not a record high. It is the second derivative of Samsung Electronics' DRAM average selling price for the second quarter of 2026. The bank estimates that ASP will rise approximately 46% quarter-over-quarter, which is exceptionally strong. However, the second derivative—the rate of change of that growth—is estimated at negative 48 percentage points. In plain terms, ASPs are still rising rapidly, but the pace of acceleration is decelerating sharply.

This is the kind of nuance that separates a mature understanding of the cycle from a superficial one. A positive first derivative with a sharply negative second derivative is the classic signature of a market that is approaching a plateau, not a cliff. It does not signal an imminent downturn. It signals that the rate of improvement is peaking, which historically has been the moment when investors need to shift their focus from momentum to sustainability. The question is no longer how high prices can go this quarter. It is what sustains them into 2027 and beyond, particularly as the composition of demand shifts toward HBM.

## The HBM Pricing Mechanism Is Undergoing a Structural Recalibration That Could Redefine the Cycle

The most underappreciated insight in this report is the upward revision to 2027 HBM pricing expectations. The bank has revised its estimate for Samsung Electronics' 2027 HBM pricing growth to 44% year-over-year, up from 14% previously. That is a dramatic upgrade, and the bank explicitly states that there could be additional upside risk given the tight supply-demand balance and the widening gap between conventional DRAM and HBM pricing.

The logic behind this revision is worth unpacking. Historically, HBM pricing has been negotiated largely in isolation from the conventional DRAM market, driven by the specific supply-demand dynamics of high-bandwidth memory and the technological complexity of its production. What the report suggests is that this separation may be breaking down. As conventional DRAM pricing has remained elevated and continues to rise, it is creating a floor—and potentially a rising anchor—for HBM pricing negotiations. If foundry customers see that the underlying commodity memory components are expensive, they may be less able to push aggressively for lower HBM prices.

This has profound implications for the earnings power of memory manufacturers. HBM is already a high-value product with significant technological barriers to entry. If its pricing becomes structurally linked to a strong conventional DRAM market, the margin profile of the entire memory industry could shift permanently upward. The cycle would no longer be characterized by violent swings between boom and bust, but by a higher, more stable plateau of profitability, supported by the insatiable demand for AI compute.

But this thesis is not yet proven. The report does not fully answer whether the linkage between conventional DRAM and HBM pricing is a temporary phenomenon driven by current tightness or a permanent structural change. It also does not address the risk that hyperscalers—who are the primary buyers of HBM—may eventually push back against rising prices by investing more aggressively in alternative architectures or by vertically integrating their own memory solutions. These are open questions that the current data cannot resolve, and they represent the most important analytical tension in the report.

## The Demand Side Is Strong but Not Uniform: The Smartphone Signal Is the Weakest Link

Not all demand indicators in the report are equally constructive. China's smartphone shipments showed two consecutive months of year-over-year growth, with May recording a 19% increase. That is a positive reversal from the prior month's decline, and it suggests that the Chinese consumer electronics market is stabilizing after a prolonged downturn. However, the bank's own China team expects second-quarter 2026 shipments to decline 14% year-over-year, primarily due to rising memory prices dragging down end demand.

This is a critical tension that the report surfaces but does not fully resolve. The same pricing strength that benefits memory manufacturers is beginning to act as a headwind for downstream demand in the smartphone market. If memory prices continue to rise, smartphone OEMs will face margin pressure, which will eventually translate into lower unit volumes or a shift toward lower-memory configurations. Either outcome would reduce the total addressable market for DRAM in the mobile segment.

The smartphone signal matters because it is the canary for the broader consumer electronics market. If rising memory prices begin to choke off demand in the largest end-market for DRAM, the cycle could face a self-limiting dynamic. The strong pricing that investors are celebrating today could be the very mechanism that sows the seeds of the next downturn. The question is whether the growth in AI-driven demand for HBM is large enough to offset any weakness in the consumer segment. The report's data suggests that, for now, the answer is yes. But the trajectory of that offset is not guaranteed, and the smartphone data provides the first warning sign that the demand picture is not uniformly robust.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions That Determine Positioning

For investors trying to translate this report into actionable strategy, the data points converge on three critical questions that should determine positioning. These are not questions that the report answers definitively, but they are the questions that separate a thoughtful investment thesis from a reactive one.

First, is the upward revision to 2027 HBM pricing an inflection point or a one-time adjustment? The bank's revision from 14% to 44% year-over-year growth is massive, and it reflects a genuine change in the supply-demand outlook. But it is based on expectations that strong conventional DRAM pricing will influence HBM negotiations. If conventional DRAM pricing begins to moderate in the second half of 2026—which the second derivative data suggests is possible—the rationale for that revision weakens. Investors need to monitor whether HBM pricing momentum can decouple from conventional DRAM or whether it remains tethered.

Second, how much of the current demand is inventory build versus genuine consumption? The report shows record export numbers and triple-digit revenue growth across multiple indicators. But in a cycle that has already run for several quarters, the risk of double-ordering and inventory accumulation increases. The report does not provide granular data on channel inventory levels, which is a meaningful gap. Investors who want to position for the next phase of the cycle need independent visibility into whether end-customers are buying for consumption or for safety stock.

Third, what is the threshold at which rising memory prices begin to destroy demand in the smartphone and PC markets? The China smartphone data provides an early data point, but it is not conclusive. The report does not model the price elasticity of demand for DRAM in consumer end-markets, and that is the most important variable for determining how long the current upcycle can sustain itself. If the elasticity is low, prices can rise significantly before volumes are affected, and the cycle has further to run. If elasticity is high, the cycle may be closer to its peak than the headline numbers suggest.

These are the questions that the report raises but does not answer. They are the natural starting point for any investor who wants to move beyond the consensus narrative and build a differentiated view.

## The Full Picture Requires Granular Data That Only a Dedicated Analysis Can Provide

The DRAM sentiment indicator is a powerful tool, but it is a composite of many signals, each of which tells a different part of the story. The spot pricing data, the server ODM revenue trends, the Korea export numbers, the Nanya and Supreme revenue trajectories, and the second derivative of Samsung's ASP all point in a broadly constructive direction. But the nuance lies in the divergences: the smartphone weakness, the deceleration in ASP growth, and the still-unresolved question of how HBM pricing will evolve in a tightening market.

Investors who rely on a single indicator—whether it is spot pricing or export data—risk missing the complexity of a market that is being reshaped by the simultaneous forces of AI-driven demand and conventional cyclical dynamics.

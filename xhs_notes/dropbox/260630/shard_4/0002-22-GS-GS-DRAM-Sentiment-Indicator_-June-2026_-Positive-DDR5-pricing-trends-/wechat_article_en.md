# The DRAM Cycle Has Entered a New Phase Where Pricing Power Is Broadening Beyond HBM

The conventional DRAM market has been treated as a cyclical laggard for the past two years, overshadowed by the explosive growth of high-bandwidth memory tied directly to AI accelerator demand. That narrative is no longer sufficient. As of June 2026, a carefully constructed sentiment indicator tracking nine distinct data points across the DRAM ecosystem is pointing moderately positive for the second consecutive reading, but the composition of that signal reveals something more important than the headline direction alone.

What matters most is not that the overall indicator is positive. What matters is that the sources of strength have shifted. The current cycle is no longer a single-product story driven entirely by HBM pricing negotiations between hyperscalers and memory manufacturers. Instead, pricing momentum is now propagating through conventional DRAM products, particularly DDR5, and doing so at a pace that is beginning to reshape expectations for 2027 HBM pricing as well. This creates a strategic inflection point for investors who have been positioned for a narrower, more predictable recovery.

The evidence is accumulating across multiple independent data streams. DDR5 spot pricing has risen 20 percent since the beginning of May and now trades at a 25 percent premium over May contract pricing. Korea's monthly DRAM export revenue reached a record high in May, surpassing the previous peak from March by 21 percent. Nanya Tech, a Taiwanese DRAM supplier, recorded its tenth consecutive month of triple-digit year-over-year revenue growth, with May revenue surging 730 percent. These are not isolated data points. They are mutually reinforcing signals that the demand recovery is broadening.

For serious investors, the critical question is no longer whether the DRAM cycle is recovering. The critical question is whether the current pricing dynamics in conventional DRAM will pull 2027 HBM pricing higher than current consensus estimates, and what that would mean for the relative positioning of memory manufacturers versus AI infrastructure plays.

## The Conventional DRAM Recovery Is Now Independently Verifiable Across Production, Distribution, and End Demand

The most important analytical shift in this cycle is that conventional DRAM pricing is no longer merely a residual beneficiary of HBM supply constraints. It is being driven by its own demand drivers, and those drivers are visible in data that do not rely on any single company's guidance.

Server ODM revenue from the five largest Taiwan-based manufacturers rose 53 percent year over year in May, driven by rack-level AI server shipments and accelerating ASIC-based server volumes. This is not a generic server refresh cycle. The composition of shipments is shifting toward higher memory-content configurations, which directly supports DRAM bit demand. Aspeed, the dominant supplier of baseboard management controllers for servers globally, reported May revenue up 69 percent year over year, confirming that server build activity remains robust even against a difficult comparison from the prior year.

China smartphone shipments, a demand variable that many analysts had written off as structurally impaired, recorded 19 percent year-over-year growth in May, marking the second consecutive month of expansion. This matters because smartphone DRAM content continues to increase with each generation, and China represents roughly 30 percent of global smartphone demand. The conventional wisdom that rising memory prices would crush end demand in price-sensitive markets is being challenged by actual shipment data.

Supreme Electronics, a major Taiwanese memory distributor, reported May revenue up 253 percent year over year and 54 percent month over month. Distributor revenue is a leading indicator of channel inventory restocking and end-market pull. When a distributor reports sequential growth of this magnitude, it suggests that the channel is not merely filling orders but actively building inventory in anticipation of further price increases.

Taken together, these data points form a coherent picture. Production is running at high utilization, server build activity is accelerating, smartphone demand is holding up, and the distribution channel is signaling confidence in continued pricing strength. The recovery in conventional DRAM is real, and it is broad.

## The DDR5 Premium Over Contract Pricing Creates a Structural Tension That Will Force Contract Renegotiations

The gap between spot and contract pricing in DDR5 is the single most important tactical signal in the current market. DDR5 spot pricing now sits 25 percent above May contract pricing, while DDR4 spot commands a 45 percent premium. These spreads are not sustainable in a market where contract pricing is typically renegotiated quarterly.

When spot prices trade significantly above contract prices, it creates an incentive for buyers to secure additional supply through the spot market, which in turn tightens the spot market further and puts upward pressure on the next round of contract negotiations. This dynamic is already visible in the sequential momentum of Korea's DRAM export revenue, which rose 21 percent month over month in May. Export revenue captures both contract and spot transactions, and the acceleration suggests that higher-priced spot transactions are becoming a larger share of the mix.

The implication for the second half of 2026 is straightforward. Contract pricing for DDR5 is likely to move higher in the July and October negotiation cycles, and the magnitude of those increases will depend on how quickly the supply side responds. Memory manufacturers have been disciplined about capacity additions, prioritizing HBM capacity expansion over conventional DRAM. If that discipline persists, the spot-contract gap may widen further before it closes.

For investors, the key question is whether the conventional DRAM pricing cycle has room to run beyond the current consensus estimates. The data from May and June suggest that it does, and that the risks are skewed to the upside for memory manufacturers with significant conventional DRAM exposure.

## Rising Conventional DRAM Prices Are Reshaping the 2027 HBM Pricing Debate in Ways the Market Has Not Fully Priced

The most strategically consequential insight from the June sentiment indicator is the relationship between conventional DRAM pricing and HBM pricing expectations for 2027. The report notes that investor expectations for 2027 HBM pricing continue to rise, and the stated reason is telling: the currently strong conventional DRAM pricing is expected to be taken into account when discussing HBM pricing for next year.

This is not an obvious connection. HBM is a premium product that requires advanced packaging and higher testing costs, and its pricing has historically been negotiated independently of conventional DRAM. But the logic is straightforward. HBM is built on a DRAM base die, and when conventional DRAM wafers are expensive and supply-constrained, the opportunity cost of allocating wafer capacity to HBM increases. Memory manufacturers have a strong incentive to demand higher HBM prices to compensate for the foregone revenue from conventional DRAM sales.

The report's authors have already revised their 2027 HBM pricing growth estimate for Samsung Electronics to 44 percent year over year, up from 14 percent previously. But they explicitly state that there could be additional upside risk given the tight HBM supply-demand balance and the widening gap between conventional DRAM and HBM pricing. This is a significant admission from a sell-side team that has been covering the memory sector through multiple cycles.

The second derivative of Samsung's DRAM average selling price for the second quarter of 2026 is estimated at negative 48 percentage points, meaning the pace of ASP growth is decelerating. But a deceleration from a very high base is not the same as a peak. The absolute level of ASPs continues to rise, and the sequential increase of approximately 46 percent quarter over quarter in the second quarter is itself a powerful data point. The second derivative is a warning flag, not a sell signal.

## One Critical Question the Sentiment Indicator Cannot Answer

For all its analytical rigor, the nine-component sentiment indicator has a structural blind spot that investors need to acknowledge. The indicator is overwhelmingly backward-looking. It uses data from May and June to assess current conditions, but it does not incorporate forward-looking supply forecasts or demand elasticity estimates.

The China smartphone data illustrates the problem. While May shipments showed 19 percent year-over-year growth, the report also notes that the China team expects second-quarter shipments to decline 14 percent year over year, primarily due to rising memory prices dragging down end demand. The sentiment indicator captures the positive May data point but does not weight it against the negative forward outlook. This creates a potential divergence between what the indicator says about current conditions and what the actual demand trajectory may look like in the third and fourth quarters.

Similarly, the indicator includes the second derivative of Samsung's DRAM ASP, which is negative, but it does not explicitly model the point at which ASP deceleration turns into an absolute decline. The history of DRAM cycles suggests that the transition from deceleration to contraction can happen quickly, especially if demand elasticity proves higher than expected at elevated price levels.

Investors using this indicator as a standalone signal should recognize that it is best interpreted as a confirmation of current momentum, not as a predictive tool for cycle timing. The most valuable use of the indicator is to identify when the composition of the recovery is changing, as it is doing now, and to adjust positioning accordingly.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning Across the DRAM Value Chain

For investors seeking to translate the sentiment indicator into actionable positioning, the following framework organizes the key variables into three distinct scenarios.

Scenario One assumes that conventional DRAM pricing continues to strengthen through the second half of 2026, driven by sustained server demand and disciplined supply. In this scenario, memory manufacturers with diversified product portfolios benefit most. The premium of DDR5 spot over contract should compress through higher contract pricing, and HBM pricing for 2027 should settle at or above the revised estimates. This is the base case implied by the current data.

Scenario Two assumes that demand elasticity in the smartphone and PC markets proves higher than expected, causing a demand pullback in the fourth quarter that reverses the conventional DRAM pricing gains. In this scenario, the spot-contract premium collapses, and HBM pricing negotiations become more favorable for hyperscaler buyers. The risk to this scenario is that it requires a demand shock that is not yet visible in any of the nine indicator components.

Scenario Three assumes that conventional DRAM pricing remains strong but that HBM pricing negotiations become decoupled from conventional DRAM entirely, with hyperscalers using their purchasing power to resist price increases. In this scenario, the relative outperformance shifts to companies with higher conventional DRAM exposure versus HBM exposure. This is the least likely scenario given the structural linkage between the two products, but it cannot be dismissed entirely.

The decision framework is not about predicting which scenario will occur. It is about identifying which data points would signal a transition from one scenario to another, and positioning ahead of those transitions. The current indicator supports Scenario One, but the forward-looking demand elasticity data from China smartphones is the most important variable to monitor for a potential shift to Scenario Two.

## The Full Picture Requires Reading Beyond the Headline Indicator

The DRAM sentiment indicator is a powerful analytical tool, but it is not a complete investment thesis. The nine components provide a systematic view of current conditions, but the most valuable insights come from understanding how those components interact and what they imply for future pricing dynamics.

The relationship between conventional DRAM and HBM pricing is the most important analytical question for the next twelve months. The current data suggests that conventional DRAM strength is pulling HBM pricing higher, but the magnitude of that effect is uncertain and depends on supply discipline and demand elasticity that cannot be captured in a backward-looking indicator.

Investors who rely solely on the headline indicator risk missing the second-order implications that will determine relative returns across the memory value chain.

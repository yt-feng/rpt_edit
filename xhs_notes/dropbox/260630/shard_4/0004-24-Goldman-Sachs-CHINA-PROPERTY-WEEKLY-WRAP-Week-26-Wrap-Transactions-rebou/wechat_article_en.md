# China's Property Market Is Not Recovering: It Is Restructuring Into a Two-Tier System

The headline numbers from Week 26 of China's property market appear to offer a moment of relief. Transaction volumes rebounded 38% week-on-week in the primary market and 27% in the secondary market, surpassing pre-festival June weekly averages by 24% and 3% respectively. For investors watching the sector through a rearview mirror, this looks like a green shoot. It is not. The data tells a story not of recovery, but of structural bifurcation—a market dividing itself into a shrinking primary channel and a stabilizing secondary channel, with policy intervention acting as a selective accelerant rather than a universal cure.

The first half of 2026 now stands as a critical demarcation point. Primary market sales volume year-to-date is down 14% year-on-year, and sits 15% below 2024 levels and a staggering 43% below 2023. The secondary market, by contrast, is flat year-on-year and has actually grown 18% above 2024 and 10% above 2023. This is not a cyclical trough. This is a reorganization of where demand is flowing and which assets are being liquidated. The question every serious investor must now answer is not whether the market has bottomed, but whether the bottom is different for different parts of the market—and what that means for capital allocation.

What makes this moment strategically important is the confluence of three forces: policy-driven supply intervention, a demand shift toward existing homes, and a valuation regime that has already priced in distress. The weekly data is a microcosm of a deeper transformation. The market is not healing uniformly; it is being remade selectively.

## The Primary-Secondary Divergence Has Become a Structural Fault Line, Not a Temporary Gap

The most consequential pattern in the Week 26 data is the widening gap between primary and secondary market performance. In the week itself, primary transactions were down 17% year-on-year despite the strong week-on-week rebound. Secondary transactions were up 6% year-on-year. Over the first half of 2026, the contrast is even starker: primary sales are down 14% year-on-year, while secondary sales are flat. Against 2024 and 2023 baselines, the divergence is extreme—secondary volumes are up 18% and 10% respectively, while primary volumes are down 15% and 43%.

This is not a short-term rotation. It reflects a permanent shift in buyer preference toward completed, visible inventory. The secondary market offers immediate possession, known quality, and no completion risk. In an environment where developer balance sheets remain under pressure and new-start data points to a high-twenties percentage year-on-year decline in June, buyers are rationally avoiding the primary channel. The implication is uncomfortable for developers: even if policy succeeds in stimulating demand, that demand is increasingly captured by the existing-home market, not by new sales.

The data on agent and seller sentiment reinforces this asymmetry. Price appreciation expectations edged higher among agents in Week 26, but not among home-sellers. This suggests that the intermediary layer sees a floor forming, but actual owners are not yet confident enough to raise asking prices. The market is liquid but not reflating. Volume is returning without price conviction. For investors, this is a signal that the recovery is transactional, not valuation-driven.

## Policy Intervention Is Targeting Inventory, Not Demand, and That Changes the Risk Calculus

The policy announcements from Henan and Hainan in Week 26 are instructive not for their scale but for their selectivity. Henan's plan to accelerate urban village renovation projects backed by state dedicated loans, with a target of substantial completion by 2028, is a supply-side measure. The acquisition of completed housing inventory for affordable rental housing, aiming to add over 50,000 units by 2030, is explicitly an inventory-destocking mechanism. Hainan's RMB 140 billion investment in urban renewal during the 15th Five-Year Plan period, with RMB 22 billion planned for 2026 alone, follows the same logic.

These are not demand-stimulus programs. They are government-led efforts to remove excess supply from the market, particularly in the primary channel. The logic is sound: if you cannot stimulate enough new demand to absorb existing inventory, you must reduce the inventory itself. But the second-order implication is that developers who rely on new sales volume will face a prolonged period of policy-induced competition from the state itself, which is now a buyer of last resort for completed units.

The data on new listing supply in June reinforces this point. While new listings accelerated 18% week-on-week in Week 26, overall June supply was limited—down 3% month-on-month and 16% year-on-year. Developers are not bringing new product to market aggressively. They are managing supply downward, partly by choice and partly by constraint. The result is a market where primary volumes are being compressed from both sides: weak demand and constrained supply.

For investors, the key question is whether inventory destocking by the state will eventually clear the path for a primary market recovery, or whether it will simply formalize a smaller, more state-dependent primary channel. The data on inventory months—currently 27.3, versus an average of 28.5 in May 2026—suggests that destocking is happening, but slowly. The market is not oversupplied in the way it was in 2024, but it is far from balanced.

## Valuation Has Already Discounted a Distressed Outcome, But the Market Is Pricing a Recovery That Has Not Arrived

The valuation data in the report is striking in its implications. Offshore coverage developers now trade at an average 42% discount to end-2026E net asset value and at 0.4 times 2026E price-to-book. Onshore coverage trades at a 36% discount to NAV and also at 0.4 times 2026E P/B. These multiples are not just low; they are below historical troughs for several metrics. The 2H2008, 2H2011, and 1H2014 troughs for offshore developers were 39%, 73%, and 58% discounts to NAV respectively, with P/Bs of 0.7, 0.9, and 0.9 times. Current valuations are deeper on NAV discount and lower on P/B than any of those cycles.

This creates a paradox. The market is pricing in a level of distress that historically has only occurred during systemic crises, yet the current environment is one of managed decline, not collapse. The stronger state-owned enterprise developers—CR Land and Greentown—outperformed in Week 26 with only a 2% week-on-week decline, while private-ownership enterprise developers fell 7% and other SOEs fell 6%. The dispersion is real, but the absolute level of valuation suggests that even the strongest names are being tarred with the same brush.

The question this raises is whether the market is correctly pricing a permanent impairment of the development model, or whether it is overcorrecting for a cyclical downturn that will eventually normalize. The data on completions—a roughly 20% year-on-year decline in June, with a full-year 2026 estimate of only a 1% decline—suggests that the supply pipeline is not collapsing entirely. It is shrinking, but not disappearing. If completions stabilize and inventory continues to be absorbed, the NAV discounts currently on offer may prove excessive.

But there is a catch. The valuation data also shows that the market is not rewarding developers for operational performance in the way it historically has. CR Land and Greentown, both rated Buy by the covering analysts, saw only a 2% weekly decline, but they still declined. The positive signals in the data—the week-on-week volume rebound, the agent sentiment improvement, the policy support—are not translating into price appreciation for developer equities. The market is looking through the near-term data to a structural problem that has not yet been solved.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Secondary Market Floor

For all the detail in the weekly wrap, one critical question remains unresolved: is the secondary market floor real, or is it a function of forced selling and temporary policy support? The data shows that secondary transaction volumes are flat year-to-date and up year-on-year, but it does not fully explain the composition of that demand. Are buyers in the secondary market end-users purchasing for occupation, or are they opportunistic investors taking advantage of price declines? The fact that price appreciation expectations are rising among agents but not among sellers suggests that the market is still searching for a clearing price.

More importantly, the report does not address what happens to secondary market volumes if the primary market begins to recover. If new-home supply increases and developers cut prices to clear inventory, will demand rotate back into the primary channel? Or has the trust deficit caused by delayed completions and developer distress permanently shifted preference toward existing homes? The answer to this question determines whether the secondary market strength is a leading indicator of stabilization or a temporary refuge that will disappear when primary supply returns.

The data on BEKE's transaction volumes—a 2% year-on-year increase in 2Q26, driven by a 23% decline in new-home transactions and a 12% increase in existing-home transactions—confirms the bifurcation but does not explain its durability. Investors need to watch whether the existing-home volume growth is broad-based across city tiers or concentrated in Tier 1 and strong Tier 2 cities. The report's city-level data is aggregated, and the dispersion may be hiding significant weakness in lower-tier markets.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Two-Tier Market

For serious investors, the weekly data should not be used to time entries and exits. It should be used to build a decision framework that accounts for the structural divergence now embedded in the market. The following framework is derived from the patterns in the report and is designed to be applied at the portfolio level.

First, distinguish between developers based on their exposure to primary versus secondary market dynamics. Developers with large unsold primary inventory in lower-tier cities face a different risk profile than those whose value is concentrated in completed assets or recurring income streams. The valuation discounts in the report are averages, and the dispersion within the coverage universe is significant. Investors should overweight developers whose NAV is supported by completed assets or service income, and underweight those whose NAV depends on future primary sales at historical margins.

Second, treat policy announcements as supply-side signals, not demand-side catalysts. The Henan and Hainan plans are inventory management tools. They do not increase household purchasing power or change mortgage affordability. Investors should monitor whether policy-induced inventory reduction leads to price stabilization in specific cities, and whether that stabilization is reflected in secondary market pricing before it appears in primary market volumes.

Third, use the completions data as a lead indicator for developer financial health. The GSPC tracker implies a 20% year-on-year decline in June completions, but full-year 2026 is estimated at only a 1% decline. If completions track toward the full-year estimate, it suggests that the worst of the supply contraction is behind the market. If June's decline deepens further, it signals ongoing distress in the construction pipeline that will delay revenue recognition and pressure balance sheets.

Fourth, compare current valuation multiples to the historical troughs cited in the report. The offshore coverage universe at 0.4 times 2026E P/B is below the 2H2008 trough of 0.7 times. The onshore universe at 0.4 times is below the 2H2008 trough of 1.6 times. These comparisons are not investment recommendations, but they establish a factual basis for debating whether the market is pricing a permanent structural decline or a cyclical trough that will revert. Investors who believe the former should avoid the sector entirely. Investors who believe the latter should be building positions in developers with the strongest balance sheets and most diversified revenue streams.

## The Unanswered Questions That Define the Next Phase

The Week 26 data provides a snapshot, but the strategic picture remains incomplete. Three open questions will determine whether the current stabilization is a prelude to recovery or a pause before further decline.

First, can the secondary market absorb enough inventory to meaningfully reduce the months of supply in the primary channel? At 27.3 months of inventory, the market is still heavily oversupplied in historical context. The secondary market is growing, but it is not growing fast enough to single-handedly clear the primary overhang.

Second, will the policy focus on urban renewal and affordable housing create a new source of demand, or will it simply redirect existing demand from the private market to the state channel? If the state becomes a dominant buyer of completed inventory, it may stabilize prices but crowd out private developers from the recovery.

Third, how will the valuation gap between stronger SOE developers and the rest of the market resolve? The outperformance of CR Land and Greentown in Week 26 suggests that investors are discriminating, but the overall sector decline of 6-7% week-on-week indicates that discrimination is not yet rewarded. A sustained divergence would signal that the market has found a basis for differentiation. A continued broad decline would suggest that the sector is being treated as uninvestable regardless of quality.

These are not questions that a single week of data can answer. They require sustained observation of transaction volumes, pricing trends, policy implementation, and developer financial disclosures. The weekly wrap provides the raw material for that analysis, but the synthesis requires a framework that extends beyond the numbers.

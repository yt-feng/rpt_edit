# Mega Projects Are Reshaping U.S. Construction, But The Recovery Is More Fragile Than It Looks

The U.S. construction equipment and rental market is telling two stories at once. On the surface, the headline numbers are striking: total non-residential construction starts surged approximately 60% year-over-year in May, mega project starts exploded by roughly 420% year-over-year, and a single LNG export facility in Louisiana worth approximately $14 billion broke ground in just one month. These figures suggest a booming market, driven by large-scale industrial policy and energy infrastructure investments. But beneath this veneer of strength lies a more complex and precarious reality. The non-residential square footage actually declined by nearly 10% year-over-year, residential starts continue to weaken, and housing affordability remains below pre-pandemic levels. The market is not uniformly recovering; it is bifurcating between a small number of massive, policy-driven projects and a broad base of activity that is struggling to gain momentum.

This divergence matters for investors in construction equipment, rental companies, and building materials. The traditional cyclical signals are breaking down. A 60% jump in construction starts by value sounds like a classic recovery, but when that growth is concentrated in a handful of mega projects, the demand for equipment, labor, and materials becomes lumpy, unpredictable, and prone to bottlenecks. The rental equipment market, in particular, faces a strategic challenge: how to allocate capital and fleet capacity when the largest projects are few, massive, and geographically concentrated, while the underlying small and mid-sized project pipeline remains tepid.

The report from a leading institutional research firm provides the data to unpack this dynamic. The May Dodge Construction Starts data, combined with the latest mega project tracking, reveals a market that is being pulled in opposing directions. For strategic investors, the key question is not whether construction activity is growing, but whether the current composition of growth is sustainable and where the true risk-adjusted opportunities lie. The answer has profound implications for equipment manufacturers, rental operators, and the broader industrial economy.

## Non-Residential Starts Are Surging in Value But Shrinking in Square Footage, Revealing a Market Driven by Inflation and Mega Projects, Not Broad-Based Demand

The headline figure for May non-residential construction starts is eye-catching: total value increased roughly 60% year-over-year, accelerating from the prior month's approximate 30% growth. Breaking this down, the building segment grew approximately 30% year-over-year, while the non-building segment—which includes infrastructure, pipelines, and heavy civil work—exploded by roughly 100% year-over-year. On the surface, this looks like a broad-based acceleration.

But the square footage data tells a different story. Non-residential square footage declined by nearly 10% year-over-year in May, reversing the prior month's approximate 10% increase. On a month-over-month basis, square footage grew only 12%, weaker than the typical seasonal average of around 15%. This means that the value growth is not coming from building more space; it is coming from building more expensive space. Non-residential pricing increased roughly 40% year-over-year, up sharply from the prior month's approximate 15% increase.

The implications are twofold. First, inflation in construction costs is running hot and accelerating. This is not simply a pass-through of material costs; it reflects tight capacity in specialized trades, engineering, and project management for complex projects. Second, the mix of projects is shifting toward high-value, capital-intensive structures such as LNG facilities, data centers, and public infrastructure, which have high costs per square foot, while lower-value categories like warehouses and miscellaneous buildings are declining. The warehouse category, in particular, has been a significant drag, reflecting the post-pandemic normalization of logistics demand.

For equipment manufacturers and rental companies, this means that demand for certain types of heavy equipment—large cranes, earthmoving machinery for site preparation, and specialized tools for energy and industrial projects—may be strong, while demand for general-purpose equipment used in warehouses, retail, and smaller commercial projects may be soft. The rental market must navigate this divergence carefully, avoiding over-investment in fleet types that serve the weakening segments while capturing the opportunities in mega projects.

## Mega Projects Now Dominate Non-Residential Starts, Creating a Winner-Take-Most Dynamic That Distorts Traditional Demand Signals

The most striking data point in the report is the trajectory of mega project construction starts. In May, mega project starts totaled approximately $33 billion, up roughly 100% from the prior month, and exhibited year-over-year growth of approximately 420%, accelerating sharply from the prior month's approximate 150% growth. The largest single project to break ground was the LNG Export Facility in Louisiana, valued at approximately $14 billion, which alone represented roughly 40% of all mega project starts in May.

This concentration is not a one-month anomaly. During April, mega projects accounted for approximately 30% of all non-residential starts, well above the trailing twelve-month average of approximately 20%. Over the last twelve months, LNG, public infrastructure, and data centers have represented the largest share of mega projects at roughly 65%, followed by manufacturing, renewables, and other categories. Notably, the manufacturing portion, which used to be the largest at around 23%, has encountered headwinds, particularly on the electric vehicle side.

This shift has profound implications for the construction equipment and rental ecosystem. Mega projects are fundamentally different from traditional construction activity. They have longer lead times, more complex permitting and financing requirements, and a different demand profile for equipment. A single $14 billion LNG facility will require massive earthmoving, pile driving, and heavy lifting equipment for several years, but it will not generate the steady, diversified demand that supports a broad rental fleet. Companies that are heavily exposed to mega projects may see volatile revenue streams and face execution risks, while those that serve the small and mid-sized project market may see stagnant demand.

The data also raises a strategic question: how sustainable is the mega project pipeline? The report notes that manufacturing mega projects, particularly in EVs, are facing headwinds. If the current wave of LNG and data center projects begins to slow due to regulatory hurdles, permitting delays, or changes in energy policy, the entire non-residential construction market could face a sharp deceleration. The current growth is not broad-based; it is dependent on a relatively small number of very large decisions.

## Residential Construction Is Weakening Again, and Housing Affordability Remains a Structural Constraint That Could Offset Any Benefit from Lower Interest Rates

While non-residential construction grabs headlines, the residential sector is sending a cautionary signal. In May, residential construction starts declined approximately 7% year-over-year in value, a deterioration from the prior month's approximate 3% decline. In square footage terms, the decline was approximately 4% year-over-year, reversing the prior month's approximate 2% increase.

The composition within residential is also shifting. Two-family houses have reverted to positive growth, increasing nearly 15% year-over-year in value, while apartments declined 13% and one-family houses declined 3%. This suggests that the market is bifurcating within residential as well, with demand concentrated in a narrow segment of the market.

The fundamental issue is housing affordability, which remains below the levels seen in 2019-2020. The report notes that early reads for 2026 show a small deterioration in affordability after the improvement seen in 2025. This is critical because the conventional narrative has been that lower interest rates would eventually unlock housing demand. But if affordability is deteriorating again, it may reflect that home prices and construction costs are rising faster than the decline in mortgage rates. Higher inflation in construction costs is eating into any benefit from lower financing costs.

For equipment and rental companies, the residential weakness is a headwind for certain types of equipment used in homebuilding and apartment construction. However, it also creates an opportunity: if housing affordability remains constrained, demand for rental housing could remain strong, supporting multi-family construction in certain markets. The key is to distinguish between regions and sub-segments that are experiencing genuine demand from those that are simply facing cyclical headwinds.

## The Report Raises Unresolved Questions About the Sustainability of the Mega Project Pipeline and the Risk of a Sharp Slowdown in 2027

The report provides an excellent snapshot of current conditions, but it also leaves several critical questions unanswered. First, how deep and durable is the pipeline of mega projects beyond the current wave? The report shows that LNG, public infrastructure, and data centers have dominated the last twelve months, but manufacturing has already started to decelerate. If the policy environment shifts or if energy prices change, the pace of new mega project announcements could slow dramatically.

Second, the report does not fully address the capacity constraints that mega projects create. When a single $14 billion project breaks ground in one month, it absorbs a significant share of available engineering, project management, and specialized equipment capacity. This can drive up costs and extend timelines for other projects, creating a crowding-out effect that the headline data does not capture.

Third, the report highlights that non-residential square footage is declining even as value surges, but it does not fully explore what this means for equipment utilization. If the average project is becoming more capital-intensive but less space-intensive, the demand for certain types of equipment may be structurally different from historical patterns. Rental companies need to understand whether their fleet composition is aligned with this new reality.

Finally, the report touches on the residential sector's weakness but does not provide a clear framework for when a recovery might materialize. Housing affordability is a function of prices, wages, interest rates, and construction costs, and all four are moving in conflicting directions. The report leaves it to the reader to assess the probability of a recovery in 2027 versus further deterioration.

## A Decision Framework for Equipment and Rental Investors: Assess Exposure Along Three Dimensions—Project Type, End Market, and Pricing Power

Given the bifurcated nature of the construction market, a one-size-fits-all investment approach is unlikely to succeed. Instead, investors should evaluate companies based on three criteria.

First, assess exposure to project type. Companies that derive a significant portion of revenue from mega projects in LNG, data centers, and public infrastructure are likely to see strong near-term demand, but they face higher execution risk and potential volatility if the pipeline slows. Companies that serve the broad small and mid-sized project market may see stagnant volumes but could benefit from pricing power if capacity remains tight.

Second, evaluate end-market diversification. The report shows that non-residential is strong in value but weak in square footage, while residential is weakening. Companies with balanced exposure across both sectors may be better positioned to weather a downturn in one segment. Conversely, companies that are overly reliant on residential or on a single type of non-residential project face concentrated risk.

Third, analyze pricing power. The 40% year-over-year increase in non-residential pricing is a powerful tailwind for companies that can pass through cost increases. Equipment manufacturers with strong brand positions and rental companies with high utilization rates may be able to sustain margin expansion even if volumes soften. Companies that lack pricing power may see margins compress as costs rise.

The report also provides a useful lens for looking at specific sub-sectors. For example, the building materials section notes that companies with exposure to U.S. non-residential infrastructure, such as CRH, have high beta to this end market, which could surprise positively. Similarly, companies like Amrize, with dominant positions in U.S. cement and exposure to structurally strong inland markets, may have superior pricing power. In the equipment rental space, the report's analysis of United Rentals and Sunbelt suggests that large, diversified rental companies may be better positioned to navigate the current environment than smaller, specialized players.

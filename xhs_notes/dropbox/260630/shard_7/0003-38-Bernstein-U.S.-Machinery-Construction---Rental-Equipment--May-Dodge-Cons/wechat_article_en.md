# Mega Projects Are Reshaping U.S. Construction, But the Real Story Is the Widening Gap Between Value and Volume

The U.S. construction equipment and rental market is sending a signal that is easy to misinterpret. At first glance, the data looks robust. Total non-residential construction starts surged approximately 60% year-over-year in May, building starts rose roughly 30%, and non-building infrastructure expanded by 100%. These are headline numbers that could justify optimism across the machinery and rental ecosystem. But the optimism is premature. The real picture is more complex, and it carries strategic implications for investors, operators, and corporate planners who need to position for the next 12 to 24 months.

The critical insight from the latest Dodge construction starts data is this: the market is bifurcating along lines that many observers have not yet fully appreciated. Value is soaring, but physical volume is stagnating or declining. Non-residential square footage fell nearly 10% year-over-year in May, even as project values exploded. Pricing inflation, not real activity, is driving the apparent growth. This divergence is not a statistical anomaly. It is a structural shift that reflects the changing composition of construction activity, the rise of mega projects, and the persistent affordability constraints in residential markets.

For anyone following the machinery, rental, and building materials sectors, this is the single most important dynamic to understand. The old models that treated value growth as a proxy for activity volume no longer hold. The market is being pulled in two directions, and the companies that navigate this divergence successfully will be those that adjust their strategies accordingly.

## The Composition of Construction Activity Is Shifting Toward Capital-Intensive, Low-Volume Mega Projects

The most striking pattern in the May data is the extraordinary growth in mega project starts. These projects, defined as large-scale initiatives typically exceeding $1 billion in value, surged approximately 420% year-over-year, reaching roughly $33 billion in total starts for the month. The largest single project, an LNG export facility in Louisiana valued at approximately $14 billion, accounted for about 40% of all mega project activity in May. This is not a one-month anomaly. Over the trailing twelve months, LNG, public infrastructure, and data centers have represented approximately 65% of all mega project starts.

The implication for the machinery and rental market is profound. Mega projects are capital-intensive but physically concentrated. They generate enormous value in construction spending but require relatively limited square footage compared to traditional commercial or industrial developments. A $14 billion LNG facility occupies a fraction of the physical space that a comparable value of warehouse, office, or retail construction would require. This is why non-residential square footage declined year-over-year even as total project value grew by 60%.

This shift is not temporary. The structural drivers behind mega project growth are durable. The U.S. is in the midst of a multiyear build-out of energy export capacity, data center infrastructure to support artificial intelligence and cloud computing, and public infrastructure renewal under federal programs. These are not cyclical phenomena that will reverse in a few quarters. They represent a fundamental reallocation of construction capital toward projects that are large, long-duration, and low-density in terms of physical footprint.

For equipment manufacturers, rental companies, and materials suppliers, this means that aggregate demand metrics are becoming less reliable as indicators of underlying activity. A company that tracks value growth alone will overestimate the need for equipment, labor, and materials. A company that tracks square footage alone will underestimate the strategic importance of mega projects. The correct approach is to disaggregate the market into its component segments and treat mega projects as a distinct category with its own demand dynamics, pricing behavior, and competitive structure.

## Non-Residential Value Growth Is Masking a Broad-Based Decline in Physical Activity

The May data reveals a striking divergence within non-residential construction itself. While total non-residential value grew approximately 60% year-over-year, the square footage decline of roughly 10% was driven by specific categories. Warehouses and miscellaneous buildings were the primary contributors to the decline, even as manufacturing and office construction posted strong readings in physical terms.

This pattern matters because it signals that the recovery is uneven and that the traditional drivers of equipment demand are weakening. Warehouses have been a dominant source of construction activity in recent years, fueled by e-commerce logistics expansion. The year-over-year decline in warehouse square footage suggests that this cycle may be maturing. If warehouse construction continues to slow, it will remove a significant source of demand for earthmoving equipment, material handling systems, and rental machinery.

The office sector, meanwhile, presents a more nuanced picture. Office square footage grew 36% year-over-year in May, which appears encouraging. But this growth comes from a very low base. Office construction has been depressed for years due to remote work trends and shifting corporate real estate strategies. A single large project can distort the year-over-year comparison. The question is not whether office construction is recovering, but whether the recovery is sustainable and broad-based. The data does not yet support a confident affirmative answer.

Manufacturing construction remains a bright spot, with square footage growing 144% year-over-year. This reflects the continued build-out of advanced manufacturing facilities, including semiconductor plants, battery factories, and other projects supported by federal industrial policy. But even here, the trajectory is not uniform. The manufacturing portion of mega projects, which was once the largest category, has encountered headwinds, particularly on the electric vehicle side. The composition of manufacturing investment is shifting, and not all subsegments will grow at the same pace.

The overarching point is that non-residential construction is not a single market. It is a collection of submarkets with different drivers, different trajectories, and different implications for equipment demand. Investors and operators who treat non-residential as a monolithic category will miss the most important strategic questions: where is growth real, where is it inflationary, and where is it fading?

## Residential Construction Faces a Structural Affordability Constraint That Interest Rate Cuts Alone Cannot Solve

The residential sector presents a different but equally challenging picture. In May, residential construction starts declined approximately 7% year-over-year in value and approximately 4% in square footage. This continues a pattern of weakness that has persisted despite expectations that lower interest rates would drive a recovery.

The data within residential is instructive. Two-family houses grew nearly 15% year-over-year in value, reversing a negative trend. But apartments declined 13%, and single-family homes declined 3%. The mix is shifting toward lower-density housing, which has different implications for equipment demand than multifamily construction.

The root cause is housing affordability, which remains below 2019-2020 levels despite the improvement seen in 2025. Early reads for 2026 show a small deterioration after the improvement, suggesting that the affordability problem is not resolving quickly. Higher inflation is eroding purchasing power, and the relationship between interest rates and housing starts is not as straightforward as many models assume.

This matters for the machinery and rental market because residential construction is a significant source of demand for certain types of equipment, including compact earthmoving equipment, aerial work platforms, and concrete-related machinery. If residential starts remain depressed, it will create a headwind for companies that have significant exposure to this segment.

The strategic question is whether residential construction can recover without a more fundamental improvement in affordability. Lower interest rates help, but they are not sufficient. Home prices remain elevated, construction costs are high, and wage growth, while positive, has not kept pace with the cumulative increase in homeownership costs. The data suggests that the residential recovery will be slower and more uneven than many hope.

## The Report Raises a Critical Unanswered Question About the Sustainability of Pricing Power

One of the most striking data points in the report is the approximately 40% year-over-year increase in non-residential pricing in May, compared to roughly 15% in the prior month. This is a dramatic acceleration, and it raises a question that the report does not fully answer: is this pricing power sustainable, or is it a temporary phenomenon driven by project mix and supply constraints?

The argument for sustainability is that mega projects and large-scale manufacturing facilities are less price-sensitive than traditional construction. These projects are often backed by corporate balance sheets, government funding, or long-term offtake agreements. The developers have less incentive to negotiate aggressively on equipment and materials costs, because the overall project economics are dominated by factors other than construction input costs. If this is correct, pricing could remain elevated even if physical activity slows.

The argument against sustainability is that the pricing spike reflects a temporary mismatch between supply and demand. The construction industry is still recovering from the pandemic-era disruptions to supply chains and labor availability. As these constraints ease, pricing could moderate. Additionally, if square footage continues to decline, equipment utilization rates could fall, putting downward pressure on rental rates and equipment prices.

The report does not resolve this tension, and it is a question that investors must answer for themselves. The answer has direct implications for the earnings outlook of equipment manufacturers, rental companies, and materials suppliers. If pricing is sustainable, then companies with pricing power will continue to generate strong margins even if volume growth is modest. If pricing is temporary, then the current earnings strength could prove to be a peak.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Divergence Between Value and Volume

For readers who need to translate this analysis into actionable decisions, the following framework is designed to help assess exposure and positioning across the construction and rental ecosystem.

First, assess your exposure to mega projects versus traditional construction. Mega projects have different demand characteristics, different pricing dynamics, and different competitive structures. Companies that are well-positioned to serve mega projects, either through specialized equipment, engineering services, or project management capabilities, are likely to benefit from the current trend. Companies that are primarily exposed to traditional commercial and residential construction face a more challenging environment.

Second, evaluate the role of pricing in your revenue and margin structure. If your business model relies on volume growth to generate earnings, the current environment is unfavorable. If your business model can capture pricing gains even in a low-volume environment, the outlook is more positive. This is particularly relevant for rental companies, where utilization rates and rental rates are both critical to profitability.

Third, monitor the trajectory of housing affordability and residential starts. The residential sector is unlikely to recover quickly, and companies with significant residential exposure should plan for continued weakness. The wildcard is whether a more significant decline in interest rates or a correction in home prices can change the affordability calculus.

Fourth, watch for signs that the manufacturing construction boom is broadening or narrowing. The headwinds in the electric vehicle segment are a warning sign that not all manufacturing investment is equally durable. Companies that are diversified across manufacturing subsegments are better positioned than those that are concentrated in a single area.

Fifth, consider the implications of the divergence between value and volume for capital allocation decisions. In an environment where pricing is strong but volume is weak, the optimal strategy may be to invest in pricing power and cost control rather than capacity expansion. Companies that build capacity based on value growth alone risk overinvesting in a market where physical demand is not keeping pace.

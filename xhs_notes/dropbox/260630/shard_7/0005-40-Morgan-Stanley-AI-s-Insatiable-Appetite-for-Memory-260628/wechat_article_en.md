# AI's Memory Demand Is Becoming Structural, and That Changes the Software Investment Thesis

The most important data point this week for software investors was not a software earnings report at all. It was Micron's fiscal third-quarter results, which showed revenue surging nearly 350 percent year over year to $41 billion. The obvious narrative is about data center buildout. The more consequential narrative is about what is changing inside the workloads themselves. As artificial intelligence shifts from discrete prompt-response interactions toward persistent, agentic workflows, the memory requirements of these systems are exploding. The market is beginning to debate whether this wave of demand is less cyclical than previous memory cycles and more structural. That debate matters profoundly for software investors, because it signals a shift in the underlying architecture of enterprise technology spending.

The implications extend far beyond semiconductor companies. When memory becomes a structural bottleneck and a strategic asset, the software companies that sit atop this infrastructure must adapt their business models, their product architectures, and their cost structures. The European software landscape is already showing early signals of this transformation. SAP's CEO stated publicly that within three to four years, there may be no human developers writing software inside the company. Springer Nature is deploying AI tools to compress a 200-day publishing process. Informa is positioning its B2B live events as a differentiated human alternative in an AI-saturated world. These are not disconnected anecdotes. They are the first symptoms of a structural reordering of how software is built, sold, and valued.

The core argument of this analysis is straightforward: the memory-intensity of agentic AI is creating a new infrastructure layer that will reshape software economics, and investors who treat this as a temporary cycle risk missing the most important strategic shift in enterprise technology since the move to cloud.

## The Shift from Cyclical to Structural Memory Demand Creates a New Infrastructure Constraint for Software

The semiconductor industry has long been defined by boom-bust cycles in memory pricing. When demand surges, manufacturers add capacity, oversupply follows, prices collapse, and the cycle repeats. What makes the current moment different is the nature of the demand driver. Previous cycles were driven by discrete products: PCs, smartphones, cloud servers. Each of those products had relatively predictable memory requirements. An AI agent that persists across sessions, calls multiple tools, reads documents, spawns sub-agents, and iterates toward an outcome consumes dramatically more memory per workload than a simple chatbot query.

The evidence is in Micron's 16 signed long-term strategic customer agreements. Companies do not lock themselves into multi-year memory contracts for a cyclical product. They do so when memory has become a strategic input that constrains their ability to deploy AI at scale. This is the same pattern we saw with cloud computing: what began as a cost-saving measure became a structural dependency that reshaped the entire enterprise software stack. The difference is that memory is a hardware constraint, not a software abstraction. If the demand is structural, then the supply response will need to be equally structural, which implies sustained pricing power for memory manufacturers and sustained cost pressure for the software companies that consume memory.

For software investors, the so-what is uncomfortable. Most enterprise software companies do not model memory as a strategic cost driver. They model server costs, cloud fees, and engineering headcount. If memory becomes a binding constraint on workload deployment, then the unit economics of AI-enabled software products may deteriorate faster than consensus expects. Companies that can optimize their memory footprint will have a competitive advantage. Companies that cannot will face margin compression.

## SAP's Vision of Zero Human Developers Signals a Radical Restructuring of Software Cost Structures

When the CEO of the largest European software company says that in three to four years there may be no human developers writing software inside the organization, the statement deserves more than a headline. It signals a fundamental rethinking of how software value is created and captured. SAP's logic is not about cost reduction alone. It is about a quality-over-quantity hiring philosophy in which one highly skilled AI expert replaces three traditional hires. The company is redesigning compensation packages to attract AI talent in a scarce market, which implies that the savings from reduced headcount will be reinvested in higher-cost AI specialists.

This has two strategic implications. First, the traditional model of software development as a labor-intensive craft is being replaced by a model of software development as a capital-intensive process in which the primary inputs are compute, memory, and AI models. Second, the competitive dynamics of the software industry will shift from who can hire the most developers to who can deploy AI coding tools most effectively. Companies with large existing codebases and complex enterprise workflows may have an advantage, because their proprietary data and domain expertise become the training material for AI agents. New entrants without this historical data may struggle to achieve the same level of code quality and business logic integration.

The open question is whether this transformation will compress software margins or expand them. If AI reduces development costs faster than it reduces pricing power, margins expand. If AI commoditizes software functionality and intensifies competition, margins compress. The answer depends on whether the AI systems themselves become a source of durable differentiation or a baseline capability available to all competitors.

## Academic Publishing Shows How AI Can Monetize Process Efficiency Without Disrupting Incumbent Economics

The Springer Nature technology event provides a useful counterpoint to the disruption narrative. Academic publishing is an industry with high margins, long-established workflows, and significant bottlenecks. The average time from submission to publication is approximately 200 days, and for Nature-branded journals it reaches 300 days. Springer Nature is deploying AI tools at multiple points in this process: helping authors identify suitable journals, supporting faster initial manuscript assessment, recommending reviewers, and redirecting manuscripts to better-fitting journals within the ecosystem.

The strategic insight is that these tools address existing bottlenecks rather than creating new revenue streams. The primary benefit is faster publication cycles, which increase the volume of research that can be processed within existing editorial capacity. For a publisher with high fixed costs and high margins, volume growth translates almost directly into incremental profit. The risk is that AI tools commoditize the peer review process and reduce the value of the publisher's curation function. If AI can assess manuscript quality as effectively as human editors, the premium that top journals command may erode.

The current evidence suggests that publishers are navigating this risk effectively. AI adoption among researchers is already high, with approximately 75 percent using AI tools and about one-third willing to pay for them. This willingness to pay is critical. It suggests that AI tools in academic publishing are being positioned as value-added services rather than cost-reduction mechanisms. The question is whether this pricing power is sustainable as AI tools become more widely available and more standardized.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered Whether Memory Demand Will Transform Software Business Models or Merely Inflate Them

The most important analytical gap in the current debate is the mechanism by which structural memory demand will affect software company financials. There are three plausible pathways, and they lead to very different investment conclusions.

The first pathway is cost inflation. If memory becomes a larger share of total infrastructure cost, software companies that price on a per-user or per-workload basis may see their cost of goods sold rise faster than revenue. This would compress gross margins unless pricing is adjusted. The second pathway is product transformation. If memory constraints limit the complexity of AI workloads that can be deployed, software companies may need to redesign their products to be more memory-efficient. This would create a period of elevated R&D spending and uncertain outcomes. The third pathway is competitive reordering. If memory access becomes a strategic advantage, companies with preferential access to memory supply or with proprietary memory optimization techniques may gain market share at the expense of competitors.

The current report provides data on memory demand and on software company actions, but it does not provide a framework for distinguishing among these pathways. Investors need to know which companies have pricing power to pass through higher infrastructure costs, which companies have the engineering capability to optimize memory usage, and which companies have the balance sheet to invest through a period of uncertainty. Without this framework, the observation that memory demand is structural remains interesting but not actionable.

## A Decision Framework for Software Investors in the Age of Structural Memory Demand

The following framework translates the analytical insights from this report into specific questions that investors should ask about each software company in their portfolio.

First, assess memory dependency. What percentage of the company's cost of goods sold is related to memory and compute infrastructure? Companies that run their own data centers or that deploy large-scale AI workloads will have higher exposure than companies that are primarily SaaS providers with lightweight applications. The higher the memory dependency, the more sensitive the company is to structural changes in memory pricing.

Second, evaluate pricing power. Can the company pass through higher infrastructure costs to customers? Companies with switching costs, proprietary data, or mission-critical workflows have more pricing power than companies in competitive markets with low differentiation. The presence of long-term customer agreements, as seen in the memory market itself, is a positive signal.

Third, analyze product architecture. Is the company's software designed to be memory-efficient, or does it assume abundant cheap memory? Companies that have invested in optimization and compression technologies will be better positioned than companies that rely on brute-force approaches. This is particularly relevant for companies deploying AI agents that require persistent context and multi-step reasoning.

Fourth, consider competitive dynamics. Does structural memory demand favor incumbents or disruptors? Incumbents with large existing customer bases and proprietary data may have advantages in training more efficient AI models. Disruptors with clean-slate architectures may have advantages in building memory-efficient systems from the ground up.

Fifth, monitor management commentary. Are software company management teams discussing memory as a strategic issue or as a commodity input? The difference in framing is revealing. Companies that treat memory as strategic are likely investing in optimization and long-term supply relationships. Companies that treat memory as a commodity may be caught off guard by structural price increases.

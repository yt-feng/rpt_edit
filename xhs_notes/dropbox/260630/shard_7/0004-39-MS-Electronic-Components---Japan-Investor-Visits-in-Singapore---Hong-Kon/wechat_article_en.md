# The MLCC Market Is Splitting Into Two Worlds: Why Product Competitiveness Will Outlast Price Hikes

A fundamental divergence is underway in the electronic components industry, and most institutional investors are looking in the wrong direction. After conducting 31 one-on-one meetings and two group lunches with investors in Singapore and Hong Kong in late June 2026, a clear pattern emerged: nearly all the attention was focused on whether commoditized MLCC prices would rise further. The more consequential story, however, is the quiet transformation occurring at the high end of the market, where product mix improvements, not price increases, are driving structural earnings expansion. The gap between companies that raise prices indiscriminately and those that continuously enhance product competitiveness is about to become the defining investment theme of this cycle.

This is not a cyclical debate about peak pricing. It is a structural argument about which companies will compound value over the next five years. The report from a global investment bank covering 20 Japanese electronic component companies makes this case with unusual clarity. Murata Manufacturing is expected to grow its AI and data center MLCC sales by 85-90% year-over-year in fiscal year ending March 2027, with roughly 50% of that growth coming from average selling price increases driven entirely by product mix, not by raising prices on identical products. Meanwhile, commoditized MLCC prices are rising across the industry, tempting short-term profit maximization. The strategic question for investors is which path leads to sustainable value creation.

The timing matters because we are approaching an inflection point. The total capacitance required per AI server accelerator board is roughly doubling with each new generation of GPUs, while installation space remains fixed. This creates a technical constraint that only companies with deep materials science capabilities can solve. The companies that solve it will not need to raise prices. Their customers will pay a premium for smaller, higher-capacitance components because there is no alternative. The companies that merely ride the commodity pricing wave will face a very different outcome when the cycle turns.

## The High-Value MLCC Market Is Structurally Underappreciated Because Investors Are Focused on the Wrong Pricing Signal

The most revealing finding from the investor meetings was the disconnect between what institutions wanted to discuss and where the real earnings power is forming. Investors were almost entirely focused on the extent of price hikes in commoditized MLCC products. They paid little attention to the likelihood of earnings expansion driven by growth in high value-added products for AI servers. This is a classic mistake in technology investing: focusing on the visible, cyclical variable while ignoring the invisible, structural one.

The data is striking. For Murata, the projected 85-90% year-over-year increase in AI and data center MLCC sales in F3/27 breaks down into roughly 40% volume growth and 50% ASP growth. That ASP growth does not assume any unit price increases for identical products. It reflects a shift in mix toward products that are fundamentally more expensive because they are technically more difficult to manufacture. The MLCCs demanded by AI servers, such as 1608 size 100μF, 1005 size 47μF, and 0603 size 10μF, require miniaturization and capacitance levels that only one company can currently mass-produce reliably.

The implication is that Murata is not simply benefiting from AI demand. It is benefiting from a technical monopoly in the most demanding segment of the market. While Taiyo Yuden is also expected to grow its AI MLCC sales by 82-83% year-over-year in F3/27, the absolute contribution will be smaller because its starting base is lower and its product portfolio is less advanced. The gap in capability is not static. By the time Taiyo Yuden achieves stable mass production of the MLCCs Murata currently ships, Murata will likely be mass-producing even smaller, higher-capacitance products. This is not a race to parity. It is a widening moat.

## Commodity Pricing Discipline Creates a Strategic Moat That Short-Term Price Hikers Cannot Replicate

The report makes a nuanced argument about pricing strategy that deserves careful attention. Murata does not proactively implement price hikes for commoditized products, even though distributor-to-customer prices for these products are rising. The reasoning is strategic rather than passive. While price increases may maximize short-term profits, they also lower barriers to entry. When prices rise in commoditized segments, competitors from China, South Korea, and Taiwan are incentivized to add capacity and capture share. Over the medium to long term, this erodes market position.

This is a classic prisoner's dilemma in industrial economics. The rational short-term move for any individual company is to raise prices when demand is strong. But if every company does so, the industry becomes more attractive to new entrants, capacity expands, and the inevitable downturn becomes more severe. The companies that resist this temptation are making a long-term bet that their cost competitiveness and technological leadership will allow them to maintain or grow market share through the cycle.

The report identifies a clear pattern: many companies in China, South Korea, and Taiwan that pursue short-term profit maximization through indiscriminate price increases appear not to allocate sufficient resources to R&D or production technology improvement. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle. They capture profits today but fail to build the capabilities needed to compete tomorrow. When the commodity cycle turns, they will face margin compression and share loss simultaneously.

For investors, the implication is that current profitability in commoditized MLCCs may be misleading. Companies that appear cheap on near-term earnings may be destroying long-term value. The companies that appear expensive because they are investing through the cycle may be the ones that compound earnings for years.

## The ABF Substrate Market Faces a Credibility Gap Between Company Guidance and Consensus Expectations

The report's analysis of Ibiden introduces a different tension. While the company is expected to continue expanding earnings, the market consensus appears too high. The report forecasts operating profit of ¥94.7 billion in F3/27, below the company's own guidance of ¥90 billion but well below the FactSet consensus of ¥99.7 billion. The gap widens in subsequent years. For F3/28, the report forecasts ¥128.5 billion against a company target of ¥150 billion and consensus of ¥151.5 billion. For F3/31, the gap is even larger: ¥242.6 billion against a company target of ¥300 billion.

The bullish case for Ibiden rests on ABF package substrates for NVIDIA's Rubin platform. The report estimates that volume shipments began in 4Q F3/26 and that sales for Rubin will exceed those for Blackwell in 1Q F3/27. The technology transition to EMIB-T is expected to drive further value addition and higher pricing. But the report argues that meaningful earnings contribution from EMIB-T will not materialize until F3/29, and that it will be difficult for these products to achieve profit margins comparable to NVIDIA's existing offerings.

This creates an interesting analytical puzzle. The market appears to be pricing in a trajectory of earnings growth that assumes both rapid volume ramp and high margins. The report suggests that the volume ramp is real but the margin trajectory is overly optimistic. The key variable is the pace at which EMIB-T becomes a meaningful profit contributor. If it takes longer than the market expects, or if margins are lower, the current valuation may be difficult to justify.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Second-Order Effects of Capacity Constraints and Competitive Response

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several important questions unresolved. The first concerns capacity. Taiyo Yuden froze capital expenditure in the second half of 2024, limiting MLCC production capacity growth to around 5% in F3/26. This constraint has prevented it from capturing new business in the AI server segment. But what happens when capacity constraints ease across the industry? Will the current pricing discipline hold, or will a wave of new capacity trigger a price war in commoditized products?

The second unresolved question is about the competitive response from Korean and Chinese manufacturers. The report notes that Samsung Electro-Mechanics (SEMCO) holds an estimated 22.5% market share in AI server high value-added MLCCs, second only to Murata. As AI server demand grows, SEMCO and other players will have strong incentives to invest in the technical capabilities needed to compete at the high end. The report suggests that Murata's technological lead is widening, but competitive dynamics in electronics manufacturing can shift more quickly than in other industries.

The third question is about the end-demand trajectory for AI servers themselves. The report's analysis assumes continued growth in GPU generations and corresponding increases in MLCC content per board. But what if the pace of GPU innovation slows? What if AI workloads shift toward inference rather than training, changing the component mix? These are not idle questions. They are the kind of second-order uncertainties that separate good analysis from great investment decisions.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the MLCC and ABF Substrate Markets

The report provides the raw material for a structured investment framework, but it does not spell it out explicitly. Based on the analysis, investors should consider three dimensions when evaluating electronic component companies.

The first dimension is product mix trajectory. Companies should be evaluated not on their total revenue growth but on the share of revenue coming from products where technical barriers to entry are high and pricing power is structural. Murata's AI and data center MLCC sales are expected to grow from 10-15% of total MLCC sales in F3/26 to 20-25% in F3/27. This mix shift is the primary driver of earnings growth. Companies that cannot demonstrate a similar trajectory are more exposed to commodity cycles.

The second dimension is pricing philosophy. The report makes a compelling case that companies which avoid indiscriminate price hikes in commoditized products are making a strategically sound choice, even if it depresses short-term profits. Investors should distinguish between companies that are investing their current profits into R&D and capacity for future growth and those that are extracting profits without reinvesting. The former group will compound value over time. The latter group will face a reckoning when the cycle turns.

The third dimension is technology roadmap credibility. In both MLCCs and ABF substrates, the key question is whether a company's stated technology roadmap is achievable within the expected timeframe. Murata's ability to mass-produce the most demanding MLCCs today gives it credibility for its next-generation roadmap. Ibiden's EMIB-T trajectory is less certain, and the report explicitly questions whether the market's margin expectations are realistic. Investors should apply a credibility discount to companies whose technology milestones are further in the future or whose competitive positioning is less clear.

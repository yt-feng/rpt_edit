# The Real MLCC Growth Story Is Not Price Hikes—It Is Product Mix Migration, And Most Investors Are Looking in the Wrong Direction

The most important finding from 31 investor meetings in Singapore and Hong Kong is not about pricing power in commoditized electronic components. It is that the market is systematically underestimating how structural demand shifts in AI infrastructure are reshaping the value chain for multilayer ceramic capacitors, and that the gap between companies that compete on technology and those that compete on price will define the next cycle of corporate value creation.

Investor attention during these meetings was overwhelmingly focused on one question: how much can companies raise prices for commoditized MLCCs? That is the wrong question. The right question is how quickly can a company shift its revenue mix toward high-value-added products that command inherently higher unit prices without requiring a price hike at all. The distinction is not semantic. It is the difference between a cyclical earnings pop and a structural earnings upgrade.

The report covering these meetings makes a clear argument that deserves more attention than it has received. Murata Manufacturing, the largest MLCC producer globally, is not relying on price increases to drive its growth. The company expects ASP growth of roughly 50 percent in its AI and data center MLCC sales in fiscal year ending March 2027, but that growth comes entirely from a richer product mix—selling more of the tiny, high-capacitance capacitors that AI server accelerator boards require—not from raising prices on existing products. Volume growth of approximately 40 percent combines with that mix-driven ASP increase to produce an 85 to 90 percent year-over-year revenue jump from AI and data center applications alone.

This is not a cyclical story. It is a structural one. Each new generation of GPUs requires roughly twice the total capacitance in MLCCs on the accelerator board, while the physical space available for those capacitors does not expand. The only solution is to use smaller, higher-capacitance components that are significantly more difficult to manufacture. Murata has invested for years in refining barium titanate particle size, increasing layer counts, and narrowing external electrodes to squeeze more dielectric volume into smaller packages. Those investments are now paying off in a way that price-focused competitors cannot replicate quickly.

## The Market Is Fixated on Commodity Pricing, but the Real Value Creation Is in Product Mix Migration Away from Commodities Entirely

The report reveals a striking disconnect. Investors spent most of their meeting time asking about price hikes for commoditized MLCCs—the standard capacitors used in smartphones, PCs, and consumer electronics. Yet the report's most compelling data point is that Murata's AI and data center MLCC sales will grow from 10 to 15 percent of total MLCC sales in fiscal 2025 to 20 to 25 percent in fiscal 2026, and that the pace of growth is expected to continue at similar rates from fiscal 2027 onward.

This is not a temporary shift. It represents a permanent reallocation of production capacity and R&D resources toward the highest-value segments of the market. The companies that can execute this migration will see their average selling prices rise naturally, without ever having to announce a price increase. The companies that cannot will remain trapped in the commodity segment, where pricing is determined by supply-demand balances in China, South Korea, and Taiwan, and where margins are structurally lower.

The report makes a pointed observation that deserves emphasis: Murata does not proactively raise prices for commoditized products, even though distributor prices to customers are rising. The reasoning is strategic. Short-term price maximization in commodities lowers barriers to entry, encourages capacity expansion by Chinese, Korean, and Taiwanese competitors, and ultimately erodes market share over the medium to long term. Murata is effectively choosing to sacrifice short-term commodity profits to protect its long-term competitive position. This is exactly the kind of strategic discipline that the market systematically undervalues.

## The Competitive Moat in High-Value-Added MLCCs Is Wider Than the Market Assumes

The report provides a market share estimate for AI server high-value-added MLCCs that should give pause to anyone betting on rapid catch-up by second-tier players. Murata holds approximately 41 percent of the total MLCC market, but its share of the high-value-added segment is likely much higher. The report notes that demand is increasing significantly for specific products—1608 size 100 microfarad, 1005 size 47 microfarad, and 0603 size 10 microfarad capacitors—and that only Murata can stably mass-produce all of these today.

Taiyo Yuden, the third-largest player with roughly 11 percent market share, is working to develop comparable products. But the report raises a critical question that it does not fully answer: by the time Taiyo Yuden achieves stable mass production of the current generation of high-value-added MLCCs, will Murata already be mass-producing even smaller, higher-capacitance devices? This is the classic innovator's dilemma applied to passive components. The leader is not standing still. It is investing in the next generation of miniaturization and capacitance density while competitors are still trying to replicate the current generation.

The report also notes that Taiyo Yuden froze capital expenditure in the second half of 2024, limiting its MLCC production capacity growth to around 5 percent in fiscal 2025. That constraint not only limits its ability to capture new AI-related business today but also signals a strategic posture that is more defensive than offensive. When a company cannot invest in capacity to serve the fastest-growing end market, it is effectively ceding the future to its better-capitalized competitor.

## The Underweight Rating on Ibiden Reflects a Gap Between Aspiration and Execution That the Market Has Not Yet Priced

The report's treatment of Ibiden, the ABF package substrate manufacturer, is notably more cautious than the market consensus. The report expects Ibiden to continue expanding earnings, but it explicitly states that market expectations are too high. The numbers tell the story clearly. The report forecasts operating profit of 94.7 billion yen for fiscal 2026, below the FactSet consensus of 99.7 billion yen. For fiscal 2027, the gap widens: the report forecasts 128.5 billion yen against a consensus of 151.5 billion yen. And for fiscal 2030, the report forecasts 242.6 billion yen against the company's own target of 300 billion yen.

The source of the gap is instructive. The report acknowledges that Ibiden has begun volume shipments of ABF package substrates for NVIDIA's Rubin platform and that sales for Rubin will exceed those for Blackwell in the first quarter of fiscal 2027. It also acknowledges that ABF package substrates are becoming more value-added, driven in part by increases in substrate size. But the report questions whether the EMIB-T product, which is expected to contribute meaningfully from fiscal 2028 onward, can achieve profit margins comparable to NVIDIA's existing offerings.

This is a second-order question that the report does not fully resolve. EMIB-T represents a technological step forward, but the profit margin trajectory depends on pricing power, yield improvements, and the competitive response from other substrate manufacturers. The report's caution suggests that the market is extrapolating the current demand surge too far into the future without adequately discounting the risks of capacity additions, technological parity, and customer concentration.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: Three Open Questions That Should Define the Next Phase of Analysis

The report is unusually candid about what it does not know, and those gaps are as valuable as the conclusions it does draw. Three questions stand out as unresolved and worth exploring further.

First, the report does not specify when Taiyo Yuden will achieve stable mass production of the full suite of high-value-added MLCCs that AI servers require. The timing matters enormously. If Taiyo Yuden can close the gap within 12 to 18 months, the competitive dynamics could shift. If the gap persists for three to five years, Murata's competitive moat becomes nearly insurmountable. The report implies the latter but does not provide the detailed roadmap that would allow investors to make their own judgment.

Second, the report does not fully address the supply-demand balance for commoditized MLCCs. It notes that distributor prices are rising, but it does not quantify how much of that increase is sustainable versus how much is driven by temporary inventory restocking or capacity constraints. The 2018 versus 2026 comparison, which was a major topic in investor meetings, suggests that the market is trying to determine whether the current cycle is different from the last one. The report provides the framework for that analysis but stops short of a definitive answer.

Third, the report does not resolve the profit margin question for Ibiden's EMIB-T products. It expects pricing to significantly exceed existing ABF package substrates, but it questions whether margins can match NVIDIA's existing offerings. The difference between "pricing is higher" and "margins are comparable" could be driven by yield curves, manufacturing complexity, or customer concentration. The report leaves this as an open analytical challenge for readers to investigate further.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask Before Making a Bet on Japanese Electronic Components

The report's analytical structure lends itself to a straightforward decision framework that investors can apply across the coverage universe.

First, ask whether the company's revenue growth is driven by product mix improvement or by price increases on existing products. Companies that grow through mix improvement are building structural competitive advantages. Companies that grow through price increases on commodities are generating cyclical earnings that will reverse when the cycle turns. The report makes this distinction explicit for Murata and Taiyo Yuden, but the same logic applies across the sector.

Second, ask whether the company's capital expenditure strategy is aligned with the fastest-growing end markets. The report highlights that Taiyo Yuden's capex freeze limited its capacity growth to 5 percent, while Murata is investing to capture 85 to 90 percent AI revenue growth. The alignment between investment and opportunity is a leading indicator of future market share. Companies that underinvest in the fastest-growing segments are making a strategic choice that will show up in the numbers two to three years later.

Third, ask whether the market's earnings expectations are based on reasonable assumptions about technology roadmaps and competitive responses. The report's underweight rating on Ibiden is based on the gap between the company's targets and the analyst's more conservative forecasts. That gap is not about pessimism. It is about recognizing that technology transitions are rarely as smooth as company guidance suggests, and that competitive dynamics in high-value-added segments can shift faster than the market expects.

## The Unresolved Analytical Tension: Why the Full Report Matters

The most valuable insight from this report is not any single data point or recommendation. It is the analytical tension between two competing narratives about the Japanese electronic components sector. One narrative says that the AI-driven demand surge is lifting all boats, that pricing power is returning to the industry, and that the cycle is different this time. The other narrative says that structural advantages are accruing to a small number of companies with genuine technological moats, and that the rest of the industry is experiencing a cyclical tailwind that will fade.

The report clearly leans toward the second narrative, but it does not force the conclusion.

EN_A: So this global investment bank report came out after they held over 30 investor meetings in Singapore and Hong Kong. It covers Japanese electronic component makers. What’s the one takeaway you think people should focus on?
EN_B: I think the biggest insight is that the market is way too focused on price hikes for MLCCs. But the real driver of long-term value is product mix upgrading, not just raising prices. The report makes a clear distinction between Murata and Taiyo Yuden, and they see very different paths ahead.
EN_A: So you’re saying it’s not about whether they raise prices, but what they sell?
EN_B: Exactly. For Murata, the report says about 50% of its MLCC sales growth comes from higher average selling prices. But that’s not because they raised prices on existing products. It’s because they’re selling more high-value products, especially for AI servers. Each new GPU generation roughly doubles the total capacitance needed, but the space on the board doesn’t grow. So you need smaller, higher-capacity MLCCs. Murata is ahead in that technology.
EN_A: What about Taiyo Yuden? The market seems pretty excited about their AI exposure too.
EN_B: The report is more cautious. It says only Murata can reliably mass-produce all the key MLCC specs needed for AI servers. By the time Taiyo Yuden catches up, Murata will probably be making even smaller and more advanced products. Also, Taiyo Yuden froze capital spending in the second half of 2024, so its capacity growth is very limited. Even if demand is there, they might not be able to capture it.
EN_A: That leads to a bigger question. Why do some companies keep growing while others just grab short-term gains?
EN_B: This is actually the most interesting argument in the report. It says companies that keep raising prices on commoditized products might boost short-term profits, but they also lower the barrier to entry. That makes it easier for Chinese, Korean, and Taiwanese competitors to come in. In contrast, companies like Murata stay flexible on pricing for basic products, but keep investing heavily in R&D and process improvement. That’s how they protect their market share over the long run.
EN_A: So it’s not a simple story of “who raises prices more wins”?
EN_B: Not at all. The report even says this gap in corporate value will become more obvious over time. But interestingly, investors at those meetings almost only asked about price hikes. Almost nobody asked about the growth from high-value products. That might be a real gap in perception.
EN_A: The report also covers Ibiden, the ABF substrate maker tied to Nvidia. What’s the take there?
EN_B: The rating is underweight. The report expects Ibiden’s earnings to keep growing, but it thinks market expectations are too high. They’ve started shipping ABF substrates for Nvidia’s Rubin chip, but the report says the new EMIB-T technology won’t make a significant profit contribution until fiscal 2029. And even then, it’s hard to see it matching the margins of Nvidia’s existing products. So the big question is: how much is the market overestimating?

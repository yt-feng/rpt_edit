# AI's Insatiable Appetite for Memory Is Reshaping the Software Industry's Structural Economics

The most important software story this quarter is not about a new application or a cloud migration trend. It is about memory. When Micron reported fiscal third-quarter revenue up nearly 350% year-over-year to $41 billion, the market focused on data center buildout. That is the proximate cause, but it is not the strategic insight. The deeper shift is that artificial intelligence is transitioning from a prompt-and-response model to an agentic architecture, and that transition demands exponentially more memory per workload. This is not a cyclical semiconductor story. It is a structural change in how software is built, deployed, and monetized, and it has direct implications for every investor in European software.

The evidence is mounting from multiple directions simultaneously. SAP's CEO stated publicly that within three to four years, there may be no humans developing software inside the company. Academic publishers like Springer Nature are deploying AI tools to compress 200-day publication cycles. Informa is positioning its live events business as a counterweight to digital saturation. These are not isolated data points. They are signals of a reordering of the software industry's cost structure, talent requirements, and competitive dynamics.

The argument here is straightforward but non-consensus. The market is treating AI memory demand as a hardware story. The more consequential read is that this demand reflects a fundamental shift in software architecture that will reshape enterprise software spending, alter the economics of software development, and create new winners and losers among European software companies. Investors who frame this as a memory-chip cycle risk missing the structural transformation underway.

## Agentic AI Workloads Are Transforming Memory Demand from Cyclical to Structural, and Software Companies Must Adapt Their Product Economics

The conventional wisdom holds that memory is a commodity business driven by supply-demand imbalances and capital expenditure cycles. Micron's 16 signed long-term strategic customer agreements suggest something different is happening. These are not spot-market purchases. They are commitments that reflect a view that memory demand will remain elevated for years, not quarters.

What changed? The nature of AI workloads. Early generative AI applications were stateless. A user typed a prompt, received a response, and the interaction ended. Agentic AI is fundamentally different. These systems plan, call tools, read documents, retain session history, spawn sub-agents, and iterate toward an outcome. Each of these steps requires context to be stored and retrieved. The memory footprint per user interaction is orders of magnitude larger than a simple chat completion.

The software implication is direct. Companies building enterprise AI applications must now account for memory costs as a meaningful line item in their unit economics. This is not theoretical. It is already reshaping how software products are priced and packaged. The question for investors is which companies have pricing power to pass through these costs, and which will see margins compressed.

## SAP's Vision of a Developer-Free Future Signals a Radical Reshaping of Software Cost Structures and Talent Models

When the CEO of Europe's largest software company says there may be no human developers in three to four years, it demands attention. Christian Klein's statement to the Australian Financial Review was not hyperbole. It was a strategic signal about how SAP is rethinking its cost structure and talent pipeline.

The logic is compelling. AI-powered coding tools and agents are already demonstrating the ability to write, test, and deploy code at a fraction of the time and cost of human developers. SAP's hiring philosophy, articulated at Sapphire 2026, confirms the direction. The company is pursuing a three-to-one replacement ratio, where one highly skilled AI expert replaces three traditional developers. Compensation packages are being redesigned to attract scarce AI talent rather than traditional software engineers.

This has profound implications for the European software sector. Labor costs have historically been the largest expense for enterprise software companies. If AI can compress development time by 50% or more while reducing headcount, margins should expand significantly. But there is a catch. The transition period will be messy. Companies must invest heavily in AI infrastructure and talent while their legacy cost structures remain. The winners will be those that manage this transition without disrupting existing customer relationships or product quality.

## Live Events and Academic Publishing Demonstrate That AI Creates Both Disruption and Defensive Value in Unexpected Places

The conventional narrative positions AI as a threat to human-centric businesses. Informa's B2B live events business challenges that assumption. In an AI-saturated world where digital outreach risks losing authenticity, face-to-face human connection becomes more valuable, not less. Informa's scale and leadership in live events provide a differentiated platform that AI cannot replicate.

The numbers support this view. A recent sum-of-the-parts analysis suggests Informa's fair value is more than 20% above current levels, with live events commanding a high-teens EV/EBIT multiple. The company is a durable 6% underlying revenue grower with 12% EPS growth including buybacks and a dividend yield above 2.5%. This is not a growth story. It is a quality and value story that the market may be mispricing because it is distracted by AI narratives.

Academic publishing tells a similar story from a different angle. Springer Nature's technology event highlighted that AI is not destroying value in this sector but enhancing it. The average publication process takes 200 days, and Nature-branded journals take 300 days. AI tools are being deployed to accelerate manuscript assessment, reviewer identification, and manuscript routing. Approximately 75% of researchers already use AI tools, and one-third are willing to pay for them.

The strategic insight is that AI is creating process efficiencies in industries where bottlenecks have constrained growth for decades. The academic publishers already earn high margins. If AI can compress timelines and improve researcher experience, those margins should expand further. The open question is whether publishers can monetize specific workflow tools effectively, but the direction of travel is clear.

## The Report Raises Critical Questions It Does Not Fully Answer About the Sustainability of Memory Demand and the Pace of Software Transformation

Any honest analysis must acknowledge what remains uncertain. The report presents compelling evidence that memory demand is becoming structural, but it does not fully address the counterargument. What happens if agentic AI adoption slows? What if a new architecture emerges that is more memory-efficient? The long-term customer agreements are suggestive, but they are not guarantees.

Similarly, the transformation of software development described by SAP's CEO is plausible but the timeline is aggressive. Three to four years is a short period to re-skill an entire workforce and redesign compensation structures. The practical challenges of maintaining legacy systems, managing customer migrations, and ensuring security during a rapid transition are non-trivial.

The academic publishing story raises another unresolved question. If AI tools improve efficiency, who captures the economic value? Publishers argue they will monetize workflow tools. But researchers are price-sensitive, and institutions face budget constraints. The history of enterprise software suggests that efficiency gains tend to flow to customers over time, not to vendors. The margin expansion thesis depends on publishers maintaining pricing power, which is not guaranteed.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of what can be known at this stage. The most valuable research identifies where the evidence points while acknowledging the limits of prediction.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Which Software Companies Win in the Agentic AI Era

The strategic implication for investors is not to bet on or against AI. It is to identify which companies have the structural advantages to benefit from the transition. A simple framework can help.

First, does the company have pricing power over memory and compute costs? Companies that can pass through infrastructure costs to customers will protect margins. Those that compete on price or have commoditized products will see compression.

Second, does the company have a defensible data or human connection advantage? Informa's live events and Springer Nature's peer review network are examples of assets that AI cannot easily replicate. Companies with proprietary data, regulatory moats, or high-touch customer relationships are better positioned.

Third, can the company absorb the talent transition without disrupting operations? The shift from traditional developers to AI experts is not frictionless. Companies with strong balance sheets, diversified talent pools, and patient management teams will navigate the transition more smoothly.

Apply these questions to the European software coverage universe. Some companies will emerge stronger. Others will face existential challenges. The next twelve to eighteen months will separate the two groups.

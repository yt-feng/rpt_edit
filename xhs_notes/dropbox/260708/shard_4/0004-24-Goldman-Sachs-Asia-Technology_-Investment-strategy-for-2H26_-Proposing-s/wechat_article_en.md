## The Supply-Demand Tightness Is Broadening, Not Peaking, and This Creates New Entry Points

The most common mistake in technology cycle research is mistaking a pause for a peak. The profit-taking that hit AI-related hardware stocks entering July 2026 is a healthy correction after a rapid surge, not a signal that the cycle has turned. The two fundamental drivers that underpin this cycle are intact: semiconductor and component supply has not caught up with demand, and the industry has not shifted from a focus on high value-added technology toward cost reduction. As long as these two conditions hold, the structural case remains valid.

What has changed is the scope of the opportunity. In the first half, the tightness was concentrated in core segments like memory and high-bandwidth components. Now, that tightness is extending into areas that were previously less affected. Niche electronic components such as MLCCs have seen valuations rise as supply constraints emerge. Electronic materials have followed a similar trajectory. Looking ahead to the second half, the evidence points toward further spillover into silicon wafers and analog and power semiconductors, particularly outside of China.

This broadening matters for two reasons. First, it means the AI research theme is no longer limited to a narrow set of familiar names. Observers who missed the initial rally or who are looking for fresh exposure have new sub-segments to evaluate. Companies like Shin-Etsu Chemical and SUMCO in silicon wafers, and Renesas Electronics and Rohm in analog semiconductors, are positioned to benefit from this next wave of tightening.

Second, the broadening of tightness changes the risk profile of the overall opportunity. When supply-demand imbalances are concentrated, the market can quickly price in the expected benefits. When they spread across multiple sub-segments, there is a greater chance that individual companies will be mispriced relative to their true exposure. This creates the conditions for selective alpha generation, but only for those who apply the right analytical framework.

## Stock Selection in a Risk-On Market Requires Four Specific Criteria, Not Just Sector Exposure

When an entire sector is rising, any stock with the right label performs. When the rally matures, the dispersion between winners and losers widens dramatically. The second half of 2026 is precisely such a phase. Owning AI hardware exposure is no longer sufficient. Observers must distinguish between companies that will truly benefit from the extended cycle and those whose valuations have run ahead of their fundamentals.

The analytical framework for this distinction rests on four criteria. First, the company must demonstrate significant benefits from higher prices or average selling prices. In a supply-constrained environment, pricing power is the clearest signal of value capture. Companies that can pass through cost increases or command premiums for scarce components will see margin expansion, not just revenue growth.

Second, the company must have the ability to unlock capacity constraints through timely investments. This is where the market often misprices risk. Observers tend to focus on current tightness without adequately evaluating which companies are best positioned to add capacity. The winners will be those with the balance sheet strength, operational expertise, and supply chain relationships to bring new capacity online faster than competitors.

Third, valuations must lag despite future profit contributions from AI. This is the most counterintuitive criterion. The best opportunities often lie in companies where the market has not yet fully discounted the AI-related earnings stream. These may be names that are associated with legacy businesses or that have not been classified as pure AI plays. The valuation discount creates a margin of safety that pure-play AI names no longer offer.

Fourth, the company should have idiosyncratic drivers not priced into consensus. Company-specific catalysts, whether product launches, strategic partnerships, or operational improvements, provide an additional layer of return that is independent of the macro environment or the AI cycle's trajectory.

This framework is not theoretical. It was applied on July 6 when one major research team upgraded Nittobo to a positive view, citing prolonged supply-demand tightness in T-Glass and its positive impact on pricing. The same framework is being used to evaluate other names like Panasonic, Sumitomo Electric Industries, Lasertec, and Ebara, each of which presents a different risk-return profile based on its specific exposure to these four criteria.

## Defensive Plays in a Risk-Off Market Should Come from AI Disruption, Not Away from It

The conventional approach to defensive positioning in technology is to rotate into low-beta, stable-growth names that are insulated from the cycle. The second half of 2026 calls for a different approach. The most effective defensive plays may be stocks that are beginning to capture new business opportunities precisely because of AI disruption.

This logic is counterintuitive but powerful. When the market turns risk-off, the initial reaction is often to reduce exposure to the highest-beta names, which are typically the AI hardware plays. But the rotation does not have to go into completely unrelated sectors. It can go into companies where AI is creating new demand for services that are less capital-intensive and more recurring in nature.

The threat of AI is itself generating demand for software and systems integration. Companies that help other businesses navigate AI disruption, implement AI solutions, or protect against AI-related cybersecurity risks are seeing new revenue streams emerge. These are not speculative future opportunities. They are current business lines that are gaining traction as enterprises across industries confront the implications of generative AI.

In Japan, names like NOM Institute, Baycurrent, NEC, Fujitsu, and Recruit Holdings exemplify this dynamic. These companies have established businesses in IT services, systems integration, and platform solutions. The AI wave is adding a new growth vector to their existing operations, one that is less tied to the capital expenditure cycles of hyperscale data centers and more driven by the steady demand for digital transformation and security.

The strategic implication is clear. In a risk-off scenario, these stocks may be oversold simply because they are grouped with technology, even though their business models are fundamentally different from the hardware names that drive the initial risk-off moves. For those who have built positions in AI hardware, these names offer a natural hedge that maintains exposure to the AI theme while reducing portfolio volatility.

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several meaningful questions unresolved. The answer depends on factors that are inherently uncertain. Capacity addition timelines in semiconductor manufacturing are notoriously difficult to predict. Similarly, the pace of demand growth from edge AI and physical AI devices, which the report identifies as the next potential driver, remains an open question. If these applications ramp faster than expected, the spillover effects could be more dramatic and arrive sooner. The report explicitly excludes China from its analysis of these sub-segments. This is a deliberate choice, but it leaves a gap in the overall picture. For those who are more confident in the risk-on environment, the allocation can be smaller but should not be zero.

The final element of the framework is monitoring. The two key signals to watch are signs that semiconductor or component supply is catching up with demand, and signs that the industry is shifting from high value-added technology toward cost reduction. As long as both signals remain absent, the cycle remains intact. If either signal appears, it is time to reassess the entire thesis.

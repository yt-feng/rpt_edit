## Global LEO satellite installed base: 24k/305k units by 2028E/31E

Following the acceleration in LEO satellite launches in 2025, we expect to see continuous growth of satellite installed base from 10k units in 2025 to 13k/17k units by 2026/27E (vs. our last estimate of 12k/15k units by 2026E/27E). Global satellite operators have updated their satellite plans for LEO orbit, and increased target volumes. Among operators' filings, Amazon LEO received approval from the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) to launch 4,500 additional LEO satellites (link) in February 2026. Chinese satellite operators submitted applications to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) to seek approval for 200k+ medium- and low-Earth orbit satellites (link).

## Blue-sky scenario

We present a blue-sky scenario for the global LEO satellite installed base, which sees the installed base reaching 27k/396k units by 2028E/31E (vs. our base case of 24k/305k units by 2028E/31E), considering the potential for better-than-expected launch capacity of rockets, faster-than-expected monetization of satellite communications, the long-term goals of LEO satellite operators, and the potential upside from space data centers.

In our blue sky scenario, the major upside comes from China LEO satellites, which we do not include in our base case. While technological feasibility remains to be verified, we see strong potential for space data centers given their unlimited access to low-cost solar power supply and their edge-computing capacity, which can handle satellite data directly. Our blue sky scenario is based on the assumption that the 200K LEO constellation plan submitted by Chinese vendors will be achieved in the long-term to reflect the potential for faster commercialization.

## China's progress

Accelerating installed base: We expect LEO satellites operated by Chinese vendors to achieve an installed base of 1.9k by 2028E and 24k by 2031E in our base case. In our blue sky scenario, the installed base reaches 101k by 2031E. By end-2025, Chinese vendors were operating 253 units of satellites, marking a deployment ramp-up.

Leading vendors have already laid out deployment plans: Key Chinese LEO constellations include GW (Guowang), G60 (or SpaceSail) and Honghu, all of which are large-scale communication constellations that will launch over 10,000 satellites. The GW constellation submitted plans for 12,992 satellites to the ITU in 2020 (per Xinhuanet, link), of which 10% or 1,299 units will need to be launched by 2029. G60 has planned for 15,000 units of satellites, with the goal of reaching 1,296 units by end-2027 and 15,000 units by 2030 (per Xinhuanet, link). Honghu also submitted a plan for 10,000 units of LEO satellites to the ITU in 2024 (per Sina, link), implying that 1,000 satellites need to be in operation by end-2033. Apart from these plans, Chinese satellite operators collectively submitted applications for 200k satellites to the ITU by end-2025 (link), which we view as a strategic move to secure spectrum and orbit resources.

Rockets in fast development: Chinese LEO operators now mainly use LM-6A and LM-12 rockets by China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation (CASC), which are non-reusable. However, development of reusable rockets is accelerating, with more scheduled test flights in 2026. In February 2026, the LM-10 by CASC successfully achieved a first-stage soft-landing splashdown in the sea (link). The Zhuque-3 rocket by LandSpace achieved partial success in a test flight in December 2025, and another recovery test flight will be conducted in 2Q26 (per CCTV, link). The Hyperbola-3 reusable rocket by i-Space will conduct its first launch by end-2026 (per hket, link). The PALLAS-1 reusable rocket by Galactic Energy will also conduct its first launch in 2026 (per futurephecd.com, link).

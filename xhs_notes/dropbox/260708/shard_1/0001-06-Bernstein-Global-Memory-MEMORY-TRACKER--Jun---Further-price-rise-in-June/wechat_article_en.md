NAND is following a similar trajectory, with blended contract prices expected to rise approximately 60% quarter-over-quarter in 2QCY26, driven by strength in mobile NAND and enterprise SSDs.

These numbers are eye-catching. They suggest a market still gripped by shortage, where suppliers hold the advantage and customers scramble for allocation. Yet beneath the headline figures, a more nuanced story is unfolding. The pace of price increases is already moderating as we move into the third quarter. Spot prices for DRAM have stabilized and even risen further, but NAND wafer spot prices have resumed their decline. Long-term agreements are being signed with major US cloud service providers, locking in pricing ceilings that may cap future upside. And demand changes in consumer segments, though more limited than feared so far, are beginning to materialize.

The central question for observers is no longer whether memory prices will rise. It is when they will peak, how long the plateau will last, and what the subsequent adjustment will look like. The data available today offers powerful clues, but also leaves critical uncertainties unresolved. This is a moment that demands strategic thinking, not just tactical extrapolation.

## The June Data Confirms a Powerful 2QCY26 Upswing, But the Deceleration Into 3QCY26 Is Already Visible

The June contract price data, compiled from TrendForce and DRAMeXchange sources, paints a picture of a market that is still very much in the supplier's favor. The weighted average of DRAM contracts signed in June indicates that conventional DRAM prices will rise 74% quarter-over-quarter in 2QCY26, a figure that actually came in approximately 10 percentage points higher than what May contracts had suggested. This is meaningfully above most sell-side models and underscores just how severe the supply-demand imbalance has become.

The composition of this increase matters. Server DRAM, the segment most directly tied to AI infrastructure buildout, is seeing a 67% sequential increase. Mobile DRAM is closer to 80%, driven by LPDDR5 tightness. Specialty DRAM, serving applications from networking to automotive, is surging by over 100%. PC DRAM, at 49%, is the relative laggard but still represents an extraordinary quarterly move by historical standards.

NAND tells a similar story, though with important structural differences. While NAND wafer contract prices rose only modestly in June, the overall blended NAND price is still expected to increase roughly 60% in 2QCY26. This is because mobile NAND and enterprise SSDs, which account for the vast majority of NAND bit consumption, are seeing price increases of 70-80%. The wafer market, where spot prices actually declined 3-4% in June, is a small and increasingly unrepresentative segment.

The critical inflection point, however, is the trajectory into 3QCY26. TrendForce has already revised its PC DRAM price expectations upward to 15-20% quarter-over-quarter, but this still represents a dramatic deceleration from the 45-50% increase seen in 2QCY26. Server DRAM is expected to rise 13-18% quarter-over-quarter, mobile NAND just 5-10%. The pattern is consistent across every segment: prices are still rising, but the slope is flattening.

## The Market Is Splitting Into Two Distinct Regimes: AI-Driven Server Demand Versus Consumer Pull-Forward

One of the most important analytical insights from the current data is that the memory market is no longer a single, homogeneous entity. It is fracturing into two regimes with fundamentally different demand drivers and pricing dynamics.

On one side sits the server and AI infrastructure segment. Here, demand remains strong. US cloud service providers continue to absorb incremental supply as quickly as suppliers can allocate it. Server DRAM spot prices rose 6-26% month-over-month in June, with DDR5 modules showing particular strength. Spot prices remain considerably above contract prices, a clear signal that the secondary market still sees acute shortage. The LTA negotiations with US CSPs have largely concluded, with pricing ceilings that vary by supplier. For Micron, which prefers longer contract durations, the ceiling appears to be near its 2QCY26 contract level. For Samsung and SK hynix, the ceilings may be higher, implying continued but more limited upside.

On the other side sits the consumer segment, encompassing PCs, smartphones, and consumer electronics. Here, the picture is more nuanced. Smartphone unit volumes are now expected to decline 16% this year, with 2QCY26 alone dropping more than 10% year-over-year. Smartphone OEMs are already adjusting production plans and reducing memory content per device. The PC market has held up better than expected, supported by strong demand for new MacBook models and Microsoft promotions, but this looks increasingly like pulled-forward demand rather than genuine organic growth.

The risk is that consumer demand changes, which have been more limited than feared so far, accelerate in 2HCY26 as the cumulative impact of price increases passes through to end-user pricing. Smartphone OEMs, in particular, are turning cautious. They have already absorbed two quarters of unprecedented memory cost increases and are now facing the reality that they cannot pass all of these costs through to consumers without affecting volume. The pull-forward effect may have masked underlying conditions, but it cannot sustain itself indefinitely.

## Long-Term Agreements Are Reshaping the Cycle, But Their Net Effect Remains Ambiguous

The proliferation of long-term agreements in the memory industry represents one of the most significant structural shifts in recent years, and the current cycle is the first real test of how they will function under changing conditions.

LTAs serve multiple purposes. For suppliers, they provide revenue visibility and reduce the risk of a sharp adjustment when demand softens. For customers, they guarantee supply allocation in a tight market and provide pricing predictability. In theory, this should make the memory cycle less volatile, reducing both the amplitude of upswings and the depth of downswings.

The data from the current cycle offers early evidence that this theory may hold, but it also raises important questions. The LTA price ceilings negotiated with US CSPs appear to cap further upside for a significant portion of server DRAM volume. If Micron's ceiling is already at its 2QCY26 contract level, then any further price increases would have to come from volume not covered by LTAs, which is likely to be a shrinking share of total shipments.

For Samsung and SK hynix, whose ceilings may be higher, the LTA structure still imposes a constraint. Once the ceiling is reached, further ASP increases become dependent on mix shifts toward higher-value products or on non-LTA transactions. This creates a natural deceleration mechanism that may cause prices to plateau rather than continue rising linearly.

The ambiguity lies in the consumer segment, where LTA adoption is less advanced. Chinese CSPs are still in discussions, and consumer customers have been slower to sign agreements. This means that the segment most vulnerable to demand changes also has the least contractual protection. If consumer demand weakens significantly, the absence of LTAs could actually amplify the adjustment, as spot prices fall more freely and contract prices follow with a lag.

## The Report Leaves Critical Questions Unanswered About the Timing and Shape of the Peak

For all the richness of the June data, several key analytical questions remain unresolved. Those who rely solely on the current price trajectory risk being caught off guard by the transition that is already beginning.

The first question is the exact timing of the peak. The report models memory prices to gradually peak and begin normalizing from 2HCY27 into CY28. This is a relatively moderate view, implying that prices will remain elevated for another 12-18 months before any meaningful adjustment. But the deceleration into 3QCY26 is sharper than this timeline might suggest. If the sequential price increases continue to narrow at their current rate, the absolute peak in pricing could arrive earlier than the model implies, perhaps as soon as late 2026 or early 2027.

The second question is the shape of the adjustment. Will prices decline gradually as capacity comes online and LTAs provide a floor, or could there be a sharper drop if demand changes accelerate? The report acknowledges that demand changes in consumer segments will eventually happen, but the magnitude is uncertain. If smartphone and PC volumes fall more than expected, the oversupply could be significant even with disciplined capacity additions.

The third question is the interaction between conventional memory and HBM. HBM pricing is largely set by annual contracts and is steadier than conventional DRAM. But HBM also consumes significant wafer capacity that could otherwise be used for conventional products. The delayed ramp of HBM4 has actually freed up some capacity for server DRAM, but this is a temporary dynamic. As HBM4 ramps, the capacity allocation could shift again, potentially tightening conventional supply even as demand softens.

These are not academic questions. They have direct implications for the earnings trajectories of memory manufacturers and for the valuation multiples that observers should consider.

## A Framework for Understanding the Memory Cycle

Given the complexity of the current environment, a structured approach to evaluating memory companies can be helpful. The following framework can help separate signal from noise.

First, assess exposure to server and AI demand versus consumer markets. Companies with higher exposure to server DRAM and enterprise SSDs are better positioned to navigate a consumer slowdown. The LTA structures with US CSPs provide a degree of revenue visibility that consumer-facing segments lack. Conversely, companies with significant exposure to mobile and PC memory face different dynamics if demand changes accelerate.

Second, evaluate LTA coverage and pricing ceilings. Suppliers that have locked in favorable LTA terms with major customers will see more stable revenue streams, but their upside may be capped. Suppliers with less LTA coverage have more potential upside if prices continue to rise, but also more downside risk in an adjustment. The key metric is the proportion of revenue covered by LTAs and the level at which price ceilings are set.

Third, monitor the trajectory of consumer demand closely. The pull-forward effect is real, and its reversal could happen quickly. Leading indicators include smartphone OEM production plans, PC sell-through data, and memory content per device. If these indicators change, the market could transition from shortage to surplus faster than current models anticipate.

Fourth, consider the capacity outlook. New wafer capacity takes 18-24 months to come online, and the industry has been disciplined in its expansion plans. But if demand softens while capacity additions continue, the supply-demand balance could shift dramatically. The key question is whether suppliers will maintain discipline or revert to volume-driven behavior as the cycle matures.

Finally, maintain a time horizon that extends beyond the current quarter. The memory cycle is long and powerful, but it is also self-correcting. High prices incentivize supply additions and demand changes. The current environment is notable for near-term earnings, but the sustainability of those earnings into 2027 and 2028 is the critical variable for long-term considerations.

# Broadlines & Hardlines Retailing

Agentic Commerce - More Opportunity than Risk

Following up on our recently published Episode 14: Retail vs. AMZN in an Agentic World report we published with JPM Internet Analysts Doug Anmuth & Bryan Smilek (see the slides and webinar replay), this note takes a deeper look at the opportunities and risks ahead in the world of AI and agentic commerce. Team Internet believes that AI will ultimately serve as an incremental discovery channel and help expand the e-commerce TAM. From our perspective, we also see agentic as more of an opportunity at this point, albeit with puts/takes by category. Simplistically, LLMs represent a more potent version of “googling” and follow “threats” posed by other customer acquisition venues (e.g., TikTok, Pinterest, and Shopify). Despite high investor anxiety as these platforms emerged, all of these channels simply became another entry point to retailers’ respective categories that companies needed to embrace. As such, we echo the need for our companies to invest to win with the large LLMs, while harnessing their own agents “on platform” (i.e., their own websites/apps) to drive customer acquisition/retention, product discovery, basket building, and conversion. Investments in both areas can optimize proprietary data collection, an important sales and advertising revenue consideration.

Unclear monetization path shifts AI platforms' focus from agentic commerce to digital advertising. While OpenAI initially charged retailers an affiliate fee ( $\sim 4\%$ on avg. per JPM Analyst Brenda Duverce) on transactions completed through its Instant Checkout offering, AI platforms don't currently participate in the economics of agentic commerce transactions after this was deprecated and retailers now control checkout. In our view, there currently isn't much visibility as to how AI platforms will monetize agentic commerce transactions, with their focus seemingly narrowing on digital advertising (presumably LLMs clip some of the potential from retail media, but not material at this point).

Notably, unlike the original Amazon threat, retailers are generally being front-footed, including partnering with the likes of Open AI and Google to both shape the direction of agentic commerce and to stay on the front edge of the learning curve (the opposite of what happened in the 2010-15 timeframe when AMZN rose to e-commerce dominance). As noted below, WMT is leading the pack, as it should given its broad product offering and marketplace model, with TGT on solid footing, and W, BBY, HD, LOW, ULTA, and ASO all partnering with Google on UCP (Universal Commerce Protocol - an open-source set of standards that enables retailers to allow AI agents to browse their websites, build carts, and ultimately check out, Figure 2). The two biggest retailer moat-protectors are (1) they have retaken control of the transaction with checkout now facilitated through retailer app integrations on the LLMs (after OpenAI and the retailers shifted away from Instant Checkout where OpenAI controlled the transaction), and (2) retailers still handle fulfillment, keeping true disintermediation at bay. Notably, as an example of how retailers can benefit in an agentic world, BBY mgmt spoke to its depth of knowledge/data for its category to match a search to the best product as a competitive advantage that only increases in an LLM world (e.g., best college student laptop vs. best laptop for mom, gaming, or coding), albeit acknowledging that it (and most retailers, in our view) needs to better enable the data links between its website and the LLMs.

\- Risk categories: grocery and brands with DTC fulfillment. On the other hand, retailers in highly emotive categories (e.g., beauty, pet) and where browsing/basket building is important (e.g., home decor) remain in the low-risk bucket.

\- Retailers need to partner with LLMs. Early engagement with the major LLMs to shape agentic commerce and create data connections, while implementing retailer app integration into the LLMs to support checkout, are important steps to maintaining category leadership, supporting product discovery, and harvesting data. WMT (Sparky), TGT, HD (Magic Apron), LOW (MyLow), ULTA (ULTA AI), WSM (Olive), ASO (Scout), and DKS (Coach) are all in-house agents. Ultimately, to optimize success, we believe retailers will need to both engage through/with the major LLMs and also use their own agents on their owned platforms to drive customer acquisition/retention/satisfaction, product discovery, basket building, and proprietary data collection.

## The Agentic Debate: Disintermediation Risk Remains Low

While adoption remains early stages, the current state of agentic commerce reflects consumers primarily using LLMs for product discovery, inspiration, and basket building, with the potential progression to consumers transacting through AI platforms and delegating responsibility for automated replenishment of commoditized products, with discrete opportunities and risks for both these and more considered purchases. The bigger disintermediation risk comes when LLM agents become “smart enough” to handle the entire transaction through checkout, thereby disintermediating the retailer, which gives the LLM access to data and all the goodness that comes along with it (e.g., advertising revenues). That said, even if/when we cross that bridge, the most significant difference is that LLMs don’t control the distribution process, with the likes of WMT and AMZN still having scaled supply chain advantages vs. virtually all of the broader retail set, and many brands/vendors generally reluctant to carry the burden of distributing directly to the consumer.

At a high level, we see two areas of concern. First, LLMs elevate price discovery, which creates potential downward pressure on price-sensitive categories. This certainly pushes grocery/CPG to the top of the risk bucket, but it also creates opportunities for retailers where the consumer sees private label as a strong substitute (e.g., Kirkland Signature and Amazon Basics). Second, LLMs can potentially disintermediate retailers of sought-after brands in style categories where the vendor has an established direct to consumer mechanism. Apple in consumer electronics is a good example and we point to Nike's history of episodically disintermediating Dick's and other retailers given the desire to capture the full retail margin. Indeed, branded footwear and apparel seem particularly exposed on this front. On the other hand, retailers in highly emotive categories (e.g., beauty, pet) and where browsing/basket building is important (e.g., home furnishings/decor) remain in the low risk bucket.

## The Current State of Affairs: Agentic Threat Remains Limited

AI Platforms remain $<1\%$ of total web traffic across key e-commerce marketplaces and retailer sites (Figure 1), but marketplaces/retailers have experienced stronger engagement, conversion, and AOVs from AI-enabled customers. In our view, customer adoption of AI Platforms for the full shopping experience (bridging from discovery and basket building to checkout) is a function of existing collaborations/partnerships between retailers and AI platforms, with the shift from model-controlled checkout environments (e.g., OpenAI Instant Checkout) to retailer-controlled ones (e.g., app integrations via UCP) a key step in accelerating agentic commerce proliferation as consumers will now have better access to accurate and localized pricing, promotions, loyalty programs, inventory availability, etc. while also protecting retailer/marketplace advertising programs.

## Invest Early to Stay Ahead of the Curve

What is the internet infrastructure supporting agenetic commerce? Various e-commerce industry participants are collaborating to create an open standard of Internet infrastructure supporting AI-driven transactions. \~20 retailers & Internet companies are collaborating on Google's UCP council, including AMZN, BBY, HD, LOW, META, MSFT, ETSY, SHOP, TGT, ULTA, WMT, W, and others. Google's Universal Cart enables consumers to add items to their cart across multiple merchants and across services, and it leverages intelligent reasoning to provide personalized shopping assistance. The UCP enables seamless checkout from the Universal Cart. While OpenAI & Stripe have co-developed the Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP), Team Internet's view is that its scope is more narrowly focused on enabling secure checkout via Stripe vs. broader full-funnel agentic commerce capabilities, though it does offer agentic discovery. Anthropic's MCP enables developers to connect data sources and AI tools, though it is not primarily confined to e-commerce and instead is focused on broader web infrastructure. As noted above, enabling merchant scale and ease of AI integrations will be key to scaling agentic commerce.

Companies that invest ahead of the curve will see a first-mover advantage in terms of customer trust/mindshare, reinforcing the retailer's brand perch as a “shopping destination" and providing valuable data that is collected from the LLM queries.

\- Integrating with LLMs via UCP is a critical first step to both gaining share from agentic commerce adoption and maintaining a customer data advantage.

\- Retailers that also invest in a robust, proprietary data infrastructure stand to differentiate themselves, including both customer data to understand purchasing behavior (AMZN, COST, ULTA, and others with strong loyalty programs stand out in this regard) as well as accurate inventory/fulfillment systems so that consumers can trust localized/store-level product pricing and availability information. Many retailers have invested in the latter to contend with the e-commerce threat, given solid inventory visibility is required to support omnichannel retail (e.g., can't promise delivery speeds without understanding inventory positioning at a granular level), while the customer data/loyalty opportunity has been growing in recent years to support budding retail media ventures (and this becomes increasingly important in an agentic commerce world to improve personalized demand prediction and relevant basket-building recommendations, etc.).

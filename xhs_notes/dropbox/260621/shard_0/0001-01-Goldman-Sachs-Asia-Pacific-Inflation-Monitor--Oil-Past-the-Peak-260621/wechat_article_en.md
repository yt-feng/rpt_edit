# The Asia-Pacific Inflation Peak Has Passed, But the Real Test for Central Banks Is Just Beginning

The energy shock that ripped through Asia-Pacific following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz is receding faster than most forecasters anticipated just two months ago. Brent crude, which spiked above $140 per barrel in April, has collapsed back toward $80, and a global investment bank’s commodity strategists now see the Q4 benchmark at that level, down from a prior $90 forecast. This is not merely a data point. It is a structural turning point for the region’s inflation dynamics, and it carries a clear strategic implication: the window for central banks to ease policy is opening, but the asymmetry of the recovery across economies will define winners and losers in fixed income and currency markets.

The headline narrative is straightforward. Pre-war, Asia-Pacific CPI was broadly in line with or below central bank targets. The conflict drove headline and core inflation above those targets across most economies. Now, with oil past the peak and import prices set to ease materially in the next monthly update, the risk to inflation forecasts has shifted to the downside. But beneath this surface, a more complex and consequential story is unfolding. The divergence between economies with fiscal capacity to subsidize energy and those without, between tight labor markets and slack ones, and between countries that secured alternative supply routes and those that did not, will determine how quickly inflation normalizes and how aggressively policymakers can act.

The report that underpins this analysis provides the data. The argument that follows is about what that data means for investors who need to position for the next twelve months.

## The Energy Supply Normalization Is the Dominant Variable, Not Demand Slowdown

The most powerful force driving Asia-Pacific inflation lower over the next quarter is not a collapse in consumer demand or a central bank-induced recession. It is the physical return of oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz. The report’s exhibit on estimated oil exports through the strait shows a dramatic collapse from normal levels of approximately 3 million barrels per day to near zero in early May, followed by a tentative and volatile recovery. This is a supply-side shock, and its reversal is now priced into the forward curve.

The implication is critical: disinflation in this cycle will be led by the energy component of CPI, not by core goods or services. For investors, this means that the trajectory of headline inflation will improve faster than core inflation in most economies. Central banks that target headline measures will see a reason to pause or cut sooner. Those that target core will remain cautious. The report’s data on refined product prices confirms this: while Brent has fallen sharply, refined product prices remain notably above pre-war levels, suggesting that passthrough to consumers is sticky and that the full disinflationary benefit will take months to materialize.

The strategic question is not whether inflation will fall, but how much of the decline is already discounted by markets. The bond market has likely priced a rapid normalization. If the recovery of Hormuz flows stalls or is disrupted again, the current easing in financial conditions could reverse sharply.

## Lower-Income Economies Face a Steeper Disinflation Path, But Also the Most Room for Policy Relief

The report highlights a stark asymmetry: inflation pressures have been particularly acute in lower-income economies with more limited subsidies. The seasonally-adjusted CPI inflation over the past three months in the Philippines and Thailand remains over 10% annualized. These are the economies where households and businesses have felt the full force of the energy shock because governments lacked the fiscal space to cap retail fuel prices.

Yet the same data shows that these economies have also seen much lower month-on-month headline CPI inflation in May. This is the first sign of the turning point. For investors, this creates a compelling tactical opportunity. If inflation in these economies is peaking and set to fall rapidly as energy costs decline, their central banks have the most to gain from easing. The Philippine peso and Thai baht, which have been under pressure from imported inflation, could see significant relief. Local currency bonds in these markets may offer the highest total return potential if the disinflation narrative plays out as the data suggests.

The risk, however, is that core inflation in these economies remains elevated due to second-round effects. Wage pressure, while mixed across the region, could be a persistent factor in lower-income countries where labor markets are tighter. The report notes that wage inflation has been stable or lower in most of the region, but the data is not granular enough to rule out a lagged passthrough. This is the key uncertainty that the report leaves open.

## The Divergence in Core Inflation Behavior Is the Report’s Most Underappreciated Insight

A surface reading of the report suggests that core inflation has been well-behaved across the region. The median core CPI for the Asia-Pacific economies covered has remained within a narrow range, and the interquartile range has not expanded dramatically. But this aggregate hides a critical divergence. The report’s data on core inflation measures by economy shows that some countries have seen core inflation rise significantly while others have remained stable.

The strategic implication is that the disinflation trade is not uniform. Investors cannot treat Asia-Pacific as a single bloc. The report provides the framework for identifying which economies are likely to see core inflation fall quickly and which are at risk of sticky core inflation. The key variables are the degree of energy subsidy, the tightness of the labor market, and the exposure to imported food and raw material prices.

Economies with large fiscal buffers, such as South Korea and China, were able to absorb much of the energy shock through subsidies and strategic reserves. Their core inflation dynamics are likely to normalize faster. In contrast, economies like India and Indonesia, which have less fiscal room and higher food price weights in their CPI baskets, may see core inflation remain elevated even as headline inflation falls. This is where the report’s data is most valuable, and where the full report provides the necessary granularity to make country-level investment decisions.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: What Happens to Wage Inflation in Japan?

The report makes a passing observation that wage inflation has been mixed across Asia-Pacific, but stable or lower in most of the region, with one notable exception: Japan. This single line is perhaps the most important unresolved analytical question in the entire report.

Japan’s labor market has been tight for years, and the Bank of Japan has been waiting for wage growth to become self-sustaining before normalizing policy. If the energy shock has pushed Japanese wages higher, even temporarily, the BOJ could face a dilemma. Higher headline inflation from energy could be transitory, but if it feeds into wage negotiations and becomes embedded in services inflation, the central bank may need to tighten policy even as the rest of the region is easing.

The report does not provide the data to answer this question definitively. It notes the exception but does not explore its implications for the yen, Japanese government bonds, or regional yield curves. For readers who need to make a decision on Japan exposure, this is the gap that the full report would need to fill. The question is not whether Japan’s inflation is transitory, but whether the transmission mechanism from energy to wages is stronger than in other economies. If it is, Japan is a structural outlier that demands a completely different portfolio strategy.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios for the Next Six Months

Based on the report’s data and logic, investors can construct a simple but powerful decision framework. The three key variables are the speed of the Hormuz recovery, the persistence of core inflation in lower-income economies, and the behavior of Japanese wages.

Scenario One: Fast Normalization. Hormuz flows recover fully by end-July, Brent falls to $80 or below, and core inflation in most economies falls back toward pre-war levels. In this scenario, the investment opportunity is clear: overweight local currency bonds in the Philippines, Thailand, and India, where the disinflation surprise will be largest. Underweight Japan, where the BOJ may still need to normalize. This is the base case implied by the report’s oil forecast.

Scenario Two: Sticky Core. Hormuz recovers, but core inflation in lower-income economies remains elevated due to second-round effects from food and wages. In this scenario, the disinflation trade is more nuanced. Investors should favor economies with strong fiscal positions and flexible exchange rates, such as South Korea and Singapore. Avoid economies where fiscal constraints limit the ability to respond to persistent inflation.

Scenario Three: Supply Disruption Renewed. The recovery of Hormuz stalls, or a new geopolitical event drives oil prices back above $120. In this scenario, the entire trade reverses. Inflation expectations become unanchored, and all Asia-Pacific central banks are forced to tighten. The only safe haven is the US dollar and short-duration instruments. This is the tail risk that the report’s downside scenario captures, with Brent potentially falling to $50 in a demand collapse, but the path to that outcome is through renewed supply disruption first.

Each scenario leads to a different portfolio allocation. The report provides the data to assess the probabilities. The full report, with its detailed country-level exhibits and central bank analysis, is the tool to refine those probabilities and make the final call.

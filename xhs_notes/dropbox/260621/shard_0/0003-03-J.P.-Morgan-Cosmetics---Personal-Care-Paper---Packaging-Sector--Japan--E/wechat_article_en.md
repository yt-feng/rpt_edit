# A Middle East Détente Creates a Divergent Earnings Landscape for Japanese Consumer and Industrial Stocks

The June 17 memorandum of understanding between the US and Iran, which effectively pauses the escalation of hostilities in the Middle East, is not merely a geopolitical headline. For investors in Japanese equities, it signals a structural shift in the earnings trajectory of several key sectors. The immediate market reaction has been muted, but the analytical work beneath the surface reveals a clear, actionable thesis: the stabilization of crude oil prices and the easing of regional supply-chain fears create a meaningful divergence between personal care companies and paper & packaging firms, with the former emerging as the primary beneficiaries and the latter facing a more nuanced, and in some ways riskier, path forward. The next fiscal year is not a time for blanket sector plays; it is a time for stock-specific conviction based on cost structure, pricing power, and regional exposure.

The core insight from a recent detailed sector analysis is that the earnings impact of this geopolitical shift is not uniform. While a $1 per barrel change in crude oil prices translates into a predictable ¥500-600 million impact on core operating profit for companies like Unicharm and Kao, and a ¥100-200 million impact for Lion, these are merely the mechanical sensitivities. The strategic question is not *how much* earnings will move, but *which* companies are structurally positioned to benefit from a sustained period of lower input costs and improved consumer sentiment, and which are inadvertently exposed to the secondary effects of a sudden price decline. The answer, as the analysis makes clear, hinges on the interplay between input-cost pass-through mechanisms, inventory cycles, and the nature of demand in each sub-sector.

## The Detente's Primary Benefit Flows to Personal Care Companies with High Crude Oil Sensitivity and a Middle East Presence

The most direct and positive earnings catalyst from the improved Middle East situation is for the personal care subsector, specifically for Unicharm, Kao, and Lion. The logic is straightforward but the implications are deeper than a simple cost reduction. These companies have manufacturing cost structures that are meaningfully tied to crude oil market conditions, primarily through raw materials for packaging, plastics, and certain chemical inputs. The recent decline in crude oil prices, enabled by the détente, directly alleviates cost-growth concerns for their fiscal year 2027.

However, the strategic insight lies in the timing of this benefit. The report estimates a 4-6 month lag for input price fluctuations to be fully reflected in earnings. This means that the positive effect of lower crude prices will arrive precisely when it is most valuable: as a counterbalance to the inevitable profit pressure expected in the second half of fiscal year 2026. That pressure stems from two sources: a pullback from household stockpiling demand that built up during the period of heightened geopolitical tension, and the lingering impact of high-cost inputs that were secured at inflated prices earlier in the year. The détente, therefore, does not eliminate a near-term earnings headwind; it provides a powerful offset to it, smoothing the earnings trajectory and reducing downside risk.

Furthermore, for Unicharm specifically, the risk that has been weighing on its share price is not just cost-driven. The worsening situation in the Middle East created a direct concern about its sales environment in that region. The memorandum of understanding directly addresses this overhang, reducing the probability of a disruptive demand shock. The combined effect of lower input costs and a stabilized regional sales backdrop creates a powerful earnings tailwind that is more structural than cyclical.

## For Cosmetics Companies, the Benefit is Indirect but Potentially More Durable, Centered on Consumer Sentiment and Tourism

The cosmetics subsector presents a different analytical challenge. The direct input-cost impact of the Middle East situation was always limited, given the low cost ratios of raw materials and packaging relative to the final product price. The stock market, correctly, had limited concerns about cost inflation for companies like Shiseido and Kosé compared with their personal care counterparts. Therefore, the direct benefit from lower crude oil prices is minimal and should not be the primary investment thesis.

The real opportunity for cosmetics companies lies in the second-order effects of the geopolitical improvement. The easing of tensions has the potential to improve consumer sentiment, particularly in key Asian markets, and to drive an increase in tourist numbers, especially from China and other parts of Asia. This is a demand-side catalyst that is far more powerful than any cost-side benefit. The strategic question for investors is whether this improvement in sentiment is already priced in. The report's analysis suggests that the market has not yet fully discounted this potential. The risk, however, is that the link between geopolitical calm and consumer spending is not linear. A recovery in tourism and discretionary spending requires not just an absence of conflict, but a restoration of confidence. This may take longer than the 4-6 month lag for cost pass-through in personal care. The cosmetics sector, therefore, offers a more patient, but potentially more durable, recovery narrative.

## The Paper and Packaging Sector Faces a Strategic Dilemma: Lower Costs Undermine Pricing Power

This is where the report's analysis becomes most contrarian and strategically valuable. On the surface, an improvement in the Middle East situation should be unambiguously positive for the paper and packaging sector. These companies, including Oji Holdings, Nippon Paper Industries, and Daio Paper, are significant consumers of energy and raw materials linked to crude oil. A decline in crude prices alleviates cost deterioration, and the share prices of these companies should strengthen.

However, the report identifies a critical strategic flaw in this simple narrative. Major paper manufacturers were planning to raise prices from July-August, particularly for graphic paper. The current sharp drop in crude oil prices undeniably carries the risk that these price negotiations will become harder, if not impossible, to execute. Customers, seeing lower input costs for the producers, will resist price increases with greater force. This creates a strategic dilemma: the cost relief from lower crude prices is being offset by a loss of pricing power.

The deeper implication is that the paper sector's business model, which relies on periodic price hikes to restore margins, is being disrupted. The report notes that the price decline might also delay the establishment of systems to pass on costs, including fixed costs, to product prices. This is not a temporary issue. If the market perceives that the industry's ability to raise prices is structurally impaired by a more stable cost environment, the valuation of these companies could suffer a permanent downgrade. The mechanical earnings sensitivity to crude oil (approximately ¥300 million per $1/bbl for Oji and Nippon Paper) is only part of the story. The strategic risk to the business model is far more significant.

## The Report's Unresolved Question: How Durable is the Geopolitical Calm, and What is the True Inventory Cycle Risk?

Every strategic analysis must confront its own limitations. The most significant unresolved question in this report is the durability of the US-Iran understanding. The memorandum of understanding is a tentative agreement. The analysis correctly assumes a "do not deteriorate" scenario, but it does not fully model the consequences of a renewed escalation. For investors, this creates a binary risk that is difficult to hedge. A re-escalation would reverse the crude oil decline, re-introduce the Middle East sales risk for Unicharm, and restore the pricing power of the paper sector, but at the cost of higher input costs.

Furthermore, the report's analysis of the inventory cycle is a critical area that warrants deeper investigation. It highlights the risk of profit pressure in 2H FY2026 from a pullback in household stockpiling demand and the high cost of inputs secured at inflated prices. The magnitude of this "inventory hangover" is not quantified. How much stockpiling occurred? How long will it take to destock? For personal care companies, this is a transitory headwind that will be offset by lower future input costs. But the timing of these two forces—the hangover and the benefit—is the key to quarterly earnings forecasting. A deeper dive into channel inventory data would be required to build a precise earnings model, and that data is not publicly available in this report.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Prioritize Cost Pass-Through and Regional Exposure

How should an investor translate this analysis into a portfolio decision? The framework is built on two axes: the company's ability to pass on costs to consumers, and the nature of its exposure to the Middle East region.

The first axis is pricing power. Personal care companies, with their branded consumer products and relatively inelastic demand, have a higher ability to pass on costs over time. The 4-6 month lag is a timing issue, not a structural impediment. The paper sector, operating in a more commoditized market with powerful customers, has lower pricing power. The current environment actively undermines their ability to execute planned price increases.

The second axis is regional exposure. Companies with direct sales or significant operations in the Middle East, such as Unicharm, benefit from a direct reduction in geopolitical risk. Cosmetics companies benefit from a broader improvement in Asian tourism, which is a more indirect but potentially larger driver.

Within this framework, the most attractive investments are those that combine high pricing power with a direct reduction in regional risk. The least attractive are those where lower input costs are actively destroying the conditions required for margin recovery. The report's analysis strongly suggests that the personal care sector, particularly Unicharm, Kao, and Lion, sits in the first category, while the paper sector sits in the second.

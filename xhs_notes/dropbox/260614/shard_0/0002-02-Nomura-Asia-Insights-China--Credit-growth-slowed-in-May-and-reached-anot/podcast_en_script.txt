EN_B: Exactly. April was the same story. And the report says the composition of credit in May looks very similar to the previous two months. The main drags were new RMB loans and government bond issuance. Corporate bond issuance was relatively strong, but not enough to offset the weakness.
EN_A: What about corporate loans? The headline figure for new corporate loans actually looked higher than a year ago, right?
EN_B: On the surface, yes. But the report highlights a key distortion. Almost all of that increase came from bill financing. New bill financing surged to 557 billion RMB in May, compared to just 75 billion a year earlier. Bill financing is not real credit demand. It is basically banks padding their numbers to meet targets. If you strip that out, actual corporate loan demand looks much weaker.
EN_A: So it is kind of inflated?
EN_B: Exactly. Inflated. And the household sector is even more concerning. New household loans were negative in May, minus 141 billion. Both short-term and long-term loans were negative. EN_A: This has been negative for a couple of months now. Does the report explain why?
EN_B: It points to ongoing deleveraging in the household sector. But why is deleveraging continuing? Is it about income expectations or a shift in views on the property market? The report does not fully answer this yet. That is one of the open questions we can explore further.
EN_A: Okay, and what about government bonds? Their issuance was also below expectations?
EN_B: Yes. Net government bond issuance was 1.22 trillion RMB in May, down from 1.46 trillion a year earlier. That is a bit surprising because you would expect fiscal policy to be more aggressive. The report expects government bond issuance to accelerate in the coming months, as Beijing needs to support fixed asset investment. But whether that actually happens, and when, is another open question.
EN_A: And what is the central bank doing? I saw the report mentions they are draining liquidity.
EN_B: That is a very interesting twist. In May, the interbank rate DR007 dropped to below 1.3 percent, well below the central bank’s policy rate of 1.4 percent. Banks have a lot of cash, but companies are not borrowing, so banks are dumping cash into the bond market, pushing the 10-year government bond yield down to around 1.7 percent. The central bank did not like that. So they started draining liquidity by shrinking open market operations and guiding state-owned banks to reduce interbank lending.

They think the central bank has three objectives: first, to restore the signaling function of the interest rate corridor; second, to stop idle financial circulation that could create risks in the bond market; and third, to stop the self-reinforcing decline in government bond yields. The report maintains its baseline forecast of no rate or RRR cuts this year.

# China's Credit Contraction Is Not a Liquidity Problem — It Is a Demand Problem That Policy Tools Alone Cannot Solve

The headline numbers from China's May credit data tell a story of incremental deterioration, but the underlying composition reveals something far more consequential: the world's second-largest economy is experiencing a structural breakdown in credit demand that policy stimulus is failing to reverse. Outstanding aggregate financing growth fell to 7.7% year-on-year, a fresh historical low, while outstanding RMB loan growth dropped to 5.5%, also an all-time low. These are not merely cyclical soft patches. They represent a deepening disconnect between the monetary authority's efforts to inject liquidity and the real economy's willingness to borrow.

What makes this moment different from previous slowdowns is that the usual transmission mechanisms have broken down. Banks are flush with cash. Interbank rates have drifted well below the policy rate. The People's Bank of China has been forced to actively drain liquidity — a counterintuitive move during a credit slowdown — because the funds are not reaching productive borrowers. Instead, they are circulating within the financial system, compressing bond yields and building vulnerabilities that the central bank now sees as a risk to financial stability.

For investors and strategists, the May data demands a reassessment of the China narrative. The conventional view that Beijing can always stimulate its way out of a downturn is being tested by a structural shift in household and corporate behavior. The question is no longer whether policy will ease further, but whether easing can work at all under current conditions.

## The PBoC's Liquidity Drain Is Not a Policy Mistake — It Is a Recognition That Excess Liquidity Has Become Destabilizing

The most analytically challenging feature of the current environment is the People's Bank of China's simultaneous response to weak credit and excess liquidity. On the surface, these two conditions appear contradictory. How can credit be contracting while the banking system is awash in funds? The answer lies in the composition of credit demand and the channels through which liquidity flows.

Since early April, interbank rates have drifted well below the PBoC's 1.40% seven-day reverse repo rate, with DR007 touching sub-1.3% levels in May. This occurred because earlier injections via medium-term lending facilities, pledged supplementary lending, and outright reverse repos had left banks with abundant cash but few willing borrowers. Rather than extending loans to the real economy, banks channelled funds into the bond market, compressing the 10-year Chinese government bond yield to approximately 1.7%.

The PBoC's response — shrinking daily open market operations to record lows and instructing state-owned banks to curb interbank lending through window guidance — has been widely misinterpreted as a signal that the central bank is preserving space for future rate cuts or reserve requirement ratio reductions. This interpretation is almost certainly wrong. The PBoC's objectives are more fundamental: restoring the rate corridor's signalling function, arresting the financial idle circulation that risks building vulnerabilities in the bond market, and stopping the self-reinforcing decline in government bond yields.

The implication for investors is clear. The PBoC is not tightening because it wants to constrain growth. It is tightening because the existing liquidity is not supporting growth and is instead creating financial stability risks. This distinction matters because it suggests that further monetary easing, in the absence of demand-side fixes, may be counterproductive. The central bank's baseline, according to the report, is no rate or RRR cuts in 2026.

## The Corporate Loan Data Is Worse Than It Appears — Bill Financing Is Masking a Collapse in Genuine Credit Demand

The headline figure for new corporate loans in May appears adequate at first glance. New RMB loans to the corporate sector increased to RMB640 billion from RMB530 billion a year earlier. But this surface-level improvement evaporates upon decomposition.

New medium- to long-term corporate loans fell to negative RMB20 billion in May, compared with positive RMB330 billion a year earlier. New short-term corporate loans were largely unchanged at RMB100 billion. The entire increase in corporate lending came from bill financing, which surged to RMB557 billion from RMB75 billion a year earlier. Bill financing is not a genuine indicator of corporate credit demand. It is a short-term, low-risk instrument that banks use to meet regulatory lending targets when there are no real borrowers. Stripping out this distortion, the headline new RMB loan figure would look far weaker than the already disappointing consensus miss.

This pattern has now persisted for multiple months, suggesting it is not a temporary anomaly but a structural feature of the current credit cycle. Corporate borrowers are not taking on long-term commitments. They are not investing in capacity expansion, equipment, or real estate. The negative reading for medium- to long-term corporate loans is particularly concerning because it indicates that even large, creditworthy firms are unwilling to commit to multi-year borrowing at current interest rate levels.

For investors, the message is that the corporate sector is in a de facto credit strike. This is not a function of loan pricing — yields are already near historic lows. It is a function of demand expectations. Companies are not borrowing because they do not see sufficient return on investment in the current macroeconomic environment. No amount of liquidity injection can solve this problem until the demand outlook improves.

## The Household Sector Is Deleveraging at an Accelerating Pace, and This Trend Shows No Sign of Reversing

The household sector data in May is alarming by any historical standard. New household loans remained negative for the month, falling to negative RMB141 billion from positive RMB54 billion a year earlier. Both components were in negative territory: new short-term household loans at negative RMB84 billion, and new medium- to long-term household loans — primarily mortgages — at negative RMB57 billion.

This is not a one-month aberration. The protracted contraction in household loans suggests a sustained deleveraging cycle that is becoming entrenched. Households are not only refraining from taking on new debt; they are actively paying down existing obligations. This behavior is rational from a microeconomic perspective — households are responding to falling home prices, weak employment confidence, and uncertain income prospects — but it is devastating for the macroeconomic credit multiplier.

The decline in household deposits adds another layer of concern. New household deposits turned negative in May, dropping to negative RMB110 billion from positive RMB470 billion a year earlier. This marked the first negative print for the month of May since 2015. The combination of falling deposits and falling loans suggests that households are not simply shifting assets between categories. They are reducing both their financial assets and their liabilities simultaneously — a classic symptom of balance sheet recession dynamics.

The policy implications are profound. Traditional property-sector stimulus measures, such as lower mortgage rates or reduced down-payment requirements, are unlikely to be effective when households are actively reducing leverage. The household sector's behavior is consistent with a shift in preferences toward liquidity and safety, not a response to borrowing costs. Until household confidence in future income and asset prices stabilizes, credit demand from this sector will remain structurally weak.

## The Government Bond Issuance Gap Raises Questions About Fiscal Transmission — But Acceleration Is Likely

Government bond issuance was again surprisingly weaker than year-earlier levels in May. Net government bond issuance fell to RMB1,222 billion from RMB1,459 billion a year earlier. This is puzzling given the widespread expectation that fiscal policy would be the primary driver of credit expansion in 2026.

The report suggests that government bond issuance should accelerate in coming months, driven by Beijing's need to support contracting fixed asset investment. This is a reasonable baseline expectation. The fiscal pipeline is substantial, and local government special bond quotas remain underutilized. If issuance does accelerate, it will provide a mechanical boost to aggregate financing growth, even if the underlying credit demand from the private sector remains weak.

However, the fiscal transmission mechanism is not straightforward. Government bond issuance absorbs liquidity from the banking system, which could put upward pressure on interbank rates at a time when the PBoC is already draining liquidity. There is a tension between the fiscal authority's desire to issue more bonds and the monetary authority's desire to manage financial stability risks. How this tension is resolved will determine the trajectory of credit growth in the second half of the year.

For investors, the key question is whether faster government bond issuance can offset the drag from household and corporate deleveraging. The arithmetic suggests it can, at least in terms of aggregate financing growth. But the quality of credit expansion matters. An aggregate financing recovery driven entirely by government bonds is not the same as one driven by private sector borrowing. It does not signal a recovery in investment demand, and it does not generate the same multiplier effects on employment and income.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer — And Why These Open Questions Matter for Investors

The report provides an excellent diagnostic of the current credit environment, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. These gaps are not weaknesses in the analysis; they reflect genuine uncertainties that investors must navigate.

First, the report does not fully explain why M1 growth ticked up to 5.5% year-on-year in May from 5.0% in April, even as credit demand deteriorated. M1, which includes cash and demand deposits, is often viewed as a leading indicator of economic activity. Its improvement alongside weakening credit data is a puzzle. One possible explanation is that enterprises are hoarding cash rather than investing it — a precautionary motive that would be consistent with the broader deleveraging narrative. But the report does not explore this tension, and it remains an open analytical question.

Second, the report does not address the external sector's role in the credit dynamics. The RMB has appreciated modestly against the US dollar year-to-date despite a widening of the US-China rate differential. This is unusual and suggests that capital flows are being influenced by factors beyond interest rate differentials — possibly including trade flows, reserve management, or policy intervention. The implications for domestic credit conditions are unclear. A stronger RMB could reduce export competitiveness and further weaken corporate demand for credit, but it could also reduce imported inflation and give the PBoC more room to ease. The report does not resolve this ambiguity.

Third, the report does not model the feedback loop between credit contraction and asset prices. Falling household loans are correlated with falling property prices, which further weakens household confidence and reduces the collateral value of real estate. This dynamic can become self-reinforcing, and the report does not provide a framework for estimating how far this cycle can run before it stabilizes.

These open questions are not reasons to dismiss the report's conclusions. They are reasons to treat the outlook with humility and to recognize that the current environment is unprecedented in several dimensions.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position for a Credit Environment That Defies Historical Precedent

Given the structural nature of the current credit contraction, investors need a framework that goes beyond simple cyclical positioning. The following decision framework is designed to help allocate attention and capital across the most relevant scenarios.

First, distinguish between liquidity-driven and demand-driven credit problems. The current environment is demand-driven, which means that traditional fixed-income strategies that benefit from monetary easing may not work as expected. Bond yields have already compressed to near-record lows, and the PBoC is actively pushing back against further declines. Investors should not assume that weak credit automatically translates into lower yields. The relationship has broken down.

Second, focus on the composition of aggregate financing rather than the headline growth rate. If government bond issuance accelerates and private sector borrowing remains weak, the aggregate financing growth rate may stabilize or even improve modestly. But this would be a misleading signal. Investors should track the share of aggregate financing coming from government bonds versus private sector loans. A recovery that is entirely fiscal-driven is not a recovery in private sector confidence.

Third, monitor household deposit behavior as a leading indicator of confidence. The negative reading for household deposits in May is a warning signal. If this trend continues, it would suggest that households are not only deleveraging but also drawing down precautionary savings to fund consumption or debt repayment. This would be a more severe phase of the balance sheet adjustment and would have negative implications for consumption-driven sectors.

Fourth, watch the PBoC's liquidity management operations for signs of a policy pivot. The current stance of draining liquidity while credit contracts is unusual and may not be sustainable. If the PBoC shifts back to net injection mode, it would signal that the central bank has accepted the need to support the bond market even at the cost of financial stability risks. Such a pivot would be bullish for bonds in the near term but would raise longer-term questions about the effectiveness of monetary policy.

Fifth, prepare for the possibility that the current environment persists for longer than consensus expects. Structural deleveraging cycles in other economies — Japan in the 1990s, parts of Europe after 2008 — lasted years, not quarters. The May data suggests China may be in the early stages of such a cycle. Portfolio construction should account for the possibility of a prolonged period of weak credit growth, low investment demand, and policy experimentation.

# The Market Has Misread the Geopolitical Risk in Chinese Biotech: Why Restricting Out-Licensing Would Hurt the US More Than China

The healthcare sector in Asia has been underperforming year-to-date, and the conventional explanation points to geopolitical anxiety. Investors have been selling on good news, even after the disclosure of promising clinical data at major medical conferences. The prevailing narrative is straightforward: proposed US legislation targeting Chinese biotechnology investments creates an unacceptable regulatory overhang, and capital is rotating away from the sector until clarity emerges. This interpretation, while superficially plausible, mistakes a political signal for an economic reality.

The core argument of this analysis is that the market has overreacted to the proposed restrictions on out-licensing deals between Chinese biotech companies and US partners. A careful examination of the data reveals that such legislation, even if enacted, would inflict greater damage on the US biotechnology industry than on its Chinese counterpart. The reason is structural: China has become an indispensable global hub for early-stage drug discovery, and the US pharmaceutical ecosystem has become deeply dependent on accessing Chinese innovation pipelines. Severing this connection would not slow Chinese R&D; it would starve US drug development of its most prolific source of new molecular entities.

The proposed Biotech Investment National Security Act (BINSA) and the broader push to include biotechnology under the COINS Act represent a genuine political development, but they are far from a foregone conclusion. The legislative process is lengthy, the opposition from industry stakeholders is formidable, and the economic logic of self-harm is difficult to override. Yet the market has priced these risks as though the legislation were imminent and comprehensive. This creates a dislocation between price and fundamental value, and for investors willing to look beyond the headlines, that dislocation represents opportunity.

The following sections unpack why the market has mispriced this risk, what the data actually shows about the direction of deal flow, and how thoughtful investors should position themselves in this environment.

## China Has Become a Non-Negotiable Source of Global Drug Innovation, and the Data Proves It

The most critical fact that the market underappreciates is that China is no longer a low-cost manufacturing hub for pharmaceuticals; it is a genuine innovation engine. Total pharmaceutical R&D spending in China has grown from USD 26 billion in 2020 to an estimated USD 39 billion in 2025, representing an 8.5% compound annual growth rate. Throughout this period, China has consistently accounted for approximately 11% to 13% of global pharmaceutical R&D expenditure. While this share may appear modest, the output relative to spend is extraordinary.

Since 2020, China has led the world in the number of innovative drugs entering clinical trials. In 2024 alone, Chinese companies initiated 722 new drugs into clinical trials, compared to 457 from the United States, 241 from Europe, and 146 from Japan. The gap is widening, not narrowing. In 2025, China's count reached 827, while the US remained at 482. This is not a function of lower costs alone; it reflects a mature R&D ecosystem built on accumulated experience, technical know-how, and a willingness to take risks on novel mechanisms. The efficiency of this ecosystem is now structural.

The implication for US pharmaceutical companies is straightforward. If they are restricted from accessing Chinese drug candidates through licensing deals, they lose access to the single largest pipeline of early-stage innovation in the world. This is particularly acute in emerging therapeutic modalities such as antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and multi-specific antibodies, where Chinese companies have established clear leadership. The idea that US companies could simply replace this pipeline with domestic alternatives is contradicted by the data: the US is not producing comparable volume or diversity of clinical candidates. The legislation, if enacted, would not protect the US industry; it would handicap it.

## Restricting Out-Licensing Would Redirect Deal Flow to Europe and Asia, Not Back to the United States

The market seems to assume that if US companies are blocked from in-licensing Chinese assets, those assets would simply be developed in China alone, reducing global innovation. This is incorrect. The more likely outcome is a geographic reallocation of deal flow, and the United States would be the primary loser in that reallocation.

The data on out-licensing deals from Chinese companies is instructive. From 2020 through May 2026, US-based partners accounted for 42% of the number of out-licensing deals and 57% of total upfront payments. Europe represented 28% of deals and 33% of upfront payments, while Asia (excluding China) accounted for 18% of deals but only 9% of upfront payments. The US premium in deal value reflects the willingness of US companies to pay for access to Chinese innovation. If that channel is restricted, European and Asian pharmaceutical companies will step in to fill the gap.

This is not speculation; it is already happening. The momentum in out-licensing deals has been extraordinary. Upfront payments in the first five months of 2026 reached USD 4,833 million, a 93% year-over-year increase, already representing approximately 68% of the full-year 2025 total. The number of deals rose 31% year-over-year. May 2026 alone saw USD 1,250 million in upfront payments, driven by major transactions between Hengrui and Bristol-Myers Squibb, and between Innovent and Pfizer. These are not small, exploratory deals; they are significant commercial commitments.

If US companies are forced to withdraw from this market, European and Japanese firms will absorb the best assets. The consequence for the US biotech industry would not be enhanced security; it would be reduced competitiveness. The US would lose its privileged access to the world's most productive drug discovery engine, while its global competitors would gain it. The market has not priced this second-order effect, perhaps because it is counterintuitive. But it is the logical outcome of the data.

## The Legislative Path Is Uncertain, and Industry Opposition Will Be Fierce and Effective

The market is treating the proposed legislation as a near-certainty, but the political reality is more complex. The Biotech Investment National Security Act was introduced in June 2026, but the legislative process for such bills is typically measured in years, not months. More importantly, the bill faces significant opposition from powerful industry stakeholders who understand the economic stakes.

The pharmaceutical industry in the United States is not monolithic in its view of Chinese biotech. While some voices frame Chinese companies as a competitive threat, many industry leaders recognize that access to Chinese innovation is essential for maintaining their own pipelines. The argument that restricting out-licensing would harm American patients by limiting access to life-saving drugs is not merely rhetorical; it is factually grounded. Moreover, the broader political environment in the United States includes strong pressure to reduce drug prices. Restricting access to lower-cost, high-quality Chinese R&D would increase the cost of drug development for US companies, which would ultimately be passed on to patients and payers. This creates a direct conflict between the national security framing of the legislation and the domestic political imperative of affordable healthcare.

The legislative process will also face procedural hurdles. The COINS Act framework requires coordination between the Treasury Department and the Department of Defense, and the inclusion of biotechnology would require new regulatory infrastructure. The Chairman of the Select Committee on China has been vocal, but the administration's priorities may not align with this specific agenda. The market is pricing a binary outcome: either the legislation passes or it does not. The more realistic scenario is a prolonged period of uncertainty, during which deal flow continues, perhaps with enhanced due diligence but without a complete shutdown.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Valuation Disconnect and the Timing of Re-Rating

The analysis convincingly demonstrates why the market has overreacted, but it leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is the question of timing. If the market has indeed overreacted, when will the re-rating occur? The report identifies the fundamental mispricing but does not provide a catalyst timeline. Is the re-rating contingent on a specific legislative outcome, such as the failure of BINSA to advance? Or could it occur gradually as deal flow data continues to surprise to the upside?

A second unresolved question concerns the valuation dispersion within the sector. The report provides coverage of fourteen companies with recommendations ranging from Buy to Hold, but it does not fully explore how the geopolitical risk premium is distributed across sub-sectors. Are CROs and CDMOs, which have already been affected by prior legislation such as the BIOSECURE Act, priced for a different risk scenario than biotech companies with direct out-licensing exposure? The differentiation matters for portfolio construction.

A third open question is the potential for unintended consequences. If the legislation does not pass but the threat remains, Chinese companies may preemptively diversify their partnership strategies, reducing their reliance on US partners even without a legal mandate. This would be a rational response to uncertainty, but it would permanently alter the structure of the global biotech ecosystem. The report implies that deal flow will continue, but it does not fully address whether the quality of deals or the terms might shift as Chinese companies gain bargaining power from multiple potential partners.

These are not weaknesses in the analysis; they are the natural boundaries of a research report that focuses on a specific thesis. But they represent the questions that thoughtful investors must answer for themselves.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the China Biotech Geopolitical Risk

For investors trying to translate this analysis into portfolio decisions, a structured framework is essential. The first decision is whether to treat the geopolitical risk as a binary event or a continuum. The evidence strongly supports the continuum view. Even if legislation passes, it is unlikely to be a complete prohibition; more likely, it will involve enhanced review processes for certain types of transactions. The market is pricing a binary worst-case scenario, which creates an asymmetry in risk and reward.

The second decision is about time horizon. The fundamental case for Chinese biotech rests on the innovation pipeline, which is measured in years, not months. The out-licensing data for 2026 is already demonstrating that the underlying commercial logic remains intact. For investors with a twelve-month or longer horizon, the current weakness represents an entry point. For those with shorter horizons, the lack of a near-term catalyst means that volatility may persist.

The third decision is about sub-sector exposure. Companies with direct out-licensing exposure to US partners face the most headline risk, but they also have the most upside if the legislation stalls. CROs and CDMOs, by contrast, have already been through a significant de-rating and may offer a more defensive profile. The report's coverage list includes both categories, and the appropriate allocation depends on an investor's conviction about the legislative outcome.

The fourth decision is about diversification. If the thesis is correct that deal flow will shift to European and Asian partners, then investors should consider whether their exposure to Chinese biotech is adequately hedged by positions in European pharmaceutical companies that are likely to be beneficiaries of any US withdrawal. This is a portfolio-level consideration that goes beyond individual stock selection.

# The U.S. Labor Market Is Reaccelerating, and That Changes the Fed's Calculus Entirely

The May employment report was not just a data point. It was a signal that the underlying trend in U.S. job growth has shifted decisively upward. Nonfarm payrolls rose 172,000, well above both consensus expectations and the consensus estimate. Prior months were revised higher. The three-month moving average of private payrolls climbed to its highest level since March 2024. This is not noise. This is a pattern.

For months, the dominant narrative among market participants was that the U.S. economy was gradually cooling, that the labor market was softening just enough to allow the Federal Reserve to begin easing policy later this year. That narrative is now under serious pressure. The May report suggests that the current policy stance is not restraining economic activity. If anything, the labor market is gaining momentum. And if the labor market is accelerating while inflation remains above target, the Fed's path forward becomes considerably more complicated.

The critical question for investors is not whether the May report was a one-off. It is whether this reacceleration is durable and what it implies for the inflation outlook, the Fed's reaction function, and the broader macro environment. The data from the report, combined with the inflation forecasts and trade policy developments, points toward a regime in which the Fed remains hawkish for longer, possibly through the end of the year, and where the risk of further tightening cannot be dismissed.

## Employment Growth Is Broadening, Not Just Concentrated in a Few Sectors

One of the most important features of the May report is the breadth of the job gains. The diffusion indices, which measure the share of industries adding workers, improved across the one-month, three-month, and six-month horizons. This is not a story of a single sector carrying the entire economy. It is a story of cyclical strength spreading through the labor market.

Leisure and hospitality posted an outsize gain of 70,000, partly boosted by temporary factors such as the World Cup. Local government employment rose by 55,000, pushing overall government hiring higher. These increases were only modestly offset by mean-reversion in trade and transportation and a 9,000 decline related to the Spirit Airlines bankruptcy. The net result is that the private sector is adding jobs at a pace that is inconsistent with a slowing economy.

The unemployment rate held steady at 4.3 percent on a rounded basis. More importantly, measures of job losses fell in May, reversing a concerning increase in April. This brings the household survey back in line with other layoff measures, including initial jobless claims and JOLTS layoffs. All of these indicators now point to a labor market that is resilient, not fragile.

The job-finding rate picked up modestly, though it remains lackluster by historical standards. This suggests that while workers are not finding new positions as quickly as they did during the tightest phases of the post-pandemic recovery, they are also not losing jobs at an alarming rate. The labor market is in a state of balance, but it is a balance that is tilting toward strength, not weakness.

Average hourly earnings rose 0.3 percent month-over-month, driving payroll income growth to 0.4 percent. This is a level of income growth that supports consumer spending, but it also reinforces the Fed's concern that wage pressures could feed into services inflation.

The so-what for investors is straightforward. A labor market that is reaccelerating removes the primary argument for rate cuts. The Fed can no longer point to softening labor conditions as a reason to ease. Instead, the data forces officials to focus on the inflation side of the dual mandate, where the news is not reassuring.

## Inflation Is Sticky, and Core PCE Is Likely Accelerating Even as CPI Slows

The May CPI print is expected to show a moderation in core inflation, to 0.183 percent month-over-month from 0.376 percent in April. This deceleration is largely driven by a temporary boost from rent-related technical adjustments that is now fading. But beneath the headline, the inflation picture remains stubborn.

Core goods inflation is expected to be slightly positive, at 0.03 percent month-over-month. Tech-related components continue to see price increases, and while tariff-related inflation is easing, it is being offset by other pressures. Vehicle prices remain stable, but the global chip shortage, energy shocks related to the Iran war, and higher metal prices are all pushing up core goods inflation.

Supercore services inflation, which the Fed watches closely, is expected to decelerate to 0.247 percent month-over-month from 0.454 percent in April. This moderation is driven by a pullback in lodging-away-from-home prices, which tend to mean-revert, and negative residual seasonality in other personal services. But airline fares remain strong due to higher jet fuel prices, and the broader trend in services inflation is not yet convincingly downward.

The real concern, however, lies in the PCE inflation data. Despite the benign CPI print, core PCE inflation is expected to accelerate to 0.327 percent month-over-month in May from 0.239 percent in April. This divergence is driven by strength in PCE-relevant PPI components, including portfolio management and investment advice services and airline fares. The year-over-year core PCE rate is projected to firm to 3.4 percent from 3.3 percent, significantly above the Fed's 2 percent target.

There is broader evidence that inflation pressures are broadening beyond energy. The ISM services prices paid index rose to its highest level since mid-2022. Firms are reporting rising costs for freight, packaging materials, food, fertilizers, construction materials, and labor. The Beige Book described price increases as moderate to strong, faster than the moderate pace reported in the prior period.

The so-what is that the Fed cannot declare victory on inflation. The path back to 2 percent is not assured, and the May data suggests that progress has stalled or even reversed in some measures. For a central bank that has been waiting for confirmation that inflation is sustainably moving lower, the May numbers do not provide it.

## Fed Officials Are Increasingly Hawkish, and One Has Already Advocated for a Rate Hike

The May employment report and the inflation outlook have shifted the tone of Fed communication. Dallas Fed President Logan was the most explicit, stating that current economic conditions suggest monetary policy is not restraining the economy and that she is increasingly concerned that higher interest rates could be necessary later this year. This is not a hypothetical. It is an unconditional argument for tightening.

Cleveland Fed President Hammack also struck a hawkish tone, noting that the May employment report confirms the labor market is roughly in balance. She stopped short of advocating for a hike, but her message was clear: keeping rates steady is reasonable for now, but the bar for cutting is high.

New York Fed President Williams remains a dovish outlier. He expressed little concern about persistent inflation and saw no obvious reason to change forward guidance. But his views are increasingly isolated within the committee.

Ahead of the June FOMC meeting, there are signs that Fed Chair Warsh may not submit his own forecast for the policy rate, consistent with his view that forward guidance is not useful. He has reportedly tapped advisors who have previously called for drastic changes in monetary policy operations. This suggests that the Fed under Warsh may be more willing to deviate from market expectations and to prioritize inflation control over growth support.

The so-what is that the Fed is not only unlikely to cut rates in the near term. The risk of a rate hike is real and should be taken seriously. Investors who have priced in a benign outcome may need to reassess.

## Trade Policy Is a Continuity Play, Not an Escalation, but the Inflation Impact Lingers

The Trump administration announced new Section 301 tariffs of 10 to 12.5 percent on 60 trade partners. The stated goal is to replicate the IEEPA tariff framework after Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24. The move is primarily about preserving continuity in the trade regime rather than escalating tensions.

The effective tariff rate estimate remains unchanged at 8 to 9 percent. The shift from country-specific IEEPA tariffs to universal Section 122 tariffs lowered the effective rate by about 1.5 percentage points. Full implementation of Section 301 tariffs would likely reverse most of that decline, lifting the effective rate to approximately 8.5 percent from the current 7.0 percent.

There are some de-escalatory elements. Section 232 tariffs on certain metal products were cut to 15 percent from 25 percent. The domestic content threshold for imported products to qualify as made entirely from U.S. metals is set to decline to 85 percent from 95 percent. And proposed additional tariffs on Brazil are expected to be nuanced, excluding beef, coffee, metals, and other goods.

But the broader trajectory of trade policy remains inflationary. The Section 301 investigations into structural excess capacity and production could result in additional stackable tariffs. The cumulative effect of existing and proposed tariffs is to raise input costs for U.S. businesses, which will eventually pass through to consumers.

The so-what is that trade policy is not the primary driver of inflation in the current cycle, but it is a persistent tailwind. It complicates the Fed's task by adding to goods price pressures at a time when services inflation is already sticky. For investors, the implication is that trade policy will continue to be a source of uncertainty and upward pressure on prices.

## The Report Leaves Open a Critical Question: How Much Momentum Is Real Versus Transitory?

The May employment report is consistent with a reaccelerating economy, but it also contains elements that may prove transitory. The outsize gain in leisure and hospitality, partly driven by the World Cup, is unlikely to be repeated. The strength in local government hiring may reflect catch-up spending rather than a sustained trend.

The question that the report does not fully answer is whether the underlying trend in private sector hiring is genuinely accelerating or whether the May data is an outlier. The diffusion indices have improved, but they are still below the levels seen during the peak of the recovery. The job-finding rate remains lackluster. Wage growth is decelerating faster than expected.

If the May report is the beginning of a new trend, then the Fed will need to respond with tighter policy. If it is a temporary bump, then the current stance may be appropriate. The data over the next two to three months will be decisive.

For investors, the key is to avoid anchoring on a single narrative. The range of outcomes is wide, and the risk of a policy error is elevated. The Fed could err by tightening too much and slowing the economy unnecessarily, or it could err by waiting too long and allowing inflation to become entrenched.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Next Phase

For institutional investors, the May employment report and the accompanying inflation data suggest three strategic considerations.

First, duration exposure should be managed with caution. If the Fed remains on hold or shifts toward tightening, long-dated bonds will come under pressure. The yield curve may steepen as the front end reprices to reflect a higher terminal rate.

Second, sector allocation should favor areas that benefit from a strong labor market and resilient consumer spending. Cyclical sectors, including industrials and financials, may outperform defensive sectors that rely on rate cuts for their valuation support.

Third, inflation hedges remain relevant. Even if core CPI moderates in May, the broader inflation picture is not improving. Commodities, inflation-linked bonds, and real assets should continue to have a role in portfolios.

The decision framework is not about predicting the exact path of the Fed. It is about positioning for a regime in which the tail risks are skewed toward higher rates and stickier inflation, not toward easing.

## The Full Picture Demands a Deeper Dive

The May employment report, the inflation outlook, the Fed's hawkish shift, and the trade policy developments all point in the same direction: the U.S. economy is not cooling as expected, and the policy response will be more restrictive than markets have priced.

But the data is nuanced. The divergence between CPI and PCE, the transitory factors in the jobs report, and the uncertainty around trade policy all require careful analysis. The charts and detailed forecasts in the original report provide the granularity needed to make informed decisions.

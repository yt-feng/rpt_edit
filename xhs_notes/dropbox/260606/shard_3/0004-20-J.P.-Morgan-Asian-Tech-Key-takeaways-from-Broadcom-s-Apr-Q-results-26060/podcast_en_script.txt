EN_A: So Broadcom has basically locked in all the top AI players. But what does this mean for other chip companies? Are they shut out?
EN_B: Actually, the report has a key twist. Management acknowledged that Google is likely to maintain some diversity of sources for its TPU and AI compute stack. That means other vendors like MediaTek, which is already collaborating with Google on TPU, and possibly Marvell, could get a piece of the pie.
EN_A: So that is a positive signal for MediaTek?
EN_B: Exactly. The report argues that large cloud providers will increasingly work with two to three back-end design service vendors as a new normal. MediaTek and Alchip, with their structurally lower gross margins, are seen as key beneficiaries as customers move toward semi-custom or full-custom models. But whether Broadcom actually loses meaningful share in the TPU program is something the report does not fully answer yet.
EN_A: What about AI orders and the supply chain? Anything new there?
EN_B: Broadcom disclosed AI semiconductor bookings of over 30 billion dollars in the quarter. Customers are pulling orders forward because of long lead times, not just for wafers and memory, but also for power readiness. Management said visibility now extends into 2028, and they are already working on 2028 and 2029. This matches our supply chain checks that three to five year long-term agreements are in place for key bottlenecks like substrates, HBM, and CCL.
EN_A: So supply constraints will persist for a while?
EN_B: Yes, the report believes key constraints could last through 2027 and potentially into 2028. Beneficiaries include TSMC, ASE, SK Hynix, Samsung, and others. But one open question is that leading-edge wafers are still on annual negotiation cycles, and how that plays out is not fully explored in the report.
EN_A: What about Broadcom’s non-AI business? Is it recovering?
EN_B: Non-AI semiconductor revenue came in at 4.2 billion dollars, up 6% year over year, with bookings above 6 billion. Management expects third quarter revenue to improve sequentially to about 4.5 billion, up 12% year over year. The drivers are a gradual recovery in auto and industrial, an AI crowding out effect that tightens mainstream capacity, and rising AI-adjacent demand like power management chips.
EN_A: So the non-AI side is also benefiting from AI spillover?
EN_B: Exactly. This means pricing power is improving, which benefits mature foundries, OSATs, and passive component vendors. But the report does not specify which sub-segments will take off first, so that remains an area to watch.

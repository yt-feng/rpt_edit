# Broadcom’s AI Revenue Will Exceed $100 Billion by 2027, But the Real Story Is How the Supply Chain Is Being Redrawn

The most important signal from Broadcom’s April-quarter results is not the staggering $10.8 billion in AI revenue, nor the guidance that second-half 2026 AI revenue will double the first half. The critical insight is that the AI semiconductor supply chain is undergoing a structural shift from single-source dependency to multi-vendor diversification, and this shift will reshape competitive dynamics for years. Broadcom’s management acknowledged what many in the industry had suspected: hyperscalers like Google are actively maintaining multiple design partners for their custom AI accelerators. This is not a peripheral development. It is the central strategic fact that investors must grapple with as they evaluate the semiconductor ecosystem through 2028.

The numbers are impressive on their own. Broadcom’s AI semiconductor revenue reached $10.8 billion in the second quarter of fiscal 2026, representing 143 percent year-over-year growth and 49 percent of total company revenue. Management’s guidance that second-half AI revenue will double the first half implies full-year fiscal 2026 AI semiconductor revenue of approximately $56 billion. The reiterated target of exceeding $100 billion in fiscal 2027, with further substantial growth into fiscal 2028, suggests a compound trajectory that few companies in any industry can match. But the revenue trajectory, while eye-catching, is not the analytical puzzle. The puzzle is how the supply chain will accommodate this growth, and which companies will capture the value.

The report identifies several multi-year, multi-gigawatt programs with major customers: Google across multiple generations of TPUs plus AI networking under long-term agreements, Anthropic with additional TPU-based compute beginning in 2027, OpenAI with production starting in late 2026 and contractual deployments in 2027, and Meta with its MTIA XPU partnership and an initial one-gigawatt order beginning deliveries in the second half of 2027. Two additional customers, identified through supply chain checks as ByteDance and SoftBank or ARM, are expected to begin shipments in late 2026 with acceleration into 2027, with purchase orders to date cited at approximately $6 billion. This is a customer base that is both concentrated and expanding, and the expansion itself creates strategic complexity.

The tension between Broadcom’s dominant position and the hyperscaler desire for supply diversification is the central unresolved question of this earnings cycle. And it is precisely this tension that makes the report’s analysis so valuable for strategic decision-making.

## The Hyperscaler Drive for Supply Diversification Is Not a Threat to Broadcom’s Absolute Growth, But It Redefines the Value Chain

Broadcom’s management acknowledged that Google, despite a very substantial long-term commitment under their agreement, is likely to maintain some diversity of sources for its TPU and AI compute stack. This acknowledgment is significant because it confirms a pattern that supply chain analysts have observed for the past eighteen months: hyperscalers are moving from single-vendor dependency to a two- or three-vendor model for back-end design services. The report identifies MediaTek’s collaboration with Google on TPU generations v8t and v9, as well as the potential for Marvell to cooperate with Google on an LPU-like chip, as concrete evidence of this diversification in action.

The implications for the value chain are profound. When hyperscalers engage multiple design service vendors, the competitive dynamics shift from a winner-take-most model to a share-game model. Broadcom will still capture significant absolute revenue growth as the overall pie expands from $56 billion in fiscal 2026 to over $100 billion in fiscal 2027. But the marginal dollar of growth may be distributed differently than the initial dollar. The report observes that Broadcom’s lack of numerical upside to 2027 guidance, combined with rising TPU estimates and MediaTek pulling in its ASIC guidance by a year, is likely to be interpreted as a concession of meaningful share loss within the TPU program.

This is not a bearish call on Broadcom. It is a call on the structure of the industry. The hyperscalers are not reducing their total AI investment; they are reallocating it across a broader set of partners. The strategic question for investors is not whether Broadcom will grow, but whether the growth will be linear or whether the margin structure will compress as the supply chain fragments.

## The Shift Toward Multiple Design Service Vendors Creates Structural Beneficiaries in the Mid-Margin Segment of the Value Chain

The report argues that it could become the norm for large cloud service providers to engage two to three back-end design service vendors, driven by two forces. First, the product set is broadening beyond AI accelerators to include AI CPUs, LPU-like chips, and specialized inference processors. No single vendor can be best-in-class across all these product types, and hyperscalers will naturally seek specialization. Second, customers are experimenting with different commercial structures as their front-end design capabilities mature. Some are moving toward a semi-custom or CoT model, where the hyperscaler handles more of the architecture and the design vendor focuses on implementation and manufacturing interface.

This structural shift has clear implications for which companies will benefit. The report identifies MediaTek and Alchip as key potential beneficiaries. The logic is straightforward: as hyperscalers move toward CoT or semi-CoT models, they will favor vendors with structurally lower margin profiles. MediaTek’s gross margins in the low 40 percent range and Alchip’s in the mid-teens stand in stark contrast to turnkey service vendors with front-end design capabilities that command gross margins above 60 percent. The hyperscalers are sophisticated buyers. They understand that paying a 60 percent gross margin for a full turnkey service when they increasingly handle their own architecture is inefficient. They will push for lower-cost partners.

This is a classic value chain disintermediation pattern. When the customer becomes more capable at the high-value portion of the work, the supplier’s value proposition shifts from full-service integration to cost-efficient execution. The winners are the suppliers who can execute reliably at scale with a lower cost structure. The report’s identification of MediaTek and Alchip as beneficiaries is not just a stock call; it is a framework for understanding how the industry will evolve as hyperscaler in-house capabilities mature.

## Supply Constraints Will Persist Through 2027 and Potentially Into 2028, Creating Pricing Power for Bottleneck Providers

Broadcom disclosed AI semiconductor bookings of over $30 billion in the quarter, noting that customers are pulling orders forward due to long lead times. The lead times are not just for wafers and memory, but critically for power readiness. Management stated that visibility now extends into 2028, and that Broadcom is comfortable securing supply for 2026 and 2027 and is already working on 2028 and 2029.

This visibility is consistent with the report’s Asia supply chain checks, which indicate that three- to five-year long-term agreements are increasingly in place across key bottlenecks such as substrates, high-bandwidth memory, and copper-clad laminate. Leading-edge wafers remain on annual negotiation cycles, which means that wafer pricing is still subject to annual renegotiation. But the longer-term agreements for substrates and packaging materials create a structural floor under costs.

The report identifies TSMC, ASE, SK Hynix, Samsung, Unimicron, Ibiden, and EMC as key beneficiaries of this supply-constrained environment. The logic is that when customers are willing to sign three- to five-year agreements, the bottleneck providers have pricing power and visibility that allows them to invest in capacity with confidence. This is a virtuous cycle for the suppliers and a constraint for the customers.

The strategic implication for investors is that supply chain bottlenecks are not a temporary phenomenon that will resolve in 2027. They are a structural feature of the AI buildout that will persist as long as demand continues to grow at these rates. The report’s finding that Broadcom is already working on 2028 and 2029 supply suggests that the bottleneck problem is not getting solved; it is being managed through longer planning horizons and larger prepayments.

## Networking Has Become the Hidden Engine of AI Revenue Growth, But Its Contribution Will Normalize

One of the most striking data points in the report is that networking and interconnect now represents nearly 40 percent of Broadcom’s AI revenue. This reflects strong attach rates for switching, SerDes, and connectivity content across both custom XPU and non-XPU products. Management suggested that this 40 percent mix is likely elevated and could normalize closer to 30 percent over time as XPUs ramp faster in the next few quarters.

The normalization is important for two reasons. First, it provides a more accurate baseline for modeling Broadcom’s revenue composition. If networking settles at 30 percent of AI revenue, then the XPU business is even larger than the headline numbers suggest. Second, it highlights the growing importance of networking as an independent investment theme within AI infrastructure. The report notes that networking and interconnect are key themes at Computex Taipei, supported by strong demand for high-bandwidth data transmission as agentic AI drives larger scale-up and scale-out domains.

Broadcom’s product positioning in networking is formidable. The company has shipped over one year of volume on the 100-terabit Ethernet switch, the Tomahawk 6, and is planning to tape out the next-generation 200-terabit switch this quarter. The company is also building optics and co-packaged optics building blocks, including 1.6T DSPs and CW and EML lasers, positioning itself as the industry standard.

But the report also notes a tension. While CPO is a key long-term topic as copper approaches its limits, Broadcom appears to be more conservative on the CPO ramp, aligned with the ramp schedule for ASIC customers. The implication is that CPO adoption may be two years later for Broadcom’s custom ASIC customers than for Nvidia’s merchant silicon customers. This timing gap creates an analytical question: will the copper-based networking solutions continue to meet bandwidth requirements through 2028, or will the CPO ramp need to accelerate faster than Broadcom currently expects?

## The Report Leaves an Unanswered Question About How the Non-AI Recovery Interacts With AI-Driven Capacity Constraints

The report notes that non-AI semiconductor revenue reached $4.2 billion, up 6 percent year-over-year, with bookings above $6 billion as early signs of a cyclical recovery. Management guided for third-quarter non-AI semiconductor revenue to improve sequentially to approximately $4.5 billion, representing 12 percent year-over-year growth. The supply chain checks support this recovery, with momentum driven by a gradual recovery in auto and industrial, an AI crowd-out effect that is tightening capacity in mainstream, and rising AI-adjacent demand for components like power management ICs.

The crowd-out effect is analytically interesting because it creates a feedback loop. As AI demand consumes more leading-edge capacity, mainstream capacity becomes tighter, which supports pricing for mature foundries, OSATs, and passive component vendors. The report identifies Vanguard International Semiconductor, ASE, YAGEO, and Murata as beneficiaries of this dynamic.

But the report does not fully answer the question of how this dynamic will evolve. If AI demand continues to grow at the rates implied by Broadcom’s guidance, will the crowd-out effect intensify to the point where it creates supply shortages in automotive and industrial end markets? Or will the capacity additions from TSMC and other foundries be sufficient to meet both AI and non-AI demand? The report provides the data points but leaves the synthesis to the reader. This is a deliberate analytical gap that invites deeper investigation.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Evaluating the AI Semiconductor Supply Chain

For investors trying to translate this report into actionable investment decisions, the following framework may be helpful. The AI semiconductor supply chain can be divided into three layers: design service providers, manufacturing bottleneck providers, and component suppliers. Each layer has a different set of competitive dynamics and a different exposure to the diversification trend.

At the design service layer, the key question is whether a company is positioned as a full-turnkey provider with high margins or as a cost-efficient execution partner with lower margins. The hyperscaler trend toward in-house architecture favors the latter. Companies with gross margins in the 40 percent range or below are better positioned to gain share as hyperscalers push for lower-cost partners. Companies with gross margins above 60 percent face the risk that hyperscalers will internalize more of the front-end design work.

At the manufacturing bottleneck layer, the key question is whether a company has pricing power and visibility from long-term agreements. The report’s finding that three- to five-year LTAs are increasingly in place for substrates, HBM, and CCL suggests that companies in these segments have structural advantages. The risk is that capacity additions eventually catch up with demand, but the report’s data suggests that timing is likely 2028 or later.

At the component supplier layer, the key question is whether a company benefits from the AI crowd-out effect in mainstream capacity. The recovery in non-AI semiconductor revenue, combined with tightening mainstream capacity, creates a favorable pricing environment for mature foundries, OSATs, and passive components. The risk is that the non-AI recovery proves more gradual than expected, which would reduce the crowd-out benefit.

This framework is not a recommendation. It is a set of analytical lenses that investors can apply to their own research. The report provides the data; the framework provides the structure for interpretation.

# Gold’s New Problem Is Not Inflation Alone — It Is the Collision of Persistent Price Pressures and Responsible Monetary Policy

Gold has historically been viewed as a hedge against inflation and a safe haven during uncertainty. But the precious metal now faces a more complex challenge. Over the past ten trading days, gold prices have diverged from their traditional negative correlation with oil, even as Brent crude fell below USD 100 per barrel. This decoupling signals that the macro environment for gold has shifted fundamentally. The divergence is not an anomaly to be dismissed; it is the symptom of a structural tension between persistent inflation, rising real interest rates, and a coordinated global bond market sell-off that leaves gold caught in a crossfire it cannot easily escape.

The core argument is this: gold’s new problem is the simultaneous presence of sticky inflation and credible, responsible monetary policy responses. Either factor alone might be manageable, but together they create a powerful headwind. If inflation remains elevated, central banks will keep real rates high. If monetary policy remains hawkish, gold’s opportunity cost rises. The only relief for gold would come from a breakdown in one of these two pillars — either inflation subsiding faster than expected, or policy makers signaling a less aggressive stance. Until then, gold is trapped in a macro vice.

This is not a repeat of 2022. Then, a 270-basis-point repricing in real rates was neutralized by a massive 716-tonne swing in official sector gold demand. Today, the repricing is only 48 basis points year-to-date, but the offsetting demand from central banks and bar-and-coin investors, while strong, may not be sufficient to fill the gap left by collapsing ETF demand. The structure of the market has changed, and the price implications are not yet fully priced in.

## The Bond Market Sell-Off Is a Global Synchronized Event, Not an Idiosyncratic Shock, and That Makes Gold’s Headwinds More Persistent

The sell-off in government bond markets since the 10-year UST yield broke above 4.5% has been significant enough to rank as a discussion topic at the G-7 finance ministers meeting in Paris. This is not a US-only story. In the same week, yields rose by 21 basis points in the US, 24 basis points in the UK, and 23 basis points in Japan. The compounding effect across the three largest government bond markets suggests a shared macro driver rather than country-specific factors.

From Japan, the concerns center on the need for a supplementary budget including energy subsidies, following repeated official denials that such a budget was necessary. The market reacted to the credibility gap. From the UK, gilt market weakness followed local election results that raised the possibility of a leadership challenge and subsequent fiscal easing. From the US, rates strategists see a string of shocks that have left real rates too low to return inflation to target, and they remain short 10-year UST with upside risk to their 4.65% yield target. The risk of a 10-year term premium of 135 basis points implies a 5% yield on the 10-year UST.

The implication for gold is direct. Real yields are the transmission mechanism into gold’s financial fair value. When real yields rise globally, gold’s attractiveness as a non-yielding asset diminishes. The synchronized nature of this sell-off means that gold cannot find refuge in one geography while another adjusts. The headwind is truly global.

## The Link Between Real Rates and Gold Is Weaker Than It Appears Because Official Sector Demand Has Proven Resilient, but That Resilience Has Limits

It would be a mistake to extrapolate the recent 48-basis-point repricing in real rates into a linear bearish case for gold. In 2022, real rates repriced by 270 basis points, yet gold prices were supported by an extraordinary 716-tonne swing in official sector demand between the second half of 2021 and the second half of 2022. Central banks, particularly in emerging markets, stepped in as buyers of last resort, absorbing supply that would otherwise have depressed prices.

Today, central bank demand remains strong. First-quarter data showed official sector purchases above expectations, driven in part by Chinese investment demand. Bar and coin demand was 38% above last year’s running rate. This inelastic demand is stealing supply away from more elastic sources, which provides a floor under gold prices.

However, the contraction in ETF demand is the most significant change in the demand structure. ETF demand in the first quarter was 62 tonnes, down 78% from last year’s quarterly running rate. To realize the full-year assumption of around 450 tonnes of ETF demand, a meaningful resumption of inflows is needed. Without it, the demand picture becomes increasingly reliant on central banks and retail investors, which may not be sufficient to sustain prices at current levels. The 2023 analogy is instructive: even in a weak year for total demand, gold prices rose modestly. But that was a different macro regime, and the risk of extrapolating that pattern into 2026 is that the underlying conditions have deteriorated.

## The Most Important Unknown Is Whether Incoming Fed Chair Warsh Can Shift the Center of Committee Opinion — and What That Would Mean for Gold

The first Federal Reserve meeting chaired by Kevin Warsh, scheduled for June 17-18, represents a pivotal moment for gold. Warsh has previously indicated in a Wall Street Journal op-ed that the Fed should discard its stagflation forecast, that AI will be a significant disinflationary force through productivity gains, and that a reduced Fed balance sheet can be accompanied by lower policy rates. If he can steer the center of FOMC opinion from the “somewhat hawkish” characterization seen in mid-May, this could prove gold-positive.

The mechanism is not straightforward. A shift in rhetoric could move rate expectations without necessarily changing the underlying economic reality. If the Fed signals a willingness to look through near-term inflation pressures, the market might reprice rate expectations lower, reducing the opportunity cost of holding gold. This could meaningfully change the pace of ETF investment, which has flagged significantly from 2025 levels.

But there are powerful counterforces. St. Louis Fed President Musalem has cautioned that Fed policy is below neutral and that the US is unlikely to be in a high-productivity-growth period. The ECB’s April minutes indicated that a “looking through” approach without monetary policy action is increasingly unlikely to be appropriate. And market pricing still implies a 58% chance of a Fed hike by December. Warsh’s ability to shift consensus is far from assured, and the risk is that any dovish pivot is met with skepticism from both markets and other committee members.

The open question is whether Warsh can build a coalition for a more accommodative stance in the face of persistent inflation data. If he succeeds, gold could see a relief rally. If he fails, the current headwinds will intensify.

## The Supply Side Adds a Bearish Risk That Is Often Overlooked: Recycled Gold Supply Could Accelerate as Capacity Bottlenecks Ease

Most analysis of gold focuses on demand, but supply dynamics are equally important. Mined production is relatively inelastic in the short term, but recycled gold supply can respond quickly to price changes and financial stress. In recent years, recycled supply has been constrained by extrapolative price expectations — sellers waiting for higher prices — and a relative lack of household financial stress compared to the post-global-financial-crisis period.

That may be changing. First-quarter recycled gold volume was 366 tonnes, modestly higher than previous quarters. Over the balance of the year, bottlenecks in refining and recycling capacity are expected to ease, according to the World Gold Council. At the same time, weaker domestic currencies in some markets could drive distress selling. Our full-year assumption for recycled supply is 1,470 tonnes, but the upside risk is significant.

If recycled supply accelerates, it would add to the available metal in the market at a time when ETF demand is weak. This could create a surplus that pressures prices, particularly if central bank buying moderates. The supply-side risk is not yet fully priced into the market, and it represents a bearish factor that could compound the demand-side headwinds.

## A Decision Framework for Gold Investors: Three Scenarios and the Signals That Matter

For investors trying to navigate the current environment, a structured framework is essential. Three scenarios capture the range of outcomes, each with distinct implications for gold.

**Scenario One: Persistent Inflation, Hawkish Policy.** This is the base case implied by current market pricing. Inflation remains sticky due to factors beyond oil — including trend inflation measures, an elevated output gap, AI-driven demand in certain goods categories, and pipeline inflation. The Fed maintains a hawkish stance, real rates rise further, and ETF demand continues to contract. In this scenario, gold prices face sustained downward pressure, with support only from central bank and retail demand. The key signal to watch is the June FOMC meeting and whether Warsh can shift the narrative.

**Scenario Two: Inflation Moderates, Policy Eases.** If inflation data surprises to the downside, or if the Fed signals a willingness to look through near-term pressures, real rates could decline. This would reduce the opportunity cost of holding gold and could trigger a resumption of ETF inflows. Central bank demand would likely remain supportive, and gold could rally. The key signal is a sustained decline in core inflation measures or a dovish pivot from Warsh.

**Scenario Three: Stagflation or Supply Shock.** If inflation remains elevated while growth slows, the Fed faces a difficult trade-off. In this scenario, gold could benefit from its dual role as an inflation hedge and a safe haven. However, the risk is that the Fed prioritizes inflation fighting over growth, keeping real rates high. The key signal is a divergence between inflation and growth data, or a geopolitical event that disrupts supply chains.

Each scenario has clear implications for portfolio positioning. The current environment favors a cautious stance on gold, with the recognition that the downside is limited by structural demand from central banks and retail investors. The upside is contingent on a policy pivot that is not yet visible in the data.

# China’s Telcos Are No Longer Just Pipe Operators: The AI Token Plan Marks a Strategic Pivot from Connectivity to Compute

The most significant strategic development in China’s telecommunications sector this quarter is not the pace of 5G base station deployment, nor the trajectory of fiber cable exports. It is the quiet but deliberate launch of AI token plans by China Mobile, China Unicom, and China Telecom. These three operators have begun charging both consumers and enterprises for artificial intelligence compute tokens, effectively repositioning themselves from providers of passive connectivity into active platforms for AI inference. This is not a minor pricing experiment. It is a structural shift in how telcos intend to monetize their networks, and it carries profound implications for revenue models, capital allocation, and competitive dynamics across the broader technology ecosystem.

The timing is instructive. As the report shows, 5G base station deployment in China is decelerating. New additions in April 2026 stood at 51,000 units, down from 65,000 in April 2025. Accumulated additions in the first four months of 2026 reached 171,000 units, tracking at 32% of the full-year estimate—identical to the tracking rate in the same period of 2025, but on a lower absolute base. The era of rapid 5G infrastructure buildout is giving way to a period of normalization. Meanwhile, 5G subscriber growth is also slowing: 8 million net additions in April 2026 versus 19 million in April 2025. The low-hanging fruit of subscriber acquisition has been largely harvested, with penetration rates at China Mobile and China Unicom reaching 66% and 71% respectively by the end of the first quarter. Against this backdrop, the AI token plan is not merely opportunistic. It is a necessary hedge against the exhaustion of traditional growth levers.

The argument that follows is not about whether AI tokens will succeed in the near term. It is about why this move fundamentally changes the investment thesis for China’s telcos, and why the market has not yet fully priced in the second-order consequences.

## The AI Token Plan Transforms Telcos from Cost-Centric Infrastructure Plays into Revenue-Agnostic Compute Platforms

The core insight is simple but powerful: telcos are now selling access to intelligence, not just access to bandwidth. The pricing structures unveiled by the three operators reveal a deliberate attempt to create a new consumption-based revenue stream that sits alongside—and potentially above—traditional data and voice plans. China Mobile is pricing tokens at roughly 1 renminbi per 400,000 tokens, with package options ranging from 5.99 to 24.99 renminbi. China Unicom is offering a personal version starting at 15 renminbi per month and an enterprise version starting at 198 renminbi per month. China Telecom has the most granular tiering, with consumer packages from 9.9 to 49.9 renminbi monthly and enterprise packages from 39.9 to 299.9 renminbi monthly.

What matters here is not the absolute price points, which are modest by global standards. What matters is the architecture. By charging per token or per subscription, telcos are creating a direct linkage between AI usage intensity and revenue. This is fundamentally different from the traditional data plan model, where revenue is capped by data volume and speed tiers, and where incremental AI usage does not generate incremental revenue if it falls within existing data limits. The AI token plan effectively unbundles AI compute from general data consumption, allowing telcos to capture value from the specific, high-value use case of AI inference.

This shift has two immediate strategic consequences. First, it changes the marginal revenue calculus for network investment. When every AI query generates token revenue, the business case for deploying more compute capacity at the edge or within the network improves dramatically. Second, it creates a natural cross-sell opportunity: telcos can bundle AI tokens with existing 5G plans, cloud services, and enterprise solutions, increasing customer stickiness and average revenue per user without needing to raise headline data prices.

## The Deceleration in 5G Infrastructure Deployment Creates the Strategic Opening for Telcos to Monetize Existing Capacity

The report’s data on 5G base station deployment is instructive not because it signals weakness, but because it reveals a mature network that is ready for higher-layer monetization. Total accumulated 5G base stations in China reached 5.0 million units as of April 2026. The annual deployment forecast for 2026 is 540,000 units, down from 588,000 in 2025, and the trend is expected to continue declining to 500,000 units by 2027. This is not a failure of demand. It is a reflection of the fact that China’s 5G network is approaching geographic and population coverage saturation.

For telcos, this is a strategic inflection point. The heavy capital expenditure phase—which compressed free cash flow and pressured return on invested capital—is receding. Operating expenditure for network maintenance is stabilizing. The marginal cost of carrying additional AI traffic on an already-built 5G network is low. The AI token plan is therefore an elegant way to convert fixed network assets into variable revenue streams. Every token consumed represents a higher economic return on the sunk cost of base station deployment.

Furthermore, the export data in the report reinforces the global competitiveness of China’s telecom supply chain. Base station export volumes, while down 9% year-on-year in April 2026, remain elevated. Fiber cable export volumes grew 9% year-on-year in the same month, and fiber cable export value surged 287% in US dollar terms. Copper clad laminate export prices rose 67% year-on-year. These figures suggest that overseas demand for Chinese telecom infrastructure components is robust, even as domestic deployment moderates. The implication is that Chinese telcos and their suppliers are not facing a demand cliff, but rather a shift from domestic volume growth to international value growth and domestic service monetization.

## The Real Strategic Question Is Not Whether AI Tokens Will Work, but Which Telco Has the Best Network and Distribution Architecture to Scale Them

The report provides pricing data but does not fully address the competitive differentiation among the three operators. This is the section where the analysis must go beyond the data and into strategic reasoning. The critical variable is not the price per token, which is likely to converge across operators over time. It is the quality of the AI inference experience, which depends on network latency, edge compute capability, and the integration of AI models into the telco’s broader service stack.

China Mobile, with the largest 5G subscriber base at 668 million accumulated users by end of first quarter, has the most extensive distribution network and the largest potential addressable market for consumer AI tokens. But scale alone is not sufficient. China Telecom, with its more granular enterprise tiering structure, appears to be targeting the business segment more aggressively, which could yield higher average revenue per user and longer contract durations. China Unicom, with its coding and token plan discounts, is positioning itself as the developer-friendly option, which could be a smart play for capturing the upstream AI application layer.

The unresolved question is whether telcos can execute on the user experience side. AI token plans require seamless integration with AI applications, which in turn require partnerships with model providers, software developers, and cloud platforms. Telcos that treat AI tokens as a simple billing add-on will struggle to differentiate. Telcos that build an ecosystem—offering not just tokens but also curated AI applications, developer tools, and enterprise consulting—will capture disproportionate value. The report does not provide data on partnership structures or ecosystem investments, which is precisely the gap that investors need to monitor.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating Telco AI Monetization: Four Questions Every Investor Should Ask

To translate the report’s insights into actionable analysis, investors should apply a structured framework that goes beyond headline pricing and deployment numbers. The framework consists of four questions, each targeting a distinct dimension of the AI token opportunity.

First, what is the token consumption elasticity relative to data consumption? If AI token usage grows faster than overall data traffic, telcos will see revenue acceleration without proportional cost increases. If token consumption merely cannibalizes existing data plan revenue, the net effect could be neutral or negative. The report does not provide usage data, but initial pricing suggests telcos are trying to avoid cannibalization by positioning tokens as a separate, premium service.

Second, what is the competitive response from over-the-top players and cloud providers? Alibaba, Tencent, and Baidu all have their own AI services and cloud platforms. They could choose to bypass telco token plans entirely by offering AI inference directly to consumers and enterprises. Telcos’ advantage lies in network proximity: AI inference performed at the edge of the telco network can achieve lower latency than cloud-based inference. But this advantage is only meaningful for latency-sensitive applications such as real-time translation, autonomous driving, and interactive gaming. For batch processing and non-real-time inference, cloud providers may have the cost advantage.

Third, how will enterprise adoption evolve? The enterprise pricing tiers, ranging from 39.9 to 299.9 renminbi per month, are low enough to encourage experimentation but high enough to require budget approval. The real test will come when enterprises start scaling usage beyond pilot projects. If enterprise token consumption grows linearly with employee headcount or customer base, the revenue potential is substantial. If it remains confined to specific use cases, the addressable market will be limited.

Fourth, what is the regulatory posture toward AI monetization by telcos? Chinese regulators have historically encouraged telcos to innovate and increase average revenue per user, but they have also imposed price caps and service obligations. The AI token plan operates in a regulatory gray area: it is not explicitly subject to the same pricing regulations as basic telecom services, but that could change if consumer complaints or competitive concerns arise. Investors should watch for any regulatory guidance on AI token pricing, data privacy, and interoperability.

The report provides the baseline data; the second-order implications require interpretation.

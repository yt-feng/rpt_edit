# The Treasury Term Premium Compression Is Not a Signal to Chase the Rally — It Is a Warning That the Easy Money Has Been Made

The recent decline in long-end US Treasury yields has been driven almost entirely by a compression in term premium, not by a repricing of the Federal Reserve's policy path. This distinction is not academic — it is the single most important analytical fact for any rates investor today. The market has already priced in a reduction in geopolitical uncertainty and a restoration of duration's portfolio value. What it has not priced in is a shift in the distribution of risks around the Fed's next moves. That means the rally that has already occurred is fragile, and the next leg lower in yields will require a fundamentally different catalyst than the one that powered the move so far.

The relief in long-end yields has been substantial. Since the mid-month peak, 10-year Treasury yields have fallen steadily as optimism about a potential resolution to the conflict in the Middle East has rebuilt confidence in shipping flows through the Strait of Hormuz. But when we decompose the move into its two components — term premium and rate expectations — the picture is clear: the compression in term premium has done almost all the work. The expectations component of yields has contributed only modestly. This is not a rally driven by a dovish repricing of the Fed. It is a rally driven by the market deciding that the tail risks it was pricing a few weeks ago are no longer as threatening.

The implications are straightforward but often overlooked. If the rally had been driven by a change in rate expectations — for example, the market pricing in a higher probability of cuts or a lower terminal rate — then the move would have a different character altogether. It would be more durable, because it would be anchored to the central bank's reaction function. Instead, the compression in term premium is a one-time adjustment. Once the uncertainty premium is removed, there is no second layer of premium to compress unless a new source of uncertainty emerges. The market has effectively taken the "fear premium" out of long-end yields. That is a finite resource.

The question every investor should now ask is: what happens next? If the rally is to extend meaningfully from here, it will need to be led by a shift in the policy expectations component. That means either weaker macroeconomic data or a more dovish Fed. The labor market report due next week is the first major test. A benign report — one that shows continued slack and contained wage pressures — could support nearer-term forwards and steepen the curve. But a strong report that reignites inflation concerns would reverse the term premium compression and send yields higher. The two-way risk is real, and the market is not positioned for a binary outcome.

## The rally in US Treasuries has been a term premium story, not a policy story, and the next leg lower requires a fundamentally different engine

The decomposition of the yield move into term premium and rate expectations is not just an analytical curiosity. It is the key to understanding which parts of the curve will lead the next phase of the move. If the market is going to price in a more dovish Fed, the front end of the curve will have to move. So far, the front end has lagged. Hawkish Fed communication has reinforced the curve flattening, but it has also kept short-term yields anchored. The result is a curve that has flattened steadily even as outright yields have fallen.

This dynamic creates a strategic opportunity. If the next catalyst is dovish data, the front end will catch up, and the curve will steepen. If the next catalyst is renewed geopolitical tension, the long end will sell off again, and the curve will flatten further. The asymmetry favors being positioned for a steepening, but with a careful eye on the data. The 5-year point of the curve is the natural battleground. It is long enough to capture the term premium compression that has already occurred, but short enough to benefit from a shift in rate expectations. Tethering a long bias to the 5-year part of the curve is the most sensible way to express a view that the next move will be driven by policy, not by fear.

## Rates volatility has collapsed back toward the bottom of fair value, making this the right moment to consider hedges rather than chase the compression

The decline in implied volatility has been as striking as the decline in yields. After the most recent spike, implied vol on longer tails has fallen back toward the lows seen before the conflict escalated. The overall level of vol is now at the bottom of the fair value model range. This is not a signal that volatility is cheap in some absolute sense. It is a signal that the market has priced out a significant amount of tail risk, and that the remaining uncertainty is being discounted at a very low premium.

When volatility is this compressed, the natural instinct is to assume that the calm will persist. That is precisely when the risk of a reversal is highest. The rates market is currently balancing three forces that are in tension with each other: a hawkish Fed tone, optimistic growth pricing, and increased hope about a resolution to the conflict. This is not a stable equilibrium. Any one of these forces could shift abruptly, and the volatility that follows will be amplified by the fact that the market is so complacent.

The right response is not to chase the rally further. It is to use the low vol environment to put on hedges. The report suggests two specific hedge structures that are worth considering. For investors who are worried about deeper downside risks — a recession or a sharp slowdown — the recommendation is to buy modestly out-of-the-money receivers on short-to-medium tails, specifically the 2-to-5 year part of the curve, with longer expiries such as one year rather than three months. This structure protects against a scenario where the front end reprices dovish and yields collapse. For investors who are worried about renewed escalation — a reversal of the geopolitical relief — the recommendation is to buy short-expiry at-the-money payers on longer tails, specifically the 10-year point. This structure protects against a scenario where term premium re-expands and long-end yields spike.

The key insight is that both hedges are cheap right now. Volatility is low, and the market is not pricing in either tail scenario with any conviction. That is exactly when hedges are most valuable. Waiting until the volatility spike is underway means paying a much higher premium. The time to hedge is when nobody thinks you need one.

## European duration remains attractive despite the rally, because the combination of a hawkish ECB, commodity relief, and weak activity data is a structural tailwind

The European rates story is different from the US story in one crucial respect: the ECB is actually tightening, not just talking about it. The combination of a high likelihood of a June hike, ongoing weakness in activity surveys, and the cap on inflation risks from the prospect of a Middle East deal is a powerful tailwind for European duration. The report recommends staying long 5-year forward 5-year EUR real yields, and that recommendation still makes sense even after the rally.

The outperformance of EUR rates relative to US rates since mid-April has been driven by a divergence in activity signals. The US economy has been surprisingly resilient, while Europe has been stuck in a soft patch. That divergence is likely to persist, and it supports wider cross-market spreads. The front end of the European curve will remain sensitive to news on energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz, but the structural case for European duration is intact as long as the ECB remains on a tightening path and activity data remains weak.

The sovereign spread compression in Europe has been impressive, but it has run ahead of the relief in core rates and rates volatility. The retracement of post-conflict widening has been large, and the share of the retracement decreases with credit risk. This means that the highest-quality sovereigns — such as France and the EU — have seen the most compression, while the lower-quality sovereigns — such as Italy — have seen less. The hurdle for further tightening is now high, but sovereign credit still offers a reasonable carry trade as long as the Euro area remains a receiver rather than a provider of bearish shocks. Domestic politics and fiscal policy are unlikely to be material headwinds in the near term.

## The UK gilt market has already repriced term premium, and the next move will be front-end-led as macro data softens and political risks persist

The gilt market has had a strong week, driven by the same global relief that has lifted Treasuries and European bonds. But the UK story has its own dynamics. The bull-flattening of the gilt curve is consistent with term premium compression, and our estimates show a sharp fall in term premium after the rise in early May. Gilts have also outperformed swaps, which points to a relaxation of supply-related risks. But the report argues that durable relief for gilts will have to be front-end-led.

The logic is straightforward. Macro data in the UK continues to soften, which should eventually force the Bank of England to adopt a more dovish stance. The fiscal situation and political risks — including the upcoming by-election — are likely to remain in focus over the coming weeks and months. These factors are headwinds for the long end, but tailwinds for the front end. The recommendation is to use 1-year forward 2-year to 10-year GBP OIS steepeners, with a target of 55 basis points and a stop at 25 basis points. The negative carry on this trade is not particularly punitive, and the asymmetric payoff is attractive.

The key question for gilt investors is whether the term premium compression has run its course. The report suggests that it has. If that is correct, then the next move in gilts will be driven by the front end, and the curve will steepen. Investors who are positioned for further flattening are swimming against the tide.

## Japan remains the outlier: domestic factors, not global relief, will determine the direction of JGB yields

The Japanese rates story is the most idiosyncratic of all the major markets. The market is pricing over a 70 percent probability of a Bank of Japan hike in June, but our economists think a July hike is more likely. Governor Ueda's speech on June 3 will be a critical signal. His recent comments — that the BOJ would conduct policy for "stable inflation" and is more attuned to supply risks at the long end — supported long-end outperformance. But the relief from the potential US-Iran agreement has been limited. The JGB curve remains steep, and the repricing in yields over May was concentrated in forwards further out, with very little movement in the front end.

This is a bear steepening regime, and bear steepening regimes are inherently unstable. The report argues that domestic factors are the primary driver of higher risk premium in Japan, and it will take more than global rates relief to bring lasting stability. The 5-year point of the JGB curve is likely to bear a greater share of bearish pressure over time. Fiscal news in June, such as the details of the supplementary budget, could raise risk premium further.

The unresolved question for Japan is whether the BOJ will actually deliver the hike that the market is pricing. If the BOJ disappoints, the front end could rally sharply. If the BOJ delivers, the curve could steepen further. The asymmetry favors being positioned for a steepening, but the timing is uncertain.

## What the report does not fully answer: the interplay between fiscal dominance and term premium in a world where central banks are still shrinking balance sheets

The report provides a detailed decomposition of term premium dynamics across the US, Europe, the UK, and Japan. But it leaves one important question largely unanswered: how does the ongoing reduction in central bank balance sheets interact with the term premium compression that we have already seen?

The Federal Reserve is still shrinking its balance sheet. The Bank of England is still in quantitative tightening mode. The ECB is still reducing its portfolio. In theory, the removal of a large, price-insensitive buyer from the market should increase term premium, all else equal. But the report shows that term premium has actually compressed. This suggests that the reduction in geopolitical uncertainty has been a more powerful force than the reduction in central bank demand. But what happens when the geopolitical uncertainty is fully priced out? Will the balance sheet reduction reassert itself as a source of upward pressure on term premium?

This is not a question that the report answers definitively. It notes that swap spreads remain slightly wide versus fundamentals, and that the funding backdrop is benign. But it does not provide a framework for thinking about the medium-term trajectory of term premium in a world where central banks are still shrinking balance sheets and fiscal deficits remain large. That is a gap that readers will need to fill with their own analysis.

## Decision framework: three questions every rates investor should ask before acting on this report

The report contains a wealth of tactical recommendations, but it is more useful as a framework for thinking about the rates market than as a set of trade ideas to be followed blindly. Here is a simple decision framework that every rates investor should use before acting on any of the report's conclusions.

First, what is your view on the geopolitical outlook? If you believe that the conflict in the Middle East is heading toward a durable resolution, then the term premium compression is justified, and you should be looking for opportunities to add duration, particularly in the front end. If you believe that the resolution is fragile or that the risks are being underestimated, then the term premium compression is a gift that should be used to reduce duration or add hedges.

Second, what is your view on the macro data? If you believe that the US labor market is softening and that wage pressures are contained, then the next move in yields will be driven by a repricing of the Fed path, and the front end will lead. If you believe that the labor market remains tight and that inflation risks are still to the upside, then the term premium compression will reverse, and the long end will sell off.

Third, what is your view on central bank balance sheets? If you believe that the reduction in central bank holdings is a secondary factor that will be overwhelmed by other forces, then you can ignore it. If you believe that the balance sheet reduction is a structural source of upward pressure on term premium, then you should be cautious about adding long-end duration at these levels.

These three questions will tell you which parts of the report are relevant to your portfolio and which are not. The report is a map, not a destination. You have to decide where you want to go.

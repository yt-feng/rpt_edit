# The Quality Cross Over

USD and EUR BB spreads have continued to outperform single-Bs, consistent with an ongoing ‘flight to quality’ within high yield, which has pushed the B/BB spread differential near the upper end of its historical range.   
However, this has not extended cleanly to the cross over between the low end of investment grade (BBB) and the high end of high yield (BB), where valuations remain broadly mid-range versus history. In the USD market, we think the BB/BBB ratio is fairly priced amid a somewhat more challenging growth and inflation mix. In EUR, by contrast, we see scope for more decompression given a more adverse macroeconomic backdrop and incremental technical pressures, including elevated fallen angel risk.   
While ‘generic’ rating screens (for example, BBB and BBB- debt with a Negative Outlook/Watch) can be a useful directional indicator of fallen angel risk, history suggests they capture only part of the story. Since 2010, we estimate that roughly half of fallen angels across both the USD and EUR markets have come from issuers that did not clearly signal an imminent downgrade ahead of the event. The same ‘surprise’ dynamic is even more pronounced on the upside, when tracking rising stars.   
We introduce a new framework for modeling fallen angel and rising star risk using historical rating-transition behavior, alongside a subjective overlay. On that basis, we raise our 2026 USD rising star forecast to \$75 billion (from \$40 billion previously). Elsewhere, the framework is broadly consistent with our existing 2026 forecasts: USD fallen angels of \$60 billion, and EUR rising stars and fallen angels of €20 billion and €30 billion, respectively. In EUR, that profile would imply the first year since 2020 where fallen angels outpace rising stars.   
- Fallen angel risks are not meaningfully reflected in either region. A related nuance is that the market impact of fallen angels has been asymmetric. In the USD market, downgrade risk is often well telegraphed and fallen angels have tended to outperform post-downgrade. In EUR, they have more often underperformed—in part reflecting the higher prevalence of single-agency rating structures and the potential for downgrade risk to materialize more abruptly.

# Revisiting Cross Over Dynamics and Fallen-Angel Trends

# BBs benefit from a ‘flight to quality’ within HY

In the USD market, spreads of BB-rated bonds have notably outperformed B-rated bonds since late 2025 (Exhibit 1). The OAS ratio between the two rating cohorts is hovering near the widest level of the post-financial crisis era. We believe the resilience of BBs reflects, in part, a ‘flight to quality’ positioning within the USD HY market. We attribute this sentiment to the overhang of geopolitical and macroeconomic uncertainty—even as the directional tone has felt ‘risk on’ at the index level. The relative underperformance of B-rated spreads is also consistent with the ‘atypical beta’ behavior we highlighted in late April.

Notably, however, the ‘flight to quality’ hasn’t translated into the relative value trade off between the low end of IG (BBBs) and the high end of HY (BBs). As shown in Exhibit 2, the ratio of BB spreads vs. BBBs ranks towards the middle-to-lower end of the historical distribution—signaling that the higher rated BBB cohort has not outperformed BBs. While historical valuations imply some modest scope for compression in BB spreads relative to BBBs, we believe the more likely outcome is for the ratio to remain range bound around current levels, amid a more challenging growth and inflation mix.

# We expect a greater technical headwind from ‘fallen angels’ in the EUR market

Beyond the well-telegraphed divergence in the macro backdrop between the US and Europe, we also expect technical conditions to differentiate across regions. In Europe, we see scope for a larger supply headwind into the BB segment from fallen angels, whereas USD BB technicals should benefit from more rising stars than fallen angels.

To assess fallen angel and rising star risk over the remainder of 2026, we introduce a new framework based on historical one year rating-transition behavior by rating bucket. The left panels of Exhibits 5 and 6 summarize the historical probability of becoming a fallen angel or a rising star over a one-year horizon, by rating bucket. The results are broadly intuitive: since 2010, bonds rated BBB- on Negative Outlook or Downgrade Watch have exhibited the highest propensity to become fallen angels, while BB+ credits with a Positive Outlook or Upgrade Watch have shown the highest likelihood of migrating back to investment grade (i.e., becoming rising stars).

That said, the right panels of both exhibits show that ratings and outlooks are, at best, an imperfect guide to forward-looking fallen angel and rising star risk: since 2010, issuers originally rated BBB/BBB- or higher without a Negative Outlook or Downgrade Watch have accounted for roughly half of all fallen angel volumes in both markets. The signal is especially noisy during abrupt macro shocks—such as COVID-19—when fundamentals can deteriorate faster than rating frameworks can adjust.

Accordingly, while our fallen angel and rising star forecasts are anchored in historical transition behavior, we would stress that forecast uncertainty remains elevated and, at times, warrants a subjective overlay.

For example, our model would imply roughly \$85 billion of USD rising stars in 2026; however, we are tempering that signal given the somewhat more challenging growth and inflation backdrop expected by our US economists, which may slow the pace of upgrades. As a result, we raise our official USD rising star forecast to \$75 billion (from \$40 billion previously; Exhibit 7).

Elsewhere, the framework is broadly consistent with our existing forecasts for 2026: USD fallen angels of \$60 billion, and EUR rising stars and fallen angels of €20 billion and €30 billion, respectively. In the EUR market, that profile would imply the first year since 2020 in which fallen angels outpace rising stars, while the USD market is set up for yet another year of net rising stars.

This divergence is starting to show up in year-to-date net rising-star activity: the EUR market is roughly flat at €1 billion, versus \$5 billion in the USD market.

Notably, however, the market does not appear to be pricing substantial fallen-angel risk in either region. Exhibit 8 shows that the share of the BBB-segment of the EUR and USD IG markets (unsecured bonds only) trading at spreads wider than the average BB+ remains around 2% and 15%, respectively, near the low end of the past decade.

# Sector drivers: Likely to be Energy intensive in EUR, Software in USD

The diverging paths between rising stars and fallen angels in the EUR and USD markets are further reinforced when we examine the composition of debt that has driven rising-star activity in recent years, alongside the segments that currently screen as most vulnerable to fallen angel risk.

First, the rising-star wave since 2021 has been more concentrated in European financials (Banks accounted for 30% of rising stars since 2021; Exhibit 9). While bank balance sheets remain strong, the sector faces incremental headwinds from higher taxes, slower growth, and, in some countries, lingering fiscal risks—all of which likely limit further upside rating momentum (at least in the near term). By contrast, USD rising stars have been more diversified across sectors, including Energy, Autos, and TMT.

Second, the current energy shock is weighing more heavily on energy intensive and interest rate sensitive sectors, which represent a large share of BBB/BBB- rated bonds currently on Negative Outlook or Downgrade Watch in the EUR market. For example, Automotives, Chemicals, REITs, and Food and Beverage account for 24% of EUR BBB and BBB- issuers on Negative Outlook or Downgrade Watch, versus 16% in the USD market (Exhibit 10). Idiosyncratic, issuer-specific, fallen angel risk is present in both markets, concentrated in Banks in the EUR market, and Software in the USD market.

# Fallen angels weigh more heavily on performance in the EUR market

In Exhibits 11 and 12, we capture a sample of fallen angels (since 2010) across the EUR and USD markets, to assess performance. Our sample uses iBoxx and Bloomberg data, and includes 155 fallen angels in the EUR market and 354 fallen angels for the USD market.

In the USD market, we find that fallen angels have tended to outperform after a downgrade (Exhibit 11). In many cases, the move to high yield is well telegraphed, and subsequent HY index inclusion can broaden the buyer base. In addition, it is common for HY investors to have a favorable view of the credit quality of recent fallen angels—especially relative to the ‘existing’ HY universe and even more so if there is a stated plan to return to IG-rated metrics.

In the EUR market, however, fallen angels have tended to underperform relatively consistently following a downgrade. One important driver appears to be the prevalence of single-rated issuers in Europe. In the EUR BBB market, 19% of notional outstanding has only one rating agency, versus less than 1% in the USD market (Exhibit 12). As a result, fallen-angel risk can materialize more abruptly and be less fully anticipated than in the USD market, where a ‘true’ fallen angel typically requires a second downgrade (per index eligibility rules).

EUR fallen angels have also tended to have more pronounced downside outcomes. Since 2010, 5% of the number of bonds that become fallen angels in the EUR have been downgraded to CCC+ or lower within one year, versus 1.5% in the USD market (Exhibit 13). That said, the market does appear to price some of this risk. BBB issuers rated by just one agency typically trade at slightly wider spreads versus their peers, particularly when also on Negative Outlook (Exhibit 14).

# Why does Europe have such a high share of single rating-agency issuers?

Both the number of issuers and the share of issuers with only one rating in Europe have risen steadily since 2010 (Exhibit 15).

Key drivers appear to be structural and regulatory. Under the EU CRA Regulation, Article 8d, issuers (or related third parties) that intend to appoint two or more credit rating agencies are required to consider appointing at least one credit rating agency with no more than 10% total EU market share; if they do not, they must document that decision.

In practice, this can increase the cost and operational burden of maintaining a multi-rating strategy, particularly for smaller issuers. As a result, some issuers may opt to remain single-rated rather than add a second (or third) relationship that could trigger additional documentation and governance steps.

This also interacts with issuer mix and market structure. As Exhibit 16 suggests, single-rated issuers tend to be smaller (lower average notional per issuer), and a large share of new entrants to the EUR credit market begin with only one rating. Given Europe's more fragmented market, we do not expect this dynamic to fade quickly (at least absent regulatory change).

ZH_A: 最近有一份来自某外资投行的研报，标题问了一个很有意思的问题：世界真的能少用9%的石油吗？B，你读完这份报告有什么印象最深的判断？

ZH_B: 最让我惊讶的是，这个9%的需求下降不是发生在经济危机中，而是发生在日常生活看起来几乎正常的情况下。在中国，没有政府强制节能，没有大规模限行，但石油需求突然就掉下去了。

ZH_A: 这听起来有点反直觉。需求下降不是因为经济崩了，那是什么在驱动？

ZH_B: 报告的核心观点是，这更多是一种“替代”而非“衰退”。消费者在悄悄做经济选择。面对更高的汽油、柴油和机票价格，他们转向了电动巴士、高铁、电动出租车这些更便宜的替代方案。

ZH_A: 所以这意味着，我们看到的不是一个简单的需求消失，而是一个结构性转变的开始？

ZH_B: 对，这正是报告要回答的关键问题。它把这次冲击和1973年的石油危机做了对比。当时石油深度嵌入经济每个角落，替代方案几乎没有，所以引发了严重衰退。但现在，现代能源系统本身就是对那次危机的回应——我们有战略石油储备、燃油效率标准、多样化的发电来源。

ZH_A: 那这次冲击会带来类似规模的结构性变化吗？

ZH_B: 报告认为可能，但方向不同。1973年推动的是更高效地使用能源。而这次，两次涉及主要产油国的大规模战争，可能加速的是经济活动与石油消费本身的脱钩。

ZH_A: 具体到不同油品，哪些需求可能回不来，哪些会恢复？

ZH_B: 报告把石油需求分成了几类。最有可能永久性损失的，是汽油。在中国，电动车渗透率已经很高，一旦消费者换了车，很少会换回来。报告估计中国汽油需求损失约18万桶/日，其中70%可能永远不会恢复。柴油也有类似风险，尤其是在中国。

ZH_A: 那化工原料和航空煤油呢？它们会恢复吗？

ZH_B: 化工原料需求损失约240万桶/日，报告认为大部分会恢复，因为很难找到替代品。航空煤油也一样，约50万桶/日的损失主要是因为航线调整和运营中断，一旦供应链稳定，大部分会回来。但报告也留了一个悬念：更高的票价和不确定性，会不会加速远程会议替代商务旅行？

ZH_A: 这个悬念很有意思。那燃料油呢？

ZH_B: 燃料油损失约60万桶/日，但报告认为很大一部分可能不会恢复。因为航运公司已经开始慢速航行、优化路线，这些行为一旦变成常态，就很难逆转。报告类比了2008年金融危机后的航运业——慢速航行从临时措施变成了结构性操作模式。

ZH_A: 听起来，这份报告对“哪些需求能回来”给出了非常细致的判断。但有没有它没有完全展开的问题？

ZH_B: 有，而且很关键。报告承认，在非洲和东南亚的部分地区，数据的可见性非常有限。另外，虽然它讨论了替代的规模，但没有深入分析：如果油价持续高企，这种替代的速度会快到什么程度？这需要读者自己去完整报告里找答案。

EN_A: So there is this research note from a global investment bank that starts with a pretty provocative question: Can the world actually live with nine percent less oil? B, what was your biggest takeaway?

EN_B: The most striking thing for me was that this nine percent drop in oil demand did not happen during a recession. It happened while daily life in China looked almost normal. No government mandates to save energy, no major travel restrictions, and yet oil demand just fell off a cliff.

EN_A: That sounds counterintuitive. If it is not an economic collapse, what is driving it?

EN_B: The report argues it is more about substitution than contraction. Consumers are making quiet economic choices. Faced with higher gasoline, diesel, and airfare prices, they are switching to cheaper, lower-carbon alternatives like electric buses, high-speed rail, and electric taxis.

EN_A: So what does that mean? Are we looking at a temporary blip or a structural shift?

EN_B: That is exactly the question the report tries to answer. It draws a fascinating comparison with the 1973 oil shock. Back then, oil was deeply embedded in every part of the economy, alternatives barely existed, and the result was a severe recession. But the modern energy system was built in response to that crisis. We now have strategic reserves, fuel efficiency standards, and diversified power generation.

EN_A: So does this shock have the potential to trigger changes of a similar magnitude?

EN_B: The report thinks yes, but the direction is different. In 1973, the push was to use energy more efficiently. This time, two major wars involving large oil producers over the past five years could accelerate something broader: the decoupling of economic activity from oil consumption itself.

EN_A: Let us break it down by product. Which types of oil demand are likely gone for good, and which ones will come back?

EN_B: Gasoline is the clearest case for permanent loss. In China, EV adoption is already high, and once consumers switch, they rarely switch back. The report estimates China lost about 180,000 barrels per day of gasoline demand, and 70 percent of that may never return. Diesel has a similar risk profile, especially in China.

EN_A: What about petrochemical feedstocks and jet fuel? Those seem harder to replace.

EN_B: Exactly. The report says most of the roughly 2.4 million barrels per day lost in petrochemical feedstock is likely to return once supply normalizes, because substitution is difficult. Jet fuel is similar. The roughly 500,000 barrels per day loss is mostly due to operational disruptions like rerouted flights and inventory management. But here is the cliffhanger: could higher fares and prolonged uncertainty permanently shift some business travel to remote meetings?

EN_A: That is a good悬念. What about fuel oil?

EN_B: Fuel oil is different. The report estimates about 600,000 barrels per day of loss, and a meaningful share may not come back. Shipping companies have started slow-steaming and optimizing routes. Once those practices become embedded, they tend to stick. The report compares it to what happened after the 2008 financial crisis, when slow-steaming became a structural operating model for parts of the global fleet.

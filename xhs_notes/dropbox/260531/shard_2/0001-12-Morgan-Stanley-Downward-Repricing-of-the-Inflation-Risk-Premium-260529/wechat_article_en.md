# The Belly of the JGB Curve Is Signaling That Markets Have Misread the Inflation Story

The most consequential shift in Japanese government bond markets this week is not a move in yields, but a repricing of what those yields mean. The belly of the curve—the five-year sector in particular—has outperformed sharply, and this is not a routine technical adjustment. It is the market's quiet admission that it had been pricing in an inflation risk premium that the real economy does not support. Investors who have positioned for a Bank of Japan forced into aggressive tightening by an energy-driven inflation overshoot are now confronting evidence that consumer price pressures are softening, not accelerating.

This matters now because the window for acting on this repricing may be narrower than it appears. The Middle East situation remains fluid, fiscal expansion risks are mounting in the super-long sector, and the market's own narrative is shifting from forward-looking inflation fears to backward-looking economic data. The belly's rally is not over, but it is entering a phase where conviction, not consensus, will determine returns.

The argument of this article is straightforward: the recent belly-led rally in JGBs reflects a downward repricing of excessive inflation risk premium, and there is scope for further gains under both de-escalation and escalation scenarios. But the opportunity is conditional on understanding which risks are being discounted and which are not. The report from which this analysis draws makes a compelling case for maintaining 5-year JGB outright longs while avoiding the super-long sector. The strategic question for investors is whether they are prepared to act on a thesis that runs against the prevailing noise.

## The Market Was Pricing Inflation Risk That Consumer Data Never Confirmed

The belly of the JGB curve had been underperforming for months. The rationale was clear: markets feared that the Bank of Japan might fall behind the curve, particularly after a strong April Corporate Goods Price Index print that showed sharp increases in petroleum and coal products as well as chemicals. JGB breakevens rose, reinforcing concerns about second-round effects through corporate pricing behavior. The narrative was compelling—if energy costs keep rising, firms will pass them through, and the BoJ will have to hike.

But the consumer inflation data tell a different story. April nationwide CPI showed that core-core inflation, which excludes both fresh food and energy, actually slowed. So did the "US-style" core measure, which excludes all food and energy. The headline acceleration was entirely energy-driven. The Nikkei CPINow index, a more timely gauge, points to continuing disinflation even into May. The Teikoku Databank survey of major food manufacturers shows that the number of June price hikes is well down from 2025 levels, and that firms are increasingly justifying increases by citing higher ingredient costs rather than labor costs—a shift that suggests they fear consumer backlash if they push too far.

The pattern is unmistakable: the forward-looking inflation overshoot risk that markets had priced into the belly was excessive. The real economy is not generating the kind of sustained, broad-based price pressure that would force the BoJ's hand. Governor Ueda's remarks at a recent international conference reinforced this, as he used historical oil shock examples to argue that "initial conditions matter enormously" and explicitly stated that Japan has not experienced a 1970s-style wage-price spiral. Medium- to long-term inflation expectations have shifted only modestly, from near-zero to a range of 1.5 to 2-plus percent depending on the measure.

The strategic implication is that the belly's rally has further room to run. The inflation risk premium that was embedded in five-year JGB yields is being unwound, and the process is not yet complete. Investors who continue to hold or add to 5-year outright longs are betting that the market will increasingly focus on real economic data rather than hypothetical inflation scenarios. This is a bet on the BoJ's credibility and on the structural disinflationary forces that remain embedded in the Japanese economy.

## The Belly Can Rally Under Both De-Escalation and Escalation Scenarios

One of the most striking features of the current environment is that the belly's outperformance is not dependent on a single geopolitical outcome. Under a de-escalation scenario, where Middle East tensions ease and energy prices stabilize or decline, the inflation risk premium would naturally compress further. The belly would rally as markets price out the tail risk of an energy-driven inflation spike.

But even under an escalation scenario, the belly can still rally. The key insight is that a prolonged disruption to energy supplies would eventually impact the real economy through weaker demand, not just through higher prices. Markets are beginning to recognize that the net effect of a protracted Middle East conflict could be disinflationary for growth, even if it is initially inflationary for energy. The BoJ would face a difficult trade-off, but the balance of risks would shift toward maintaining accommodation rather than tightening prematurely.

This is not a contrarian view for its own sake. It is grounded in the observation that the belly had been pricing in an inflation risk premium that was excessive relative to the actual inflation data. Whether the catalyst for repricing is de-escalation or a shift in focus to real economic weakness, the direction of travel is the same. The report identifies ample potential for further rally in the belly zone, and the trade ideas reflect this conviction.

The risk to this view is that soaring energy prices cause medium- to long-term inflation expectations to strengthen further. But the data so far do not support that scenario. The consumer price indices that exclude temporary factors are softening, and corporate pricing behavior is becoming more cautious. The burden of proof is shifting to those who argue that inflation will accelerate.

## The Super-Long Sector Remains Vulnerable to Fiscal Expansion Risks That the Report Does Not Fully Resolve

While the belly offers a relatively clean trade on inflation risk premium repricing, the super-long sector is mired in a different set of uncertainties. The report flags that markets are worried about fiscal expansion pressures, including potential "bridging bonds" and relief spending related to the Middle East situation. The government is considering a framework for using these bonds to fund growth strategy measures and crisis management investment, with the promise that they will be repaid using future returns on growth investments.

The accounting logic is that these bonds are fiscally neutral because they should not cause the debt-to-GDP ratio or the primary balance to deteriorate. But the net effect is still an increase in bond supply. The critical question that the report does not fully answer is whether offshore investors will accept this distinction. If they view bridging bonds as no different from special deficit-financing bonds, the super-long sector could see a sharp selloff reminiscent of mid-May.

The report suggests that the Ministry of Finance will focus on T-bills and retail JGBs to minimize the impact on secondary market interest rate risk. But this is a tactical response, not a strategic solution. The underlying fiscal trajectory remains uncertain, particularly with the government yet to identify permanent funding sources for proposed tax credit schemes and consumption tax cuts. The super-long sector is pricing in a risk premium for this uncertainty, and that premium could widen further.

The strategic implication is clear: the belly and the super-long sector are trading on fundamentally different narratives. The belly is repricing inflation risk downward, while the super-long is pricing in fiscal risk that could increase. This divergence creates an opportunity for relative value trades, but it also means that a blanket bullish view on JGBs is not justified. The report's recommendation to maintain short 30-year JGB ASW positions reflects this asymmetry.

## The Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguish Between Inflation Risk and Fiscal Risk

The report provides the raw material for a decision framework, but it does not spell it out explicitly. The framework that emerges from the analysis is threefold.

First, assess the inflation risk premium embedded in the belly. The report argues that it is excessive relative to actual consumer inflation data. Investors should compare the market-implied inflation expectations with the high-frequency indicators like the Nikkei CPINow index and the Teikoku Databank survey. If the gap is wide, the belly offers a compelling long.

Second, distinguish between headline inflation and core inflation. The market has been reacting to headline energy price increases, but the BoJ's reaction function is more sensitive to core and core-core measures. Governor Ueda's remarks confirm this. Investors should focus on the measures that exclude temporary factors, not the ones that capture them.

Third, evaluate the fiscal risk in the super-long sector separately. The belly's rally does not immunize the rest of the curve from supply concerns. The bridging bonds proposal is not yet fully specified, and the market's reaction will depend on the details of the repayment mechanism. Investors who are bullish on the belly should not assume that the same logic applies to 20-year or 30-year bonds.

The report leaves several meaningful open questions. Will the BoJ's terminal rate indeed be lower than the market currently prices? How will offshore investors react to the bridging bonds framework? Can the government identify permanent funding sources for its fiscal plans without triggering a selloff? These are not rhetorical questions. They are the analytical gaps that separate a good trade from a great one.

## The Next Phase of the Trade Requires Conviction, Not Consensus

The belly's recent outperformance is not a one-off event. It is the beginning of a repricing process that could extend for weeks or months. But the easy gains may already be behind us. The initial move reflected a correction of the most egregious mispricing. The next phase will require investors to maintain conviction in the face of headlines that could temporarily reverse the trend.

The Middle East situation remains in constant flux. A new incident could reignite inflation fears and cause the belly to underperform again, at least temporarily. The report acknowledges this, noting that the situation is still uncertain with no official announcement from either side. But it also argues that the belly's reaction function has changed. Markets are no longer as concerned about inflation overshoot risk as they were before.

This is the crux of the strategic argument. The market has undergone a regime shift in how it interprets geopolitical risk in the context of Japanese rates. The belly's resilience in the face of renewed tensions is evidence that the inflation risk premium is being repriced downward. Investors who wait for perfect clarity will miss the opportunity.

The report's trade ideas are specific and actionable: maintain 5-year JGB outright longs, maintain short 30-year JGB ASW positions, and maintain receive July BoJ Monetary Policy Meeting OIS. These are not recommendations for the faint-hearted. They require a clear-eyed assessment of the risks and a willingness to hold positions through noise.

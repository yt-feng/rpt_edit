ZH_A: 最近很多投资者都在讨论日本央行的加息路径，市场预期比很多人想象的要激进得多。这份研报的核心判断是什么？

ZH_B: 报告的核心观点是，市场可能高估了日本央行加息的幅度和速度。我们预计日本央行下次加息在2026年12月，然后2027年6月再加一次，最终政策利率到1.5%。但市场目前定价是到2027年底接近1.75%，很多宏观投资者甚至预期终端利率在2%或更高。

ZH_A: 所以你们的判断比市场更保守？为什么会有这么大的分歧？

ZH_B: 关键在于通胀的性质。我们认为日本的通胀主要是由汇率贬值和供给侧问题驱动的，而不是需求过热。比如美国式的核心CPI，剔除食品和能源后，5月份只有1.1%，而且最近几个月还在减速。这说明潜在通胀并没有看起来那么强。

ZH_A: 但日本央行的官员一直在说通胀有上行风险，甚至提到“核心通胀可能偏离2%目标”。你们怎么看这个矛盾？

ZH_B: 这是一个很好的问题。报告里专门回应了这一点。有些投资者引用日本式核心CPI，也就是剔除新鲜食品后的数据，5月份是2.7%，看起来很高。但这个指标包含了非新鲜食品和能源，不能真实反映潜在通胀。日本央行自己测算的美国式核心CPI，剔除制度因素后，5月份只有1.4%，远低于2.7%。

ZH_A: 所以你们认为通胀的核心驱动力是外部因素，而不是国内需求？

ZH_B: 对。过去三年，日元贬值和进口价格上涨是主要推手。最近大米价格飙升也推高了通胀。但报告指出，这些效应会逐渐消退。日本央行自己的研究显示，日元贬值5%，在第三年对CPI的拉动只有0.5到0.8个百分点。而国内需求方面，实际私人消费刚刚恢复到疫情前水平，花了七年时间。

ZH_A: 那工资增长呢？今年的春斗工资谈判结果很好，平均涨幅5.12%，基础工资涨了3.5%。这不是会推动通胀吗？

ZH_B: 工资增长确实强劲，但向价格的传导并不充分。报告强调，虽然企业继续提价，但最近的价格调整幅度并没有特别大。工资到服务通胀的传导还在进行中，但比市场预期的要慢。所以日本央行关注的工资-价格机制，目前还没有形成强有力的循环。

ZH_A: 那消费复苏呢？最近实际消费活动指数创了39个月新高，这不是需求拉动通胀的信号吗？

ZH_B: 这里有几个需要注意的地方。第一，按季节调整后，消费指数只是刚刚超过疫情前水平。第二，实际工资增长最近转正，但可能再次跌回负值。第三，消费改善的最大推动力是耐用品，但这里面有提前购买的因素。比如2027年空调环保标准更严，加上内存价格上涨，导致需求前置，未来几个月可能出现回调。所以消费需求并不足以让日本央行加快加息步伐。

ZH_A: 报告里还提到了中性利率。日本央行的估计范围是名义中性利率1.1%到2.5%，你们怎么看？

ZH_B: 我们认为1.5%是一个合理的基准。日本央行的六种模型中，有三种收敛在-0.5%的实际中性利率附近。而且央行明确表示，不会预先定义中性利率的具体水平，也不会把它作为主要沟通工具。所以我们的1.5%终端利率预测，与中性利率的合理估计是一致的。

ZH_A: 听起来你们的观点很系统，但市场定价和你们有差距。那报告里提到哪些问题还没有完全展开？

ZH_B: 比如财政政策的影响。日本政府的新财政支出计划有多大，会不会推高国内需求，从而迫使日本央行更激进地加息？这个问题报告有初步分析，但具体细节需要看完整版。另外，日本央行委员会的人员变动，2027年7月还有两位委员要替换，这可能进一步提高加息的门槛。

ZH_A: 这些悬念听起来确实值得深挖。如果听众想了解完整报告，应该怎么做？

EN_A: So a lot of investors are talking about the Bank of Japan’s rate path, and market expectations are way more aggressive than some forecasts. What is the core takeaway from this report?

EN_B: The report’s main argument is that the market might be overestimating how fast and how high the BoJ will hike. We expect the next hike in December 2026, followed by another in June 2027, bringing the policy rate to 1.5%. But markets are pricing in almost 1.75% by the end of 2027, and many macro investors expect a terminal rate of 2% or higher.

EN_A: So you are more cautious than the market. Why such a big gap?

EN_B: It comes down to what is driving inflation. We believe inflation in Japan has been mainly driven by currency depreciation and supply-side issues, not by strong demand. For example, US-style core CPI, which strips out food and energy, was only 1.1% year-on-year in May and has been decelerating. That suggests underlying inflation is not as strong as headlines suggest.

EN_A: But BoJ officials keep warning about upside risks to inflation, even saying core inflation could deviate above the 2% target. How do you reconcile that?

EN_B: That is a great question. The report addresses this directly. Some investors point to Japan-style core CPI, which excludes fresh food only, and that was at 2.7% in May. It looks high. But that measure includes non-fresh food and energy, so it does not properly reflect underlying inflation. The BoJ’s own measure of US-style core CPI, adjusted for institutional factors, was only 1.4% in May, well below that 2.7% figure.

EN_A: So you think the main drivers are external, not domestic demand?

EN_B: Exactly. Over the past three years, a weak yen and higher import prices have been the key drivers. More recently, a sharp rise in rice prices added to headline inflation. But the report argues these effects will fade over time. The BoJ’s own research shows that a 5% yen depreciation only adds 0.5 to 0.8 percentage points to CPI by the third year. On the demand side, real private consumption has only just recovered to pre-Covid levels, after seven years.

EN_A: What about wage growth? This year’s Shunto negotiations were strong, with average wage increases of 5.12% and base pay up 3.5%. Doesn’t that feed into inflation?

EN_B: Wage growth is indeed strong, but the pass-through to prices has not been particularly strong. The report notes that while firms have continued to raise prices, recent price revisions have not been that aggressive. The transmission from wages to services inflation is still incomplete and is happening more gradually than many market participants expect. So the wage-price spiral the BoJ is watching is not yet a powerful force.

EN_A: And what about the consumer spending recovery? The real consumption activity index hit a 39-month high in May. Doesn’t that signal demand-pull inflation?

EN_B: There are a few caveats here. First, on a seasonally adjusted basis, consumption has only just risen above pre-Covid levels. Second, real wage growth recently turned positive but could dip back into negative territory, even if temporarily. Third, the biggest driver of the improvement is durable goods, and some of that is likely front-loading. For example, stricter environmental standards for air conditioners from 2027 and rising memory prices are leading to early purchases. That could mean a payback in the coming months. So consumption demand is not strong enough to force the BoJ to hike faster.

EN_A: The report also talks about the neutral rate. The BoJ’s range is 1.1% to 2.5% for the nominal neutral rate. Where do you land?

EN_B: We see 1.5% as a reasonable benchmark. Three of the six models the BoJ uses converge around -0.5% for the real neutral rate. And the BoJ has made clear it does not want to predefine a specific level or use it as a main communication tool. So our 1.5% terminal rate forecast is consistent with that.

EN_A: So your view is well-supported, but the market disagrees. What questions does the report leave open?

EN_B: One big one is the new fiscal spending plan. How expansionary will it be? Will it push domestic demand enough to change the BoJ’s calculus? The report touches on this, but the full version goes deeper. Another is the BoJ board changes. Two more members are being replaced in July 2027, which could raise the hurdle for further hikes. These are worth exploring.

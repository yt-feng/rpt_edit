# BlackRock’s Margin Inflection Is the Real Story, Not Just Inflow Volume

BlackRock generated $200 billion in net inflows during the second quarter of 2026 and delivered 8% organic base fee growth, yet the most consequential signal from the quarter was not about volume at all. Management explicitly stated that the current ~46% operating margin is not a ceiling, a direct challenge to the skepticism that has persisted since the GIP and HPS acquisitions. This matters because the company trades at roughly 18.5 times next-twelve-months earnings, a 5% discount to its own historical average and a 9% discount to the S&P 500, a gap that historically has been only 2%. The entire case hinges on whether that discount closes, and the margin commentary provides the mechanism.

The tension is clear. BlackRock has been delivering above-target organic growth for quarters, but the market has priced in doubt about whether the acquired private markets and alternative asset management businesses would compress margins rather than expand them. The second-quarter results flipped that narrative. Operating margin reached 45.9%, the highest since 2021, and management described a structurally higher margin trajectory driven by business mix shift rather than one-off cost cuts. For a firm managing over $13 trillion in assets, the difference between a 46% margin and a 48% margin translates into billions of dollars of incremental operating income. That is the kind of compound effect that drives sustained EPS revision cycles.

The thesis is no longer about whether BlackRock can gather assets. It can, and it does. The question is whether the assets it gathers are increasingly higher-margin, and whether the firm can operate those businesses with the cost discipline of a technology company rather than the overhead structure of a traditional asset manager. The second-quarter evidence suggests the answer to both is yes, and that has direct implications for valuation.

## The Organic Growth Engine Has Shifted from Scale to Mix, and That Changes the Margin Calculus

For years, BlackRock’s organic growth story was dominated by iShares core equity and bond ETFs, products with razor-thin fee rates that generate enormous scale but limited incremental margin expansion. That pattern is still present — iShares generated $178 billion of inflows in the quarter, led by $85 billion in Core Equity and $61 billion in Index bond ETFs. But the composition within iShares is shifting. Active ETFs contributed $20 billion of inflows, and Precision exposure ETFs added another $15 billion. These categories carry meaningfully higher fee rates than core index products, and they are growing faster than the core franchise.

The broader point is that organic base fee growth of 8% in the quarter and 10% over the last twelve months is not coming from a single product line. It is broad-based across ETFs, systematic and tax-aware strategies, and private markets. Management described this as sustainable, citing three structural drivers: the expansion of the ETF franchise into active and precision categories, the runway for private markets fundraising and deployment, and the accelerating demand for systematic and tax-optimized strategies. Each of these drivers has a higher fee rate than the corporate average, and each is at a relatively early stage of penetration.

The fee rate dynamic matters because BlackRock’s margin story is not primarily about cost cutting. It is about revenue mix. When a dollar of revenue shifts from a 10-basis-point index ETF to a 40-basis-point active ETF or a 150-basis-point private credit mandate, the incremental margin on that dollar is far higher than the corporate average. Management’s confidence that current margins are not a ceiling is grounded in this mix shift, not in arbitrary expense targets. The 2021 margin of ~47% was achieved with a less scaled private markets and systematic business. The current business has more high-fee revenue in the pipeline, which means the margin ceiling should be higher, not lower.

## Private Markets Are the Multi-Year Margin Driver, and the Insurance Channel Is the Catalyst Most Observers Underappreciate

Private markets generated $15 billion of inflows in the quarter, with $6 billion in private credit deployment, $5 billion in infrastructure activity, and $3 billion in a partial funding of a private equity outsourcing mandate. These numbers are consistent with BlackRock’s Investor Day target of $400 billion in gross fundraising through 2030, a target that implies a substantial acceleration from the current $22 billion run rate. But the more important structural point is the insurance channel.

BlackRock manages approximately $800 billion in insurance assets. Management noted that insurance clients are increasingly looking to rotate 5% to 10% of that capital into private markets to capture the 150 to 350 basis points of yield pickup over Treasuries that private credit and infrastructure debt offer. Even at the low end of that rotation range, that represents $40 billion to $80 billion of assets moving from low-fee fixed income mandates into higher-fee private markets products. The integration of GIP and HPS on the origination side is specifically designed to capture this flow, particularly in digital infrastructure financings where BlackRock can originate large-scale deals and distribute them to insurance clients.

The fee rate on private markets declined 4 basis points quarter over quarter to roughly 81 basis points, driven by realizations in infrastructure equity early in the quarter and the timing of flows. But management sees 80 to 81 basis points as the jumping-off point for the third quarter, with gradual fee rate expansion expected as infrastructure flagships close and HPS wealth vehicles return to positive flow. The catch-up fees associated with those closes will provide an additional near-term boost. Over a multi-year horizon, the private markets fee rate should trend higher as the mix shifts toward direct lending and infrastructure debt, categories with fee rates above the private markets average.

## Systematic and Tax-Aware Strategies Are an Underappreciated Source of Both Growth and Margin Leverage

Systematic and tax-aware strategies generated $27 billion of net inflows in the quarter, and the growth trajectory is accelerating. Aperio and SpiderRock now manage approximately $210 billion in assets, up fourfold over the last five years. The composition within Aperio is shifting: historically focused on direct indexing, the firm saw second-quarter flows evenly split between direct indexing and its long-short product. Management described the usage of tax-optimized strategies as still in early stages, with clients increasingly focused on after-tax returns.

The margin implications are direct. Systematic strategies sit within Active Equities, a segment that has turned organic growth positive in recent quarters after years of outflows. The newer products — systematic active ETFs and the global equity market fund — are supporting organic growth in both iShares and Liquid Alternatives. These products have fee rates above the corporate average and operate with technology-driven cost structures that allow for high incremental margins. As these strategies scale, they contribute disproportionately to margin expansion without requiring proportional increases in headcount or compensation expense.

The tax-aware franchise has an additional advantage: it creates a sticky client relationship. Tax-optimized strategies are customized and difficult to replicate, which reduces the probability of client defection during market downturns. This stickiness supports revenue stability, which in turn supports the case for a higher valuation multiple.

## The Margin Trajectory Supports a Path to 48% Operating Margins by 2028, and That Implies a Structural EPS Revision Cycle

Management identified two primary levers for margin expansion. The first is driving fee-related earnings margins toward the levels of public alternative asset managers, which average approximately 55%. The second is the combination of higher fee rates on new flows, strong organic growth, and technology-driven operating leverage. These are not cost-cutting initiatives. They are structural improvements in the revenue mix and the cost structure that compound over time.

The current operating margin of 45.9% is already the highest since 2021, and management was explicit that this is not a ceiling. The path to 48% by 2028 implies roughly 150 basis points of annual margin expansion, a pace that is achievable given the mix shift underway. Consensus estimates for 2028 margins are below 47%, which means there is room for upward revision as the trajectory becomes clearer. Each 100 basis points of margin expansion at current revenue levels adds roughly $300 million to operating income, or approximately $1.80 to EPS on a fully diluted basis.

The EPS implications are significant. The analyst estimates for 2026 through 2028 have already been revised upward to $55.54, $64.37, and $75.05, respectively, driven by the stronger organic growth and margin outlook. But the margin story has not yet been fully reflected in consensus estimates. If BlackRock delivers 48% margins by 2028, the earnings trajectory could be meaningfully higher than current projections, particularly if equity markets remain supportive and organic growth sustains in the 7% to 8% range.

## The Valuation Discount Creates an Asymmetric Risk-Reward Setup

BlackRock currently trades at approximately 18.5 times next-twelve-months earnings, a 5% discount to its historical average of 19.5 times and a 9% discount to the S&P 500, compared to a historical discount of roughly 2%. The implied multiple based on historical relationships is approximately 20 times, which would represent roughly 8% upside from current valuation levels before any earnings growth is considered.

The earnings growth story is what makes the setup asymmetric. If BlackRock generates mid-teens EPS growth over the next several years — which is achievable given the organic growth trajectory, margin expansion, and share repurchase — then the combination of multiple re-rating and earnings growth implies substantial total upside from current levels. That target assumes a normal markets backdrop and does not require heroic assumptions about market returns or fee rate expansion.

The risk to the thesis is primarily on expenses and equity market weakness. If markets decline sharply, organic growth will slow, performance fees will decline, and the margin expansion story will be delayed. But the structural drivers — the mix shift toward private markets, systematic strategies, and technology — are independent of the market cycle. They will compound regardless of near-term volatility, and they provide a buffer against the cyclicality that has historically constrained asset manager valuations.

For those weighing whether the current discount represents an opportunity or a value trap, the framework should focus on three variables: organic growth sustainability, margin trajectory, and multiple convergence.

The structural drivers — active ETF adoption, insurance rotation into private markets, and tax-aware demand — are multi-year trends that are still in early stages. As long as these trends remain intact, organic growth should remain above the asset management industry average.

Second, evaluate the margin trajectory. Management has provided a clear framework: current margins are not a ceiling, and the path to 48% by 2028 is driven by mix shift rather than cost cutting. The key leading indicators are the private markets fee rate, the growth of active and precision ETFs, and the scaling of systematic and tax-aware strategies. If these indicators continue to improve, margin expansion will follow.

Third, monitor the discount to historical valuation and to the S&P 500. The current discount of 9% to the S&P 500 is unusually wide compared to the historical average of 2%. If BlackRock delivers on organic growth and margins, that discount should narrow, providing a tailwind to total return independent of earnings growth. The convergence does not need to happen quickly. Even a gradual reversion to historical levels over two to three years would add several percentage points to annualized returns.

The three variables are interdependent. Strong organic growth supports margin expansion, margin expansion supports earnings revisions, and earnings revisions support multiple convergence. The second-quarter results provided evidence that all three are moving in the right direction. The question is whether the market will recognize the structural improvement before the discount fully closes.

EN_A: Oil just had its biggest single-day jump in six years. But stocks barely flinched. What’s going on?
EN_B: That’s exactly the question this research report tries to answer. It asks: at what point does higher oil actually trigger a serious selloff?
EN_A: And apparently we’re not there yet.
EN_B: Not even close. The report says Brent crude at current levels, around 80 to 90 dollars a barrel, is still well below what it calls the pain threshold.
EN_A: That sounds like a specific number.
EN_B: It is. The report points to 110 dollars a barrel, and crucially, it has to stay there for a while, not just spike and drop.
EN_A: Why 110? What’s so special about that level?
EN_B: The report breaks it down into three historical conditions. First, the oil spike itself has to be big and sustained. We’re talking a 50 to 100 percent increase that lasts for months.
EN_A: How does today compare?
EN_B: Today, Brent is up only about 18 percent from before the conflict. That’s nowhere near the quadrupling we saw in 1973 or the doubling during the Gulf War.
EN_A: What’s the second condition?
EN_B: Central banks have to be forced into a hawkish pivot to fight the inflation. In 2022, the Fed hiked by over 500 basis points. Right now, the market is pricing in only about 55 basis points of tightening by next April.
EN_A: So central banks are not panicking.
EN_B: Exactly. And a big reason is that the inflation pressure is partly coming from stronger growth, not just higher oil. That gives them room to look through it.
EN_A: And the third condition?
EN_B: Economic damage. In past oil shocks, you saw immediate deterioration in jobs data. In 1973, unemployment shot up. In 1990, US payrolls had their biggest drop in seven years. This time, US payrolls are still solid, and the unemployment rate just hit a 12-month low.
EN_A: So none of the three conditions are met.
EN_B: That’s the report’s core argument. That’s why the cross-asset reaction has been so muted.
EN_A: But let’s say oil does hit 110. What happens then?
EN_B: That’s where consumers start to feel it. Inflation expectations get challenged. Central banks can no longer look through it.
EN_A: And you get a feedback loop.
EN_B: Exactly. Back in late March, the S&P 500 hit its low for the year and credit spreads peaked exactly when oil was above 110. Once you’re there, tighter financial conditions and falling equities start to hurt consumer spending, which feeds back into the economy.
EN_A: That sounds a lot like 2022.
EN_B: It does. In 2022, Brent stayed above 100 dollars for about six months, and that eventually led to a bear market. So the duration matters as much as the price.
EN_A: What does the oil curve tell us right now?
EN_B: It’s in backwardation. That means futures prices are lower than spot prices. Investors are betting this spike is temporary.
EN_A: So the market is basically saying, this too shall pass.
EN_B: For now, yes. Unless there’s a major supply disruption, like damage to infrastructure that takes months to repair. That’s the scenario the report flags but doesn’t fully explore.
EN_A: What else is missing from the report’s analysis?
EN_B: One thing is that the report assumes the economy is still growing. If the data starts to weaken, the same oil shock could have a much bigger impact. Also, the report doesn’t fully model what happens if a major chokepoint like the Strait of Hormuz gets disrupted.

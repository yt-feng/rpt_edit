## The Shift from Modular to World-Model Architecture Creates a Structural Moat That Competitors Cannot Quickly Replicate

The single most important technical detail in the report is the architectural change from a single-stage end-to-end modular design to a “world model plus reinforcement learning” dual-engine framework. This is not a marketing distinction. It is a structural advantage that compounds over time. A single-stage end-to-end model learns to imitate driving demonstrations, but it has no internal representation of the world. It cannot simulate what might happen next. It cannot predict the behavior of a pedestrian obscured by a truck or a cyclist about to swerve into traffic. The world model solves this by learning a spatiotemporal understanding of driving environments from real-world expert driver data. It can simulate long-tail scenarios—the rare but dangerous events that challenge autonomy programs—and dynamically predict future traffic states.

The reinforcement learning engine then uses these simulations to train the system to make decisions, not just to mimic. This is the difference between a system that can drive on a highway it has seen a thousand times and a system that can handle a construction zone in a city it has never visited. The test drive confirmed that HSD V2.0 executes defensive driving and navigation maneuvers that closely mimic a professional human driver. That capability is not easily replicated. Competitors that have invested heavily in end-to-end architectures face a costly retooling of their data pipelines, training infrastructure, and validation processes. Horizon has already made that transition and is now accumulating real-world driving data from its production vehicles to improve the world model further. Each mile driven makes the moat deeper.

## The BYD Relationship Generates Three Distinct Revenue Streams, Making the “Xuanji A3” Announcement a Net Positive for Horizon

The market’s reaction to BYD’s “Xuanji A3” chip announcement was a textbook case of surface-level analysis. The assumption was that a major customer building its own chip would reduce Horizon’s addressable market. The reality is more nuanced and far more favorable to Horizon. Management disclosed that Horizon is empowering the “Xuanji A3” chip with its Brain Processing Unit (BPU) intelligent acceleration engine. This means Horizon receives volume-based intellectual property royalties on every “Xuanji A3” chip BYD produces. The estimated royalty is between $10 and $50 per chip. For a chip with over 2,100 TOPS of computing power when three are clustered, this is a high-margin revenue stream that requires no manufacturing capacity from Horizon.

Second, Horizon remains the key chip supplier for BYD’s “God Eye C” highway NOA solution. This is the volume play. “God Eye C” is destined for BYD’s mid-to-low price brand products, where unit volumes are highest. The “Xuanji A3,” by contrast, is intended for high-priced vehicles. These two products target different price segments and do not compete. Third, management indicated that Horizon is expected to supply a new high-end chip for BYD’s “God Eye B” solution, alongside Horizon’s own HSD autonomous driving software. The computing power of this new chip likely falls between the J6H at 256 TOPS and the J6P at 560 TOPS. This third stream is the most strategically significant because it embeds Horizon’s software into BYD’s premium product line, creating a dependency that goes beyond hardware supply.

The net effect of the “Xuanji A3” announcement is that Horizon has converted a potential competitive challenge into three revenue streams: IP royalties on BYD’s chip, volume chip supply for the mass market, and a high-end chip-plus-software bundle for premium vehicles. This is not a weakening of the relationship. It is a deepening that diversifies Horizon’s revenue and raises switching costs for BYD.

## The Gradualist Path to L4 Autonomy Turns L2+ Production Data into a Competitive Advantage That Robotaxi Pure-Plays Cannot Match

Horizon is pursuing a “gradualist approach” to L4 autonomous driving and robotaxi commercialization. The strategy is deceptively simple but strategically profound. The company is leveraging its core advantage: the efficient utilization of extensive L2+ mass production data and engineering experience accumulated from millions of pre-installed vehicles. The robotaxi algorithm framework will be identical to the existing L2+ HSD algorithm. This means every mile driven by a Horizon-equipped production vehicle improves the robotaxi system. No separate data collection fleet is required. No expensive sensor suite is needed for training. The data comes from customers driving their own cars.

This is the opposite of the approach taken by robotaxi pure-plays, which must deploy dedicated fleets with expensive sensor arrays and safety drivers to collect data. Those companies face a unit economics problem that Horizon does not. Horizon’s data acquisition cost is effectively zero because the data is a byproduct of selling chips to automakers. The company is currently testing robotaxis with drivers and safety drivers, with plans to gradually transition to fully driverless operations. The transition will be measured and iterative, but the data advantage is structural. A pure-play robotaxi company must spend capital to collect each mile of data. Horizon collects data at scale from vehicles that are already on the road for other purposes.

The implications for the competitive landscape are significant. If Horizon’s gradualist approach succeeds, it will have achieved L4 capability at a fraction of the cost of its competitors. The same algorithm that guides a family sedan on a highway commute will guide a robotaxi through downtown Shenzhen. The marginal cost of adding robotaxi capability to an existing chip customer is near zero. This creates a platform dynamic where Horizon can offer L4 functionality as a software upgrade to its installed base, rather than requiring a separate vehicle or a new hardware purchase.

The central question is whether Horizon will be valued as a chip supplier or as an autonomy platform. The difference in valuation multiples is substantial. It demonstrates that Horizon can monetize its intellectual property without shipping a physical chip. If this model expands to other automakers, the revenue mix shifts toward higher-margin, recurring income. Observers should monitor the share of revenue from IP royalties and software licenses in upcoming earnings reports. A sustained increase above 15-20% of total revenue would support the platform thesis.

Second, does the HSD software attach rate increase as Horizon’s chip shipments grow? The current model sells chips with basic ADAS functionality. The next step is to upsell HSD V2.0 as a software option on those same chips. If Horizon can convert even 10-15% of its installed base to paid HSD subscriptions, the incremental revenue would be highly profitable and would demonstrate that the software has standalone value. The test drive results suggest the product quality is there. The question is whether automakers will pay for it.

Third, does the robotaxi commercialization path remain on track without requiring significant capital expenditure? Horizon’s gradualist approach is capital-light by design, but it requires patience. The market may penalize the stock if the transition to fully driverless operations takes longer than expected. Observers should compare Horizon’s cumulative spending on robotaxi development against its chip revenue. If the robotaxi program is funded organically by chip profits, the risk is contained. If it requires external capital, the dilution risk increases.

The current share price of 4.42 HKD against a target of 9.40 HKD implies that the market is pricing Horizon as a chip supplier with execution risk. The evidence from the test drive, the BYD relationship, and the chip shipment forecasts suggests that the platform thesis is more credible than the market believes. The valuation gap will close when observers recognize that Horizon’s architectural advantage, data flywheel, and diversified revenue streams have created a business that is structurally different from its peers.

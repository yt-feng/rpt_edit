## Three Phases of a Slowdown: Demand Weakness, Supply Disruptions, Chinese Competition

German car production slumped in three distinct phases. In the first phase between 2018 and 2021, lower demand weighed on German production (Exhibit 5, left). While the German car industry experienced stronger demand than its European peers until 2018, demand weakened relative to its peers pre-Covid, as diesel sales plummeted in the wake of the diesel scandal and stricter environmental regulations in Europe. Subdued demand during Covid hit Germany in a broadly similar way to other European economies.

The second phase, during 2021 to 2023, was characterised by global supply chain disruptions, particularly for semiconductors (Exhibit 5, right). While supply shortages weighed on production across the EU, the German car industry was affected relatively more strongly, although it then saw a stronger rebound in 2023.

Finally, after recovering from supply chain disruptions, the third phase since 2024 has been characterised by subdued demand, with the German car industry affected similarly to the mean across the EU.

Our equity analysts expect the share of BEVs (battery electric vehicles) and PHEVs (plug in hybrid vehicles) in global car sales to increase to almost 40% by 2030, further rising to about 70% in 2040. The shift to EVs is putting pressure on labour demand and car suppliers, particularly those focused on the combustion powertrain. Electric engines are less complex than combustion engines and the associated transmission system, with estimates suggesting that electric engines require only two-thirds of the labour input of combustion engines. Employment in the auto industry has already declined by about 150,000 since its peak in 2019, with suppliers bearing the majority of those losses. A study by the ifo institute indicated that around 250,000 jobs that depend on combustion engines would be affected by the transition to EVs between 2019 and 2030. While overall job losses could be cushioned somewhat by rising labour demand for EV components such as batteries, this hinges on a material share of production occurring in Germany. $^{9}$ Reallocation of labour towards the growing defence industry could also cushion job losses in the auto sector.

That said, while battery production has been rising in Germany, a substantial share of domestic absorption is currently met by imports (Exhibit 11, left) and production of critical inputs required for battery production is concentrated outside of Europe (Exhibit 11, right), lowering the domestic value-added share of EV production. Estimates put the relative European value added of a BEV produced in Europe at 75%, compared with 90% for a European-produced combustion engine car.

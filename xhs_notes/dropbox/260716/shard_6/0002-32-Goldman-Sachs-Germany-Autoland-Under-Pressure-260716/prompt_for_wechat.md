In addition to the increase in competition from China, the shift to EVs is changing the structure of the German car industry. Our equity analysts expect the share of BEVs (battery electric vehicles) and PHEVs (plug in hybrid vehicles) in global car sales to increase to almost 40% by 2030, further rising to about 70% in 2040. Since 2022 the mix of German car production has shifted towards EVs (Exhibit 10, left). Last year BEVs and PHEVs accounted for around 40% of production and exports (Exhibit 10, left), about 10pp higher than the share of EV registrations. $^{8}$ Non-plugin hybrids (HEVs), which account for another 30% of registrations, are currently not produced in Germany but imported.

The shift to EVs is putting pressure on labour demand and car suppliers, particularly those focused on the combustion powertrain. Electric engines are less complex than combustion engines and the associated transmission system, with estimates suggesting that electric engines require only two-thirds of the labour input of combustion engines. Employment in the auto industry has already declined by about 150,000 since its peak in 2019, with suppliers bearing the majority of those losses. A study by the ifo institute indicated that around 250,000 jobs that depend on combustion engines would be affected by the transition to EVs between 2019 and 2030. While overall job losses could be cushioned somewhat by rising labour demand for EV components such as batteries, this hinges on a material share of production occurring in Germany. $^{9}$ Reallocation of labour towards the growing defence industry could also cushion job losses in the auto sector.

That said, while battery production has been rising in Germany, a substantial share of domestic absorption is currently met by imports (Exhibit 11, left) and production of critical inputs required for battery production is concentrated outside of Europe (Exhibit 11, right), lowering the domestic value-added share of EV production. Estimates put the relative European value added of a BEV produced in Europe at 75%, compared with 90% for a European-produced combustion engine car.

Taken together, continued subdued demand in Europe, rapidly rising competition from Chinese producers in their home market (and particularly in export markets), US tariffs and the shift to electric vehicles make for a challenging environment for the German auto industry.

Orders and European demand have stabilised, suggesting that the decline in auto production may have bottomed out for now. However, structural headwinds are likely to limit any recovery and pose risks to the downside. Based on our autos analysts' longer-term projections for car sales by market, we estimate that the German autos industry could shrink by more than 8% by 2030 in a scenario where German exporters lose further market share in China, exports fail to recover in the US, market shares in Europe decline slightly, and further electrification reduces the domestic value-added share of cars (Exhibit 12, left). In this scenario, the drag from autos on aggregate growth would be about -0.1pp per year (Exhibit 12, right).

In a downside scenario where increased competition from China more strongly weighs on German market share in Europe and abroad, autos value added could decline by 16% between 2025 and 2030, resulting in a 0.2pp drag on GDP growth.

That said, while risks for the industry are tilted to the downside, a build-out of the domestic EV value chain could be an opportunity for the German auto industry. In a scenario where production shifts towards EVs more quickly but a larger share of the EV value added is captured domestically and market shares remain at their 2025 levels, value added could increase by $5\%$ , resulting in a tailwind to growth of 0.06pp.

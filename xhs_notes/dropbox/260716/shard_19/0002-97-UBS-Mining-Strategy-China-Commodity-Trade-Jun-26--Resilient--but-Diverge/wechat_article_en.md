# China's Commodity Trade Data Reveals a Market Driven by Supply Dynamics, Not Demand Strength

China’s June 2026 commodity trade data tells a story of resilience on the surface but reveals a far more complex and divergent reality underneath. Iron ore imports surged to 112.6 million tonnes, up 6% year-on-year and 15% month-on-month, reversing a weak May and returning to the 110–120 million tonne per month range that characterized late 2025. Aluminium exports hit 0.71 million tonnes, a striking 45% year-on-year increase, running at an annualized rate of 8.5 million tonnes. Coal imports rebounded sharply to 42.8 million tonnes, up 30% year-on-year and 29% month-on-month. At first glance, these numbers suggest a robust Chinese economy absorbing raw materials at an accelerating pace.

That impression is misleading. Beneath the headline strength, the composition and context of these flows point to a market increasingly driven by supply-side factors, inventory dynamics, and policy interventions rather than genuine end-use demand. Iron ore imports are rising despite soft domestic steel demand and elevated port stocks. Aluminium exports are surging because of temporary supply outages outside China, not because of structural demand acceleration. Coal imports are rebounding due to domestic mine disruptions and seasonal power needs. The pattern that emerges is one of divergence: bulk commodities face a deteriorating fundamental outlook even as their trade volumes appear strong, while copper and aluminium continue to benefit from tighter supply-demand balances, though aluminium’s support is already fading. For observers and strategists, the critical task is to distinguish between trade flows that reflect genuine demand and those that merely reflect supply noise.

## Iron Ore Imports Are Running on Supply Momentum, Not Demand Pull, and the Price Floor Is Weakening

The 112.6 million tonne iron ore import figure for June represents a meaningful recovery from the soft May reading, and annualized imports are now running at approximately 1.35 billion tonnes per annum. This pace is consistent with strong 2026 supply from Australia and Brazil, the early ramp-up of Simandou, and ongoing iron grade degradation at major producers that forces higher volumes to maintain iron content. Yet the demand signals are conspicuously absent. China’s net steel exports are down 5% year-to-date. Iron ore port stocks remain elevated at approximately 158.5 million tonnes, below the all-time high of 167 million tonnes reached in March but still high by historical standards. Export shipments from major producers continue to lift.

The divergence between rising imports and weak end-demand indicators is the central tension in this market. Imports are being driven by plentiful supply and inventory restocking dynamics, not by a surge in Chinese steel production. The spot price, currently around $98 per tonne, sits just inside the cost support level represented by the 95th percentile of the global cost curve at $99–101 per tonne. That proximity to cost support has given some market participants comfort that prices cannot fall much further. But the logic is backward. Cost support is a lagging indicator that shifts as supply grows and marginal producers are displaced. With Simandou output ramping up and major producers maintaining high shipment volumes, the cost curve itself is likely to flatten and shift lower. The report’s projection of iron ore drifting toward $92 per tonne through 2026, 2027, and 2028 is not a bearish outlier but a conservative extrapolation of the supply-demand arithmetic. The real risk is that prices fall below that level if Chinese steel demand weakens further.

## Copper’s Supply Constraint Is Structural, Creating a Multi-Year Deficit That Anchors Prices Above $6 Per Pound

Copper presents the cleanest case in the Chinese trade data, precisely because its dynamics are driven by supply scarcity rather than demand variability. Copper concentrate imports were essentially flat in June at 2.33 million tonnes, with year-to-date volumes down 1%. This flatness is not a sign of weak demand but of tight upstream supply. Treatment and refining charges remain low, reflecting the shortage of concentrate feedstock. The outages at Grasberg and Cobre Panama, combined with muted production trends in Chile and Peru, have created a persistent bottleneck in the concentrate market.

The response to this bottleneck is visible in the refined copper data. Refined copper imports rose 5% month-on-month to 478,000 tonnes, but remain 5% lower year-to-date. As concentrate becomes harder to source, Chinese smelters and traders are increasingly turning to refined metal to fill the gap. This substitution effect is the mechanism through which the concentrate shortage transmits into the broader copper market. The underlying demand drivers—electrification, data center buildout, and artificial intelligence infrastructure—remain structurally strong. The result is a market that the report characterizes as likely to run deficits over the next one to three years, anchoring prices around $6.00 to $6.50 per pound.

The strategic implication is clear. Copper is the one major commodity in this dataset where supply constraints are genuine, deep, and unlikely to resolve quickly. The concentrate shortage is not a cyclical hiccup but a structural feature of an industry that underinvested in new mine capacity during the previous price cycle. Observers should treat any price pullback in copper as a point of interest rather than a signal of weakening fundamentals, provided the demand story remains intact.

## Aluminium’s Current Strength Is a Temporary Function of Supply Disruptions That Are Already Reversing

Aluminium exports from China reached 0.71 million tonnes in June, a 45% year-on-year increase that appears to validate the narrative around the metal. The strength reflects robust demand outside China, driven by the fact that 2.5 to 3 million tonnes of downstream aluminium supply remain offline in the Middle East and at Mozal. The market is in deficit in 2026, and prices are supported by cost inflation, low visible inventories, and the copper-aluminium substitution dynamic, with the copper-to-aluminium price ratio exceeding 4 times.

But the sustainability of this support is the critical question. The report explicitly states that tightness is expected to ease over the next six to twelve months as approximately 3 million tonnes of Middle East supply restarts and Indonesian capacity accelerates. These supply additions are projected to drive approximately 5% global supply growth in 2026 and 3% in 2027, compared to demand growth of roughly 2.5 to 3%. The arithmetic is straightforward: supply growth is outpacing demand growth, and the deficit is closing. The report expects the market to rebalance into 2027 and 2028 rather than move into surplus, but the period of acute tightness that has driven recent price strength is passing.

For observers, the implication is that the current level of Chinese aluminium exports is unsustainable. As Middle East supply normalizes and Indonesian capacity ramps, the export arbitrage that has driven Chinese shipments will narrow. The recent strength in Chinese metal exports will reverse, and prices will rebase above the cost curve but with less upside than current tight conditions imply. Aluminium remains a constructive story over the medium term, but the near-term risk is that the market has already priced in the peak of tightness.

## Steel and Coal Tell Opposite Stories About Chinese Industrial Activity, but Neither Supports a Demand-Led Recovery

Steel exports reached 10.3 million tonnes in June, flat month-on-month but up 7% year-on-year. Net exports, after accounting for negligible imports of approximately 0.4 million tonnes, stood at 9.9 million tonnes. The annualized net export rate of 118 million tonnes per annum is below the late-2025 peaks of 10.5 to 11.3 million tonnes per month, but it represents a material recovery from the January and February trough of approximately 7.4 million tonnes per month following export license tightening.

The key dynamic in steel is that the export arbitrage remains open and Chinese mills remain profitable, which limits the downside to production even as domestic demand weakens. Steel is the clearest example of a commodity where Chinese trade flows are being driven by the profitability of exporting surplus production rather than by genuine demand growth. The structural export surplus is a feature of China’s steel industry that will persist as long as domestic capacity exceeds domestic demand and export markets remain accessible.

Coal tells a different but equally instructive story. Coal imports rose 30% year-on-year and 29% month-on-month to 42.8 million tonnes, rebounding sharply from a weak April and May. The report identifies four drivers: domestic supply disruptions following a Shanxi mine explosion, strong seasonal power demand growth, a focus on thermal coal strategic stockpiles, and possible gas-to-coal switching. These are all temporary or policy-driven factors. The coal data does not signal a sustained recovery in industrial activity; it signals a response to specific supply shocks and policy priorities.

## Rare Earths Exports Confirm That Geopolitics, Not Demand, Now Dictates Strategic Material Trade Flows

Rare earths exports declined to 5.1 kilotonnes in June, down 34% year-on-year and 7% month-on-month, extending a downtrend that began after a strong 2025. The weakness is policy-driven rather than demand-led. Tighter controls on the production and export of strategic materials are constraining outbound volumes. The report’s characterization is precise: rare earth trade flows are increasingly decoupled from market fundamentals, with geopolitics and export regulation the dominant drivers.

This decoupling has two implications. First, rare earth prices and trade volumes will become less reliable as indicators of underlying demand. Observers who rely on Chinese export data to gauge the health of the rare earths market are reading a distorted signal. Second, the policy-driven nature of the decline means that any relaxation of export controls could produce a sharp rebound in volumes, creating a binary risk for observers positioned on either side of the trade. The strategic materials market is now a policy play, not a commodity play.

## A Decision Framework for Observers: Distinguish Between Supply-Driven and Demand-Driven Trade Flows

The June trade data provides a natural framework for commodity observers. The first question to ask about any commodity is whether its trade flow is being driven by supply dynamics or demand dynamics. Iron ore, steel, and coal are all currently supply-driven. Iron ore imports are rising because supply is abundant and cost support is eroding, not because Chinese steel mills are running at capacity. Steel exports are holding up because mills are profitable and the export arbitrage is open, not because global demand is accelerating. Coal imports are rebounding because of domestic disruptions and policy stockpiling, not because industrial activity is surging.

Copper is the outlier. Its trade flows are genuinely demand-constrained by supply scarcity. The concentrate shortage is structural, and the resulting deficit is likely to persist for years. Aluminium is in a transitional zone: its current strength is supply-driven by Middle East outages, but those outages are reversing, and the demand story, while real, is not strong enough to sustain the current level of tightness.

The second question is whether the supply-driven dynamic is self-correcting or self-reinforcing. In iron ore, the supply growth from Simandou and other projects is self-reinforcing in its downward pressure on prices, because lower prices do not immediately reduce supply from low-cost producers. In aluminium, the supply disruptions are self-correcting because the offline capacity is returning. In rare earths, the policy-driven decline is self-correcting only if policy changes, which is unpredictable.

The final question is whether the demand story, where it exists, is structural or cyclical. Copper’s demand story is structural, driven by electrification and AI infrastructure. Steel’s demand story, to the extent it exists outside China, is cyclical and vulnerable to a global economic slowdown. Coal’s demand story is a mix of seasonal and policy factors that provide no sustained tailwind.

Observers who apply this framework to the June data will conclude that copper remains the most compelling long-term observation among the major commodities, that aluminium’s near-term strength should be treated with caution, and that iron ore, steel, and coal are all facing changing fundamentals that their current trade volumes obscure.

# China's New Volume-Based Procurement Expansion Threatens to Reshape the Competitive Landscape for Western MedTech Companies in the World's Second-Largest Healthcare Market

On 14 July 2026,China's National Health Commission,alongside two other regulatory bodies,issued a notice that signals a fundamental shift in the country's procurement framework for medical equipment. For the first time,volume-based procurement (VBP) will extend into capital equipment—the high-value,high-margin category that had previously remained outside the program's reach. This is not an incremental policy adjustment. It is a structural redefinition of how medical technology companies will access the Chinese market. For Western medtech firms that have relied on China as a growth engine,the implications are immediate,measurable,and strategically consequential.

The notice establishes a three-tier VBP framework that will cover everything from multi-million-dollar radiotherapy systems to conventional imaging devices. National-level VBP will now include Class A large-scale equipment—radiotherapy platforms and any first-time installed medical device priced above RMB 50 million. Provincial VBP will absorb Class B equipment,including PET-CT,PET-MR,laparoscopic surgical robots,and conventional radiotherapy systems. Municipal VBP will sweep in everything else,with a mandate that all provinces complete at least one round of VBP by the end of 2026. The message is unambiguous:China intends to use its purchasing power as a lever to drive down equipment prices across the entire medtech spectrum.

The timing matters. This policy arrives at a moment when Western medtech companies are already navigating a challenging global capital expenditure environment. Hospital budgets in developed markets remain constrained,and emerging markets have become increasingly important for volume growth. China,representing a significant share of revenues for companies like Elekta (13% of group revenues),GE HealthCare,Siemens Healthineers,and Philips,has been a reliable source of mid-single-digit growth expectations. Those expectations now face a direct and credible threat.

What makes this policy particularly potent is not just the price pressure it creates,but the mechanism through which it operates. The notice proposes a national filing database for high-value medical equipment to increase price transparency. Bid evaluation criteria will now consider product quality,services,and price,with the cost of consumables and maintenance explicitly factored into assessments. This means that the total cost of ownership—not just the initial purchase price—will become a competitive variable. For Western companies whose business models depend on high-margin service contracts and consumable revenue streams,this represents a double jeopardy:lower equipment prices and compressed aftermarket profitability.

## Elekta Faces the Most Direct and Material Risk,as Its China Strategy Collides with the New VBP Framework

Elekta's exposure is the most acute among the Western medtech players. China accounts for 13% of group revenues,and management has explicitly listed "Expand in China" as a key strategic pillar,guiding for mid-single-digit revenue growth in the country over fiscal years 2026 through 2029. The new VBP framework directly undermines that ambition.

The national VBP tier covers Elekta's MR-linac Unity,a high-end radiotherapy platform for which there is no significant local competition. On the surface,this might seem like a protective moat. However,the risk is not market share loss but price erosion. Provincial procurement for other large-scale medical equipment has already demonstrated the magnitude of price cuts that Chinese authorities can achieve—CT scanner prices fell by approximately 45% in the first quarter of 2026 alone. If similar discounts are applied to Unity,the platform's high profitability—a key contributor to Elekta's margin structure—could be substantially eroded.

The provincial VBP tier covers the rest of Elekta's portfolio,including its conventional radiotherapy system,Evo. Here,the competitive dynamics are entirely different. United Imaging,a Chinese domestic manufacturer,already sells a Halcyon-like O-ring linear accelerator in China. Elekta currently holds roughly 40% market share in China's conventional radiotherapy market,making it the largest player and,by extension,the most likely share donor. When local competitors offer comparable products at significantly lower prices,and when provincial procurement authorities are incentivized to select the lowest-cost option,market share erosion becomes not a possibility but a probability.

The strategic challenge for Elekta is compounded by its lack of diversification. Unlike Siemens Healthineers,which can offset China weakness with strength in the US market,or Philips,which has lower total revenue exposure to China,Elekta's growth narrative is heavily dependent on the Chinese market. The new VBP framework does not merely slow that growth;it calls into question whether the growth is achievable at all under the current business model.

## The Decision Framework:How Companies Should Assess Their Exposure and Strategic Response

For medtech executives and investors evaluating the implications of this policy,three variables determine the magnitude of risk:revenue concentration in China,product differentiation relative to local competitors,and the extent to which a company's business model depends on aftermarket revenue streams.

The first variable is straightforward. Companies with a higher percentage of group revenues derived from China face greater earnings risk. Elekta at 13% is at the top of the exposure list. GE HealthCare,while not disclosing an exact percentage,has the highest China revenue share among the imaging players. Siemens Healthineers and Philips have lower concentrations,which provides a natural buffer.

The second variable is more nuanced. Product differentiation can take two forms:technological superiority that local competitors cannot replicate,or brand and service advantages that command a premium. The Unity MR-linac represents the first type of differentiation—no local competitor offers an equivalent product. However,as the CT pricing precedent demonstrates,even differentiated products face price pressure in a VBP framework. The question is not whether prices will fall,but by how much. For products without differentiation,such as conventional radiotherapy systems or standard ultrasound devices,the risk is more binary:market share loss is likely.

The third variable is the most insidious. The new bid evaluation criteria explicitly consider the cost of consumables and maintenance. This means that companies cannot compensate for lower equipment prices by maintaining high service margins. The entire revenue and profit model must be re-evaluated. For companies like GE HealthCare and Siemens Healthineers,where service contracts and consumables represent a significant and high-margin revenue stream,this represents a structural compression of profitability that goes beyond the initial equipment sale.

The strategic response options are limited but not nonexistent. Companies can accelerate localization by manufacturing more products within China,potentially qualifying for preferential treatment in procurement decisions. They can double down on innovation to maintain technological distance from local competitors. Or they can adjust their geographic revenue mix to reduce dependence on China. Each option carries its own costs and risks,and the optimal choice will vary by company.

## The Second-Order Implications Extend Beyond Pricing to Market Structure and Competitive Dynamics

The VBP expansion has implications that go beyond the immediate pricing pressure on Western medtech companies. First,it will accelerate the internationalization of Chinese medtech manufacturers. United Imaging and Mindray,which currently compete primarily in the domestic market,will gain scale,revenue,and clinical validation through expanded VBP contracts. This will make them more formidable competitors in other emerging markets and,eventually,in developed markets. Western companies that view China risk as a China-only problem are underestimating the long-term competitive threat.

Second,the policy will likely compress the total addressable market for high-end medical equipment in China. When prices fall,volume typically increases,but the relationship is not linear in medical technology. Hospital procurement decisions are influenced by budget cycles,replacement schedules,and clinical need. A 45% price reduction on CT scanners does not necessarily generate 45% more unit sales. The revenue pool for Western companies in China will likely shrink,not just in terms of margins but in absolute terms.

Third,the profitability of the medtech sector as a whole may face structural compression. China has historically been a high-growth,high-margin market for Western companies. If that market transitions to a low-growth,low-margin environment,the implied valuation multiples for the sector will need to adjust. Companies that have relied on China growth to justify premium valuations will face earnings revisions and multiple compression.

The policy also introduces execution risk for management teams. Diverting resources to navigate the China VBP environment may come at the expense of investment in other growth markets or R&D pipelines. The opportunity cost of managing regulatory and pricing risk in China is real and should be factored into investment decisions.

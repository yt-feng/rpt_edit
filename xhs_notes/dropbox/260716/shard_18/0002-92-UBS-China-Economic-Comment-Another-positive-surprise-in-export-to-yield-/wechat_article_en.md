## The Technology Export Boom Is Reshaping China’s Comparative Advantage,Not Just Its Trade Balance

The IT export basket grew 72.4% year-on-year in June,only slightly below May’s exceptional 76.0% pace. Within this,integrated circuit exports surged 122% year-on-year—a growth rate rarely seen in recent history and one that reflects both volume expansion and pricing dynamics. Computer exports grew 53.0% year-on-year,while mobile phone exports moderated to 27.5% from 43.5% in May. The moderation in phones and computers relative to integrated circuits is instructive:it suggests that China is exporting more of the high-value semiconductor components that go into finished devices,rather than just assembling those devices. This is a shift up the value chain.

The sustained strength in technology exports matters for two reasons. First,it insulates China’s trade performance from the kind of demand shocks that would hit consumer discretionary goods more severely. Technology products,particularly those linked to AI infrastructure and data center buildout,are driven by investment cycles that have longer visibility than consumer spending cycles. Second,it creates a self-reinforcing dynamic:the more China exports integrated circuits and computing equipment,the more it imports the same categories of goods to feed its domestic production and re-export ecosystem. This is not a simple processing trade model;it is a complex,multi-layered integration into global technology supply chains where China occupies both upstream and downstream positions.

The practical consequence for corporate strategists is that China is becoming a more difficult competitor to displace in technology hardware. The combination of scale,infrastructure,and deepening technical capability means that even as trade tensions persist,the cost and complexity of decoupling from China’s technology export ecosystem continue to rise.

## Non-Technology Exports Are Recovering,But the Composition Shift Has Strategic Significance

The improvement in non-technology exports is noteworthy not because it changes the overall trajectory—technology and autos still dominate—but because it broadens the base of China’s external demand resilience. Consumer goods exports turned positive at 4.0% year-on-year after months of contraction. Furniture exports accelerated to 9.0% year-on-year,and home appliances to 15.3% year-on-year. These categories had been in prolonged decline through the second half of 2025,with furniture contracting at double-digit rates in several months.

The recovery appears to be demand-driven rather than price-driven. The sequential improvement in these categories coincided with heatwaves across multiple markets,which likely boosted demand for cooling appliances and related home goods. This is a reminder that China’s export base,while increasingly technology-focused,retains the flexibility to respond to global demand shifts in traditional manufacturing categories.

The strategic implication is more subtle. The recovery in consumer goods exports suggests that China has not ceded its traditional manufacturing strengths even as it pivots to technology. For multinational companies sourcing from China,this means the country remains a reliable supplier across a wide range of categories. For competitors in Southeast Asia and South Asia,it means that China’s market share in low-to-medium technology goods is not eroding as quickly as some models predicted.

## Import Patterns Confirm That China Is Building an AI-Infrastructure Ecosystem,Not Just Consuming Technology

The import data provides the clearest signal of China’s strategic intent. IT-related imports remained the primary driver of overall import growth,which accelerated to 36.0% year-on-year in June. Within this,imports of IT components rose 67.6% year-on-year,led by integrated circuits at 72.6% year-on-year. The standout figure,however,is automatic data-processing equipment imports,which surged 157% year-on-year—an unprecedented pace that points directly to rapid investment in AI data-center infrastructure.

This is not a consumption story. China is importing the capital goods necessary to build out its own AI computing capacity. The scale of these imports suggests that domestic production of advanced computing equipment is not yet sufficient to meet demand,and that Chinese enterprises are racing to deploy AI infrastructure at a pace that outstrips domestic supply capabilities. For global semiconductor and equipment companies,this represents a significant demand driver that is likely to persist as long as the AI investment cycle continues.

The commodity import data reinforces the strategic narrative. Crude oil import volumes fell to one of the lowest levels in recent history,even as natural gas import volumes improved. The decline in crude oil imports likely reflects limited urgency to rebuild inventories,given improved shipping conditions through the Strait of Hormuz. But it also signals that China’s energy demand profile is shifting,with natural gas gaining share and crude oil demand potentially plateauing as the economy becomes more technology-intensive and less energy-intensive per unit of output.

## The External Sector Cannot Rescue the Domestic Economy Alone,Making the July Politburo Meeting a Critical Policy Juncture

The strongest argument for policy action in the near term is not that exports are weak—they are clearly strong—but that they are insufficient to offset the drag from domestic softness. China’s trade surplus widened to over USD 125 billion in June,a record monthly level. This surplus reflects both strong exports and relatively subdued import demand outside of the technology sector. A surplus of this magnitude creates its own risks,including potential trade friction with major partners and upward pressure on the renminbi,which could eventually erode export competitiveness.

The policy calculus is therefore finely balanced. Too much stimulus could overheat the export sector and exacerbate trade tensions. Too little stimulus could allow domestic weakness to spread,eventually pulling down import demand and creating a drag on the global economy. The July Politburo meeting is the natural venue for calibrating this balance. The most likely outcome is targeted fiscal support aimed at domestic consumption and infrastructure investment,combined with continued monetary accommodation to keep financing conditions favorable for the technology sector.

But the direction of travel is clear:China is not just exporting more;it is exporting differently,and that difference matters.

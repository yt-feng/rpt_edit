# Asian Activity: What EMAX proposes, China disposes

Three months into the Middle Eastern conflict, Asian activity is presenting an interesting dichotomy. While China's resilience to the shock is being tested – with weak April data pointing to downside risks to 2Q growth – upside risks have emerged in EMAX as tech tailwinds combine with policy supports to offset energy headwinds.

Bucking the signals imparted by stronger-than-expected PMIs and exports, April domestic activity data disappointed across the board. Industrial production fell 1.0%m/m sa – the sharpest monthly contraction in three years – despite an acceleration in high-tech manufacturing IP. That said, this comes on the back of five successive monthly gains, pushing up the quarterly annualized momentum of IP towards 11% (q/q, saar) before the April print. On its part, the April weakness appears to be related to energy-supply disruptions tied to the Strait of Hormuz closure: crude oil throughput fell and chemicals output – including sulfuric acid, chemical fiber and ethylene – also contracted. Small and private refiners and petrochemical firms rely more on the Strait of Hormuz oil and ostensibly had less flexibility to switch suppliers. Policy settings likely compounded the hit, as the export ban on refined oil exports and the cap on domestic refined oil prices reduced SOE refiners' incentives to run at high-capacity utilization. Higher energy costs and uncertainty over the duration of the Middle East conflict may also have encouraged near-term inventory drawdowns to support the export rebound rather than a production ramp-up.

That said, domestic demand also surprised to the downside. Retail sales softened further amidst a fading policy impulse. Meanwhile, Fixed Asset Investment (FAI) fell 8.0% oya in April underpinned by a broad-based pullback across manufacturing (-4.3% oya), infrastructure (-4.5% oya) and real estate FAI (-20.1% oya). Outside of high-tech manufacturing, investment generally cooled or contracted, amid supply disruptions and margin squeeze from higher raw material costs. Infrastructure FAI weakness was also broad-based across major segments, reflecting slower fiscal outlays and fewer eligible projects. While special CGB issuance picked up in April and May, special LGB issuance slowed materially, leaving year-to-date government bond issuance less front-loaded than we initially expected. Despite the energy shock, policy has remained notably patient versus peers in the region such as Japan and Korea, with no incremental offset and a slowdown after the front-loaded delivery in 1Q.

The pullback in IP and domestic demand creates downside risks to 2Q GDP risks (4.0% q/q saar; 4.9% oya). Meeting our 2Q GDP forecast will now require more than 1% m/m sa IP rebounds in both May and June. If April weakness persists, fiscal delivery could accelerate in coming months. In particular, if 2Q growth materially undershoots the full-year target of 4.5-5%, the case for additional fiscal support in 2H would strengthen, although conviction is lower than in the past given that the target range offers policymakers more flexibility than in prior years.

# Tech exuberance spills over into prices

That said, tech inflation is running at a 70% annualized rate – explaining more than half the recent growth in nominal tech exports – in the three months through March.

# The hawkish tilt in EMAX central banks continues

As the hit from the Middle East conflict is less visible than feared and price pressures continue to build, central banks in the region are tilting hawkish. We have recently added hikes to the Philippines, Korea and Indonesia and signaled the possibility of hikes in Thailand later this year.

While we only expect the Bank of Korea to move later this year, next week's BoK meeting will be particularly significant, marking both the first meeting under the new governor and the first economic outlook update since the Middle East conflict began. In response to the upside surprise to 1Q GDP growth and rising inflationary pressures, we anticipate the BoK will revise up its growth and inflation forecasts and adopt a more hawkish tone in its communication. Although there is likely to be broad agreement on the direction of inflation risks, the magnitude and duration remain uncertain, prompting the BoK to balance preemption with caution.

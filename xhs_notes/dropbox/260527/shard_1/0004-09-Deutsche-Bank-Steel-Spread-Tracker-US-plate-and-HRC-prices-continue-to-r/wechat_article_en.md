# Trade Policy Is Reshaping Steel Margins Faster Than Demand, and Investors Must Rethink Regional Exposure

The global steel market is no longer a single cycle. It has fractured into three distinct regimes, each governed by a different set of drivers. In the United States, prices and spreads continue to climb, supported by pricing discipline and solid lead times. In Europe, policy intervention is beginning to matter more than demand. In China, the world's largest steel producer is seeing export prices soften even as domestic rebar holds. The divergence is not temporary. It reflects a structural reordering of how trade policy, carbon costs, and regional self-sufficiency interact. For investors, the implications are clear: the old playbook of betting on a global steel recovery is obsolete. The new imperative is to identify which regions and producers benefit from policy-driven margin expansion, and which remain exposed to the demand uncertainty that still hangs over the entire sector.

This is not a market where a rising tide lifts all boats. The tide is rising in some ports and receding in others. The report from a global investment bank makes this explicit: Western prices and margins have been firm, and the European Union's plans for a significant step-up in trade protection should ultimately support a sustained rebound in European spreads. The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism adds another layer of support. Yet the same report flags that recent dynamics in the Middle East have started to weigh on sentiment, and a negative impact on demand remains the main concern in the near term. This tension between policy support and demand risk is the central strategic question for steel investors today.

The data from the week of May 15 to May 22 tells the story with precision. US hot-rolled coil prices rose USD13 per tonne to a range of USD1,190-1,205. US plate prices rose a similar amount to USD1,350-1,365. Year to date, US HRC is up USD196 per tonne, and plate is up approximately USD266. In Europe, Northern European HRC was stable at EUR680-695 per tonne, while Southern European prices slipped EUR8. Chinese export HRC prices fell USD5 to USD500-510 per tonne. The spread between German BOF HRC and raw material costs now sits at USD231 per tonne, significantly above mid-cycle levels. The US BOF spread stands at USD638 per tonne. These numbers are not just price movements. They are signals of how different market structures are producing different outcomes.

## The U.S. Market Is Running on Domestic Discipline, Not Global Demand

The most striking feature of the current steel landscape is the resilience of US prices. American HRC has risen 20 percent year to date. Plate has risen 24 percent. This is happening against a backdrop of global macroeconomic uncertainty and softening Chinese export prices. The explanation lies not in demand strength but in structural factors unique to the US market.

Domestic pricing discipline among US mills has been a consistent theme. Lead times remain solid, and producers have shown a willingness to push through price increases even when order books are not stretched. This is a behavioral shift from previous cycles, when US mills would chase volume and undermine their own pricing power. The current discipline suggests that consolidation and the shift toward electric arc furnace production have changed the incentive structure. EAF producers, with their lower capital intensity and greater operational flexibility, are less inclined to run at full utilization just to cover fixed costs. They can afford to hold the line on price.

The raw material cost structure reinforces this advantage. US BOF spread versus key raw materials is USD638 per tonne. The EAF spread, calculated as HRC minus scrap, stands at USD616 per tonne. Both are at levels that would be considered extraordinary in most other regions. The US market is effectively generating super-normal margins for producers who can access domestic scrap or iron ore at competitive rates.

The question that follows is whether these spreads are sustainable. The report notes that Chinese HRC export prices, when adjusted for transport costs and the 50 percent Section 232 duty, imply a US parity price of USD910-930 per tonne. Current US prices are significantly above that level. This means the tariff wall is working exactly as intended: it is shielding domestic producers from low-cost imports. But it also means that US prices are now decoupled from global benchmarks to a degree that invites political and competitive scrutiny. If demand weakens, the gap between US prices and import parity could narrow rapidly. For now, the discipline holds, but investors should watch for any sign that domestic producers begin to compete more aggressively among themselves.

## European Policy Momentum Is Building, But the Demand Signal Remains Muddled

Europe presents a more complex picture. Northern European HRC prices are essentially flat. Southern European prices are down slightly. The BOF spread for German HRC versus raw material costs has increased to USD231 per tonne, which is above mid-cycle levels but far below the US. The real story in Europe is not current pricing but the policy trajectory.

The EU is planning a significant step-up in trade protection. The exact mechanisms are still being debated, but the direction is clear. The combination of safeguard measures, anti-dumping duties, and the phased implementation of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is designed to create a more封闭 market for European steel producers. CBAM is particularly important because it adds a cost to imported steel that was previously externalized. Over time, this should narrow the cost advantage of producers in regions with less stringent carbon regulations.

The impact on spreads could be substantial. The report shows that marginal European producers, when full CO2 costs are included, are operating at essentially zero spread. This is not a sustainable position. As CBAM raises the cost of imports and as trade protection limits volumes, the marginal cost curve should shift upward, allowing more efficient European producers to capture higher margins. This is the logic behind the constructive view on the sector expressed in the report.

Yet the demand side remains the unresolved variable. The report explicitly flags that a negative impact on demand is the main concern in the near term. Geopolitical tensions, particularly in the Middle East, are weighing on sentiment. Industrial production data across Europe has been mixed. The automotive sector, a major consumer of steel, is facing its own structural challenges. Policy can support margins on the supply side, but it cannot create demand where none exists. If European industrial activity slows further, even the most aggressive trade protection may not be enough to sustain spreads at current levels.

This creates a strategic dilemma for investors. The policy tailwind is real and likely to strengthen. But the demand headwind is equally real. The outcome will depend on timing. If demand stabilizes or recovers in the second half of the year, the combination of policy support and cyclical improvement could produce a powerful margin expansion. If demand deteriorates, policy will only limit the downside, not generate upside. The report's constructive stance implies a bet on the former scenario, but the risk is asymmetric.

## Chinese Export Softness Reveals a Structural Overhang That Will Shape Global Prices

The third leg of the global steel market is China, and the signal from Chinese export prices is unambiguous: they are softening. Chinese HRC export prices fell USD5 per tonne to USD500-510. Domestic HRC prices also fell, by RMB72 per tonne to RMB3,413. Rebar, by contrast, rose slightly. The divergence between rebar and HRC within China suggests that construction demand is providing some support, while industrial demand is weaker.

The implications for global markets are significant. Chinese steel exports have been a persistent source of downward pressure on international prices. When Chinese mills are profitable at current export prices, they have an incentive to maintain high export volumes. The report indicates that Chinese export prices have risen USD42 per tonne over three months and USD52 year to date, but the recent week-on-week decline is a warning sign. If Chinese domestic demand continues to soften, mills will have even more incentive to push volumes into export markets.

The US tariff wall largely insulates the American market from this dynamic. Europe is more exposed. The EU's safeguard measures have been effective in limiting Chinese import volumes, but they are not a complete barrier. If Chinese export prices fall further, the pressure on European domestic prices will increase, even with trade protection in place. The report's constructive view on European steel assumes that policy will be sufficient to offset this pressure. That assumption will be tested in the coming months.

For investors, the key variable to watch is the Chinese domestic demand trajectory. The Chinese property sector remains under significant stress. Infrastructure investment has been a stabilizer but is not growing at the pace needed to absorb excess capacity. The Chinese government's policy response will determine whether the steel industry faces a prolonged period of overcapacity or a managed adjustment. Either way, the export channel will be the release valve, and that means Chinese export prices will continue to act as a ceiling on global steel prices outside the protected US market.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered the Question of How Carbon Costs Will Reshape Competitive Dynamics

One of the most consequential developments in the steel industry is the rising cost of carbon emissions, yet the report leaves room for deeper analysis of how this will play out across different producer types. The data on marginal producers with full CO2 cost is revealing. For these producers, the spread between German HRC prices and cash costs is essentially zero. They are operating at breakeven or worse when carbon costs are included.

This creates a clear competitive advantage for producers with lower carbon intensity. Electric arc furnace producers, which use scrap rather than iron ore and coke, have significantly lower carbon emissions per tonne of steel. In Europe, where carbon prices are high and rising, this advantage is becoming a material factor in profitability. The report highlights Aperam and Acerinox as preferred names in stainless steel, in part because of their exposure to attractive markets and solid cash returns. But the carbon angle deserves more explicit attention.

The unanswered question is whether the market will begin to price carbon costs into steel prices more transparently. If buyers start to demand low-carbon steel and are willing to pay a premium for it, the competitive landscape could shift dramatically. Producers with high carbon intensity would face a double penalty: higher costs and potentially lower prices as their product becomes less desirable. This is a second-order effect that the report touches on but does not fully explore.

For investors, this means that simple spread analysis based on raw material costs may be insufficient. A complete assessment of producer profitability must include carbon costs, and more importantly, must consider how those costs will evolve under different policy scenarios. The EU's CBAM is already in motion. Similar mechanisms are being discussed in other regions. The steel industry is at the beginning of a long-term transition that will reward low-carbon producers and penalize laggards.

## A Decision Framework for Steel Investors: Three Questions to Answer Before Allocating Capital

The report provides extensive data on prices, spreads, and costs. But data without a framework is noise. For serious investors, the challenge is to translate this information into actionable decisions. The following framework is designed to do exactly that.

First, determine your regional exposure. The US, Europe, and China are no longer part of a single steel cycle. They are three distinct markets with different drivers and different risk profiles. The US offers high spreads and policy protection, but at the cost of potential vulnerability to domestic demand shocks and political risk around tariffs. Europe offers policy momentum and the potential for margin expansion, but with significant demand uncertainty and the overhang of Chinese exports. China offers volume but at compressed margins and with structural overcapacity. The right allocation depends on your view of these three factors.

Second, assess producer type within each region. Not all steel companies are created equal. BOF producers are exposed to iron ore and coking coal costs. EAF producers are exposed to scrap prices. Within Europe, producers with lower carbon intensity have a structural advantage that will grow over time. Within the US, the advantage of domestic scrap availability is already priced into spreads. The report's top picks in carbon steel include Voestalpine, which is described as the most solid equity story based on steady free cash flow generation and an undervalued rail-infrastructure business, and thyssenkrupp, on possible catalysts and policy tailwinds. In stainless steel, Acerinox is preferred for its US exposure and solid cash returns, while Aperam is favored for its reversion potential and organic growth. These picks reflect a judgment about which producers are best positioned within the current regime.

Third, stress-test your assumptions about demand. The report flags demand as the key concern, and it is the variable most likely to surprise. A scenario where global industrial activity slows more than expected would hurt all regions, but it would hurt Europe most because European spreads are more dependent on policy support than on structural cost advantages. A scenario where demand stabilizes or improves would amplify the benefits of policy support in Europe and pricing discipline in the US. The range of outcomes is wide, and the correct investment strategy depends on where you think the demand trajectory is headed.

ZH_B: 报告特意提到了这一点。这些自动化公司普遍没有看到客户出现异常的“提前订单”行为，所以现在的需求大部分是真实的需求，而不是库存堆积。这意味着周期的健康度比较高。

ZH_A: 那这个周期的顶点大概会在什么时候？

ZH_B: 报告给出了一个具体的预测框架：中国的增长峰值可能在2026年上半年，而全球的峰值要到2027年第一季度。而且报告强调，上行周期在见顶之后还会持续超过一年。

ZH_A: 除了周期判断，报告还提到了一个很热的概念：物理AI。这和我们平时聊的大语言模型AI有什么不同？

ZH_B: 物理AI简单来说就是把AI能力放到机器人身上，让它们能感知、能决策、能自主行动。报告特别关注了发那科在这个领域的进展。

ZH_A: 发那科做了什么特别的事情？

ZH_B: 报告提到，发那科在去年12月推出了一款机器人，几个月内就收到了数千台的订单。相比之下，安川电机在类似产品发布两年半后，才收到了大约200台的订单。差距非常明显。

ZH_A: 那发那科是怎么做到这种领先的？

ZH_B: 报告分析了两点。第一，发那科的所有机器人，不是某一个特定系列，而是全部都能接入英伟达和谷歌的物理AI平台。第二，发那科对物理AI的定位很务实，它不认为这是颠覆性的技术或短期增长引擎，而是把它看作一个持续扩展机器人应用边界的工具。

ZH_A: 所以物理AI不是要取代现有机器人，而是让机器人能做更多事情？

ZH_B: 没错。报告还提到了一个更具体的方向：人形机器人正在走向商业应用的首个阶段。

ZH_A: 人形机器人？之前总觉得那是实验室里的东西。

ZH_B: 报告走访发现，人形机器人的第一个杀手级应用正在仓库和工厂里出现，主要是各种物料搬运。根据中国厂商的说法，人形机器人的数量增长已经超过了每年200%。

ZH_A: 那它是不是会取代现在工厂里那些传统的工业机器人？

ZH_B: 报告给出的结论很明确：人形机器人是补充，不是替代。报告举了一个非常生动的例子：在发那科自己的工厂里，机器人已经在制造机器人了，这已经是全球自动化程度最高的场景之一，但人类工人仍然在做包装、装卸、检测和线束安装这些工作。

ZH_A: 这些工作为什么自动化不了？

ZH_B: 因为现有的自动化设备很难处理这些需要高度灵活性和判断力的任务，而这正是人形机器人未来可以填补的空白。报告还提到，一些自动化零部件公司正在调整策略，从卖单个的减速器、电机，转向卖集成的驱动模组，甚至直接做整机机器人。这么做是为了保护利润和加速应用开发。

ZH_A: 听起来像是整个产业链都在为下一个阶段做准备。那报告里有没有提到竞争格局的变化？比如中国公司对日本公司造成了什么样的压力？

ZH_B: 报告专门有一段讨论了这个问题。所有日本自动化公司都承认中国技术确实在进步。但有意思的是，基恩士和发那科通过持续创新，保持了稳定的市场份额，让自己成了竞争对手很难追上的“移动靶”。

ZH_A: 那其他日本公司呢？

ZH_B: 安川电机选择牺牲市场份额来保住利润。欧姆龙则推出了针对中国市场的“低端”SKU，希望夺回份额。但报告对欧姆龙的策略表示怀疑，认为这不一定有效。

ZH_A: 所以创新才是护城河。那报告里还提到了一个很关键的指标：定价能力和利润率。在这方面，哪些公司表现最好？

ZH_B: 报告指出，在成本上升、通胀和关税压力下，公司的定价能力出现了明显的分化。最优秀的创新者，比如基恩士和发那科，即使处于分散的市场中，也能把增加的成本完全转嫁出去。而像SMC和Harmonic Drive，虽然所在市场更集中，却只能部分转嫁成本。

ZH_A: 这意味着什么？

ZH_B: 这意味着定价能力不完全取决于市场集中度，而是取决于你的产品是否真正不可替代。报告还用利润率数据做了一张表，基恩士和亚德客的利润率在周期中一直非常稳定，明显优于其他公司。

EN_A: So you just came back from a tour of 14 automation companies in China and Japan. What’s the single biggest takeaway from that trip?

EN_B: The biggest takeaway is that the global factory automation upcycle is real, it’s broad-based, and it’s accelerating. And this isn’t a short-lived spike. The report suggests this cycle will last more than a year after it peaks.

EN_A: That’s a strong call. But what’s the evidence? How do we know this isn’t just noise from a few companies?

EN_B: Well, we visited some of the biggest names in the industry. In China, Inovance reported April orders up 40% year-over-year. And Japanese companies like Keyence and FANUC all confirmed the strength. This was in April, when China’s fixed asset investment data was actually weak. So the demand is quite resilient.

EN_A: 40% is huge. But sometimes companies order ahead of time just to build inventory. Is there any sign of that kind of “pull-forward” demand?

EN_B: That’s a great question, and the report specifically addresses it. The companies generally said they haven’t seen any abnormal “advance orders.” So the current demand is mostly real, end-user demand, not inventory building. That makes the cycle much healthier.

EN_A: Okay, so real demand. When do you think this cycle tops out?

EN_B: The report provides a clear framework. It expects the peak in China to be in the first half of 2026. Globally, the peak is expected in the first quarter of 2027. And again, the upcycle is expected to last more than a year after that peak.

EN_A: Let’s talk about another big theme in the report: Physical AI. How is that different from the AI we talk about in software?

EN_B: Physical AI is about putting intelligence into machines so they can perceive, decide, and act in the physical world. The report highlights FANUC as a clear leader here. They’re not just talking about it; they’re actually doing it.

EN_A: What did FANUC do that stands out?

EN_B: They launched a new robot last December. Within a few months, they received orders for thousands of units. For comparison, Yaskawa launched a similar product and got only about 200 orders over two and a half years. The gap is enormous.

EN_A: What makes FANUC so different?

EN_B: The report points to two key things. First, all of FANUC’s robots, not just a specific line, can work with Physical AI platforms from companies like Nvidia and Google. Second, FANUC sees Physical AI not as a disruptive technology or a quick growth hack, but as a complementary tool to expand what robots can do and make them easier for new customers to use.

EN_A: So it’s more about evolution than revolution. What about humanoid robots? Is that still just a lab experiment?

EN_B: Not anymore. The report says we’re seeing the first commercial applications. The first “killer apps” are in warehouses and factories for material handling. And based on comments from Chinese players, the volume of humanoid robots is growing more than 200% year-over-year.

EN_A: So are humanoid robots going to replace the industrial robots we already have in factories?

EN_B: No, and the report is very clear on this. They are complementary, not a replacement. The report gives a great example. In FANUC’s own factory, which is probably one of the most automated in the world, robots are making other robots. But human workers are still there doing packaging, loading, inspection, and wiring. Those tasks are very hard to automate with existing equipment. That’s where humanoids will fit in.

EN_A: So the industry is preparing for this shift. How are component suppliers adapting?

EN_B: They are moving from selling individual parts like reducers and motors to selling integrated actuator modules, or even entire robots. Companies like Harmonic Drive and Inovance are doing this to protect their margins and speed up application development.

EN_A: Let’s talk about competition. Chinese companies are getting better. How are the Japanese giants responding?

EN_B: All the Japanese companies acknowledge that Chinese technology is improving. But the response varies. Keyence and FANUC are maintaining their market share through constant innovation. They make themselves a moving target. Yaskawa is letting its market share decline to protect its margins. Omron is trying a different approach, launching low-end products just for the Chinese market. But the report is skeptical that will work.

EN_A: So innovation is the real moat. What about pricing power? Who can actually raise prices?

EN_B: This is where the report gets really interesting. It shows that pricing power is not just about market concentration. The best innovators, like Keyence and FANUC, can pass on all their cost increases, even in fragmented markets. But companies like SMC and Harmonic Drive, even in more concentrated markets, can only pass on part of the cost.

EN_A: What does that tell us?

Keyence and AirTAC have been very stable. Others, like SMC, have seen their margins erode without a clear plan to fix it. Harmonic Drive is trying, but getting back to their old high levels is going to be very difficult.

# Bank Stocks Are Not Just Cheap—They Are Structurally Repricing for a New Era, and the Mid-Year Check-In Confirms the Thesis Is Still Building

The first half of 2026 has been a study in cognitive dissonance for US bank investors. On one hand, the fundamental narrative has rarely been more supportive. Industry loan growth accelerated to 6 percent year-over-year in the first quarter, with large caps posting 9 percent growth. Net interest income has proven resilient even as rate-cut expectations have been pushed further into 2027. Investment banking revenue surged 30 percent year-over-year, and trading revenue rose 17 percent. Earnings per share revisions have been positive. Yet bank stocks, particularly the large-cap money center banks, have underperformed the S&P 500 by more than 650 basis points year-to-date. Something is breaking in the transmission mechanism between earnings power and stock prices.

The disconnect is not a mystery. It is the result of a concentrated volley of negative headlines that have landed on the sector since January: the proposed cap on credit card interest rates, renewed concerns about non-bank lending and credit quality, the specter of artificial intelligence disruption in wealth management, and the resurfacing of funding-model anxiety driven by the CLARITY Act. Each of these headlines has been absorbed by the market as a reason to discount bank earnings, even as the underlying data has continued to improve. The mid-year check-in from a major global investment bank makes this tension explicit. The report revisits ten questions first posed in January and finds that while most of the core answers remain unchanged, the conviction on several has shifted from "Yes" to "Maybe." That shift is not a sign of weakness in the thesis. It is a sign that the market is now pricing in a more complex, multi-dimensional reality—one that rewards selectivity, patience, and a willingness to look through headline noise.

The central argument of this article is that the current underperformance is a structural repricing opportunity, not a cyclical deterioration. The banking sector is undergoing its most significant regulatory and competitive transformation since the Great Financial Crisis. The three Rs—Regulation, Rates, Rebound—remain intact, but they are being tested by stagflation risks and a rate environment that, while higher for longer, is creating pressure on net interest margins and rate-sensitive sectors like housing and commercial real estate. The market is waiting for macro clarity. When it arrives, likely after Labor Day and into the mid-term election window, the combination of single-digit forward price-to-earnings multiples and double-digit EPS growth could drive a powerful re-rating. The report's historical analogy to the late 1990s and early 2000s is compelling: banks outperformed the S&P 500 for three consecutive years in both periods, driven by de-regulation, a strong economy, and tech-led productivity gains. The conditions today are more similar to those eras than at any point since the crisis.

This article will unpack the report's findings across five dimensions: the structural case for a re-rating, the divergent performance between capital markets and Main Street banks, the regulatory tailwind that is still underappreciated, the unresolved question of AI disruption, and a decision framework for investors trying to navigate the second half of 2026. The goal is not to summarize the report but to extend its logic into actionable strategic insight.

## The Re-Rating Case Rests on a Structural Discount That Has Not Been This Wide Since the 1990s

The most important insight from the mid-year check-in is not about earnings or loan growth. It is about valuation. Large-cap banks currently trade at 9 to 10 times forward 2027 earnings per share, a discount to the S&P 500 that has not been this pronounced since the early 1990s. The report shows that the spread between the S&P 500 ex-Magnificent Seven and the BKX index has widened to roughly 8 to 9 times, a level that historically has preceded multi-year periods of bank outperformance. The logic is straightforward: when the discount becomes this extreme, it reflects not just cyclical caution but a structural pessimism that may be overdone.

The report draws a direct parallel to the 1990s, when bank price-to-earnings ratios expanded by approximately 7 times over a five-year period. The catalysts then were the lifting of interstate banking restrictions, a booming US economy, and the early stages of internet-led productivity gains. The catalysts today are strikingly similar: the unwinding of punitive post-GFC regulations, fiscal stimulus supporting GDP growth, and a generational capex cycle driven by artificial intelligence. The report notes that bank EPS growth in the late 1990s was low double-digit, and that the same trajectory is plausible now. The difference is that the market is not yet pricing that growth into multiples.

What makes this moment distinct from the post-GFC era is that banks have regained control over their capital management. For nearly 15 years, unpredictable regulatory changes eroded the sector's competitiveness and compressed valuations. The March 2026 capital proposals, which were largely as expected by the industry, have removed a major source of uncertainty. The report describes the excess capital unlock at regional banks as a positive surprise. This is not a minor detail. It means that banks can now deploy capital more freely into buybacks, dividends, and organic growth—actions that directly support EPS and returns on equity. The market has not yet fully discounted this shift.

The so-what is clear: investors who wait for macro clarity to arrive before acting will likely pay a higher price. The re-rating will not be gradual. It will occur when the market collectively decides that the headline risks are manageable and that the earnings trajectory is real. The report's mid-year check-in suggests that moment is approaching, not receding.

## Capital Markets Banks Have Led, but the Second Half of 2026 May Belong to Main Street

Year-to-date performance dispersion within the banking sector has been unusually wide. Capital markets pure plays—GS, MS, and the trust banks—have outperformed, while money center banks and super-regionals have lagged. The report shifts its view on regional banks versus global systemically important banks from "No" in January to "Maybe" now. This is not a flip. It is a recognition that the drivers of performance are becoming increasingly idiosyncratic rather than size-driven.

The logic is as follows. Capital markets momentum has been extraordinarily strong, and the report expects it to continue. Investment banking backlogs, M&A pipelines, IPO activity, and debt issuance all point to a sustained tailwind. The pure plays are well-positioned to capture this momentum, and the report favors them on the back of positive EPS revision cycles and improving capital flexibility. The trust banks, particularly BNY, are singled out as beneficiaries of both capital markets activity and structural trends in AI and digital asset adoption.

But the second half of 2026 could see a rotation. The report notes that regional banks are awaiting macro clarity that could drive a re-rating. Domestic capex momentum, combined with improving capital flexibility from the Standardized Approach capital proposals, underpins a forecast for double-digit EPS growth in both 2026 and 2027. The regional banks are trading at even deeper discounts than the large caps, and they offer more direct exposure to the domestic economy. If the macro narrative shifts from stagflation fear to growth confidence, the regional cohort could reassert itself over Wall Street.

The key question for investors is whether the capital markets momentum is sustainable enough to justify current valuations at the pure plays, or whether the discount at regionals represents a better risk-reward. The report does not resolve this tension, but it provides the framework: the second half will be about Main Street reasserting itself, provided the macro data cooperates. The decision hinges on whether one believes the domestic capex cycle is broadening beyond AI and data centers, as the report suggests is beginning to happen.

## The Regulatory Tailwind Is Real, but Its Full Impact Depends on Tailoring and Liquidity Rules

The report is unambiguous on regulation: the answer to whether regulatory shift will remain a tailwind is "Yes," unchanged from January. The capital proposals have been largely as expected, and the excess capital unlock at regionals has been a positive surprise. But the report also identifies the next frontier: tailoring and liquidity rules. These are the areas where the most consequential changes may still lie ahead.

The strategic implication is that the regulatory tailwind is not a single event but a multi-year process. The initial wave of clarity on capital has already been absorbed. The next wave—which could involve more nuanced treatment of liquidity requirements and a narrowing of the regulatory arbitrage gap between banks and non-banks—has the potential to further improve the competitive position of regulated entities. The report notes that the regulatory arbitrage gap is narrowing, which is a structural positive for banks that have been losing market share to non-bank lenders and fintechs.

What the report does not fully answer is how quickly these changes will materialize and whether the political calendar will accelerate or delay them. The 2028 election window is cited as a potential catalyst for M&A acceleration, but the same logic applies to regulatory finality. Investors should expect a series of incremental steps rather than a single transformative event. The tailwind is real, but it requires patience.

## The AI Question Is the Most Unresolved and the Most Consequential for Long-Term Positioning

The report shifts its view on whether bank stocks will become an AI play from "No" in January to "Maybe" now. This is the most intellectually honest answer in the entire check-in. The narrative around AI and banking has been skewed toward disruption: job displacement, deposit disintermediation, and the obsolescence of software-driven revenue streams like wealth management. The report acknowledges this but argues that banks are likely to be net beneficiaries of AI-driven productivity gains.

The evidence is still early. The report points to the historical analogy of the late 1990s, when banks outperformed during the initial internet-led productivity boom. The argument is that banks are large-scale data processors, and AI is a data-processing technology. The potential for cost reduction, risk management improvement, and revenue enhancement is significant. But the report is careful not to overclaim. It explicitly states that a structural return-on-equity shift is unlikely. AI will improve margins at the margin, but it will not transform the fundamental economics of banking.

The unresolved question is whether the market will eventually reward banks for their AI investments or continue to penalize them for the perceived disruption risk. The report does not provide a clear answer, and that is appropriate. The data is not yet there. What the report does provide is a framework: banks that are best positioned for AI adoption—particularly those with large scale, significant data assets, and a clear technology strategy—are likely to emerge as winners. The trust banks, with their custody and asset-servicing businesses, are cited as being particularly well-positioned. The pure-play investment banks, with their capital markets and wealth management franchises, face a more complex calculus.

For investors, the AI question is not a near-term catalyst. It is a five-to-ten-year structural theme that will separate winners from losers. The report's shift from "No" to "Maybe" is a signal that the market is beginning to engage with this question more seriously. The full answer will emerge only as banks report more granular data on AI-related cost savings and revenue opportunities.

## A Decision Framework for the Second Half: Three Questions Every Bank Investor Must Answer

The report's ten questions provide a comprehensive diagnostic, but they do not directly translate into a portfolio decision. The following framework is designed to help investors translate the report's insights into actionable choices.

First, what is your macro view? If you believe the economy is heading toward stagflation, the bank thesis breaks down. Higher-for-longer rates will pressure net interest margins, credit quality will deteriorate, and the rebounding capital markets will not be enough to offset the damage. If you believe the economy is heading toward a soft landing or a mild acceleration, the bank thesis is intact, and the current valuation discount represents a compelling entry point. The report's base case is the latter, but it explicitly acknowledges the stagflation risk.

Second, which segment of the banking universe aligns with your risk tolerance? Capital markets pure plays offer momentum and EPS revision upside but trade at higher multiples and are exposed to market volatility. Regional banks offer deeper discounts and direct exposure to the domestic economy but require macro clarity to re-rate. Money center banks offer a hybrid exposure but are caught between the crosscurrents of capital markets strength and consumer slowdown concerns. There is no one-size-fits-all answer. The report favors capital markets momentum in the near term but sees regional banks as the more compelling opportunity for the second half.

Third, how do you weigh the AI question? If you believe AI disruption is overhyped, the current discount on large-cap banks is a gift. If you believe AI disruption is underappreciated, the discount is a value trap. The report leans toward the former but does not dismiss the latter. The most prudent approach is to favor banks with clear AI adoption strategies and diversified revenue streams that are less exposed to disintermediation risk.

This framework does not produce a single answer. It forces a decision based on conviction. The report's value is that it provides the data and the historical context to make that decision with more confidence.

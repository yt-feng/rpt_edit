# Earnings Growth Is Still Driving the Market — But the Path from Here Requires a Strategy, Not a Forecast

The single most important fact about the current US equity market is this: earnings growth has mathematically accounted for the entire S&P 500 return over the past two years. The index has risen roughly 40% since May 2024, and the forward P/E multiple today is exactly what it was then: 21x. Every dollar of price appreciation has been backed by a dollar of earnings improvement. This is not a valuation-driven rally. It is a fundamentally anchored one, and that distinction matters enormously for how investors should think about the next 12 to 18 months.

A global investment bank recently raised its S&P 500 year-end target to 8000, implying a 6% return from current levels, and increased its 2026 and 2027 EPS forecasts to $340 and $385 respectively. The argument is straightforward: continued earnings growth should drive continued equity market upside. But the report is careful to note that the path will be bumpy, that valuation multiples are unlikely to expand, and that several conditions historically associated with the end of high-concentration bull markets are beginning to emerge — even if they remain mostly absent today.

The question for serious investors is not whether the market can go higher. The question is what kind of market this will be: one where broad-based earnings gains lift all boats, or one where a narrow set of winners must carry an increasingly heavy burden. The answer will determine not only portfolio construction but also risk management, sector allocation, and the role of optionality in a strategy.

This article unpacks the core logic of that report, extends its reasoning into second-order implications, and builds a decision framework for navigating what comes next. It does not summarize. It argues.

## The Market Has Already Transitioned From Multiple Expansion to Earnings-Driven Returns — And That Changes the Rules of Engagement

For much of the post-pandemic period, equity returns were powered by a combination of earnings recovery and multiple expansion. Low interest rates, fiscal stimulus, and a benign inflation outlook allowed investors to pay higher prices for future earnings. That regime is over. Year to date, the S&P 500 has risen 10%, forward EPS estimates have risen 15%, and the P/E multiple has actually declined by 4%. The arithmetic is unambiguous: every dollar of price gain has come from earnings, not sentiment.

This shift has profound implications. In a multiple-expansion environment, the best strategy is often to own the assets with the highest beta to falling discount rates — long-duration growth stocks, speculative technology names, and levered plays on macro recovery. In an earnings-driven market, the winning strategy is to own the companies delivering the strongest upward earnings revisions, regardless of sector or style. The report confirms this pattern: stocks with the strongest earnings revisions have generally outperformed year to date, and this is expected to continue.

The implication is that macro forecasting becomes less important than micro stock selection. Investors who spend their time debating the Fed's next move or the trajectory of GDP growth may be missing the point. The market is rewarding companies that can demonstrate real, measurable earnings improvement. The burden of proof has shifted from narrative to numbers.

## The AI Infrastructure Trade Is Not a Bubble — But It Is Creating a Concentration Risk That Demands Active Hedging

One of the most contentious debates in markets today is whether the AI infrastructure build-out represents a sustainable investment theme or a speculative mania. The report provides a data-driven answer: it is neither pure mania nor pure safety. Since the start of the year, S&P 500 stocks involved in the AI infrastructure complex have returned 33%, but their consensus forward earnings have risen 30%. The price move is almost entirely explained by earnings revisions. This is not the pattern of a bubble. It is the pattern of a trade that is being validated by fundamentals.

However, the report also flags a critical risk: the very success of this trade raises the bar going forward. The beneficiaries of AI infrastructure investment will account for roughly half of S&P 500 EPS growth this year. That means the market's fate is increasingly tied to a narrow set of companies — hyperscalers, semiconductor firms, power infrastructure plays, and data center operators. If those companies fail to deliver on elevated expectations, the entire index could suffer.

This is where the historical analogies become useful but also dangerous. The report notes that current market valuations, concentration, and recent returns are reminiscent of past overextended markets — 1929, 1973, 1987, 2000, and 2021. But it also points out that the conditions that marked the ends of those bull markets — speculative mania and deteriorating macro fundamentals — are mostly absent today. The Speculative Trading Indicator remains well below prior highs. IPO activity, while increasing, has been modest. Retail trading is elevated but not extreme.

The conclusion is nuanced: the AI trade is not a bubble, but it is a concentrated bet that requires active risk management. Investors should own exposure to earnings-driven upside in this space, but they should also balance it with equities that have earnings tailwinds and minimal correlation to the AI trade. The report calls this an "insensitive portfolio," and the logic is sound. Concentration is not the same as speculation, but it carries its own risks.

## The Macro Environment Is Supportive But Not Benign — And the Oil Shock Could Be the Catalyst That Changes Everything

The report's base case is constructive: continued earnings growth, moderate investor sentiment, and a valuation multiple that remains stable around 21x. But it also identifies a specific macro risk that could disrupt this outlook: the oil shock resulting from the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Higher energy prices should result in weaker consumer spending, more pressure on profit margins, higher inflation, and less Fed easing than previously expected.

This is not a forecast. It is a scenario analysis. But it is a scenario that investors must take seriously because it directly threatens the two pillars of the current bull market: earnings growth and stable valuations. If oil prices remain elevated, the combination of disappointing growth and tightening financial conditions could create exactly the conditions that have marked the ends of previous bull markets.

The report's economists maintain a constructive base case, but the downside risks are real. And here is where the report leaves an important question unanswered: how much oil price increase would be required to tip the economy into a recessionary regime? The report does not provide a threshold. It does not model the transmission mechanism in detail. It simply flags the risk and moves on. For serious investors, this is precisely the kind of question that demands further analysis. The full report may contain more detail, but the public version leaves room for independent scenario planning.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: How to Distinguish Between a Correction and a Regime Change

The report is excellent at describing the current state of the market and the most likely path forward. But it is less explicit about how investors should distinguish between a normal correction — the kind that happens in every bull market — and a genuine regime change that would require a fundamental shift in strategy.

Consider the following: the report notes that Momentum-driven rallies and midterm election seasonality both argue for moderating returns in the coming months. It also notes that investor sentiment remains moderate and that an improvement in the geopolitical outlook would extend the upswing. These are tactical observations, not strategic ones. But the distinction between a tactical pullback and a structural downturn is precisely what determines whether an investor should hedge, reduce exposure, or simply ride out the volatility.

The report does not provide a clear framework for making that distinction. It does not identify specific thresholds for the Speculative Trading Indicator, the breadth of earnings revisions, or the level of the P/E multiple that would signal a shift from a healthy bull market to an overextended one. This is not a criticism. It is an acknowledgment that the most important questions in investing are often the ones that cannot be answered with precision. But it does mean that readers must supplement the report's analysis with their own judgment.

## A Decision Framework for the Next 12 Months: Three Questions Every Investor Should Answer

Based on the report's logic, here is a practical decision framework for investors. It does not provide a single answer. Instead, it forces a series of choices that will determine portfolio construction and risk management.

First, what is your view on earnings revisions breadth? The report argues that stocks with the strongest earnings revisions will continue to outperform. But breadth matters. If the number of companies with positive revisions begins to shrink, the market becomes more fragile. Investors should monitor the ratio of positive to negative revisions and be prepared to reduce exposure if that ratio turns negative for two consecutive months.

Second, how do you assess the AI concentration risk? The report recommends balancing AI exposure with low-correlation earnings plays. But the size of that balance depends on your conviction in the AI theme. If you believe the infrastructure build-out is in its early stages, you might accept higher concentration. If you believe the bar is already too high, you should reduce exposure and increase diversification. There is no right answer, but there is a wrong one: ignoring the question entirely.

Third, what is your oil price scenario? The report flags the oil shock as a risk but does not quantify it. Investors should define their own threshold — say, a sustained price above $100 per barrel — and decide in advance what actions they would take. Would they reduce equity exposure? Rotate into energy stocks? Buy put options on the S&P 500? The time to make these decisions is now, not when the price is already moving.

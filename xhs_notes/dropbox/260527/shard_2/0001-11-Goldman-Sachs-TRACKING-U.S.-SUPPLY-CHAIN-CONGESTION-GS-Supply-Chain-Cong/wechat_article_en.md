# The U.S. Supply Chain Is Not Congested, But That Is Not the Same as Being Stable

The most important signal in the latest U.S. supply chain tracking data is not the congestion itself, but what the absence of congestion reveals about the underlying structure of freight markets. The weekly bottleneck scale remains at 2 on a 10-point scale, a level that is statistically indistinguishable from the pre-COVID baseline. The composite index rose 10% week-over-week, but this is a blip, not a breakout. Any executive who interprets this as a return to normalcy is making a category error.

The data tells a story of a supply chain that has healed from the acute trauma of 2021-2022, but has not yet stabilized into a predictable equilibrium. The index is low, but the volatility in individual metrics is higher than what a truly fluid system would produce. West Coast rail intermodal traffic growth accelerated sharply to +9% year-over-year, while ocean shipping rate growth decelerated from +18% to +14% year-over-year. Container ship backlogs on the East Coast ticked up from 4 to 5. These are not the movements of a system at rest.

The real strategic question is not whether congestion will return, but whether the current state of low congestion is durable. The answer depends on factors that the tracking data alone cannot resolve: tariff policy, geopolitical conflict, and the structural reshaping of global trade routes. For investors and supply chain executives, the low congestion environment is an opportunity to prepare for scenarios that the index does not yet capture.

## The Composite Index Masks a Growing Divergence Between West Coast and East Coast Dynamics

The headline weekly composite index increased 10% week-over-week, which is notable but not alarming. What is more significant is the divergence in the underlying components. West Coast container ship backlogs moved from 0 to 1, a trivial change in absolute terms but one that breaks a pattern of zero backlogs that had persisted for several weeks. East Coast backlogs rose from 4 to 5, marking a sustained elevation relative to the West Coast.

This divergence matters because it reflects a structural shift in port utilization patterns that predates the current low-congestion environment. During the peak congestion era of 2021-2022, West Coast ports were the primary bottleneck, with dozens of ships waiting at anchor off Los Angeles and Long Beach. The East Coast was a relief valve. Today, that dynamic has inverted. East Coast ports are showing persistent, low-level congestion while West Coast ports are operating at near-perfect fluidity.

The implication is that the supply chain has rebalanced, but not symmetrically. Shippers have permanently diversified their port strategies, and the East Coast is absorbing a larger share of traffic than it was designed to handle. The absolute level of congestion is low, but the East Coast is operating closer to its capacity ceiling than the headline index suggests. A modest demand shock or a disruption at a single East Coast port could produce a localized bottleneck that the national index would understate.

## Rail Intermodal Traffic Growth Is Accelerating, But Velocity and Dwell Tell a Different Story

West Coast Class 1 rail intermodal traffic growth accelerated sharply in the most recent week, with average year-over-year growth of 9% compared to 3% in the prior week. Union Pacific traffic growth swung from -0.8% to +8.4% year-over-year, while BNSF traffic growth moved from +7.8% to +9.7%. On its face, this looks like a demand recovery.

But the service metrics do not align. Union Pacific intermodal velocity growth decelerated from 2.5% to 4.2% year-over-year, while BNSF velocity actually declined, moving from -0.6% to -3.7% year-over-year. System dwell times were essentially flat for Union Pacific at 19.9 hours, but rose for BNSF from 22.3 to 23.0 hours. More volume with slower velocity and higher dwell is not a sign of a system operating efficiently. It is a sign of a system absorbing more traffic at the cost of operational quality.

The disconnect between volume and service metrics is a classic leading indicator of congestion. When volume growth outpaces velocity improvement, the system is filling up. The question is whether the system has enough slack to absorb further volume increases before service degradation triggers a self-reinforcing cycle of delays and inventory hoarding. The current data suggests that slack is diminishing, even if the absolute level of congestion remains low.

## Ocean Shipping Rate Growth Is Decelerating, But the Base Effect Creates a False Sense of Calm

Ocean container shipping rates from East Asia to the U.S. West Coast grew 14% year-over-year in the most recent week, down from 18% in the prior week. This deceleration is consistent with a market that is finding equilibrium after a period of tariff-driven front-loading and inventory rebalancing.

But the year-over-year comparison is misleading. The base period includes weeks when rates were already declining from their pandemic peaks. A 14% year-over-year increase in a low-congestion environment is not normal. It suggests that the structural floor for ocean rates has shifted upward, even if the cyclical peak has passed. The question is whether this new floor is durable or whether it reflects temporary factors such as rerouting around geopolitical hotspots and carrier capacity discipline.

The deceleration in rate growth is a positive signal for importers, but it is not a signal that rates will return to pre-pandemic levels. The supply chain is operating at a higher cost base than it was in 2019, and that cost base is unlikely to deflate significantly. The implication for investors is that transportation cost inflation is structurally higher, even in a low-congestion environment.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer Why the Composite Index Rose 10% Week-Over-Week Despite All Individual Metrics Being Mixed

The weekly composite index increased 10% week-over-week, yet the individual metrics that comprise the index were mixed. Container ship backlogs increased on the East Coast but were flat on the West Coast. Rail traffic accelerated, but velocity and dwell deteriorated. Ocean rate growth decelerated. Chassis dwell times were unchanged on average.

This pattern suggests that the composite index may be capturing something that the individual metrics do not fully explain. It is possible that the index is picking up a compositional shift in the types of goods moving through the supply chain, or a change in the timing of shipments that is not visible in the weekly data. It is also possible that the index is overweighting certain metrics that are more volatile than others, producing a composite signal that is not fully representative of the underlying system state.

The report does not provide a decomposition of the composite index to explain which metrics drove the week-over-week increase. This is a meaningful analytical gap. Without this decomposition, it is difficult to determine whether the 10% increase is a signal of emerging congestion or simply noise in a low-volatility environment. For serious analysts, this gap means that the composite index should be used as a directional guide, not a precise measurement.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Low-Congestion but High-Uncertainty Environment

The current supply chain state presents a strategic paradox: the system is not congested, but the factors that could produce congestion are more numerous and less predictable than they were a year ago. Executives and investors need a framework for making decisions under this type of uncertainty, not a forecast of where the index will be next month.

The framework has three layers. The first layer is monitoring for inflection points in the metrics that have historically been leading indicators of congestion. Rail intermodal velocity and dwell are the most important of these. If velocity growth turns negative while dwell rises, the system is filling up. The second layer is scenario planning for exogenous shocks that the index cannot predict. Tariff changes, geopolitical disruptions, and labor actions at ports are all events that can produce congestion in a matter of days, not weeks. The third layer is stress-testing inventory and logistics strategies against a range of congestion outcomes, not just the current low-congestion baseline.

The key insight is that the absence of congestion today does not mean the system is resilient. It means the system has slack. The strategic imperative is to use that slack to build optionality, not to optimize for current conditions. Companies that treat the current environment as a permanent state will be caught off guard when the next disruption occurs. Companies that treat it as a window for preparation will have a structural advantage.

## The Supply Chain Is Not Broken, But It Is Not Fixed Either

The data shows a supply chain that has recovered from the acute crisis of 2021-2022 but has not returned to the steady state of 2019. The bottleneck scale at 2 is a measure of fluidity, not stability. The system is moving, but it is moving with less predictability and more fragility than the headline index suggests.

The unresolved analytical questions are what it would take for the index to move from 2 to 1, and whether that move is even desirable. A scale of 1 would imply a system operating at maximum efficiency with zero slack. That is not a state that is sustainable in the face of any disruption. A scale of 2 may be the optimal operating point for a system that needs to absorb periodic shocks without breaking. The question is whether the system can stay at 2 without tipping back toward 3 or 4.

For now, the data provides no clear answer. The composite index rose, but the individual metrics were mixed. Rail traffic accelerated, but service quality deteriorated. Ocean rate growth decelerated, but rates remain elevated. The system is not congested, but it is not stable either. That is the strategic reality that executives and investors must navigate.

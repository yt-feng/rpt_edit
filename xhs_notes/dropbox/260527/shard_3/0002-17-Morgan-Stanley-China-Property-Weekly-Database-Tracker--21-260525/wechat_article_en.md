# China’s Property Market Is Not Recovering — It Is Reorganizing, and the Divergence Between Primary and Secondary Sales Is the Most Important Signal

The narrative that China’s property market is experiencing a broad-based recovery is misleading. The latest weekly data from 50 cities reveals a structural divergence that tells a more complex and strategic story: primary market sales are declining 9% year-on-year, while secondary market sales are surging 45%. This is not a synchronized recovery. It is a market in the midst of a profound reorganization of liquidity, risk appetite, and buyer behavior. The sell-through rate of 66% in Tier 1 cities, combined with a declining asking price index, suggests that price discovery is still underway, and the traditional model of relying on new home sales as a bellwether for the sector is no longer sufficient. Investors who focus solely on primary sales volumes risk missing the real story of where value is being created and destroyed.

Why does this divergence matter now? Because it signals a shift in the fundamental dynamics of the market. In a typical recovery, primary and secondary sales move in the same direction. When they diverge this sharply, it indicates that different segments of the market are responding to different forces. The primary market, dominated by developers with high leverage and uncertain balance sheets, is struggling to attract buyers who are increasingly risk-averse. The secondary market, by contrast, is benefiting from a flight to liquidity, as buyers seek completed, tangible assets where the risk of project delays or quality issues is lower. This is not a cyclical pattern; it is a structural one that will persist as long as developer credit risk remains elevated.

The data also reveals a clear tiering effect. Tier 1 cities are showing relative resilience in primary sales, with an 11% year-on-year increase, but even this is a deceleration from the prior week’s 18% growth. Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities are experiencing outright declines of 14% and 10%, respectively. This is consistent with a market where capital is concentrating in the most liquid, highest-quality locations, while lower-tier cities face a more severe demand deficit. The secondary market strength is even more pronounced in Tier 1 cities, where sales are up 63% year-on-year, compared to 36% in Tier 2. The implication is clear: the property market is not healing from the bottom up; it is being reshaped from the top down, with the strongest assets attracting the most capital.

For investors, the key question is no longer whether the market will recover, but how the reorganization will unfold and which players will emerge stronger. The current data provides a snapshot of a market in transition, where the old rules of thumb no longer apply. The remainder of this article will unpack the implications of this divergence, identify the analytical gaps that the data does not yet answer, and provide a decision framework for navigating this uncertain environment.

## The Divergence Between Primary and Secondary Sales Is a Risk-Repricing Signal, Not Merely a Demand Shift

The most striking feature of the weekly data is the yawning gap between primary and secondary sales performance. Primary sales in 50 cities fell 9% year-on-year, while secondary sales in 10 cities rose 45%. This is not a minor statistical anomaly; it is a difference of 54 percentage points. To understand what this means, one must look beyond the headline numbers and consider the underlying drivers.

The primary market is effectively the developer market. When a buyer purchases a new home, they are taking on two risks: the risk of the asset itself and the risk of the developer’s execution. In the current environment, where developer balance sheets remain under pressure and project delays are not uncommon, the primary market is discounting this execution risk. Buyers are demanding a premium in the form of lower prices or better terms to compensate for the uncertainty. The 9% decline in primary sales, combined with the 66% sell-through rate, suggests that developers are struggling to clear inventory at prices that are acceptable to both buyers and their own margin requirements.

The secondary market, by contrast, is a market for completed assets. There is no execution risk. The buyer sees exactly what they are getting, and the transaction can be completed quickly. This liquidity premium is especially valuable in an environment where uncertainty is high. The 45% surge in secondary sales is consistent with a flight to safety, where buyers are willing to pay a premium for the certainty of a completed transaction. This is not a sign of exuberance; it is a sign of risk aversion. The declining asking price index, which fell to 18.2% from 18.5% the prior week, indicates that even in the secondary market, sellers are having to cut prices to attract buyers. The volume is up, but the pricing power is weak.

The so-what for investors is that the traditional relationship between primary and secondary markets has broken down. In a healthy market, primary sales lead secondary sales, as new supply absorbs demand and sets price expectations. In the current market, secondary sales are leading, and primary sales are lagging. This inversion suggests that the market is not functioning normally. It is being driven by a structural risk premium that will not disappear quickly. Any investor who assumes that a recovery in primary sales will automatically follow the strength in secondary sales is making a dangerous assumption. The two markets are responding to different forces and will likely remain disconnected until developer credit risk is materially reduced.

## Tier 1 Cities Are a Liquidity Haven, Not a Growth Story, and This Concentration of Capital Carries Its Own Risks

The data shows that Tier 1 cities are outperforming, but the nature of that outperformance needs to be understood correctly. Primary sales in Tier 1 cities rose 11% year-on-year, while secondary sales surged 63%. The sell-through rate in Tier 1 cities was 66%, which is identical to the overall average. On the surface, this looks like a healthy market. But the context matters.

The Tier 1 primary sales growth of 11% is a deceleration from the prior week’s 18% growth. This is not a market that is accelerating; it is a market that is stabilizing at a modestly positive level. The secondary market strength of 63% is extraordinary, but it is also a deceleration from 65% the prior week. The momentum is fading, not building. Furthermore, the property agent index in Tier 1 cities fell to 53.5 from 55.6, indicating that agent sentiment is weakening even as transaction volumes remain high. This is a classic sign of a market that is topping out, where volume is being sustained by price cuts and aggressive marketing, not by genuine demand growth.

The concentration of capital in Tier 1 cities is also creating a risk of its own. If all the liquidity is flowing into a small number of markets, those markets become vulnerable to a sudden reversal. A policy change, a macroeconomic shock, or a major developer default could trigger a rapid exodus of capital from Tier 1 cities, leaving them exposed to a sharp correction. The data does not yet show signs of this, but the declining agent index and the decelerating growth rates are warning signals that should not be ignored.

For investors, the implication is that Tier 1 cities are not a safe haven in the traditional sense. They are a liquidity haven, which means they are attractive as long as the liquidity is flowing. But liquidity can be fickle. The real question is whether the demand in Tier 1 cities is driven by genuine end-user demand or by speculative and flight-to-safety capital. The data suggests it is the latter, which makes the current strength fragile.

## The Year-to-Date Data Reveals a Market That Is Still Contracting, and the Pace of Contraction Is Not Evenly Distributed

The year-to-date figures provide a longer-term perspective that is essential for understanding the trajectory of the market. Primary sales in 50 cities are down 15% year-to-date, while secondary sales are up 5%. This is a market that is still in contraction, albeit with a bifurcated structure.

The 15% decline in primary sales year-to-date is significant because it shows that the weakness is not a recent phenomenon. It has been persistent throughout the year. The fact that the weekly decline of 9% is smaller than the year-to-date decline of 15% could be interpreted as a sign of stabilization, but this would be premature. The weekly data is volatile and can be influenced by seasonal factors, holiday effects, and the timing of new project launches. A single week of data does not constitute a trend.

The secondary market year-to-date growth of 5% is modest, especially when compared to the weekly surge of 45%. This discrepancy suggests that the secondary market strength is a recent development, concentrated in the past few weeks. It is possible that this is a seasonal effect, or it could be the beginning of a more sustained shift. The data does not provide enough information to distinguish between these two possibilities.

What is clear is that the contraction is not evenly distributed across tiers. Tier 1 cities are performing better than Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities, but even within Tier 1, the year-to-date data is not uniformly positive. The weekly data shows a deceleration, and the year-to-date data for Tier 1 is not provided in the report, which is itself a telling omission. If Tier 1 cities were performing strongly on a year-to-date basis, that data would likely be highlighted. The fact that it is not suggests that even the relative strength in Tier 1 is not enough to offset the broader weakness.

For investors, the year-to-date data reinforces the message that this is a market in transition, not a market in recovery. The contraction is real, and it is likely to persist until the underlying structural issues—developer debt, weak consumer confidence, and policy uncertainty—are addressed. The secondary market strength is a positive sign, but it is not yet strong enough to lift the entire market.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Role of Credit, Policy, and the Timing of a Potential Inflection Point

The weekly database provides a valuable snapshot of transaction volumes and prices, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. First, the data does not shed light on the role of credit. Are the buyers in the secondary market using mortgages, or are they cash buyers? If the surge in secondary sales is driven by cash buyers, it suggests that the demand is coming from a relatively wealthy segment of the population that is not reliant on credit. This would be a positive sign, as it indicates that the demand is real and not leveraged. However, if the surge is driven by mortgage-dependent buyers, it raises questions about the sustainability of the trend, especially if interest rates rise or credit conditions tighten.

Second, the data does not provide any insight into policy. The Chinese government has implemented a series of measures to support the property market, including lowering mortgage rates, relaxing purchase restrictions, and providing liquidity support to developers. The weekly data does not allow us to assess the effectiveness of these policies. Are they working? The fact that primary sales are still declining suggests that they are not yet having a meaningful impact, but it is possible that the effects are lagged and will become apparent in the coming weeks or months.

Third, the data does not address the question of timing. When will the market reach an inflection point? The year-to-date decline of 15% in primary sales is significant, but it is not accelerating. This could be interpreted as a sign that the worst of the decline is behind us. Alternatively, it could be a lull before a further deterioration. The data does not provide a clear answer.

These unanswered questions are not a criticism of the report; they are a reflection of the inherent limitations of any weekly data series. The report provides high-frequency signals, but it cannot provide the full picture. For investors, the key is to use these signals as inputs into a broader analytical framework that incorporates credit conditions, policy dynamics, and macroeconomic trends. The weekly data is a tool, not a conclusion.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Reorganization: Focus on Liquidity, Credit Quality, and Tier Selection

Given the complexity of the current market, a simple investment framework is essential. The data suggests that the most important variables to monitor are liquidity, credit quality, and tier selection.

First, liquidity is the most important factor. The secondary market is outperforming the primary market because it offers liquidity. Investors should prioritize assets and markets that offer high liquidity, as these will be the most resilient in the event of a downturn. In practice, this means focusing on Tier 1 cities and high-quality Tier 2 cities where transaction volumes are robust and the secondary market is active.

Second, credit quality matters more than ever. The primary market is being penalized because of developer credit risk. Investors should avoid exposure to developers with weak balance sheets, even if they are offering attractive prices. The risk of project delays, quality issues, or outright default is too high. Instead, focus on developers with strong credit ratings and a track record of completing projects on time.

Third, tier selection is critical. The data shows a clear hierarchy, with Tier 1 cities outperforming Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities. This is likely to persist. Investors should be cautious about allocating capital to lower-tier cities, where the demand deficit is more severe and the recovery is likely to be slower. Within Tier 1 cities, the focus should be on the most liquid sub-markets, such as central business districts and established residential areas.

This framework is not a guarantee of success, but it provides a structured way to think about the market. The current environment is too uncertain for a one-size-fits-all approach. Investors need to be selective, disciplined, and focused on the factors that matter most.

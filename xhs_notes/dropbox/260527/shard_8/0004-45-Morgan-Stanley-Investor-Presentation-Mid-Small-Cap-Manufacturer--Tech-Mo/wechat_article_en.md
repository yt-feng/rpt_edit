# The Semiconductor Supply Chain Is No Longer Cyclical — It Is Structurally Differentiating

The conventional wisdom that semiconductor equipment and materials stocks are purely cyclical plays is now outdated. A detailed analysis of mid- and small-cap technology companies in Japan reveals something far more significant: the semiconductor supply chain is undergoing a structural transformation that is creating permanent winners and losers. The divergence in growth rates across subsegments is not a temporary phenomenon driven by inventory cycles. It is the result of a fundamental shift in how chips are designed, tested, and packaged, driven overwhelmingly by artificial intelligence demand. Investors who continue to treat these companies as a single homogeneous group risk missing the most important capital allocation opportunity in the Japanese equity market today.

This matters now because the data through early 2026 shows that the recovery is not broad-based. It is highly selective. Companies exposed to wafer fabrication equipment components are seeing widening growth rates as inventory adjustments end and advance orders emerge. Test-related companies are benefiting from both higher volumes of AI devices and an increase in test items per chip. Materials companies are expanding capacity not for the next quarter, but for demand expected around 2030. These are not cyclical signals. They are strategic inflection points.

The critical insight is that the semiconductor supply chain is fragmenting into distinct growth regimes. The old model of a single industry cycle that lifts all boats is being replaced by a world where subindustry exposure, customer mix, and technological specificity determine outcomes. The report from a global investment bank makes this clear through its detailed examination of pre-process, post-process, test, and materials companies. But the implications go far beyond what the data alone shows.

Understanding this new landscape requires a framework that separates signal from noise. The sections that follow build that framework.

## AI Demand Is Reshaping the Testing Segment in Ways That Most Investors Underestimate

The testing segment of the semiconductor supply chain has historically been viewed as a lower-growth, more commoditized part of the ecosystem. That view is now dangerously outdated. The report identifies that demand for testers, probers, and probe cards is being created by two simultaneous forces: higher volumes of AI-related devices and the increase in test items associated with performance improvements. This is not a linear scaling effect. It is exponential.

When a chip becomes more complex, the number of test items does not increase proportionally to the number of transistors. It increases faster. Each new performance specification, each new thermal requirement, and each new reliability standard adds separate testing protocols. For AI accelerators and high-bandwidth memory, the testing burden is substantially higher than for conventional logic or memory chips. This means that even if unit volumes of AI chips grow at a steady rate, the demand for test equipment and materials grows at a faster rate.

The report highlights that demand for probe cards for memory is increasing not only for HBM and general-purpose DRAM, but also for NAND. This is a notable development because NAND has traditionally required fewer test cycles. The fact that probe card demand is broadening into NAND suggests that the testing intensity is spreading across the entire memory landscape. For probers specifically, the ability to improve high-precision temperature control functions and increase supply capacity is becoming a competitive differentiator. Companies that can meet these technical requirements are winning orders. Those that cannot are being left behind.

The strategic implication is clear: the testing segment is transitioning from a volume-driven business to a value-driven business. Investors should evaluate test companies not on their historical growth rates, but on their ability to capture increasing test content per device. The companies that are investing in precision temperature control, higher throughput, and broader test coverage are building moats that will persist through the next downturn.

## Materials Companies Are Making Capacity Decisions Based on 2030 Demand, Creating a New Kind of Supply-Demand Dynamic

One of the most striking findings in the report is that capacity expansion by semiconductor materials companies is aimed in part at meeting demand around 2030. From land acquisition to plant construction and equipment installation, it takes at least two years to begin mass production. This means that the capacity decisions being made today will determine the competitive landscape for the remainder of this decade.

This long lead time creates a fundamentally different dynamic than what investors are accustomed to in the semiconductor industry. In the equipment segment, capacity can be adjusted relatively quickly. In materials, the capital commitment is larger and the timeline is longer. The report notes that demand for front-end materials such as photoresists, CMP slurry, and wiring-related materials remains firm. But the real story is in advanced packaging materials, where increasing size, multilayering, and higher density are growth drivers beyond mere volume expansion.

The report also identifies a specific technological battle in the materials space. Low CTE and low dielectric materials are being driven by AI-related demand. Glass cloth, when compared with quartz glass, has inferior performance. But when combined with resin, it can differentiate through copper-clad laminate performance and lower processing difficulty. Low CTE glass has a technological barrier in terms of the difficulty of yarn production. This means that the companies that can solve the yarn production problem will capture significant value.

The strategic question for investors is which materials companies are making the right capacity bets. The report provides data on sales trends for companies like Ajinomoto, Taiyo Holdings, and Nitto Boseki, but the real insight is in the divergence. Some materials companies are seeing accelerating growth. Others are not. The difference is likely explained by exposure to AI-driven demand and technological differentiation in advanced packaging. Investors should focus on companies that are investing in capacity for the 2030 demand horizon, not those that are simply riding the current cycle.

## China Semiconductor Capex Creates a Differentiator, But the Risk Is Underappreciated

The report states clearly that semiconductor capex in China remains strong, and the degree of penetration into the China supply chain is becoming a differentiator in terms of earnings contributions. This is a double-edged sword. For Japanese mid- and small-cap companies that have successfully penetrated the Chinese market, the earnings contribution is significant. But the geopolitical risk is equally significant.

The report does not fully address the question of what happens if China's semiconductor buildout slows or if export controls tighten further. This is a meaningful analytical gap. The data shows that companies with China exposure are benefiting today, but the sustainability of that benefit is uncertain. The report mentions that procurement risk related to the Middle East has eased, but high prices and delays in price pass-throughs remain risks. This suggests that the materials segment is already dealing with margin pressure. Adding geopolitical uncertainty to the equation creates a complex risk-reward profile.

Investors need a framework for evaluating China exposure. The key variables are: the proportion of revenue derived from Chinese customers, the substitutability of the company's products, and the company's ability to redirect capacity to other markets. Companies that sell highly differentiated products that are difficult to source elsewhere have more pricing power and are less vulnerable to policy changes. Companies that sell commoditized products into China are taking on more risk than the current valuations likely reflect.

The report's data on companies like Tri Chemical Laboratories, which saw sales growth of 101% year-over-year in the first quarter of 2025, suggests that China exposure can drive extraordinary growth. But the same data shows that this growth is volatile. Tri Chemical's sales growth swung from 101% to 24% in the following quarter. This volatility is a feature of China-dependent revenue, not a bug. Investors who overweight China-exposed names need to size their positions accordingly.

## The Device Segment Reveals a Divergence That Most Top-Down Analysis Misses

The report's analysis of device-related companies is perhaps the most nuanced and most easily overlooked. It covers power equipment, computers, smartphones, PCs, home appliances, consumer electronics, industrial equipment, and automotive. The common thread is that demand is not uniform. It is highly specific to end markets.

Power equipment-related demand for battery storage systems continues, and interest in transformers for private sector demand is increasing further. This is a structural trend driven by the energy transition and data center buildout. Computer demand related to data centers remains strong, mainly AI-related. The report flags that investors should monitor tech developments such as vertical power delivery, which could reshape the power management landscape.

For smartphones and PCs, the report is cautious over potential demand weakness and lower production volumes triggered by rising memory prices. This is an important counterpoint to the AI narrative. While AI is driving growth in data centers, it is also consuming memory supply and driving up prices, which could suppress demand in consumer markets. The tension between AI-driven demand and consumer price sensitivity is a dynamic that will play out over the next several quarters.

Home appliances and consumer electronics have demand stagnation built into company plans, reflecting inflation. Industrial equipment and automotive are more promising, but with caveats. Semiconductor and defense-related areas are promising in industrial equipment. In automotive, the report advises watching demand changes stemming from differences in customer mix.

The strategic implication is that a single view on semiconductor demand is insufficient. Investors need to disaggregate demand by end market and by customer. The companies that are winning are those with exposure to data centers, AI, energy infrastructure, and defense. The companies that are losing are those tied to consumer electronics, smartphones, and PCs. This divergence will only widen as AI continues to reshape end-market demand.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Test Segment Growth Trajectory

The report provides compelling evidence that the testing segment is experiencing a structural upgrade in demand. But it does not fully answer the question of how sustainable this growth is. The increase in test items per device is real, but it is not infinite. At some point, the testing burden per chip will plateau. The question is when and at what level.

The report also does not address the potential for testing efficiency improvements that could reduce the number of test items. If AI itself is used to improve testing algorithms, the demand for test equipment could grow more slowly than expected. This is a second-order effect that the report does not explore. Similarly, the report does not consider the possibility that chip architecture changes could reduce testing requirements. For example, if chips move toward more modular designs, testing could become more standardized and less intensive.

Investors should also consider the competitive dynamics in the testing segment. The report notes that improving high-precision temperature control functions and increasing supply capacity create opportunities to win orders. But it does not address the risk that large equipment companies could enter the testing segment and compress margins for mid- and small-cap players. The testing segment is attractive precisely because it is fragmented. If consolidation accelerates, the pricing power of individual companies could decline.

These open questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are the natural result of analyzing a rapidly evolving industry. The report provides the data and the framework. It is up to investors to make their own judgments about sustainability and competitive dynamics.

## A Decision Framework for Investing in Japanese Semiconductor Mid- and Small-Caps

Based on the report's analysis, investors should use a three-part framework to evaluate opportunities in this space. The first dimension is end-market exposure. Companies with exposure to AI data centers, energy infrastructure, and defense are structurally advantaged. Companies with exposure to consumer electronics, smartphones, and PCs are structurally disadvantaged. This is the most important filter.

The second dimension is technological differentiation. In the materials segment, companies that can solve difficult problems like low CTE glass yarn production have pricing power and long-term moats. In the testing segment, companies with precision temperature control and high throughput have competitive advantages. In the equipment segment, companies with exposure to wafer fabrication equipment components benefit from secular growth in capital spending. The key is to identify companies that are not easily replaceable.

The third dimension is capacity positioning. The report makes clear that materials companies are making capacity decisions for 2030. Investors should evaluate whether a company's capacity expansion plans are aligned with the most attractive growth segments. Companies that are investing in advanced packaging materials, low CTE glass, and high-performance testing equipment are making the right bets. Companies that are expanding capacity for commoditized products may be overinvesting in a market that will face margin pressure.

The data in the report provides a rich set of inputs for this framework. The sales growth trajectories for companies like Ajinomoto, Taiyo Holdings, and Nitto Boseki show clear acceleration in the post-process segment. The pre-process data shows more mixed results, with some companies like Tri Chemical Laboratories and Fuso Chemical seeing strong growth while others like ADEKA and Kanto Denka Kogyo show more modest trends. The stock price charts reveal that the market is already pricing in some of this divergence, but the full implications of the structural shift are likely not yet reflected.

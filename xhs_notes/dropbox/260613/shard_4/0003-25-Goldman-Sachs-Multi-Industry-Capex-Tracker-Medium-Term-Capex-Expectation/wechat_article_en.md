# The Capex Cycle Is Not Broad-Based: Three Structural Super-Sectors Now Dominate Global Investment, and the Gap Is Widening

Capital expenditure expectations across the global industrial economy are rising again, and the numbers look strong on the surface. A bottom-up analysis covering approximately 3.4 trillion euros of capex across nearly 4,000 companies and 34 end-markets now shows medium-term forecasts for 2026 running nearly 13 percent above historical trend growth, compared to a historical median of roughly 2 percent. Even 2027 projections have moved up from 8 percent at the start of the year to 12 percent today. On a compound annual growth rate basis from 2025 through 2029, the tracker registers roughly 9 percent growth. These are not marginal improvements. They represent a significant upward revision cycle.

But the headline number conceals a more important structural shift. The aggregate improvement is not a broad-based recovery. It is overwhelmingly concentrated in three sectors: data centers, semiconductors, and utilities — specifically power grids. Together, these three areas now account for roughly 39 percent of total expected capex in 2026, compared to just 22 percent four years ago in 2022. The rest of the industrial economy is not participating in this cycle at the same intensity. In some cases, it is actively cutting investment.

This divergence matters for investors because it signals that the global capex cycle is no longer a single cycle. It is two cycles operating in parallel: one driven by structural demand for digital infrastructure and electrification, and another constrained by cyclical headwinds, capacity overhang, and weak pricing power. The strategic question is no longer whether capex is rising. It is whether your portfolio is positioned on the right side of the divide.

## Medium-Term Capex Forecasts Have Been Revised Sharply Upward, but the Revision Is Almost Entirely Driven by Technology and Utilities

The latest update to the capex tracker shows that 2026 forecasts have been revised upward by roughly 2 percentage points since March of this year alone. That is a meaningful acceleration in a short period. The data also reveals that the compound annual growth rate for data center capex from 2025 through 2029 is now approximately 35 percent, a 6.5 percentage point increase from the prior update. Semiconductor capex is running at roughly 23 percent CAGR, a 6.8 point increase. Even oil and gas, a sector often thought to be in structural decline, shows a 3.5 point improvement to roughly 4 percent CAGR, driven by selective investment in LNG and petrochemicals.

These revisions are not random. They reflect a consistent pattern of companies in technology and utility end-markets raising their forward guidance for capital spending. The drivers are well understood: the buildout of artificial intelligence infrastructure, the expansion of cloud computing capacity, the electrification of transport and industry, and the modernization of aging grid networks in developed economies. What is less appreciated is the scale of the reallocation. In just four years, the share of total global capex captured by these three sectors has nearly doubled.

The implication is that the traditional industrial cycle, where investment moved broadly in sync across sectors, is being replaced by a more fragmented regime. For multi-industry investors, this means that sector selection is becoming far more important than macro timing. The old playbook of buying diversified industrial exposure and waiting for the cycle to lift all boats no longer works.

## The Sectors Being Left Behind Are Not Cyclical Anomalies; They Reflect Structural Overcapacity and Weak Pricing Power

While technology and utilities are driving the aggregate numbers, other sectors are moving in the opposite direction. The largest downgrades in medium-term capex expectations have occurred in chemicals, which saw a decline of roughly 4.1 percentage points, and construction equipment, which fell by 4.0 points. In absolute terms, airports are projected to experience a negative compound annual growth rate of approximately 4 percent over the 2025-2029 period. These are not minor corrections. They represent deliberate reductions in investment plans.

The pattern is telling. Chemicals and construction equipment are sectors where global capacity has expanded significantly in recent years, often ahead of demand. In chemicals, new capacity in Asia and the Middle East has created a supply overhang that has compressed margins and discouraged further investment. In construction equipment, the post-pandemic boom in housing and infrastructure has given way to elevated inventories and slower order growth. Airports face a different but equally challenging dynamic: traffic recovery has been uneven, and the capital intensity of expansion projects makes them unattractive when uncertainty about long-term demand persists.

The key insight is that these downgrades are not simply cyclical. They reflect structural conditions that are unlikely to reverse quickly. The capex-to-sales ratio and capacity utilization rates in sectors such as telecom and consumer are currently running below their historical medians. This suggests that even if macro conditions improve, these sectors may not have the urgency to invest. They are carrying excess capacity, and until that capacity is absorbed, capital spending will remain subdued.

For investors, this creates a clear framework. The sectors with strong capex growth are those where demand is outpacing supply and where the investment is driven by secular trends rather than cyclical recovery. The sectors with weak capex are those where supply has already caught up or exceeded demand. Betting on a rotation into the laggards requires a thesis that capacity will be absorbed and pricing power will return. That thesis is not supported by the current data.

## The Concentration of Growth Raises a Critical Question about the Sustainability of the Current Capex Trajectory

When three sectors account for nearly 40 percent of total investment and are growing at multiples of the historical trend, the risk of overinvestment becomes a legitimate concern. Data center capex is growing at roughly 35 percent CAGR. Semiconductor capex is at 23 percent. These are extraordinary rates of growth, and they are being driven by a relatively narrow set of demand drivers: AI model training, cloud infrastructure, and chip fabrication.

The historical record is clear. Periods of concentrated capex growth have often been followed by corrections when demand expectations proved too optimistic. The telecom bubble of the late 1990s, the semiconductor cycle of 2017-2018, and the renewables investment boom of the early 2010s all followed similar patterns. In each case, the initial thesis was sound, but the scale of investment eventually exceeded the underlying demand.

The current cycle may be different. AI infrastructure spending is still in its early stages, and the demand for computing power continues to grow at exponential rates. Power grid investment is driven by regulatory mandates and the physical reality of aging infrastructure, which cannot be deferred indefinitely. But the risk is that the market is pricing in a linear continuation of current growth rates, while the reality may involve periods of digestion and consolidation.

The report does not fully answer this question. It provides a snapshot of current expectations, but it does not model the scenarios under which those expectations might prove too high. Investors need to ask themselves: what happens if AI adoption slows, or if chip fabrication capacity comes online faster than demand? What happens if utilities face permitting delays or cost overruns? These are not hypothetical risks. They are real constraints that could cause the current capex trajectory to moderate.

The unanswered question, then, is whether the structural growth in technology and utilities is strong enough to absorb the current level of investment, or whether the market is setting itself up for a correction. The data supports the bull case, but it does not disprove the bear case.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Map Your Portfolio Against the Capex Divergence, Not the Aggregate

The practical implication of this analysis is that investors need a framework for positioning that accounts for the divergence between structural and cyclical capex. The old approach of looking at aggregate capex growth and making sector-agnostic bets is no longer sufficient. The following framework is designed to help investors identify where they are exposed and where they need to adjust.

First, identify which of your holdings are in sectors where capex is being driven by structural demand. These are data centers, semiconductors, and power grid utilities. In these sectors, the investment cycle is likely to persist even if macro conditions weaken, because the spending is tied to long-term trends that are not easily reversed. The risk is not that spending stops, but that it slows from current elevated levels. The appropriate strategy is to own the enablers: companies that provide the equipment, components, and services that these sectors need to execute their plans.

Second, identify which holdings are in sectors where capex is being cut or is growing below trend. These include chemicals, construction equipment, airports, and consumer-facing industrials. In these sectors, the investment cycle is likely to remain weak until capacity is absorbed and pricing power returns. The risk is that earnings expectations are still too high, because analysts are extrapolating from the aggregate rather than the sector-specific data. The appropriate strategy is to be selective, focusing on companies with strong market positions and the ability to generate cash even in a low-investment environment.

Third, consider the second-order effects of the capex divergence. The sectors that are investing heavily will need inputs: industrial gases, electrical equipment, engineering services, and raw materials. The sectors that are not investing will create a drag on demand for those same inputs. This means that the divergence creates both opportunities and risks across the supply chain. Investors should map their exposure not just to end-markets, but to the underlying demand drivers.

Fourth, monitor the leading indicators that could signal a change in the trajectory. The report references barometers covering roughly 1,000 high-frequency indicators, including truck sales, industrial production, and capacity utilization. These indicators can provide early warning if the structural growth sectors are starting to decelerate, or if the cyclical laggards are beginning to recover. The key is to watch for inflection points, not just trends.

This framework is not a substitute for detailed company-level analysis. But it provides a way to think about portfolio construction that is grounded in the data rather than in macro narratives.

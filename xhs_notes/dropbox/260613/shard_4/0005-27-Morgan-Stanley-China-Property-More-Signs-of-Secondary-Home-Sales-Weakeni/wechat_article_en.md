# China's Secondary Home Sales Are Not Failing Gradually—They Are Decelerating Faster Than Policy Can Respond

The data from the first ten days of June has delivered a clear verdict: the post-policy rebound in China's secondary home sales is fading more quickly than most market participants anticipated. The 25-city composite real-time sales growth has collapsed from 30% year-on-year in April to just 9% as of June 10, despite a favorable base comparison from the Dragon Boat Festival holiday. This is not a gentle normalization; it is a deceleration that, at current trajectory, points toward negative year-on-year growth by the third quarter.

Investors who interpreted the March-April surge as the beginning of a sustainable recovery are now facing a difficult reassessment. The policy easing cycle that began in late April created genuine but temporary demand, primarily from buyers who had delayed purchases during the fourth quarter of 2025. That pent-up release is now largely exhausted. What remains is the underlying structural weakness in household confidence, income expectations, and demographic demand that no amount of mortgage rate adjustment has yet been able to reverse.

The strategic question for investors is no longer whether the property market has found a floor. It is whether the floor itself is stable enough to support selective stock picking, or whether the entire sector remains a value trap disguised as a cyclical opportunity. The answer depends on understanding why the deceleration is happening unevenly across cities, what it reveals about the durability of policy effects, and which developers have enough self-help levers to outperform even in a flat-to-declining market.

This article draws on recent research from a global investment bank's Asia Pacific property team to dissect the current data, challenge the prevailing narratives, and provide a decision framework for investors who need to navigate the next six months without relying on a macro recovery that may not arrive.

## The June Data Reveals a Structural Rather Than Cyclical Slowdown Across Most City Tiers

The headline number is striking enough: 25-city secondary sales growth falling from 30% to 9% year-on-year in just six weeks. But the pattern beneath the aggregate is what should concern investors most. The deceleration is not concentrated in any single city tier or policy regime. It is broad-based, with low-tier 2 cities such as Nanchang, Foshan, Nantong, Dongguan, Ningbo, and Xi'an experiencing the fastest rates of decline. Even more telling, several cities including Tianjin, Changsha, and Chengdu have already turned negative year-on-year.

This pattern matters because it contradicts the narrative that China's property market is simply undergoing a normal post-stimulus consolidation. If the slowdown were cyclical—driven by seasonal factors or a temporary pullback after a strong spring—we would expect to see more differentiation between cities with different economic bases, population inflows, and policy support. Instead, the data shows a synchronized weakening that cuts across city tiers, suggesting a common driver: the exhaustion of demand that was pulled forward by policy, not created by it.

Consider the divergence among cities that implemented policy easing in late April. Suzhou and Wuhan have maintained relatively robust growth, while Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and Foshan have decelerated by double-digit percentage points. This divergence is not random. The cities that are holding up better tend to have stronger economic fundamentals, more diversified industrial bases, and less dependence on property as a primary wealth driver. But even in these outperforming cities, the trend lines are sloping downward, not flattening.

The implication is clear: policy easing can shift the timing of purchases, but it cannot create new demand where household balance sheets remain constrained. The pent-up demand from the fourth quarter of 2025 has been largely released. The question now is whether the remaining buyer pool is large enough to sustain even moderate growth levels. The June data suggests it is not.

## The Policy Effect Has a Half-Life of Approximately Two Months, and That Half-Life Is Shortening

One of the most valuable insights from this research is the ability to measure the duration of policy effectiveness. The easing measures announced in late April produced a visible spike in secondary sales during May. But by early June, that spike had already faded. The half-life of the policy effect—the time it takes for the sales boost to decline by 50%—appears to be roughly eight to ten weeks.

This is shorter than many investors expected, and it has direct implications for portfolio strategy. If policy effects are becoming less durable with each successive round of easing, then the market is experiencing diminishing returns to intervention. Each new round of mortgage rate cuts, down payment reductions, or purchase restriction relaxations generates a smaller and shorter-lived sales response. This pattern is consistent with what economists call "policy fatigue": households become less responsive to incentives that they have seen before and that they expect to see again.

The diminishing effectiveness of policy also raises a more uncomfortable question: what happens when the policy toolkit is exhausted? China's authorities still have room to cut rates further and to relax purchase restrictions in Tier 1 cities. But if each round of easing produces a weaker response than the last, then even aggressive future measures may only delay the inevitable adjustment rather than prevent it.

For investors, this means that waiting for the next policy catalyst to buy property stocks is a increasingly risky strategy. The market may already be pricing in the possibility of further easing, and the actual impact on physical sales may disappoint relative to expectations. The risk/reward skew is shifting to the downside, even after an 18% pullback in sector share prices since mid-May, compared to an 8% decline in the Hang Seng Index.

## Home Prices Face a Broadly Soft Downtrend Through 2026–2027, With Only Tier 1 Cities Offering Any Upside

The most critical variable for property investors is not sales volume but price. Volume can fluctuate with policy cycles and seasonal factors, but price trends determine developer profitability, asset valuations, and the health of household balance sheets. On this front, the outlook is increasingly grim.

The research projects a broadly soft month-on-month home price downtrend through 2026 and into 2027. This is not a crash scenario—prices are expected to decline gradually rather than collapse—but it is a persistent erosion of value that will compress margins across the sector. The only exceptions are select Tier 1 cities, where better destocking progress and stronger demand fundamentals may support a mild uptrend.

The mechanism behind this price decline is straightforward: as secondary sales volumes weaken, sellers become more motivated to close transactions. Listing volumes remain elevated as households seek to monetize their property assets in a weakening market. The resulting oversupply of listings puts downward pressure on prices, which in turn discourages new buyers from entering the market, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of declining volumes and prices.

This dynamic is already visible in the data. The cities that have turned negative year-on-year in sales volume are also experiencing the most aggressive price cuts. The transaction mix is shifting toward lower-priced units, as buyers become more price-sensitive and sellers become more willing to negotiate. Rental rates are also softening, which reduces the investment case for buy-to-let buyers and further depresses demand.

For developers, this price environment means that even if they maintain sales volumes, their revenue per unit will decline. Gross margins will compress, and the value of their land banks will need to be marked down. The companies that will survive and thrive are those that have already completed most of their destocking, have low leverage, and have diversified revenue streams from investment properties that are less sensitive to residential price cycles.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Threshold for a Genuine Market Inflection

The research provides an excellent diagnostic of the current state of the market, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most critical of these is: what would it take for the market to reach a genuine inflection point, rather than another false start?

The report identifies several variables to monitor over June through August: sales volumes, home prices, secondary listing volumes, transaction mixes, and rental rates. But it does not specify what thresholds in these variables would signal a sustainable recovery versus a temporary stabilization. This is not a criticism of the research—these thresholds are inherently uncertain and depend on macroeconomic conditions, household income growth, and policy responses that cannot be predicted with precision.

Nevertheless, investors need to develop their own frameworks for distinguishing between noise and signal. A few tentative hypotheses can be derived from the data. First, a sustainable recovery would require secondary listing volumes to decline meaningfully, indicating that the supply of motivated sellers is being absorbed. Second, transaction mixes would need to shift back toward higher-priced units, suggesting that buyer confidence is returning. Third, rental rates would need to stabilize or increase, restoring the investment case for property ownership.

None of these conditions are currently being met. Listing volumes remain elevated, transaction mixes are shifting downward, and rental rates are softening. The burden of proof is on the bulls to show that these trends are reversing, not on the bears to explain why they are continuing.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguishing Between Beta and Alpha in a Weakening Market

Given the deteriorating macro picture, the most useful contribution an investor can make is to separate the companies that will benefit from any broad market recovery (beta) from those that can generate positive returns regardless of the macro environment (alpha). The research provides a clear framework for making this distinction.

Beta exposure in the property sector is driven by leverage to residential sales volumes and prices. Companies with large development pipelines, high exposure to lower-tier cities, and limited diversification into investment properties will suffer disproportionately in a declining market. Their share prices will move in lockstep with the macro data, and any recovery will require a broad-based improvement in sales and prices that the current data does not support.

Alpha exposure, by contrast, comes from self-help factors that are independent of the macro cycle. These include: the ability to maintain or grow market share in a declining market; the quality and location of the land bank; the strength of the balance sheet and access to financing; the diversification into recurring income from investment properties; and the dividend yield that provides a floor for the stock price even if capital appreciation is limited.

The research identifies two companies that exemplify this alpha profile. China Resources Land is highlighted as the top pick, followed by C&D International Investment Group. Both offer solid earnings per share outlooks, attractive dividend yields, and medium-term re-rating potential even in the absence of a notable recovery in the physical market. The valuation methodology used by the research assigns these companies higher scores on execution, scale, profitability, and financing compared to their peers, which justifies a lower discount to net asset value.

For investors considering property exposure, the decision framework should be: do you have conviction that a broad market recovery is imminent? If yes, then beta exposure through diversified developers may offer upside. If no, then alpha exposure through high-quality, low-leverage, diversified developers is the only prudent approach. The current data strongly favors the latter.

## Why the Next Six Months Will Separate the Survivors from the Value Traps

The property sector has already corrected significantly from its mid-May peak, and some investors may be tempted to buy the dip on the assumption that valuations are cheap. But cheapness alone is not a sufficient condition for investment. In a sector that is facing structural headwinds from demographic decline, household deleveraging, and policy fatigue, low valuations can persist for years, and they can get lower.

The next six months will be critical because they will reveal which companies have genuine self-help levers and which are simply riding the macro wave. As secondary sales volumes weaken further and home prices continue to decline, developers with high leverage, low-quality land banks, and limited recurring income will face increasing pressure on their balance sheets. Their ability to refinance debt, maintain dividends, and execute on new projects will be tested.

Conversely, the companies that emerge from this downturn stronger will be those that have used the period of relative stability to improve their competitive position. They will have gained market share from weaker competitors, acquired high-quality land at distressed prices, and built recurring income streams that provide a buffer against residential market volatility. These are the stocks that will generate alpha, not just beta.

The research provides the data and the framework. The execution is now up to individual investors. Those who can resist the temptation to call a macro bottom and instead focus on company-specific fundamentals will be best positioned to navigate the next phase of this cycle. Those who confuse a policy-driven bounce with a structural recovery will likely be disappointed.

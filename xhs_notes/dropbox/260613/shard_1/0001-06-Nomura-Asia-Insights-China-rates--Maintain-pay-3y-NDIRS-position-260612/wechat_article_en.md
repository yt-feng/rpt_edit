# The Liquidity Normalization in China Has Finally Arrived—And It Is Not Yet Priced In

After months of unusually flush conditions, China's money markets are tightening. The People's Bank of China has been quietly draining medium- to long-term liquidity since March, and the cumulative effect is now visible in money market rates. The 7-day repo fixing has moved back above 1.40 percent, and 1-year NCD yields have begun to rise. This shift is not a temporary blip. It is the beginning of a structural normalization that will reshape the rate landscape over the coming months.

The implications for fixed-income investors are significant. A global investment bank report has maintained its paid position in 3-year NDIRS, targeting a move to 1.60 percent by end-July. This view is grounded in two reinforcing drivers: tighter liquidity conditions and a coming surge in government bond supply. The first half of the year saw government bond issuance running well behind schedule, with only 35 percent of the annual net financing quota completed by end-May. That backlog is about to hit the market, and when it does, it will compound the liquidity drain already underway.

The argument here is not that rates will rise in a straight line. It is that the tailwind of ultra-loose liquidity that supported the bond market for months is fading. The question investors must answer is not whether rates will move higher, but how quickly, how far, and which parts of the curve are most exposed.

## The PBoC Has Shifted From Net Injector to Net Drainer of Medium-Term Liquidity

The most important structural change in China's money markets over the past three months is the reversal in the PBoC's liquidity operations. In January and February, the central bank injected a net 2.05 trillion yuan through medium- to long-term tools, far exceeding the average of prior years. That super-flush environment drove rates lower and fueled a powerful bond rally. But from March onward, the picture inverted. The PBoC net withdrew 200 billion yuan in March, 560 billion in April, and 850 billion in May, primarily through outright reverse repos. In early June, it drained an additional 300 billion via 3-month ORRs.

The cumulative net injection from medium- to long-term tools has now fallen to just 140 billion yuan year-to-date, well below the 2020-2025 average of roughly 1.1 trillion. This is the lowest level since 2021. The PBoC is not tightening in a hawkish sense. It is normalizing after an extraordinary period of accommodation. But the market had become accustomed to abundant liquidity, and the adjustment is proving consequential.

The shift matters because medium-term liquidity conditions directly affect bank funding costs and the demand for bonds. When the PBoC drains, banks have less excess reserves to deploy. They become more dependent on the NCD market for funding, and that puts upward pressure on short-term rates. The data confirm this: monthly net NCD issuance turned positive in May for the first time since October 2025, after months of negative net supply. Large NCD maturities in the coming weeks will amplify this effect.

## Government Bond Supply Is About to Become a Dominant Market Force

The second driver of the rates outlook is the looming acceleration in government bond issuance. Through end-May, only 35 percent of the annual net financing quota for central government bonds and local government bonds had been completed. This is below the 39 percent pace seen in 2025. For local government bonds specifically, the issuance pace is the slowest in years, running well below the 2021-2025 average of 38 percent.

This slow pace was partly a function of policy coordination. But it also means that a large volume of issuance must be absorbed in the second half of the year. The report estimates that monthly net CGB and LGB supply will rise to approximately 1.0 trillion yuan in June and further to 1.3 trillion in the third quarter, before declining to an average of 900 billion in the fourth quarter. These are large numbers relative to recent history.

The impact on the bond market will depend on demand. In April and May, onshore funds were aggressive buyers of long-end CGBs and policy bank bonds, using the ample liquidity and the scarcity of high-quality assets to extend duration. That buying has already begun to reverse. In early June, funds started reducing their bond holdings, first at the front end and belly of the curve, and then extending to the back end. If this outflow becomes sustained, it will add to the upward pressure on yields.

The key question is whether there will be buy-on-dip flows at critical yield levels. The report flags 1.75 percent on 10-year CGBs and 2.25 percent on 30-year CGBs as potential support levels. Insurance firms, which tend to add 30-year bonds during selloffs, have already started buying this week. This creates a kind of two-way risk: yields can rise, but the presence of structural buyers may cap the upside.

## The Short End of the Curve Is Most Exposed, and the Steepener Trade Has Yet to Convince

The most actionable insight from the report is the preference for paying 3-year NDIRS over 5-year tenors. This is not an accidental choice. It reflects a specific view on where the pressure from tighter liquidity and higher supply will be felt most acutely.

The logic runs as follows. The PBoC's liquidity drain operates primarily through short- and medium-term instruments. ORRs and MLF operations directly affect bank reserves and funding costs at the front end of the curve. Government bond supply, while it affects the entire curve, tends to have its most immediate impact on swap rates and short-dated bonds because of hedging and funding dynamics. The 3-year point on the NDIRS curve is the natural intersection of these forces.

The 5-year point, by contrast, is more influenced by growth expectations and the outlook for monetary policy. A meaningful steepening of the curve would require either a significant improvement in economic data—particularly credit demand and retail sales—or a much faster and larger tightening of liquidity than currently expected. The report suggests that neither condition is yet in place. The 10s30s spread on CGBs is already wide at about 49 basis points, which provides a buffer for the long end. Until growth data materially improve, the curve is likely to remain relatively flat at the long end while the short end bears the brunt of the adjustment.

This is a nuanced but important distinction. Many investors might assume that a bearish rates view means selling the entire curve. The report's analysis suggests the correct trade is more targeted: pay the 3-year point, where the combination of liquidity normalization and supply pressure is most concentrated.

## The Report Leaves Several Critical Questions Unanswered

For all its analytical rigor, the report does not fully resolve several questions that are central to the outlook.

First, how sustainable is the outflow from onshore funds? The report notes that funds reduced bond holdings in June after accumulating large positions in April and May. But it also acknowledges that the selling in 30-year CGBs has been small relative to the inflows seen in previous weeks. Is this the beginning of a broader de-risking, or just a tactical pause? The answer depends on whether the liquidity tightening is seen as temporary or structural.

Second, what is the PBoC's reaction function? The report interprets the central bank's OMO pattern as a signal that it wants to avoid pushing money market rates too low. But it does not specify at what level the PBoC would intervene to prevent rates from rising too fast. The 7-day repo fixing is currently moving toward 1.50 percent. If it breaches 1.60 percent, would the PBoC step in with more aggressive OMOs? The report's analysis of past patterns suggests it might, but the current environment of slower growth and fiscal stimulus complicates the calculus.

Third, what happens if bond supply accelerates faster than expected? The report's estimates for June and Q3 issuance are based on historical norms and the remaining quota. But local governments have discretion over timing. If they front-load issuance in July and August, the market could face a concentrated wave of supply that overwhelms demand. The report does not model this tail risk in detail.

These open questions are not weaknesses. They are the natural boundaries of any forward-looking analysis. But they also define the key risks that investors must monitor.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in Chinese Rates

For investors trying to translate this analysis into portfolio decisions, the following framework may be useful.

Start with the liquidity regime. The PBoC's cumulative net injection is now well below historical averages. This is the most important single indicator. As long as it remains low, the bias is for higher short-term rates. The 7-day repo fixing and 1-year NCD yields are the best proxies to track.

Next, evaluate the supply schedule. The slower the issuance in the first half, the greater the pressure in the second half. The key dates are the end of June, when tax payments and quarter-end funding needs coincide with the start of the LGB issuance ramp, and the third quarter, when monthly net supply is expected to peak.

Then, assess demand dynamics. Onshore funds have been the marginal buyer of long-end bonds. If they continue to reduce holdings, the long end will come under pressure. But insurance firms are natural buyers at higher yields. The critical level to watch is 2.25 percent on 30-year CGBs. If yields approach that level and insurance buying accelerates, it will provide a floor.

Finally, consider positioning. The report's preference for paying 3-year NDIRS is consistent with a view that the front end is most vulnerable. The 3-year point offers a clean expression of the liquidity and supply thesis without taking on the additional uncertainty of growth expectations that affects longer tenors. The fact that NDIRS are trading about 3 basis points below onshore IRS suggests that the offshore market has not fully priced in the tightening, which provides additional room for the trade to work.

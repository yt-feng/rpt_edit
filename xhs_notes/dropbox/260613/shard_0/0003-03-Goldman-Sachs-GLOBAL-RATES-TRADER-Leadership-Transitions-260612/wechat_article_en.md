# The Disinflation Trade Is Not Over: Why Markets Should Prepare for a Steeper Curve, Not a Flattening Panic

The market has begun to price in a more benign outlook for spot commodity prices, but the real battle for durable disinflation has only just moved to a new front. The critical insight for fixed-income investors is that longer-dated oil prices, not the front-month contract, will determine whether central banks can anchor yields lower over the medium term. While the immediate relief from falling spot oil has steepened yield curves and moderated front-end hike pricing, the structural path to a sustained steepening is far from assured. The coming weeks will be defined by a series of central bank meetings where the messaging around the balance of risks, particularly from the Federal Reserve under new leadership, will either confirm or disrupt the current trajectory. The most consequential question is not whether the Fed will hike again, but whether the market is correctly pricing the probability of a prolonged hold and the eventual return of a cutting bias.

The United States presents the most instructive case for the broader global rates narrative. Front-end pricing has moderated and the curve has steepened from recent lows, driven by optimism surrounding a potential US-Iran deal and the steady decline in oil prices. Yet even after this repricing, US rates markets still embed roughly 30 to 35 basis points of tightening risk through mid-2027. This implies that a significant probability of multiple rate hikes is already discounted. The controlling idea here is that the market is overestimating the likelihood of a hawkish outcome. A lengthy hold is more consistent with the economic fundamentals than a resumption of hikes, and the medium-term tail risk of rate cuts should not be dismissed. The skew, therefore, is firmly towards lower 1-year forward 1-year yields over time, with the front end of the curve best positioned to benefit from a conclusive geopolitical resolution.

The analytical challenge, however, is that the composition of recent inflation data has been firmer than the headline numbers suggest. While the May core PCE translation came in below consensus, the underlying details were less reassuring. This creates a tension: the market has already repriced inflation expectations lower, but further progress on disinflation will require a more convincing slowdown in labor market conditions or economic activity. Until that evidence materializes, there should be a floor under inflation forwards, which have already returned to pre-conflict levels. The real opportunity lies in the real rate market. Longer-term real yields remain only modestly below their post-February highs, suggesting that the bulk of the risk premium is still embedded in the real curve rather than in inflation compensation. If the market begins to price renewed concerns about the labor market or activity, real yields can fall meaningfully. The strategic trade, therefore, is to be long 10-year inflation swaps versus a fraction of receiving 10-year SOFR, capturing the asymmetry in how nominal and real rates respond to a shift in the macro narrative.

## The New Fed Chair's Communication Style Will Be the Dominant Source of Near-Term Volatility and Curve Direction

The first Federal Open Market Committee meeting under Chair Warsh introduces a layer of uncertainty that goes beyond the usual dot plot and policy statement. The market's focus will be on two dimensions: the substance of the forward guidance and the style of its delivery. On substance, the key variables are the number of 2026 hike projections in the dot plot, whether the 2027 dots show any cuts, and the framing of the labor market. A stronger emphasis on the lack of inflation pressure from the labor market or on trimmed measures of inflation would be taken as dovish and support a steeper curve. Conversely, a more vigilant signal about inflation risks would reinforce the hawkish pricing already embedded in the front end.

But the style of communication may matter even more in the near term. Chair Warsh has previously argued against the Fed providing too much guidance, warning that by publishing forecasts, officials risk becoming "prisoner of their own words." This philosophical stance represents a potential departure from the Powell era, where press conferences became an increasingly important and volatile-inducing instrument of policy transmission. The risk is that as the market adjusts to a new communication standard, rate volatility around FOMC meetings could rise initially. This is not a trivial concern. If the new Chair adopts a more deliberate vagueness, the market may struggle to calibrate the policy reaction function, leading to sharper repricing events. The prudent positioning is to maintain a steepening bias while hedging hawkish or oil upside risks with positions that would benefit from a pull-forward of hike risk, rather than betting against a flattening outright.

## European Rates Face a Divergent Path: The ECB's Persistence Problem Is Different from the Fed's

The European rate landscape is structurally distinct from the US, and the market has not fully priced this divergence. The ECB became the first major central bank to hike in the aftermath of the Iran conflict, motivated not by the magnitude of the near-term oil spike but by the persistence of inflation in its latest projections. This distinction is crucial. Front-end rates have repriced sharply on the rise in oil prices, but they have shown little decline despite the fall in the front-month contract. The better guide to inflationary risk has been longer-dated oil prices, which have remained flat and reflect more persistent upward pressure. This suggests that the market is still pricing too much relief in the UK front end, given the gap to policy rate views, while the ECB's more proactive tightening approach should see the EUR curve beyond one-year rates outperform on any selloffs.

The implication for strategy is clear: maintain a long position in 5-year forward 5-year real OIS in Europe. This trade captures the potential for both nominal rate relief and inflation curve steepening if concrete prospects for lower energy prices emerge. The structural tailwind for this view is that the hawkish aspects of the ECB's inflation projections come from persistence, not magnitude. If energy stress fades, the relief should be more durable for longer-dated real rates than for the front end, where the market has already repriced aggressively.

## UK Rates Offer the Most Compelling Asymmetric Trade on Inflation Relief, But Fiscal Risks Loom

The United Kingdom stands out as the market with the highest sensitivity to oil prices among major developed economies, even after adjusting for volatility. This creates an asymmetric opportunity. With the Bank of England expected to hold rates at the upcoming meeting, the focus will be on Governor Bailey's message. He has already signaled that the shift away from cuts constitutes a tightening in itself, neutralizing some hawkish messaging from other MPC members. The UK activity picture remains soft, and the distribution of energy prices has shifted lower from both oil market rebalancing and the prospects for a US-Iran deal. The front end of the UK curve is pricing too much hike premium relative to the fundamentals, making long positions in the front end a reasonable risk-reward proposition.

However, this trade comes with a caveat that the report does not fully resolve. Ongoing debates around defense spending are a reminder of the government's limited fiscal room, which is unlikely to improve without some macro relief on inflation and rates. Gilt risk premium is likely to remain sticky, and UK yields will move lower primarily as a function of inflation relief rather than a repricing of fiscal sustainability. The strategic implication is to stay in GBP forward 2s10s steepeners, but to recognize that the tail risk is not a hawkish BoE but a fiscal event that overwhelms the monetary policy narrative. This is an open question that the current analysis does not fully address, and it warrants close monitoring.

## The Report Leaves an Unresolved Tension: Can Disinflation Persist Without a Growth Slowdown?

The most significant analytical gap in the current framework is the assumption that disinflation can continue without a material deterioration in the labor market or economic activity. The report argues that real yields can fall meaningfully on renewed concerns about the labor market, but it does not fully address the scenario where growth remains resilient. If the US cyclically outperforms as the data suggests, and if the labor market remains tight, the floor under inflation forwards may prove more durable than expected. In that case, the steepening trade would be challenged, and the market would need to reprice the policy reaction function in a more hawkish direction. The report's recommendation to be long real rates on a beta-adjusted basis is compelling, but it relies on the premise that the next macro shock will be disinflationary. If the next shock is a growth surprise, the positioning would suffer.

This unresolved tension is precisely why the report's analysis should be viewed as a framework for decision-making rather than a definitive call. The path to a steeper curve is not guaranteed; it is conditional on the evolution of growth and inflation data. The value of the analysis lies in identifying the key variables and the asymmetric payoffs, not in predicting the outcome.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning Across the Global Rates Complex

For investors looking to translate this analysis into actionable positioning, a three-part framework is useful. First, assess the sensitivity of each market to oil prices. The UK and Canada exhibit the highest sensitivity, making them the most attractive candidates for inflation relief trades. Japan and Switzerland, by contrast, are less sensitive to oil, meaning their rate dynamics will be driven by domestic factors. Second, evaluate the central bank reaction function. The ECB is more proactive than the Fed, making the EUR curve more likely to underperform on selloffs but also more likely to steepen on relief. The BoJ remains constrained by political factors, making JGB underperformance likely unless the data turns decisively. Third, identify the source of risk premium. In the US, the bulk of risk premium is in the real curve, making real rates the most attractive vehicle for expressing a disinflation view. In Europe, the risk premium is in inflation persistence, making real OIS and inflation swaps the preferred instruments.

The most important decision, however, is not which trade to put on but how to size it. The report's analysis suggests that the asymmetric payoffs favor steepeners and long real rates, but the volatility around central bank communication and geopolitical developments argues for modest sizing and active risk management. The market is at an inflection point where the direction of travel is clear, but the timing is uncertain.

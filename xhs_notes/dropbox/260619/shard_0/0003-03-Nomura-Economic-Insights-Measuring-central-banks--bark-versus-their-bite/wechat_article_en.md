# Central Banks' Bark Is Not Their Bite: Why the ECB, BOE, and Fed Are Diverging in Action, Not Just Words

The conventional wisdom that central banks talk tough but act softly is breaking down—but only for some. A new analysis using Bloomberg's natural language processing model to measure the hawkishness of central bank communication reveals a striking divergence: the European Central Bank has been matching its aggressive rhetoric with actual rate hikes, the Bank of England has been talking without acting, and the Federal Reserve may soon lose the ability to communicate altogether. This is not a story about uniform policy tightening. It is a story about three institutions facing fundamentally different constraints, and the implications for investors are far from symmetrical.

The stakes are higher than a simple comparison of policy rates. What we are witnessing is a test of whether central banks have learned the lesson of 2021, when they fell behind the curve by dismissing inflation as transitory. The ECB appears to have internalized that lesson. The BOE seems to be reverting to old habits. And the Fed is entering uncharted territory where its primary communication tool may become effectively useless. For portfolio managers, the question is no longer whether central banks will tighten further, but which ones will follow through on their words—and which will be silenced.

This report from a global investment bank provides a quantitative framework for answering that question. By measuring the gap between central bank rhetoric and policy action, it offers a lens through which to assess not just near-term rate decisions, but the broader credibility of each institution. In an environment where inflation remains sticky in some regions and fiscal dominance looms in others, understanding who actually means what they say is the single most important macro input for asset allocation.

## The ECB Has Learned the 2021 Lesson: Hawkish Words Now Precede Hawkish Deeds

The most striking finding from the analysis is the ECB's behavior. The ECBspeak index has risen to 20.7, a level last seen in March 2022—just before the last hiking cycle began. But the critical difference is timing. In 2022, the ECB waited four months after reaching that level to deliver its first hike. This time, it hiked 25 basis points almost immediately, bringing the deposit facility rate to 2.25%. The central bank has effectively compressed the lag between rhetoric and action, and it has done so at a time when the policy rate is already significantly higher than it was at the start of the last cycle.

This matters because it signals a structural shift in the ECB's reaction function. The institution appears to have internalized the reputational damage from its "transitory inflation" misjudgment in 2021. It is now willing to act preemptively, even when the macroeconomic case is less clear-cut. The report notes that the ECB's policy rate is currently at or below estimates of the neutral rate, which means further tightening is not obviously contractionary. But that is precisely the point: the ECB is not waiting for clear evidence of overheating. It is using its bark to create space for action, and then following through.

The implication for investors is that euro area rate expectations should be taken seriously. The global investment bank's Europe economics team has already added three more 25-basis-point hikes to their forecast profile, extending through March 2027. This is not a dovish pause disguised as hawkishness. It is a genuine tightening cycle, and the communication data supports that view.

## The BOE Is Repeating the 2021 Pattern: Hawkish Talk Without Follow-Through

Contrast the ECB's behavior with the Bank of England. The BOEspeak index has risen to 4.4, a level that in the last cycle preceded the first rate hike. But this time, the BOE voted 7-2 to hold rates steady at its most recent meeting. The policy rate remains at 3.75%, and the global investment bank's Europe economics team has now revised its call to no hikes this year, followed by two cuts in the second half of 2027.

The divergence between the ECB and BOE is not random. It reflects two structural differences. First, the BOE's policy rate is starting from a much higher level—3.75% versus 2.25% for the ECB—and is already considered above neutral. This gives the BOE less room to tighten without risking overtightening. Second, the data outturns are different. The UK labor market is showing signs of weakening, and inflation has risen by less than expected. In the euro area, inflation is stickier, and the ECB faces greater concerns about second-round effects through wage growth.

But there is a deeper issue here. The BOE's behavior suggests it has not fully learned the 2021 lesson. It is talking hawkishly to anchor inflation expectations, but it is not willing to back that talk with action. This creates a credibility problem. If markets begin to doubt that the BOE will follow through on its rhetoric, the very tool of forward guidance becomes less effective. The BOE may find itself in a position where it needs to hike more aggressively later precisely because it failed to act when its bark was loudest.

For fixed-income investors, this means UK gilts may be pricing in too much hawkishness. The gap between rhetoric and action is wide, and if the BOE continues to hold, that gap will need to close through either a softening of communication or a repricing of rate expectations. The latter seems more likely.

## The Fed May Soon Lose Its Voice Entirely: A New Chair's Abhorrence to Forward Guidance

The Federal Reserve presents a different kind of problem. The Fedspeak index has risen to 7.7, a level that in the last cycle preceded the first hike by ten months. But that ten-month lag turned out to be a policy error—the Fed was well behind the curve when it finally acted. The question is whether this time will be different.

The report flags a critical complication: the Bloomberg Fedspeak index may become useless because the new Fed chair has an abhorrence to forward guidance. If the central bank's primary communication tool is effectively disabled by the person at the helm, then the entire framework for interpreting Fed policy breaks down. Investors cannot rely on the usual signals from speeches and press conferences. They must instead infer policy intentions from data alone, which introduces greater uncertainty and volatility.

This is not a minor technical issue. The Fed's credibility as an inflation fighter rests in part on its ability to communicate clearly. If the new chair refuses to provide forward guidance, the Fed loses one of its most powerful tools for shaping expectations. In an environment where inflation is rising and financial conditions remain loose—driven by fiscal dominance and the US political cycle—the risk is that the Fed falls behind the curve again, this time without the ability to correct course through communication.

The report's suggestion that rising inflation and loose financial conditions could lead to a similar outcome as 2021 is worth taking seriously. But without a functioning communication channel, the Fed's reaction function becomes opaque. Investors should prepare for a regime in which Fed policy surprises become more frequent and more consequential.

## The Analytical Gap: What the Bark-Bite Framework Does Not Yet Explain

For all its power, the bark-bite framework leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important is why the ECB and BOE are diverging so sharply despite facing similar global headwinds. The report offers two explanations—starting rate levels and data outturns—but these are proximate causes, not root ones. The deeper question is whether the ECB's willingness to act reflects a genuine institutional change or a temporary alignment of political and economic incentives.

A related question concerns the role of fiscal policy. The report notes that fiscal policies are far less expansionary this time than in 2021-22, which should reduce the pressure on central banks to tighten. But the US case suggests that fiscal dominance is still very much alive. If the new Fed chair's aversion to forward guidance is in part a response to political pressure, then the Fed's silence may be a symptom of a broader erosion of central bank independence. The framework does not address how to distinguish between a deliberate communication strategy and a coerced one.

Finally, the framework is backward-looking in an important sense. It measures the gap between rhetoric and action, but it does not predict when that gap will close. The ECB closed its gap quickly. The BOE has not. The Fed may close its gap through action or through silence. But the framework cannot tell us which path each central bank will take, or what triggers the closing of the gap. This is not a criticism of the analysis—it is a recognition that the most important questions remain open.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Use the Bark-Bite Gap

Despite these gaps, the bark-bite framework provides a usable decision tool. The key insight is that the gap between rhetoric and action is itself a signal. A wide gap combined with a high level of hawkish rhetoric suggests either that the central bank is bluffing or that it is about to act. The investor's task is to distinguish between these two scenarios.

The framework suggests three diagnostic questions. First, is the policy rate above or below neutral? If it is below neutral, as with the ECB, the central bank has room to follow through on its rhetoric. If it is above neutral, as with the BOE, follow-through is less likely. Second, are the data outturns supporting the rhetoric? If inflation and labor market data are consistent with hawkish communication, action is more probable. If the data are weakening, the rhetoric is likely a placeholder. Third, is the central bank's communication channel functioning? If the central bank is using forward guidance effectively, as the ECB is, the rhetoric is credible. If the communication channel is broken, as may be the case with the Fed, the rhetoric is noise.

Applying this framework to the current environment yields a clear set of bets. The ECB is the most likely to continue tightening, and rate expectations should be biased upward. The BOE is the most likely to disappoint hawkish expectations, and rate expectations should be biased downward. The Fed is the most uncertain, and the safest bet is to prepare for volatility rather than directional conviction.

## The Unresolved Question That Makes the Full Report Essential

The bark-bite framework raises a question it cannot fully answer: what happens when a central bank's communication tool breaks entirely? The Fed case is the most extreme, but it is not unique. If the new Fed chair's aversion to forward guidance becomes a permanent feature of the institution, then the entire edifice of central bank transparency—built over the last three decades—begins to crumble. Investors would need to develop entirely new methods for decoding policy intentions.

The report from the global investment bank provides the data and the framework, but it leaves the reader to grapple with the second-order implications. How should portfolios be positioned in a world where one major central bank is tightening credibly, another is bluffing, and a third has gone silent? What happens to cross-asset correlations when the Fed's communication channel is unreliable? These are the questions that demand a deeper dive into the original charts and analysis.

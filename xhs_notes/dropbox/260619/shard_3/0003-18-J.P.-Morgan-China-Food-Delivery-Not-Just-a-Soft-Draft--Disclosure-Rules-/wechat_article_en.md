# The Food Delivery Subsidy War Is Not Over, But the Rules of Engagement Have Changed

The market's first reaction to China's draft food-delivery subsidy rules was a shrug. Investors saw the absence of explicit subsidy caps and concluded the rules lacked bite. That reading is incomplete, and it misses the most important strategic shift underway. The draft does not need to cap every promotion to matter. It needs to make the most aggressive campaigns slower, more visible, and easier to challenge. That is precisely what it does, and that changes the competitive dynamics of China's food-delivery market in ways that are not yet priced in.

The core argument is simple but consequential: disclosure, funding-source scrutiny, and competitive-impact self-assessment are not procedural formalities. They are the enforcement mechanism. By requiring seven-day pre-disclosure of campaigns, mandating that subsidies be funded from own operating profit rather than capital advantage, and forcing platforms to document competitive impact, the draft creates an evidentiary trail that regulators, competitors, merchants, riders, and media can all scrutinize. That changes the economics of subsidy competition even before any penalty is imposed.

The timing is equally important. The draft lands during the 618 promotional window, precisely when renewed subsidy escalation would have tested the bear case for Meituan. The rules arrive as a sentiment stabilizer, but their real value lies in what they reveal about the likely direction of competition. The battleground is shifting from capital-funded cash burn toward operating execution. For investors, that means the relative value setup across Meituan, Alibaba, and JD needs to be re-examined.

## The Draft Does Not Cap Subsidies, But It Changes the Game by Targeting Speed, Visibility, and Accountability

The most damaging form of food-delivery competition is not ordinary promotion. It is fast, capital-funded subsidy escalation designed to change consumer and merchant behavior before the incumbent can respond. The draft targets that mechanism directly through three interrelated requirements.

First, the seven-day pre-disclosure rule slows the game down. Food-delivery subsidy wars are reactive and tit-for-tat. A challenger launches a promotion, the incumbent responds, and the market tests how far each side is willing to go. A seven-day public notice window reduces the value of surprise and weakens the speed advantage that capital-funded attacks rely on. The incumbent gains time to prepare, and the challenger loses the element of surprise that makes aggressive campaigns effective.

Second, the funding-source requirement creates a structural asymmetry. By requiring subsidies to come from own operating profit rather than capital advantage, the draft limits the ability of challengers to fund food-delivery losses with group-level capital. This is the load-bearing clause. If enforced strictly at the segment level, it strongly favors the incumbent with the most profitable delivery operations. If interpreted more loosely at the group level, the asymmetry weakens. But the direction is clear: capital-funded share attacks become harder to justify and execute.

Third, the competitive-impact self-assessment creates a written record that can later support enforcement. Platforms must disclose campaign design, funding source, and competitive impact. That documentation creates accountability. A campaign that must be disclosed in advance is easier for competitors, merchants, riders, media, and regulators to monitor. The reputational and enforcement risk should reduce the willingness to run extreme campaigns, even if no explicit penalty is triggered.

The so-what is clear: the draft does not need to ban every subsidy to change platform behavior. It only needs to make the most aggressive campaigns harder to launch, justify, and repeat. That is a meaningful change in the competitive environment, even if the market has not yet priced it fully.

## The Most Important Interpretive Question Is Whether "Own Operating Profit" Means Segment-Level or Group-Level, and the Answer Determines the Strategic Winners

Provision 3 is the clause that matters most, and its interpretation is the single biggest uncertainty. The requirement that subsidies come from own operating profit rather than capital advantage can be read in three ways, each with very different implications.

If the final rules require segment-level or near-segment-level profit to fund delivery subsidies, the strategic benefit to Meituan is substantial. Alibaba and JD would find it much harder to use group-level profits to support food-delivery losses. Meituan, whose delivery business is already profitable, would face less risk of a capital-funded attack on its core profit pool.

If the final rules allow group-level operating profit to satisfy the requirement, the asymmetry weakens materially. Alibaba could argue that its overall group profitability supports continued investment in food delivery. JD could make a similar case. The rule would still require disclosure and slow down campaigns, but the funding constraint would be much less binding.

If the final wording remains vague, enforcement behavior becomes more important than the text itself. Regulators would have discretion to interpret the rule case by case, creating uncertainty for all platforms. The market would need to watch actual enforcement actions, not just the rule text, to assess the competitive impact.

This interpretive ambiguity is why the draft does not yet justify estimate changes. But it is also why the probability distribution around Meituan's scenarios has shifted. The bear case, in which prolonged capital-funded competition weakens the delivery profit pool and Meituan's fair value falls to around HKD51, becomes less likely if the funding-source language is preserved with real bite. The base case and bull case become more probable.

## The Competitive Battleground Shifts from Cash Subsidy to Operating Execution, and That Favors the Incumbent

The draft changes the relative importance of different competitive levers. Before the rules, the easiest way to attack the incumbent was to use capital to subsidize losses and buy market share quickly. After the rules, that approach becomes more expensive, more visible, and easier to challenge. Competition can still happen, but it must move toward dimensions where operating capability matters more than headline cash burn.

Meituan's advantages lie in order density, routing efficiency, merchant coverage, rider network management, and consumer habit formation. These are precisely the variables that matter most when competition shifts from capital subsidy toward operating execution. A challenger cannot easily replicate Meituan's density advantage with money alone. It takes time, scale, and operational discipline to build a delivery network that matches the incumbent's efficiency.

The draft does not eliminate competition. Alibaba can still attack through fulfillment quality, merchant selection, membership benefits, and logistics innovation. JD can still invest in delivery capabilities. But the easiest form of competition, using capital to buy share through aggressive subsidies, becomes harder. The fight moves back onto Meituan's terrain.

There is a caveat. The draft also constrains Meituan's own promotional toolkit. It cannot rely on forced participation, below-cost pricing, discriminatory treatment, or surprise campaigns. The rules apply to all platforms equally. But for Meituan, the constraint is less damaging because its competitive advantage does not depend on aggressive subsidy campaigns. It depends on operational scale and efficiency.

The so-what is that the draft improves the floor for Meituan's profit pool more than it raises the ceiling. The risk of a negative-profit scenario, in which sustained loss-funded competition destroys industry profitability, becomes lower. But the industry is unlikely to return to a 2024-style profit ceiling, because redirected competition into service quality, logistics, and merchant support will still cost money.

## The Stock Implications Are Not Symmetrical: Meituan Gains Most Strategically, But the Draft Does Not Yet Justify a Rating Change

The relative stock implication favors Meituan on this specific catalyst, although not enough to prefer it outright over Alibaba at the portfolio level. Alibaba remains a broader AI, cloud, and platform optionality story. The point is narrower: on this specific food-delivery catalyst, the relative value setup favors Meituan more than the market is likely to price.

For Meituan, the draft reduces the probability of a prolonged capital-funded attack on its core delivery profit pool. It arrives at the moment when subsidy reacceleration would have been the clearest bear-case trigger. The rule shifts the fight from capital subsidy back toward operating efficiency, which is Meituan's terrain. But the draft does not yet justify an estimate change. The final wording matters, and actual subsidy intensity in the coming quarters will test whether the rules have real behavioral impact.

For Alibaba, the P&L effect is positive. Less food-delivery subsidy intensity should reduce losses in Taobao Instant Commerce and Ele.me. But the strategic effect is more complicated. Part of the Alibaba bull case assumes instant commerce can become a traffic-acquisition and user-frequency engine for Taobao. If the rule limits capital-funded delivery subsidies, that option becomes less valuable. Alibaba can still pursue the opportunity, but it may need to rely more on operating execution and less on balance-sheet-funded user acquisition. The rule taxes the optionality leg, even if it helps near-term losses.

For JD, the near-term earnings read-through is the cleanest. Its food-delivery entry has been subsidy-intensive, and the draft gives management both regulatory cover and economic reason to rationalize spend. But the strategic read is less favorable. JD's delivery push is the most dependent on capital-funded subsidy among the three platforms. If Provision 3 is enforced with real bite, the durability of JD's food-delivery position becomes harder to underwrite. The lasting impact of JD's entry may be less about permanent share gain and more about a higher sector cost floor, especially on rider wages and fulfillment standards.

## The Clearest Falsification Test Is 3Q26 Subsidy Intensity, and Investors Should Watch Three Other Catalysts

The draft is not the end of the debate. It is the start of a more observable phase. The next catalysts will determine whether the rules have real bite or remain a disclosure exercise without meaningful behavioral impact.

The most important falsification test is actual subsidy intensity in the third quarter of 2026. If reporting and industry checks show per-order subsidies continuing near first-half 2026 levels with no evidence of moderation, the interpretation that the rules are binding would weaken. Conversely, evidence of easing subsidy intensity would support a shift of probability weight from the bear case toward the base and bull cases.

Beyond the data, four catalysts matter most. First, the final SAMR text after the July 17, 2026 consultation deadline will determine whether Provision 3 keeps real bite or is watered down. Second, the first seven-day campaign disclosures will show whether platforms must reveal funding source and campaign mechanics, or whether the requirement becomes a formality. Third, third-quarter results and management commentary will test whether subsidy intensity is actually easing. Fourth, Alibaba's instant-commerce strategy and JD's reinvestment behavior will determine whether competition shifts from broad subsidy to high-AOV core attack or whether savings flow to earnings.

The draft changes the probability distribution, not the base case. A credible shift in the binding-versus-cosmetic debate would justify moving some weight out of the bear case and into the base and bull cases. But that requires evidence, not just text. The rules create the conditions for a more rational competitive environment, but the actual outcome depends on enforcement, interpretation, and platform behavior.

# The Seismic Industry Is Entering a Disciplined Multi-Year Upcycle, Not a Boom-Bust Repeat

The oil services sector has received a signal that investors have been waiting nearly a decade to hear: frontier exploration is returning. The 86th EAGE Annual Conference in Aberdeen, held in June 2026, drew record attendance of over 6,000 delegates, with C-suite engagement markedly higher than in 2025. The driving force behind this shift is not speculative exuberance but a structural necessity: reserve replacement and reserve life are firmly back on the agenda, reinforced by post-war energy security concerns. This is not a repeat of the 2010-2015 cycle. The spending discipline that defined the post-2014 era has permanently altered how capital flows into exploration, and the current inflection point reflects a fundamentally different set of incentives.

According to a fireside chat with Viridien (formerly CGG) held shortly after the conference, the seismic industry is poised for an inflection in 2027. The 2026 budgets were locked in before the recent geopolitical escalation, meaning the revenue impact has not yet materialized. Equinor's Capital Markets Day guidance of a 9% increase in capital expenditure is a directional positive, but the broader story is that exploration spending is set to rise in a measured, sustained manner. The question for investors is not whether the cycle is turning, but how to distinguish between companies that are structurally positioned to capture value and those that will merely benefit from a rising tide.

## Frontier Exploration Is Returning, but the Majors Have Lost the Expertise to Execute It Quickly

The dominant theme emerging from the EAGE conference was the return of genuine frontier exploration. Historically, approximately 80% of seismic activity was concentrated in near-field basins, where geological risk is lower and production infrastructure already exists. But near-field exploration alone is no longer sufficient to offset the steady decline in reserve life across the industry. The data is stark: the resource life of top projects has fallen from roughly 55 years in 2012 to an estimated 20 years in 2026, a 60% contraction driven by a decade of underinvestment in long-cycle exploration.

What changed in recent months is that clients are actively reopening frontier basins. Viridien flagged rising interest across Uruguay, the Equatorial Margin of Brazil, Angola, Egypt, Liberia, and a broadening of activity in the Gulf of America. Memoranda of understanding are being signed with countries that the company had never engaged before. This is not a marginal uptick; it represents a structural shift in how exploration capital is being allocated.

Yet there is a critical bottleneck: the majors have lost frontier-basin expertise. After years of focusing on short-cycle projects, portfolio optimization, and shareholder returns, the technical teams that once specialized in frontier geology have been downsized or redeployed. Rebuilding that capability takes time, and Viridien's management was explicit that the majors will need time to re-establish their frontier exploration competencies. This creates a window of opportunity for seismic companies that possess both the data libraries and the processing expertise to fill the gap. The implication is that the early phase of this upcycle will be characterized by reprocessing and data-driven insight rather than greenfield acquisition, which plays directly into the hands of companies with proprietary multi-client libraries.

## Reprocessing Is the Near-Term Workhorse, and It Plays Directly into the Structural Moat of Seismic Companies

While frontier exploration captures the headlines, the near-term revenue driver for seismic companies is reprocessing. Clients' own budgets show increasing allocation to reprocessing in frontier basins, often ahead of drilling, as they seek to lock up data early and reduce geological uncertainty. This is a behavioral shift that matters: instead of commissioning new surveys, exploration teams are extracting more value from existing data using advanced imaging techniques.

The economics are compelling. Reprocessing offers a cheaper route to better basin understanding than new acquisition. For seismic companies, it generates high-margin revenue that does not require deploying vessels or mobilizing large field crews. It also deepens the relationship with clients, as the output of reprocessing feeds directly into prospect generation and drilling decisions.

Viridien's management noted that prefunding traction is rising, which is itself a signal of client intent. When clients are willing to commit capital upfront to secure access to reprocessed data, it indicates a genuine commitment to exploration, not just optionality. Moreover, the outsourcing trend is accelerating. National oil companies, such as ADNOC, are increasingly handing the full image-to-prospect workflow to seismic players rather than building the capability in-house. This structural shift means that seismic companies are not merely data providers but are becoming integral partners in the exploration value chain. Exploration success rates remain low, typically 20-25% depending on the basin, so image quality remains the ultimate differentiator. Companies that can deliver superior imaging will command pricing power and recurring revenue.

## Vessel Supply Is Not a Constraint Today, but Pricing Dynamics Will Shift as the Cycle Matures

A common concern in any upcycle is that capacity constraints will lead to cost inflation that erodes margins. In the current environment, vessel availability is not a constraint. The streamer fleet has consolidated to approximately 16-17 active vessels, dominated by TGS and Shearwater, with a small number of additional vessels operated by PXGEO, Dubai-based players, and Chinese companies. There is no difficulty sourcing vessels today, and stacked capacity can be reactivated, albeit with a lag, if the market tightens.

Day rates have not yet increased despite the consolidation, which may seem counterintuitive. However, Viridien's management expects that if streamer costs rise, they will be passed through into multi-client library pricing. This suggests that pricing power is intact, but the timing of any increase will depend on the pace of demand recovery. The more interesting dynamic is in ocean-bottom node (OBN) surveying. OBN is approximately 4-5 times more expensive than streamer-equivalent acquisition, and while clients want more OBN data, they are price-restrained. The industry is working to reduce sensor deployment time, which should lower OBN pricing over time. This means that the OBN market may see volume growth but margin compression, while streamer-based acquisition benefits from a more favorable pricing environment.

The key takeaway is that supply-side constraints are unlikely to be the binding factor in this cycle. Instead, the limiting factor will be the availability of skilled personnel, processing capacity, and the speed at which majors can rebuild their frontier exploration capabilities. Companies that have invested in high-performance computing and proprietary algorithms will have a structural advantage, while those that rely on commoditized services will face margin pressure.

## AI in Seismic Is Accretive, Not Substitutive, and Image Quality Remains the Moat

One of the most misunderstood dynamics in the seismic industry is the role of artificial intelligence. There is a persistent narrative that AI will disrupt traditional imaging companies by enabling automated interpretation and reducing the need for specialized human expertise. The evidence from the EAGE conference and Viridien's assessment suggests the opposite.

AI cannot solve the physics-based wave equations that underpin seismic imaging. Those remain iterative, high-performance computing-intensive problems that require specialized algorithms and massive computational capacity. AI's contribution is concentrated in denoising, data cleaning, and workflow acceleration. It makes the processing pipeline faster and more efficient, but it does not replace the underlying imaging technology.

What is more significant is that end-clients are increasingly layering their own AI tools on top of seismic images to refine prospectivity. This means that the value of the underlying data is rising, not falling. If an exploration team has a high-quality seismic image, its proprietary AI models can extract more value from it. If the image is poor, no amount of AI can compensate. Image quality is the binding constraint on the value that AI tools can extract, which raises rather than commoditizes the value of high-quality data.

For seismic companies, the structural moat is not just the data library but the computing infrastructure and the proprietary algorithms that process it. Viridien's management noted that their in-house computing still requires data from the multi-client library, creating a virtuous cycle where data assets and processing capabilities reinforce each other. Disintermediation risk is low because national oil companies and independent players will likely choose to rely on specialized capabilities rather than build their own. This is a structural advantage that should persist through the cycle.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Magnitude of the Revenue Inflection

The report provides a compelling narrative for why the seismic industry is entering a multi-year upcycle, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most critical is the precise timing of the revenue inflection. Viridien expects 2027 budgets to be set materially higher, but 2026 budgets were locked in before the geopolitical escalation. This means that the revenue impact may be back-end loaded, creating a gap between the improvement in sentiment and the actual financial results.

A second unresolved question is the pace at which majors can rebuild their frontier exploration expertise. If the bottleneck is human capital and organizational capability, the revenue ramp may be slower than the market expects. Conversely, if the outsourcing trend accelerates, seismic companies could see revenue growth that outpaces the underlying exploration spend.

A third question is the pricing trajectory for multi-client library sales. While Viridien expects to pass through cost inflation, the competitive dynamics between the remaining players will determine whether pricing power translates into margin expansion or merely offsets cost increases. The report does not provide a clear framework for forecasting pricing in a consolidated but still competitive market.

Finally, the role of national oil companies in driving demand is highlighted but not quantified. The outsourcing trend is real, but the pace at which NOCs shift from in-house capability to external partners will vary by country and by the sophistication of their technical organizations. Investors would benefit from a more granular understanding of which NOCs are most likely to outsource and what the revenue potential is for seismic companies.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguishing Between Cyclical Beneficiaries and Structural Winners

The report provides the raw material for a decision framework that investors can use to evaluate seismic companies. The first dimension is the quality and relevance of the multi-client library. Companies with data coverage in the basins that are seeing renewed interest, such as the Equatorial Margin, Uruguay, and the Gulf of America, are better positioned than those with exposure to mature basins that are already well-imaged.

The second dimension is processing capability and HPC infrastructure. As AI becomes more integrated into the workflow, companies with proprietary algorithms and large-scale computing capacity will have a structural advantage. Companies that rely on third-party computing or commoditized processing will face margin pressure.

The third dimension is the exposure to the outsourcing trend. Companies that can offer the full image-to-prospect workflow, rather than just data acquisition or processing, will capture a larger share of client budgets. This is particularly relevant for NOCs that are choosing to outsource rather than build in-house capability.

The fourth dimension is balance sheet strength and the ability to invest in new surveys. The upcycle will require capital expenditure to acquire new data and expand multi-client libraries. Companies with strong balance sheets and access to prefunding from clients will be able to invest at attractive returns. Companies that are constrained by debt or cash flow may miss the opportunity.

The fifth dimension is pricing power. In a consolidated market, companies that have unique data assets or proprietary processing capabilities should be able to command premium pricing. Companies that offer commoditized services will face price competition.

Investors should use this framework to differentiate between companies that will benefit from the cycle and those that have structural advantages that will compound over multiple cycles. The report suggests that the current upcycle is disciplined and multi-year, which favors companies with recurring revenue streams, high barriers to entry, and pricing power.

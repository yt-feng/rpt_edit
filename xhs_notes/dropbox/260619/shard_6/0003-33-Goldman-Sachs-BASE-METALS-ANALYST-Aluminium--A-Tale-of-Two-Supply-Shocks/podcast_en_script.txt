EN_A: So there is this research report on aluminium with a pretty dramatic title: “A Tale of Two Supply Shocks.” What does that actually mean?
EN_B: It means the aluminium market is being pulled in two opposite directions. One shock is from the Middle East, which is cutting supply. The other is a wave of new supply coming from Indonesia and China, which is adding supply.
EN_A: So short term tight, long term loose?
EN_B: Exactly, but the timing matters a lot. The core argument is that even if the Strait of Hormuz reopens, Middle East smelters cannot restart production quickly. Damaged potlines need repairs, and restarting capacity is a slow process.
EN_A: How slow? Is there a precedent?
EN_B: The report points to an example from April 2025, when a grid outage in Iberia forced a smelter to shut down. It took until mid-2026 to fully restart. So roughly a year.
EN_A: And that affects price forecasts?
EN_B: Yes. The report raised its Q3 2026 and average 2027 price forecasts. For 2027, it went from 2750 dollars per tonne to 2950. But interestingly, that is still well below the forward price, which is around 3250.
EN_A: So the market is pricing in too much tightness?
EN_B: That is the view. The report thinks the market is overlooking the long-term supply wave.
EN_A: Where is that wave coming from?
EN_B: Indonesia is the biggest source. The report raised its 2027 Indonesian production forecast from 2.5 million tonnes to 2.9 million. Several large projects are ramping up faster than expected.
EN_A: And China?
EN_B: China is also adding. The report raised its 2027 China forecast to 46.3 million tonnes, even though there is a formal capacity cap of 45 million. Actual output has already exceeded that limit.

EN_B: It means the deficit in 2026 will shrink, and by 2027, the market will flip into a surplus. The report expects a 590,000 tonne surplus in 2027, compared to a 1.3 million tonne surplus in the previous forecast.

EN_B: Yes, but with a twist. The report actually closed a short position on December 2026 futures, which had lost money, and rolled into a short on December 2027 futures.

EN_B: Because the surplus story is more compelling for 2027. The forward price for that year is still too high, so it is a better opportunity to short. But the report admits the risk is that Middle East disruption lasts longer.

EN_B: Exactly. And the report does not fully explore what happens if the situation escalates again, or if the Indonesian ramp-up slows down. Those scenarios are probably covered in the full report.

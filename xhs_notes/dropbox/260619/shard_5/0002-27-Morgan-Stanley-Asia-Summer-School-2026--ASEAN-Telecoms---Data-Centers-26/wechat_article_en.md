# ASEAN Telecoms Are Not a Commodity Trade: The Real Driver Is Structural Competition, Not Technology

For years, the conventional wisdom on ASEAN telecoms has been simple: more users, more data, more revenue. That narrative is incomplete and increasingly dangerous for investors who rely on it. The real story in ASEAN telecoms for 2026 is not about 5G adoption rates or smartphone penetration. It is about the structural evolution of competition, the discipline of capital allocation, and the quiet but powerful emergence of data centers as a second engine for operators who own the right infrastructure.

A global investment bank report ranking ASEAN telecom markets for 2026 places Thailand first, followed closely by Singapore, with Malaysia and Indonesia tied for third, and the Philippines lagging. This ranking is not a beauty contest. It is a systematic assessment of five underlying drivers: competition intensity, capex cycle position, regulatory environment, capital allocation flexibility, and valuation. The markets that score highest are not necessarily the fastest-growing. They are the markets where the competitive structure allows operators to translate revenue into shareholder returns.

The implication is uncomfortable for those who treat all ASEAN telcos as a single trade. The divergence between markets is widening, and the winners will be determined by factors that have little to do with how many new subscribers sign up each quarter. The critical question for investors is no longer "which market has the most growth potential." It is "which market has the structure that allows operators to keep what they earn."

## Thailand's Duopoly Has Created a Pricing Environment That Other ASEAN Markets Cannot Replicate

Thailand ranks first in the 2026 assessment, and the reason is straightforward: the market has consolidated from three major players to two. The merger of TRUE and DTAC in 2022 created a duopoly alongside AIS, and the result has been a level of pricing stability that is rare in ASEAN telecoms. Blended ARPU across both operators has been trending upward, and broadband ARPU has risen as operators removed discounts.

This is not a story of technological superiority. Thailand's prepaid subscriber base remains above 70 percent, and the migration to postpaid is gradual. What matters is the competitive structure. In a duopoly, both players have a rational incentive to avoid price wars. They compete on network quality and service differentiation rather than on a race to the bottom on price. The report notes that competition has been stable post-merger, and the outlook remains rational.

The strategic lesson for investors is clear: in telecoms, market structure matters more than market size. A duopoly with rational pricing can generate higher returns on invested capital than a fragmented market with rapid subscriber growth. Thailand's top ranking is not an endorsement of its economic growth prospects. It is an endorsement of its competitive architecture.

## Indonesia's Post-Consolidation Dynamics Are Promising, But the Real Test Lies Outside Java

Indonesia has consolidated from ten mobile operators in 2008 to three today, and the report expects competition to improve in Java. The mergers of ISAT and H3I in 2022 and EXCL and FREN in 2025 have created a market where the three remaining players are converging toward an ARPU of approximately Rp45,000. This convergence is a positive sign. It suggests that the destructive pricing wars of the past are giving way to a more rational competitive environment.

However, the report also flags a critical nuance: competition is expected to rise outside Java. This is where the story becomes more complex. The newly merged operators are larger and stronger, which should allow them to invest in network coverage and quality in regions that were previously underserved. But investment requires capex, and capex pressures ROIC. The question is whether the revenue growth from expanded coverage will offset the capital required to achieve it.

For investors, Indonesia represents a classic second-derivative trade. The first derivative is the consolidation story, which is broadly positive. The second derivative is the capex cycle, which introduces uncertainty. The report ranks Indonesia's capex cycle outlook as neutral to weak, reflecting the reality that network expansion outside Java will require sustained investment. The bullish case for Indonesia depends on whether the operators can maintain pricing discipline while investing for growth. That is a delicate balance.

## Singapore's Broadband Battle Is a Warning That Mobile Stability Does Not Guarantee Overall Market Health

Singapore ranks second overall, and its mobile market is relatively stable with four players including the incumbent SingTel. However, the report highlights an emerging threat: competition in broadband has intensified sharply since SIMBA launched a 10Gbps plan at S$30 in 2024. This is not a minor skirmish. Broadband is a significant revenue stream for Singapore's telcos, and a price war in this segment can erode the gains from mobile pricing discipline.

The strategic takeaway is that telecom markets are not silos. A competitive disturbance in one segment can spill over into another. Singapore's mobile market may be rational, but its broadband market is not. The report's ranking reflects this tension: Singapore scores well on capital allocation and valuation but faces headwinds on the competition outlook.

For investors, Singapore is a reminder that market analysis must be segment-specific. A telco that looks attractive on mobile metrics alone may be exposed to competitive pressure in fixed-line or broadband. The report's five-driver framework is useful precisely because it forces a holistic assessment. Singapore's overall score of 22 is strong, but the dynamics are shifting.

## The Philippines Remains the Weakest Link, and the Third-Player Problem Is Not Solved

The Philippines ranks last in the 2026 assessment, and the reasons are structural. The market moved from two players to three with the entry of Dito in 2021, and the third player is still hanging around. This has kept competitive pressure elevated, and the report's outlook for competition is negative. Broadband penetration is low by ASEAN standards, and affordability is a major constraint. Broadband ARPU as a percentage of household income is approximately 2.5 percent, compared to the ASEAN average of 1.6 percent.

The report does not explicitly predict consolidation in the Philippines, but the logic is hard to ignore. A three-player market with low penetration and affordability constraints is not a recipe for strong returns. The two incumbent players, PLDT and Globe, have seen their combined market share erode, and the revenue mix has shifted decisively toward data. Legacy voice and SMS, which represented approximately 80 percent of revenue in 2013, are expected to fall to roughly 6 percent by 2026.

The open question is whether the Philippine market can support three profitable players over the long term. The report's ranking suggests skepticism. For investors, the Philippines is a market to watch for catalysts rather than to own for income. A consolidation event or a regulatory shift could change the calculus, but the current trajectory is unfavorable.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Unanswered Question: How Will Data Center Demand Reshape Capital Allocation?

One of the most intriguing dimensions of the report is its treatment of data centers. The report notes that telecom operators are benefiting from rising data center demand in the region, but it does not fully explore the implications for capital allocation and valuation. This is a meaningful gap.

Data centers are capital-intensive. They require significant upfront investment in land, power, cooling, and connectivity. For telecom operators, the appeal is obvious: they already own the fiber networks and real estate that data centers need. But the capital required to build out data center capacity can compete with the capital needed for network maintenance, spectrum acquisition, and dividend payments.

The report's framework includes capital allocation as one of the five key drivers, but it does not explicitly model how data center investment interacts with the other four. If a telco diverts capex from network upgrades to data center construction, what happens to its competitive position in mobile? If data center revenue grows but carries lower margins than core telecom services, what happens to ROIC?

These are second-order questions that the report does not fully answer. They are also the questions that will determine whether data centers are a tailwind or a distraction for ASEAN telcos. Investors who want to understand the full picture will need to dig deeper into the capital allocation strategies of individual operators and assess whether their data center ambitions are financially disciplined or strategically reckless.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Use the Five Drivers to Build a Market-Level Scorecard

The report's five-driver framework is not just an analytical tool. It is a decision framework that investors can use to evaluate individual telco stocks within each market. The logic is simple: score each market on competition, capex, regulation, capital allocation, and valuation. Then overlay the specific position of each operator within that market.

For example, in Thailand, the duopoly structure benefits both AIS and TRUE, but their capital allocation strategies may differ. In Indonesia, the post-merger landscape favors the larger players, but the capex requirements for ex-Java expansion introduce risk. In Singapore, the broadband price war creates headwinds for all players, but SingTel's scale and diversification may provide a buffer.

The framework also highlights the importance of regulatory risk. Regulation is scored as a separate driver, and its impact can be decisive. Spectrum auctions, interconnection fees, and network-sharing mandates can alter the competitive landscape overnight. Investors who ignore regulatory risk are investing blind.

The practical application is straightforward: before making a decision on any ASEAN telco stock, score the market across the five drivers. Then score the company relative to its peers within that market. The highest-conviction opportunities will be those where the market structure is favorable and the company is well-positioned within that structure.

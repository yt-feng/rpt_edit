# Agentic AI Is Reshaping the Server Market, and the Winners Are Not Who You Think

The next phase of artificial intelligence infrastructure buildout is not about more GPUs. It is about a fundamental shift in how AI workloads are executed, and that shift will dramatically alter the competitive dynamics among server original equipment manufacturers. The rise of agentic AI, which involves multi-step reasoning, tool calling, and iterative validation loops, places unprecedented demands on central processing units and traditional server architectures. This is not a marginal adjustment. It represents a potential tripling or quadrupling of the traditional server total addressable market by 2030, according to analysis based on recent commentary from major chip suppliers.

This development arrives at a critical inflection point for the server industry. Super Micro Computer, once the darling of AI infrastructure investors, is facing mounting reputational and compliance challenges that are already causing customers to reassess their vendor relationships. The combination of a structural demand shift toward CPU-intensive workloads and an incumbents' opportunity to capture displaced market share creates a powerful setup for Dell Technologies and Hewlett Packard Enterprise. The market has not fully priced this dual catalyst, and the upcoming earnings reports from both companies will likely serve as an important confirmation point.

The argument here is straightforward but carries significant investment implications. Agentic inference workloads are fundamentally more CPU-intensive than either training or simple chatbot inference. Training workloads are large, batch-oriented processes dominated by GPUs. Simple inference involves a single pass: prompt in, answer out. Agentic workloads, by contrast, involve multi-step task execution including tool calls, database queries, code execution, validation loops, and sub-agent spawning. These functions rely heavily on general-purpose compute and orchestration, driving demand for CPU-based servers alongside GPU clusters. The ratio of CPU to GPU in inference has already compressed from 1:8 to 1:4, and industry participants suggest it could trend toward parity or beyond.

The timing could not be more favorable for traditional server OEMs. Super Micro's most recent quarterly results revealed a declining mix of AI servers for the first time in ten quarters, dropping from 90 percent to 80 percent of revenue. This shift was margin-accretive but strategically concerning for the company's bull case. Simultaneously, the company's data center customer mix declined while enterprise mix increased. These changes reflect both genuine strength in traditional server demand and potential share losses stemming from recent reputational challenges, including a reported smuggling investigation. Customers across cloud, enterprise, and sovereign accounts are reassessing vendor risk, and the beneficiaries are likely to be Dell and Hewlett Packard Enterprise.

## The Shift from GPU-Centric to CPU-Centric Inference Creates a Structural Tailwind for Traditional Server Vendors

The conventional wisdom in AI infrastructure has been that the future belongs to GPU-accelerated systems, and that traditional servers are legacy technology destined for gradual decline. This view is becoming dangerously incomplete. Agentic AI workloads require orchestration, memory management, context handling, and iterative validation that CPUs handle far more efficiently than GPUs. A typical chatbot inference workflow involves a single GPU forward pass. An agentic workflow involves multiple tool calls, database queries, code execution steps, validation checks, and potential sub-agent spawning, all of which require CPU intervention.

The implications for server demand are substantial. AMD recently raised its server CPU total addressable market growth outlook from 18 percent to over 35 percent annually, projecting a market exceeding $120 billion by 2030. Intel noted on its earnings call that the CPU-to-GPU ratio in inference has compressed from 1:8 to 1:4 and could trend toward 1:1, while guiding to double-digit server CPU unit growth with momentum extending into 2027. Arm's CEO highlighted that agentic workloads could require more than four times today's CPU capacity per gigawatt of data center capacity.

A back-of-the-envelope analysis suggests that if CPUs account for 20 to 25 percent of traditional server average selling prices, the total traditional server TAM could reach roughly $500 to $600 billion by 2030, approximately 2.5 times the $213 billion TAM in 2025. If traditional server TAM grows at a 35 percent compound annual rate in line with server CPU growth, it would expand to approximately $955 billion, or 4.5 times 2025 levels. These are not incremental numbers. They represent a structural re-rating of the entire traditional server industry.

The buyer profile for CPU servers is also expanding beyond the traditional enterprise refresh cycle. Neocloud providers and tier-two cloud service providers are increasingly investing in CPU-based infrastructure to support agentic inference workloads. This makes the demand base both larger and more durable than in previous cycles. For Dell and Hewlett Packard Enterprise, both of which have strong positions in traditional server infrastructure, this represents a meaningful incremental tailwind that should support not just the next quarter but multiple years of growth.

## Super Micro's Compliance Overhang Creates a Rare Share Shift Opportunity That Benefits Scale Leaders

Super Micro's recent earnings beat was driven by a mix shift away from AI servers and toward traditional servers, combined with a higher proportion of enterprise customers. While this boosted gross margins, it does not signal a positive development for the company's core investment thesis. The AI server bull case relies on strong demand from large data center customers, and both of these drivers softened during the quarter. The company's management framed certain order delays as data center readiness issues, but the combination of compliance overhang, reputational damage from the smuggling case, and customer reassessment of vendor risk creates a real risk that these push-outs become cancellations.

The evidence of share shift is already emerging. Bloomberg reported that xAI, historically a Super Micro customer, was negotiating a multi-billion dollar server deal with Dell. If that shift was underway before the most recent compliance issues, the momentum is likely accelerating. Cloud customers, enterprise accounts, and sovereign buyers are all reassessing their vendor concentration risk. Super Micro's historical advantage was speed to market and deep relationships with GPU suppliers. But when compliance and reputational risk enter the equation, scale, financing capacity, and breadth of service offerings become far more important.

Dell is best positioned to capture displaced demand. The company has the largest scale among server OEMs, the broadest financing capacity, and the deepest customer relationships across cloud, enterprise, and sovereign segments. Dell has already demonstrated success with neocloud providers, a segment where Hewlett Packard Enterprise has less presence. The company's ability to finance large-scale AI infrastructure deployments gives it a structural advantage that becomes more valuable as customers seek to diversify away from a single vendor with compliance uncertainty.

Hewlett Packard Enterprise should also benefit, though to a lesser degree. The company has strong positions in enterprise and sovereign accounts, and the traditional server tailwind supports its core business. However, HPE lacks Dell's scale in large-scale AI infrastructure deployments and does not have the same level of neocloud exposure. The opportunity is real but more constrained.

## The Transition from Simple Inference to Agentic Workloads Has Not Yet Been Priced into Server OEM Valuations

The market has been focused on GPU supply chains, data center power constraints, and the training versus inference debate. The specific implications of agentic AI for CPU demand have received far less attention. This creates a potential mispricing. The traditional server TAM expansion we describe is not a speculative scenario. It is based on concrete commentary from the three major CPU vendors, each of whom sees the same structural shift occurring. When AMD, Intel, and Arm all independently raise their CPU demand outlooks citing the same driver, the signal deserves serious consideration.

The valuation disconnect is most apparent in Dell. The company's stock has performed well, but the multiple expansion we believe is justified has not fully occurred. Our analysis suggests that Dell should trade at a higher multiple than the market currently assigns, reflecting its position as the primary beneficiary of both the traditional server upcycle and the Super Micro share shift. Hewlett Packard Enterprise also deserves a higher multiple, though the magnitude is smaller given its more limited neocloud exposure and smaller scale in AI infrastructure.

For Super Micro, the risk-reward is firmly skewed to the downside. The compliance overhang creates a multi-quarter headwind that cannot be resolved quickly. The enterprise momentum that supported the recent quarter may fade as pre-scandal customer commitments are fulfilled. Data center delays raise the risk of soft cancellations. And potential risks around GPU supply add another layer of uncertainty. The company's strong position in AI servers is in jeopardy, and in a booming market, that is a particularly dangerous place to be.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Speed of Adoption and the Elasticity of CPU Demand

While the direction of the argument is clear, several critical questions remain unanswered. The first is speed. How quickly will agentic AI workloads scale from experimental deployments to production environments that meaningfully drive server procurement? The CPU vendors are signaling strong demand, but the translation from chip-level commentary to OEM revenue still depends on enterprise adoption cycles that have historically been unpredictable.

The second question is elasticity. If CPU demand in inference expands to the degree that Intel and Arm suggest, what happens to GPU demand in relative terms? The industry has been operating under the assumption that AI infrastructure spending is a GPU story. If the CPU share of inference workloads increases substantially, the total addressable market for AI infrastructure expands, but the GPU share of that market may compress. This has implications for GPU suppliers and for the entire AI supply chain.

The third question is competitive dynamics within traditional servers. If the TAM expands to $500 billion or more, the market becomes large enough to attract new entrants. Will the Chinese server OEMs that have been largely absent from Western AI infrastructure begin to compete for CPU-based workloads? Will the hyperscalers bring more server design in-house, reducing the addressable market for third-party OEMs? These questions are not answered by the current analysis and represent important areas for further research.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Positioning

For investors trying to translate this analysis into portfolio decisions, three questions provide a useful framework.

First, do you believe that agentic AI workloads will scale meaningfully over the next two to three years? If the answer is no, then the traditional server thesis is less compelling, and the focus should remain on GPU-centric infrastructure plays. If the answer is yes, then the traditional server OEMs deserve a closer look.

Second, do you believe that Super Micro's compliance issues will lead to sustained market share losses? If the answer is no, and you believe the company can resolve its governance challenges quickly, then the current valuation may be attractive. If the answer is yes, the question becomes which competitors are best positioned to capture that share.

Third, what is your time horizon? The traditional server TAM expansion is a multi-year theme that may not fully materialize in any single quarter. Investors with short time horizons may find the catalyst set less compelling than those who can hold through multiple earnings cycles. The upcoming Dell and Hewlett Packard Enterprise earnings reports will provide important data points, but the full thesis will take years to play out.

Dell emerges as the most preferred beneficiary across all three questions. The company has the scale, financing capacity, and customer relationships to capture both the structural tailwind from agentic AI and the share shift from Super Micro. Hewlett Packard Enterprise is a secondary beneficiary with a more constrained opportunity set. Super Micro faces a challenging setup where even successful execution may not be enough to overcome the compliance overhang.

The server industry is undergoing a structural transformation that has been obscured by the dominant narrative of GPU-centric AI infrastructure. The rise of agentic AI creates a powerful demand driver for traditional servers, and the competitive disruption at Super Micro creates an opportunity for scale leaders to capture market share. These two forces are converging at a time when valuations have not yet reflected the full opportunity. The next few earnings reports will provide important confirmation, but the thesis is built on multi-year trends that are only in their early innings.

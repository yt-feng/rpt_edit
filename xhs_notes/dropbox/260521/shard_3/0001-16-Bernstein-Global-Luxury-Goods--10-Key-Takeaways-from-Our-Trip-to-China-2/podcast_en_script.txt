EN_B: That’s exactly the question, but the answer isn’t simple. The report says demand is stabilizing from a low base, but it’s highly polarized. The very top spenders are still spending big, but middle-class consumers are holding back.
EN_A: So the old idea that a recovery in China means a broad rebound for all luxury brands might be wrong?
EN_B: Totally wrong. The report makes it clear that strong spending at the high end is not translating into a broad-based recovery. The middle-income consumer is cautious, more selective, and not willing to stretch for big-ticket, logo-heavy items anymore.
EN_A: Let’s talk about the bright spots. The report says Hong Kong is doing well. Why is that?
EN_B: Hong Kong stands out because the property market is stabilizing. Residential prices are firmer, transaction volumes are up, and that creates a “feelgood” effect. Combine that with 11 months of retail sales growth and improving hotel numbers, and you get a real boost for discretionary spending and luxury.
EN_A: So the wealth effect from housing is key. What about mainland China? Are tier-one cities seeing the same thing?
EN_B: They are, but it’s very uneven. Transaction volumes are up in tier-one cities, and there’s some price recovery. But the meaningful gains are concentrated in only the very best, prime locations. Most mainstream areas and mall projects are still under pressure.
EN_A: So the property recovery is local, not broad. What about the brands themselves? Is there a big gap between the winners and losers?
EN_B: That’s one of the core insights. Brands like Hermes, Louis Vuitton, Chanel, Van Cleef and Arpels, and top Swiss watchmakers are still very strong. But many tier-two and tier-three brands are struggling to stay relevant and keep their stores productive.
EN_A: So the strong get stronger, and the weak get weaker?
EN_B: Exactly. The strongest houses can still expand space, hold prices, and invest in experiences. The weaker names are already shrinking their retail networks and losing mindshare with consumers.
EN_A: How are these global brands adapting to the Chinese market? Are they just importing their global playbook?
EN_B: The report has a great term for this: “China for China.” Global brands are localizing everything. From product assortments and pricing to storytelling and clienteling. They’re building WeChat-centric CRM, partnering with local KOLs, and creating lifestyle ecosystems like cafes and museums, all designed specifically for Chinese consumers.
EN_A: That sounds like a huge investment. Are local Chinese brands taking advantage of this?
EN_B: They are, and they’re becoming serious competitors, especially in beauty, ready-to-wear, and jewelry at entry price points. Brands like Songmont and Grotto in leather goods, Laopu in jewelry, and Mao Geping in beauty are winning share by combining credible products with strong cultural storytelling and better value for money.
EN_A: So where does that leave the middle-class consumer? How are they spending?
EN_B: They are very value-conscious. They’re trading down within brands, or moving to outlets, second-hand platforms, or local brands. The report says years of price increases have made shoppers extremely price-aware. They’re not absorbing further hikes anymore.
EN_A: So to defend pricing power, global brands are leaning into experiences and activations?
EN_B: Yes. The report highlights examples like The Louis event in Shanghai, Hermes craftsmanship events, and branded pop-ups. The goal is to turn stores and malls into destinations, not just points of sale.
EN_A: What does this all mean for investors? What’s the report’s takeaway?
EN_B: The report is more constructive on the strongest global houses, like Louis Vuitton, Dior, and Richemont’s key brands.

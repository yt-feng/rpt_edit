# China's Luxury Market Is Not Recovering — It Is Restructuring, and Only the Strongest Brands Will Survive

The dominant narrative surrounding China's luxury market has shifted from crisis to cautious recovery. A global investment bank's recent field trip to Hong Kong and Shanghai reveals something far more nuanced and strategically significant: the Chinese luxury consumer is not returning to pre-2024 spending patterns. Instead, the market is undergoing a structural reordering that rewards the very top of the pyramid while leaving the middle class, mid-tier brands, and second-rate retail locations in a prolonged state of fragility.

This is not a V-shaped recovery. It is a K-shaped divergence. Spending at the ultra-high end remains robust, supported by resilient demand for top-tier hospitality, premium jewellery, and iconic hard luxury. Yet middle-income consumers, who drove the sector's double-digit growth for years, have become value-conscious, selective, and increasingly willing to trade down — into outlet channels, second-hand platforms, or domestic Chinese brands that offer better perceived value. The result is a market where Hermès, Louis Vuitton, Chanel, and Van Cleef & Arpels continue to thrive, while a long tail of tier-2 and tier-3 international brands face shrinking relevance and shrinking retail footprints.

For investors and executives, the implications are stark. The old playbook of blanket expansion across China's cities and malls is no longer viable. The new imperative is hyper-selective distribution, deep localisation, and a willingness to concede the mass aspirational segment to rising domestic competitors. The report's findings suggest that the luxury sector's next phase in China will be defined not by broad-based growth, but by a Darwinian contest for the most productive square metres, the most loyal clients, and the most culturally resonant storytelling.

## Hong Kong Is Leading the Recovery, but Its Dynamics Are Not Replicable on the Mainland

The most encouraging signal from the field trip is Hong Kong. After a prolonged downturn, the territory is experiencing a genuine revival. Residential property prices have been recovering since September 2025, with week-on-week growth averaging approximately 0.4% since January 2026. This has fed directly into a "feelgood" factor among local consumers, who have responded with 11 consecutive months of retail sales growth. Jewellery, watches, and valuable gifts — the core of Hong Kong's luxury ecosystem — have posted year-on-year growth of 25-30% in early 2026.

This is a meaningful data point, but it must be interpreted with caution. Hong Kong's recovery is being driven by a combination of factors that do not easily translate to mainland China: a concentrated property market rebound in a small geography, a return of mainland tourists who had been absent for years, and a local consumer base that is wealthier and more liquid than the average mainland Chinese household. The "feelgood" effect from rising asset prices is real, but it is concentrated among property owners in a city where homeownership rates are below 50%. The broader mainland consumer remains far more cautious.

The report's hotel RevPAR data underscores this divergence. Luxury hotels in Greater China — particularly The Peninsula — have seen a dramatic outperformance, with RevPAR growth reaching 17-19% year-on-year in late 2025. Meanwhile, mid-scale and economy hotels have flatlined or declined. This is not a sign of broad-based consumption recovery. It is a signal that the wealthy are spending more on premium experiences, while everyone else is tightening their belts. Investors should not extrapolate Hong Kong's momentum to the mainland. The two markets are operating on fundamentally different consumer dynamics.

## Brand Polarisation Has Become a Structural Feature, Not a Cyclical One

The report's most consequential finding is that brand polarisation has intensified to a degree that makes it a structural feature of the Chinese luxury market, not a temporary cyclical phenomenon. The strongest houses — Hermès, Louis Vuitton, Chanel, Van Cleef & Arpels, and top Swiss watch maisons — are not merely surviving; they are gaining share, expanding space in prime locations, and maintaining pricing power. Weaker brands, by contrast, are being forced to reduce their retail networks, cut prices, and cede consumer mindshare.

This is not simply a function of brand equity. It reflects a deeper shift in Chinese consumer behaviour. The high-end consumer, who drives the bulk of luxury spending, has become more discerning and more concentrated in their brand preferences. They are not spreading their spend across multiple labels. They are consolidating around a small set of houses that offer the highest perceived craftsmanship, exclusivity, and cultural cachet. The middle-income consumer, meanwhile, has become value-conscious and price-aware after years of cumulative price increases across the luxury sector. Instead of absorbing further hikes, they are trading down — within brands to entry-level SKUs, or out of brands entirely into outlets, second-hand platforms, or domestic alternatives.

The implication for brand strategy is clear. It is no longer enough to have a recognisable name and a presence in China's top malls. Brands must demonstrate that they can command consumer loyalty at the highest price points, while also offering a value proposition that justifies their premium in the eyes of more price-sensitive shoppers. Houses that sit in the middle — aspirational but not iconic, expensive but not exclusive — face an existential squeeze. They are too costly for the value-conscious middle class and not exclusive enough for the ultra-wealthy.

## "China for China" Is No Longer Optional — It Is the Only Viable Strategy

For years, global luxury brands approached China as an extension of their home markets, importing global collections, global marketing playbooks, and global pricing strategies. That era is over. The report highlights that "China for China" is now central to strategy, with leading brands localising assortments, storytelling, and clienteling in ways that go far beyond token gestures.

This is not merely about adding Chinese New Year capsules or hiring local celebrities. It is about rethinking the entire consumer experience from the ground up. Leading brands are building WeChat-centric CRM systems that integrate every touchpoint from pre-purchase discovery to post-sale service. They are partnering with local KOLs who understand the nuances of Chinese social commerce. They are creating lifestyle ecosystems — cafés, museums, club-like spaces — that turn stores into destinations rather than points of transaction.

The most striking examples come from the report's discussion of experiential retail. "The Louis" in Shanghai, Hermès' craftsmanship events, and branded pop-ups that transform malls into immersive environments are not marketing gimmicks. They are defensive investments in pricing power. In a market where middle-class consumers are increasingly value-conscious, the ability to charge a premium depends on offering an experience that cannot be replicated online or in an outlet. Brands that fail to invest in this level of localisation will find themselves competing on price alone — a battle they are likely to lose against well-positioned domestic brands.

## Domestic Brands Are No Longer a Niche Threat — They Are Winning the Middle Market

Perhaps the most underappreciated finding in the report is the rise of Chinese domestic brands as serious competitors in beauty, ready-to-wear, and jewellery, especially at entry and mid-price points. Songmont and Grotto in leather goods, Laopu in jewellery, and Mao Geping in beauty are not fringe players. They are winning market share by combining credible product quality with strong cultural storytelling, sophisticated digital engagement, and a value-for-money proposition that many international aspirational brands cannot match.

This is a structural shift with deep implications. For years, the conventional wisdom was that Chinese consumers preferred foreign luxury brands for their heritage, craftsmanship, and status signalling. That preference has not disappeared at the top of the market, where Hermès and Chanel remain unassailable. But at the entry and mid-levels, where brand loyalty is weaker and price sensitivity is higher, domestic brands are exploiting a gap. They offer products that are "good enough" in quality, more relevant in cultural narrative, and significantly cheaper than their foreign counterparts.

The report does not quantify the market share shift, but the direction is unmistakable. International brands that depend on aspirational middle-class consumers — the so-called "accessible luxury" segment — face a growing competitive threat from local players who understand Chinese consumer psychology better and can move faster. The days when a foreign logo alone could command a premium in China are fading. Brands must now earn that premium through product, experience, and cultural resonance.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Hong Kong's Momentum and the Depth of Mainland Property Recovery

For all its insight, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most pressing is whether Hong Kong's recovery is sustainable or merely a cyclical bounce driven by a property market rebound and a one-time return of tourist flows. The report notes that Hong Kong property prices have recovered, but the recovery is concentrated and may not have broad-based support. If property prices stall or decline again, the "feelgood" factor could reverse quickly, pulling luxury spending down with it.

A second open question concerns the depth and durability of mainland China's property stabilisation. The report shows that second-hand property prices in tier-1 cities have only recently turned positive, and the recovery is uneven. Meaningful price appreciation is concentrated in prime locations, leaving many mainstream areas and mall projects still under pressure. The wealth effect from property is the single most important driver of consumer confidence in China. If the recovery in transaction volumes does not translate into sustained price appreciation across a broader geography, the luxury market's stabilisation may prove fragile.

The report also does not address the geopolitical dimension in detail. Trade tensions, regulatory shifts, and the broader decoupling between China and the West could have unpredictable effects on luxury demand, particularly if Chinese consumers begin to associate foreign luxury brands with geopolitical risk. This is a second-order implication that the report acknowledges but does not explore in depth. Investors would be wise to monitor it closely.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Executives

The report's findings can be distilled into a practical decision framework for investors and brand executives. The first question to ask is: where does your brand sit on the polarisation spectrum? If your brand is among the top tier — Hermès, Louis Vuitton, Chanel, Van Cleef & Arpels, top Swiss watch maisons — the strategy is clear. Continue to invest in flagship locations, experiential retail, and deep localisation. The risk is not demand destruction but over-distribution. Protect exclusivity at all costs.

If your brand is in the middle tier — aspirational but not iconic — the strategic calculus is more difficult. You face a two-front war: losing high-end share to the top houses and losing entry-level share to domestic competitors. The report suggests that the only viable path is to double down on a narrow set of strengths — a specific category, a unique craftsmanship story, or a particularly loyal customer segment — and accept that broad-based growth in China may no longer be attainable. Consolidation of the retail network, aggressive cost control, and a focus on full-price sell-through are essential.

If your brand is in the turnaround category — Burberry, Ferragamo, and similar names — the report offers a cautiously optimistic view. The key metric to watch is not top-line growth but brand momentum as measured by full-price sell-through and consumer sentiment. The report notes that Burberry's "Forward" strategy has shown early signs of success, but the next leg of the turnaround will depend on driving store productivity. For investors, the risk-reward in turnaround names is binary. Success could yield substantial returns. Failure could lead to further erosion of brand equity and retail relevance.

For investors constructing a portfolio, the report's implicit recommendation is a barbell approach. Hold core positions in the strongest global houses that are best positioned to navigate a selective recovery. Complement these with selective exposure to self-help stories where the turnaround is credible and the valuation is undemanding. Avoid the middle ground — brands that are too weak to compete at the top and too expensive to compete at the bottom.

# The Data Center Pipeline Is Not a Demand Signal — It Is a Capacity Constraint Map

The North American data center project pipeline has reached 295 GW, having tripled year over year. That figure sounds like a demand story. It is not. It is a story about bottlenecks, stranded capacity, and a structural shift in how power is sourced for the largest computing infrastructure buildout in history. The headline number is impressive, but the real insight lies beneath it: the pipeline is growing faster than the industry can build, and the gap between announced projects and operational capacity is widening in ways that will reshape supply chains, operator strategies, and investor returns for years.

This is not a cyclical boom. It is a secular transformation in how digital infrastructure is planned, permitted, powered, and paid for. The data in this monthly tracker from a leading institutional research team reveals a system under enormous strain — not from lack of demand, but from constraints on land, power, equipment, and community approval. Understanding those constraints, rather than the pipeline total, is what separates informed positioning from momentum-driven guesswork.

The report introduces a new monthly data series that tracks every stage of the data center lifecycle: pipeline, under construction, stranded, and behind-the-meter. It covers operators from hyperscalers to developers to neoclouds, and it maps activity by geography and by electrical equipment exposure. For anyone investing across industrials, tech hardware, semiconductors, or power infrastructure, this framework is essential. The numbers are large, but the patterns are what matter.

## The Pipeline Has Tripled in a Year, but the Real Story Is the Shift in Who Is Driving It

The 295 GW pipeline represents roughly 22 percent of total U.S. electricity demand. That alone should command attention. But the composition of growth reveals a changing of the guard. In the most recent month, developers accounted for 86 percent of the pipeline increase, hyperscalers for 37 percent, and neoclouds for 13 percent. Within hyperscalers, Amazon was the dominant contributor. This is not a uniform buildout. It is a market where third-party developers are taking the lead, hyperscalers are scaling selectively, and neoclouds are growing but from a smaller base.

The geographic concentration is equally telling. Texas alone added 6 GW to the pipeline in a single month, representing 46 percent of total growth. Indiana added 2.3 GW. The center of gravity is not shifting toward coastal metros or traditional tech hubs. It is moving to states with available land, favorable permitting environments, and existing grid infrastructure. That concentration creates both opportunity and risk. It means supply chains, labor pools, and electrical equipment demand are becoming regionally clustered. It also means any disruption in those states — whether regulatory, environmental, or grid-related — could have outsized effects on the entire pipeline.

The year-over-year growth of 3x is staggering, but it raises a critical question: how much of this pipeline will ever become operational? The report provides an answer, and it is not reassuring.

## Stranded Capacity Is Growing Faster Than Construction, and That Changes the Risk Calculus

Stranded capacity — projects that have been cancelled, delayed, or not approved — rose to 32 GW in April, representing 11 percent of the total pipeline. That is up from 7 to 9 percent last summer. In a single month, stranded capacity grew by 3.4 GW. Developers drove more than half of that increase, and another 35 percent remains unaccounted for by operator type.

This is not a marginal issue. It is a structural feature of the current environment. Local opposition, NIMBYism, and regulatory hurdles are rising. The cancellation rate has climbed to 25 percent, up from 11 percent in March and 4 percent in December. The pace of stranding is accelerating faster than the pace of construction. That means the pipeline is becoming less reliable as a forward indicator of actual buildout. Investors who treat pipeline growth as a proxy for revenue growth are likely to be disappointed.

The implication is clear: the bottleneck is not demand. It is execution. The ability to secure permits, manage community relations, and navigate environmental reviews is becoming a competitive differentiator. Operators with strong track records in these areas will command a premium. Those without will see their projects pile up in the stranded category. For electrical equipment suppliers, engineering firms, and construction companies, the risk is not that demand disappears. It is that projects shift to the right, compress margins, or get cancelled entirely. The market is pricing in a straight-line growth story. The data suggests a more volatile path.

## Behind-the-Meter Power Is Not a Niche — It Is Becoming the Dominant Model for New Capacity

The most consequential structural shift in the report is the acceleration of behind-the-meter power. The BTM pipeline has grown to 115 GW, up 30 percent year to date. For context, only 13 GW of BTM capacity is currently under construction. The gap between pipeline and construction is enormous, but the direction is unmistakable.

Currently, only 4 percent of the installed base of data centers use behind-the-meter power. But 22 percent of capacity under construction will use it, and 39 percent of projects in the pipeline plan to use it. That is a five-year transformation in how data centers are powered. It is not incremental. It is foundational.

The operator type data is even more striking. Neoclouds are the most reliant on BTM, with 66 percent of their pipeline using it. Developers follow at 40 percent, and hyperscalers at 36 percent. This suggests that BTM is not a preference. It is a necessity for operators who cannot secure grid interconnection timelines that match their buildout schedules. For hyperscalers, the lower reliance may reflect their ability to negotiate directly with utilities and secure dedicated transmission capacity. For neoclouds, BTM is survival.

The supply chain implications are enormous. BTM projects spend roughly 30 percent more on electrical distribution equipment than grid-connected projects. That is a direct tailwind for companies like Eaton, Hubbell, and others in the electrical equipment space. It also supports the expansion plans of large engine manufacturers like Caterpillar and Cummins, who are scaling capacity in anticipation of this demand. The report notes that developers added the most to the BTM pipeline last month and have been the most aggressive over the last 12 months, accounting for roughly 60 percent of total BTM additions. This is not a speculative trend. It is happening now.

## The Electrical Equipment TAM Is Vast, but It Is Not Equally Distributed

Based on the current pipeline and company-disclosed revenue per megawatt, the report estimates total addressable markets for several electrical OEMs. The numbers are large: Quanta Services at $4 trillion, Vertiv at $914 billion, Schneider at $787 billion, Eaton at $690 billion, ABB at $590 billion, and Legence at $299 billion. These are multi-decade opportunities, not cyclical upswings.

But the distribution of that TAM matters more than the aggregate. The data center buildout is not a rising tide that lifts all boats equally. It favors companies with exposure to electrical distribution, power management, cooling, and backup generation. It penalizes those tied to commodity hardware or legacy grid infrastructure. The report's investment implications are consistent with this view: outperform ratings on Eaton, Hubbell, Legence, Jacobs, Trimble, and United Rentals, with market-perform ratings on Caterpillar, Cummins, Oshkosh, and Quanta Services.

The differentiation reflects a nuanced view of where the value is being created. Electrical equipment suppliers benefit from both volume and content per megawatt. Engineering and construction firms benefit from labor scarcity and project complexity. Rental companies benefit from the need for temporary power and cooling during construction. Engine manufacturers benefit from the shift to BTM, but face competition from alternative power sources and potential technology substitution. The TAM is large, but the winners will be those who solve the most acute bottlenecks.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask Before Positioning

The report provides data, not a playbook. But the data suggests a clear framework for decision-making. Every investor in this theme should ask three questions about any company they evaluate.

First, does the company benefit from the growth in the pipeline, or from the growth in construction? These are different signals. Pipeline growth reflects ambition, permitting, and land acquisition. Construction growth reflects actual spending, equipment orders, and labor deployment. Companies tied to construction — electrical equipment, engineering, rental, and cooling — have more visibility. Companies tied to pipeline announcements — land owners, developers, and speculators — have more risk. The gap between the two is widening, and that favors the construction-linked names.

Second, is the company exposed to behind-the-meter power, and does that exposure increase its revenue per megawatt? BTM is not just a power source. It is a content multiplier. The 30 percent uplift in electrical distribution spending is a structural advantage for companies like Eaton and Hubbell. It also creates opportunities for engine manufacturers, but only if they can maintain pricing power in a market that will attract new entrants. The companies that have already embedded BTM into their product roadmaps are better positioned than those treating it as an optional add-on.

Third, can the company manage the geographic concentration risk? Texas and Indiana are driving the current wave. But regulatory environments change, grid capacity shifts, and community opposition can emerge quickly. Companies with diversified project exposure across multiple states and operator types have lower execution risk than those concentrated in a single region or a single customer. The report shows that hyperscalers are less reliant on BTM, while neoclouds are the most reliant. That divergence reflects different risk profiles, not different demand trajectories.

These three questions do not replace detailed financial analysis. But they provide a strategic filter that separates companies with structural tailwinds from those riding a cyclical wave.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer — and Why That Matters

The report is comprehensive, but it leaves several open questions that are essential for forward-looking investors. The first is the elasticity of construction timelines. If the pipeline continues to grow at 5 percent per month while construction grows at 12 percent, how long before the industry hits a physical capacity limit on labor, equipment, and grid interconnection? The report provides the data to model this, but it does not offer a specific timeline. That is a judgment call that each investor must make.

The second open question is the role of neoclouds. They are growing fast, they are the most reliant on BTM, and they have the highest cancellation risk. The report rates CoreWeave at underperform, with a price target well below the current price. That is a specific view on a single company, but it raises a broader question: are neoclouds a structural part of the ecosystem, or are they a temporary arbitrage that will be absorbed by hyperscalers as capacity tightens? The data supports both interpretations, and the answer will determine which operators and suppliers benefit over the next three to five years.

The third question is the impact of technology substitution. The report focuses on electrical equipment and power generation, but it does not address the potential for more efficient chips, liquid cooling, or alternative data center architectures to reduce power demand per unit of compute. If AI workloads become more efficient, the pipeline may need to be revised downward. If they become more power-hungry, it may need to be revised upward. The report provides a snapshot, not a forecast. Investors must build their own assumptions about technology trajectory.

These gaps are not weaknesses. They are invitations to dig deeper. The report provides the best available data on what is happening now. The next step is to model what happens next.

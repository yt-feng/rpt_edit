# China Pharma’s R&D Efficiency Gap Is Closing: Three Companies Are Emerging as Structural Winners

The Chinese pharmaceutical and biotechnology sector has spent the last decade scaling up research and development spending at a pace that often outpaced revenue generation. For years, the dominant narrative was one of quantity: more pipeline assets, more clinical trials, and more INDs filed than ever before. The question that mattered then was whether China could build a discovery engine at all. That question has been answered. The new question, and the one that will separate long-term value creators from capital destroyers, is whether these companies can turn R&D dollars into durable revenue and licensing income with measurable efficiency.

A new analytical framework from a global investment bank now provides the most systematic answer yet. By measuring economic return on R&D, time-to-market conversion, and pipeline hit rates across nine leading Chinese pharma and biotech companies, the analysis reveals a clear bifurcation. Innovent, Akeso, and Hengrui are projected to emerge as the sector’s future champions. Hansoh currently leads in sales return on R&D but faces limited upside. Kelun-Biotech excels in licensing income return but lags in commercial-stage revenue. Meanwhile, several large pharma players are seeing their R&D returns decline as older products mature and new pipelines have not yet reached critical mass.

This is not a story about who spends the most. It is a story about who spends wisely, who converts early-stage science into approved drugs faster, and who can sustain that advantage into the next decade. The implications for portfolio construction, partnership strategy, and competitive positioning are significant. The window for identifying the winners is narrowing.

## The Most Important Metric Is Economic Return on R&D, and It Reveals a Clear Separation Between Leaders and Laggards

The report constructs two complementary measures of R&D return. The first is sales return on R&D, defined as innovative drug sales in a given year divided by the cumulative R&D spending from four to seven years prior. This captures the lagged payoff of research investment. The second is license income return on R&D, which divides licensing revenue by the cumulative R&D spending from three to five years prior, reflecting the monetization of out-licensed assets.

In 2025, Hansoh leads the peer group with a sales return on R&D of approximately 2.0 times, followed by Innovent at roughly 1.7 times. Kelun-Biotech stands out on the licensing side, with a return of 1.0 times, reflecting its success in out-licensing its antibody-drug conjugate platform to global partners. However, the forward-looking projections tell a different story. By 2030, Innovent and Akeso are expected to achieve sales returns of approximately 3.1 times, while simultaneously generating licensing returns of 0.7 to 0.9 times. Hengrui, among the larger pharma companies, is forecast to reach a sales return of 2.4 times by 2030, underpinned by a wave of new product launches.

The pattern is unmistakable. The companies that are improving their R&D returns are those that have both strong domestic commercial execution and a pipeline that is generating externally validated milestones and royalties. The companies that are stagnating or declining are those whose revenue growth is moderating while R&D spending continues to rise. This is not a cyclical phenomenon; it is a structural divergence.

## Time-to-Market and Pipeline Hit Rates Reinforce the Same Conclusion: Innovent and Akeso Are in a Different Tier

R&D efficiency is not just about how much revenue comes back relative to spending. It is also about how quickly assets move from preclinical to approval and how many of those assets actually make it. The report calculates the median time between IND application and marketing approval for all innovative products across the coverage universe. The average for oncology products is approximately 2,100 days. For non-oncology products, it is roughly 2,900 days.

Zai Lab has the shortest time-to-market in both categories, which is expected given its in-licensing model that brings in late-stage global assets requiring only Phase 3 or bridging studies in China. But among companies with substantial internal pipelines, Innovent leads. Its median time-to-market in oncology is approximately 1,067 days, roughly half the peer average. In non-oncology, it is about 2,111 days, nearly 30 percent faster than the average.

The hit rate metric reinforces this. The report defines hit rate as the total number of marketed innovative drugs divided by all INDs submitted before the end of 2023. On average, biotech companies have nearly twice the hit rate of pharma companies. Innovent and Akeso again stand out, with hit rates exceeding 40 percent. This is not a statistical fluke. It reflects disciplined pipeline management, early-stage prioritization, and a willingness to terminate assets that lack clear differentiation.

When combined, these metrics imply that Innovent and Akeso are not just spending efficiently; they are systematically better at selecting, developing, and commercializing assets. That is a durable competitive advantage.

## Size and Scale Still Matter, but Only When Paired with Efficiency

Hengrui remains the dominant player in absolute pipeline size, with more than 360 assets. Innovent has now crossed the 100-asset threshold, entering the range of mature biopharma companies. Interestingly, Innovent also has the highest proportion of potentially first-in-class assets at approximately 28 percent, compared to a peer average of roughly 20 percent.

But scale alone is not the story. The report also examines the number of clinical trials sponsored per billion renminbi of R&D spending. Here, Akeso and Hengrui are the most efficient, averaging approximately 20 trials per year per billion renminbi. This suggests that both companies are extracting more clinical data per unit of spend, a critical capability in an environment where trial costs are rising and patient recruitment is becoming more competitive.

The implication is clear. Pipeline size is a necessary condition for long-term success, but it is not sufficient. The companies that will generate the highest returns are those that combine scale with speed, conversion rates, and capital discipline. Hengrui has the scale. Innovent has the speed and hit rate. Akeso has the efficiency and a major catalyst in its AK112 program, which is approaching potential FDA approval and a milestone-royalty inflection point.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Licensing Income and the Impact of Policy Shifts

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several important questions open. The first concerns the sustainability of licensing income as a return driver. The projections for Innovent and Akeso assume that milestone payments and royalties from existing out-licensing deals will materialize on schedule and at the expected magnitude. But the history of biotech licensing is littered with delays, trial failures, and renegotiated terms. The AK112 program, for example, is a high-conviction asset, but its FDA approval timeline and commercial trajectory remain uncertain. A delay of even one year would shift the projected return profile materially.

The second open question is the impact of China’s evolving drug pricing and reimbursement policies. The report does not explicitly model the effect of Volume-Based Procurement expansions or National Reimbursement Drug List negotiations on the revenue trajectories of innovative drugs. For pharma companies like CSPC and Sino Biopharmaceutical, which have significant exposure to mature products facing pricing pressure, the return projections may prove optimistic if the policy environment tightens further.

The third question is whether the efficiency gap between the leaders and the rest can be closed through management action or whether it is structurally determined. The report suggests that Innovent and Akeso have built organizational capabilities that are difficult to replicate. But if a company like Hansoh or Hengrui were to significantly improve its time-to-market or hit rate through targeted process changes, the competitive landscape could shift again.

These are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of any forward-looking framework. Investors and strategists should treat the report’s projections as directional rather than deterministic, and should monitor the underlying assumptions as new data emerges.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Corporate Strategists

The report’s findings can be translated into a practical decision framework with three dimensions. The first dimension is return trajectory. Companies with improving or stable sales return on R&D and licensing return on R&D are likely to generate superior risk-adjusted returns over the next five years. The second dimension is operational efficiency, as measured by time-to-market and hit rate. Companies that are faster and more selective in their pipeline management have a structural cost advantage that compounds over time. The third dimension is catalyst visibility. Companies with near-term milestones, such as regulatory approvals, data readouts, or licensing payments, offer more transparent paths to value realization.

Applying this framework, Innovent and Akeso score highly on all three dimensions. Hengrui scores highly on return trajectory and operational efficiency but has a more distributed catalyst profile. Kelun-Biotech scores highly on licensing return but has limited near-term commercial revenue. Hansoh scores well on current return but has limited upside and slower pipeline conversion.

For corporate strategists, the framework suggests that the most attractive partnership targets are those with high hit rates and fast time-to-market, even if their current revenue base is small. These companies have the highest probability of generating multiple blockbuster assets over time. For investors, the framework implies that the market may still be underestimating the compounding effect of R&D efficiency. The gap between the leaders and the laggards is likely to widen, not narrow.

# China Regional Banks Are Entering a Stock-Picker’s Market: Net Interest Income Recovery Is Real, But Fee Divergence and Capital Constraints Will Separate Winners from Laggards

The first quarter of 2026 delivered a clear signal for investors in China’s regional banks: the broad-based net interest income (NII) recovery that many had hoped for has arrived. For the seven banks covered in a recent global investment bank report, average NII growth accelerated to 16% year-on-year in 1Q26, up from 13% in 4Q25, supported by early signs of net interest margin (NIM) stabilization and a 5% quarter-on-quarter loan expansion that outpaced both state-owned banks and the system-wide average. Pre-provision operating profit (PPOP) growth improved to 13% year-on-year, a meaningful step up from the 3% recorded in the prior quarter.

Yet this headline improvement masks a more complex reality. The recovery is not yet a clean profit story. Fee income trends remain uneven across banks, non-interest income continues to show volatility, and rising credit costs have capped bottom-line profit growth at just 7% year-on-year. More importantly, the divergence in asset quality trajectories and capital adequacy buffers is widening, creating a clear fault line between banks that can sustain their recovery and those that cannot.

The sector is transitioning from a period of uniform pressure into a stock-picker’s market. Investors can no longer buy the regional bank theme indiscriminately. The banks that combine strong core earnings recovery with manageable credit costs and adequate capital buffers will outperform. Those that lack any one of these three pillars face downside risks that are not yet fully priced into their valuations.

## The NII Recovery Is Genuine, But Its Sustainability Depends on Loan Mix and Deposit Cost Management, Not Just Volume

The 16% year-on-year NII growth in 1Q26 is the strongest print the sector has delivered in recent quarters, and it is not simply a function of easy comparisons. The underlying drivers are structural: regional banks have been able to grow loans at a 5% quarterly pace, more than double the rate of the broader banking system, with corporate lending acting as the primary engine. At the same time, deposit cost savings from repricing are beginning to flow through to the income statement, contributing to a 7 basis point quarter-on-quarter NIM improvement for the banks that disclose this metric.

However, the sustainability of this NII recovery is not guaranteed. The first-quarter data reflects the seasonal “good start” effect, where banks front-load loan origination to capture early-year demand. This effect may not persist through the remainder of the year. Moreover, the quality of loan growth matters as much as the quantity. Banks that are expanding their corporate loan books into higher-quality sectors with better pricing power will sustain NIM improvement longer than those that are chasing volume into more competitive or riskier segments.

The divergence in fee income performance adds another layer of complexity. Average net fee growth was 12% year-on-year in 1Q26, but this figure is misleading. One bank posted 82% fee growth, driven by its wealth management franchise, while several others reported negative fee growth. This is not a cyclical pattern; it reflects structural differences in business models. Banks that have invested in wealth management, advisory, and transaction banking capabilities are building recurring fee streams that can offset NIM compression over time. Banks that remain dependent on traditional lending income lack this buffer.

For investors, the implication is clear: NII recovery is a necessary condition for regional bank outperformance, but it is not sufficient. The banks that can demonstrate both NII momentum and fee income diversification will command premium valuations. Those that rely solely on lending volume will face increasing pressure as competition for quality loans intensifies.

## Asset Quality Divergence Is the Single Most Underappreciated Risk in the Sector

On the surface, asset quality indicators for the sector appear stable. The average non-performing loan (NPL) ratio was flat quarter-on-quarter in 1Q26, and the special mention loan (SML) ratio actually improved by 6 basis points. However, these averages obscure a critical divergence that is accelerating beneath the surface.

The banks that are performing well on asset quality are doing so because of favorable regional exposure and conservative underwriting. For example, banks with strong positions in the Chengdu-Chongqing economic circle benefit from a regional growth dynamic that supports corporate borrowers and limits stress in retail portfolios. These banks are seeing improving forward-looking indicators, including declining SML ratios and stable credit costs.

In contrast, banks with greater exposure to retail lending, particularly consumption loans and mortgages, are experiencing deteriorating trends. One bank saw its NPL ratio rise by 3 basis points quarter-on-quarter in 1Q26, while its credit cost surged by 64 basis points year-on-year to nearly 1%. Its SML and overdue loan ratios deteriorated significantly in the second half of 2025, and its NPL coverage ratio is now the lowest among its peers at 198%. This bank’s provision buffer is thin, and its ability to smooth earnings through reserve releases is severely limited.

The risk is not just that credit costs will remain elevated. The risk is that they will normalize to levels seen during previous stress cycles, which would be materially higher than current consensus estimates. For the bank mentioned above, a return to the average credit cost level of the 2015-2020 period would imply an additional drag of roughly 4-5% on net profit for every 5 basis point increase in credit costs. This is not a tail risk; it is a base case scenario that the market has not fully discounted.

Retail and SME-related risks remain the key points to watch across the sector. The banks that have managed these exposures well are those with strong risk management frameworks and conservative underwriting standards. The banks that have not will face an earnings headwind that no amount of NII recovery can offset.

## Capital Adequacy Is Becoming the Binding Constraint That Limits Growth and Dividend Visibility

Perhaps the most underappreciated theme emerging from the first-quarter results is the growing importance of capital adequacy as a differentiating factor. The average CET1 ratio for the covered banks has been declining, and several are now operating with dangerously thin buffers above regulatory minimums.

One bank’s CET1 ratio stands at just 8.59%, a mere 84 basis points above the regulatory minimum. This is the lowest buffer among its peers. The management has indicated it will follow a “capital-constrained asset growth” approach, which is a polite way of saying that loan growth will be limited by the inability to raise additional capital. This constraint has two immediate implications.

First, it limits the bank’s ability to participate in a potential macro recovery. Even if loan demand strengthens, the bank cannot grow its balance sheet without breaching capital requirements. This means its earnings recovery will be capped by its balance sheet capacity, not by market opportunity.

Second, and more importantly, it threatens dividend sustainability. Regional banks with weak capital positions face a difficult trade-off: they can either retain earnings to build capital, which means cutting dividends, or they can maintain dividends and risk regulatory intervention. For a sector where dividend yield has been a key part of the investment thesis, this trade-off is existential. The bank with the thinnest capital buffer also has the weakest earnings visibility, creating a double risk to both EPS and DPS.

The banks that are better positioned are those with CET1 ratios above 10% and clear paths to organic capital generation through retained earnings. These banks can continue to grow loans, maintain or increase dividends, and potentially return excess capital to shareholders. The market has not yet fully priced this divergence in capital strength, but it will.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Path of Credit Costs in a Slowing Economy and the Viability of the Dividend Yield Trade

The report provides a thorough analysis of first-quarter trends and makes clear rating changes based on the data. However, it leaves several meaningful open questions that investors must consider.

The most important unresolved question is the trajectory of credit costs through the remainder of 2026 and into 2027. The report notes that credit costs rose in 1Q26 and that some banks face structurally higher normalized credit costs. But it does not provide a framework for estimating how high these costs could go if the macro environment deteriorates further. The sensitivity analysis provided for one bank is useful, but it is bank-specific. A sector-wide stress scenario could reveal that current consensus estimates for credit costs are too low by 20-30 basis points, which would have material implications for earnings across the board.

The second open question is whether the dividend yield trade for regional banks is structurally broken. Several banks have positioned themselves as defensive yield names, with payout ratios around 30% and dividend yields in the 4-5% range. But as the report notes, investor appetite for non-SOE bank dividend plays appears to be fading. If capital constraints force dividend cuts at some banks, the entire yield narrative could unravel. The question is whether the higher-yielding banks with stronger capital positions can maintain their dividends, or whether the sector as a whole will need to reduce payouts to preserve capital.

Finally, the report does not fully address the implications of the thin capital buffers for the sector’s ability to support economic growth. Regional banks are the primary lenders to local governments, SMEs, and the private sector in their regions. If capital constraints force a slowdown in lending, this could have second-order effects on regional economic activity and, in turn, on the asset quality of the banks themselves. This circular risk is not captured in the first-quarter data but could become a dominant theme in the second half of the year.

## A Decision Framework for Regional Bank Investors: Three Filters to Separate Winners from Laggards

For investors navigating this increasingly complex sector, the first-quarter results provide a clear analytical framework. Every regional bank investment should be evaluated through three filters, and a bank must pass all three to warrant a positive view.

The first filter is core earnings momentum. This means NII growth that is sustainable, not just seasonal, and fee income that is growing, not declining. Banks that show both NII and fee momentum have business models that can generate organic earnings growth even in a low-rate environment. Banks that rely on NII alone are more vulnerable to margin compression and competition.

The second filter is asset quality trajectory. This is not simply about the current NPL ratio; it is about the direction of forward-looking indicators such as the SML ratio, overdue loan ratio, and credit cost trends. Banks with improving or stable forward indicators have manageable risk profiles. Banks with deteriorating indicators, particularly in retail and SME portfolios, face earnings headwinds that will persist regardless of NII trends.

The third filter is capital adequacy and dividend visibility. Banks with CET1 ratios above 10% and comfortable buffers above regulatory minimums can sustain loan growth and maintain dividends. Banks with thin buffers face a capital constraint that limits growth and threatens dividend sustainability. In a sector where dividend yield has been a key valuation support, this filter is critical.

Applying these three filters to the first-quarter data produces a clear hierarchy. The banks that pass all three filters are those with strong NII and fee momentum, improving asset quality indicators, and adequate capital buffers. These banks deserve premium valuations and are the likely outperformers. The banks that fail one or more filters face downside risks that the market has not yet fully priced.

EN_B: Exactly. The report cites ILO data: about a quarter of global jobs are exposed to AI, but exposure doesn't mean disappearance. Only 2.3% of jobs have high automation potential, while 13% have augmentation potential.

EN_B: Plenty. Agriculture in the US went from 40% of employment in the early 1900s to about 1% today, but total employment didn't collapse. Labor moved to manufacturing and services. E-commerce didn't kill retail either. The US still has about 15 to 16 million retail workers.

EN_B: That's the key difference. The report acknowledges that the adjustment could be faster and more concentrated, especially for early-career roles. But history gives us confidence that work won't disappear.
EN_A: Which economies are better positioned to handle this?
EN_B: Advanced economies have higher exposure—about 33% of jobs in high-income countries versus 11% in low-income ones. But they also have better adaptation capacity: stronger human capital, better education systems, and more flexible labor markets.

EN_B: Right. It can mean high disruption risk, but also high productivity upside. The report also notes that aging economies, like Europe and North Asia, could benefit from AI offsetting labor shortages.

EN_B: So far, no broad-based wage decline in AI-exposed occupations. But within occupations, the gap is widening.

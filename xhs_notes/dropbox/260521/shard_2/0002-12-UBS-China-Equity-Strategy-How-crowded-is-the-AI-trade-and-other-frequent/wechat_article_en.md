# AI Crowding in China Is Not the Risk Investors Think It Is — The Real Danger Is Global Contagion

The most crowded trade in China equity markets today is also the most fundamentally justified. AI hardware names have been the consensus long among portfolio managers and generalists alike, with onshore mutual fund positioning in electronics and telecom combined near historical highs, TMT turnover accounting for 37% of total A-share activity, and crowding scores for popular AI names rising materially over the past twelve months. Yet the conventional concern — that a crowded trade is inherently fragile and prone to violent unwinding — misses the point. The data shows that positioning has been elevated for most of this year without triggering a reversal, and that modest pullbacks in crowding have already occurred from the early May 2026 peak. The more relevant question is not whether the trade is crowded, but what external forces could force a reassessment that domestic fundamentals alone cannot withstand.

The answer, based on the evidence, points not to domestic overheating but to global spillover. The correlation between US and China AI tech names has picked up materially this year, meaning that a selloff in US AI proxies — triggered by rising US yields, a slowdown in mega-cap earnings, or the IPO of large LLM companies — would likely transmit directly into Chinese AI hardware names regardless of their own earnings trajectory. This is the risk that most investors are underweight in their scenario analysis. The crowding question is a distraction; the contagion question is the one that matters.

## The Crowding Narrative Is Overstated Because Positioning Has Been Stable, Not Accelerating

Investor anxiety about AI crowding typically assumes a linear buildup that reaches a breaking point. The evidence suggests otherwise. Onshore mutual fund allocation to electronics actually declined from 22% in 3Q25 to 20% in 1Q26, with the overweight relative to benchmark reduced by approximately two percentage points. The increase in telecom allocation to an all-time high of 11% offset this decline, meaning aggregate positioning in electronics and telecom combined is near historical highs but broadly unchanged over the last three quarters. This is not a story of accelerating momentum-chasing; it is a story of rotation within the AI theme.

The crowding score for a basket of popular AI names — including Cambricon, Foxconn Industrial Internet, Zhongji Innolight, and others — tells a similar story. Scores increased materially over the past twelve months but have pulled back modestly from the highest level recorded in early May 2026. The average crowding score as of mid-May sits below the peak, suggesting that some marginal participants have already trimmed positions. Meanwhile, TMT turnover as a share of total A-share turnover, while elevated at 37%, remains below the historical peak of 43% reached in 2023.

The implication is clear: the AI trade is not becoming more crowded; it is sustaining a level of crowding that the market has already proven it can absorb. The real risk is not that domestic positioning is too high, but that the trade is increasingly correlated with global macro and US tech sentiment, over which Chinese fundamentals have no control.

## Foreign Flows Reveal a Divergence That Creates Both Risk and Opportunity

Foreign investor behavior adds a critical layer to the crowding analysis. Cumulative foreign net inflows into Korea and Taiwan equities — both global AI proxies — have turned negative this year, suggesting broad profit-taking in these markets. EM funds remain underweight North Asian markets including China, Taiwan, and Korea, with China's active weight at minus 6% as of 1Q26, near the five-year minimum.

This divergence is instructive. Foreign investors are reducing exposure to AI proxies in Korea and Taiwan while domestic Chinese investors maintain high positioning in A-share AI hardware names. If global AI sentiment deteriorates, foreign selling in Korea and Taiwan could accelerate, and given the rising correlation between US and Chinese AI tech names, the spillover into Chinese A-shares could be disproportionate to the change in Chinese fundamentals.

But the divergence also creates an opportunity. Foreign underweight in China means that if the AI narrative strengthens further — driven by accelerating earnings growth, new LLM model launches, or policy support — there is ample room for foreign repositioning to provide additional upside. The risk is asymmetric: downside from global contagion, upside from foreign re-entry. The question is which scenario is more probable, and that depends on factors outside China's control.

## Earnings Growth Is the Anchor, But It Is Not Immune to Imported Inflation

The fundamental case for AI hardware remains strong. Semiconductor earnings grew 64% in 1Q26, and the sector trades at 59x forward P/E, 2.5 standard deviations above its historical average. This valuation multiple is high, but it is supported by accelerating earnings momentum. The key debate among investors is whether imported inflation — driven by the recent surge in energy prices — could squeeze corporate margins and derail the earnings story.

The report's analysis offers a counterintuitive conclusion: higher PPI may not hurt overall listed company earnings. Historically, higher PPI has led to higher revenue and earnings growth, even during supply disruption episodes like the Russia-Ukraine war in 2022. Exporters tend to perform well during periods of imported inflation because they have pricing power to pass on costs. Listed companies generally have better ability to pass on higher costs than unlisted peers, and the sectors most vulnerable to energy price increases — utilities and food and beverage — make up only about 10% of the overall index.

Furthermore, energy shocks typically benefit the renewable sector, which constitutes a large portion of the index, and consumers are partially insulated because only a portion of higher energy costs are passed through to retail prices. The net effect is that imported inflation, while a concern for the broader economy, is unlikely to be the trigger that breaks the AI trade.

This analysis is reassuring, but it leaves an open question: what if PPI rises not from a one-time energy shock but from sustained structural inflation driven by de-globalization and supply chain reshoring? The report's historical comparisons may not fully capture a scenario where cost pressures are persistent rather than cyclical. Investors should stress-test their earnings models under a multi-year inflation scenario rather than assuming the historical relationship holds indefinitely.

## HSTECH Faces a Liquidity Drain That Is Structural, Not Cyclical

The Hang Seng Tech Index has benefited from a series of positive catalysts — new LLM models from Deepseek and Tencent, easing food delivery competition, US-China leadership summit outcomes, and higher oil prices supporting EV demand. Yet the index may take longer to outperform than many expect, and the reason is not fundamental but structural: investor attention has shifted elsewhere.

Discussions with investors suggest that focus remains concentrated on hardware tech, power equipment, and other North Asian names, drawing liquidity away from Hong Kong internet names. This is not a temporary rotation; it reflects a deeper reassessment of where the highest-conviction AI exposure lies. Hardware names offer direct exposure to the AI supply chain with visible earnings growth, while internet names face regulatory uncertainty, competitive pressure, and slower monetization of AI capabilities.

The upcoming lock-up expiry for LLM companies in July adds another layer of near-term pressure. Historically, companies facing lock-up expiry tend to underperform in the lead-up to the date, and if these LLM companies are included in HSTECH, the index itself could face short-term headwinds. The report includes a schedule of lock-up expiries, but does not fully explore the second-order effects: could the lock-up expiries trigger forced selling by index funds or ETF rebalancing? Could they create a buying opportunity for long-term investors willing to absorb supply? These are the questions that separate tactical traders from strategic allocators.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered What Could Trigger a Regime Change in AI Positioning

The report identifies several signposts that investors are watching — rising US interest rates, IPOs of large LLM companies, and a slowdown in earnings growth — but does not assign probabilities or thresholds for each. This is not a weakness; it reflects genuine uncertainty. The more important analytical gap is the absence of a framework for distinguishing between a normal pullback within an ongoing trend and a regime change that ends the AI rally.

Consider the historical analogies. The TMT bubble in 2000 was not popped by a single event but by a gradual realization that earnings would not catch up to valuations. The semi-conductor rally in 2023-2024 was interrupted by export controls and geopolitical shocks, not by crowding. The current AI cycle has both earnings support and valuation expansion, making it difficult to identify the catalyst that would trigger a sustained reversal.

Investors need to build their own decision framework. One approach is to monitor the correlation between US and Chinese AI names: if the correlation rises above current levels and US AI names begin to decline, that is a signal to reduce exposure. Another is to track the pace of earnings revisions: as long as earnings estimates continue to rise, the valuation premium is justified. A third is to watch the lock-up expiry schedule and positioning in HSTECH: if forced selling creates dislocations, it may be a buying opportunity rather than a reason to exit.

The report's preference for A-share ChiNext, tech hardware, and electrical equipment — and its removal of the property underweight while retaining underweight in consumers — provides a clear portfolio tilt. But the most valuable insight is not the sector recommendation; it is the recognition that the biggest risk to the AI trade is not domestic crowding but global contagion. Investors who focus only on positioning data are missing the forest for the trees.

# The Sugar Market Is Correctly Bearish Near Term, But Dangerously Underpricing the Medium-Term Tightening Cycle

The global sugar market is caught in a dangerous analytical trap. Consensus has turned constructive on the medium-term outlook, pointing toward a gradual tightening of balances into 2026/27. Yet the price action tells a different story: near-term futures remain under persistent pressure, hovering around 15 cents per pound, and the market continues to extrapolate this weakness forward. The critical question is not whether the cycle will turn, but when—and whether investors are positioned for the asymmetry that timing creates.

The answer from the most recent Sugar Week in New York is clear: the market is underpricing timing risk in a profound way. Brazil's ability to flood supply in the near term is real, driven by a larger-than-expected crop, rapidly building ethanol inventories, and a policy environment that is pushing mills toward maximum sugar production. But this near-term relief is coming at the expense of future supply. Producer margins are already negative at current prices, cane renewal is being deferred, and the structural tightening that follows from an aging cane profile is not yet reflected in anyone's base case. The market sees the supply cap from Brazil. It is not yet pricing the supply cliff that follows.

This is not a call to fight the tape. Near-term prices can remain pressured, and the data supports that view. But the strategic insight is that the risk-reward has become deeply asymmetric. The downside from here is limited by production costs and policy floors. The upside, driven by El Niño, ethanol policy shifts, and supply response lags, is substantial and underappreciated. The market is focused on the wrong time horizon.

## The Brazilian Supply Surge Is Real, But It Is a Self-Limiting Phenomenon

The immediate pressure on sugar prices comes from one source: Brazil's Center-South region is delivering a larger and faster crop than the market had anticipated. Cane crushing is running ahead of schedule, and with ethanol prices collapsing sharply since March, mills have every incentive to maximize sugar production. The sugar mix has shifted decisively toward sugar, and the market is now pricing in a near-term surplus that is weighing on the front end of the curve.

But this dynamic is not sustainable. The reason is straightforward: current sugar prices are below the marginal cost of production for many Brazilian mills. Production costs in Brazil are estimated above 16 cents per pound, while the nearest NY11 contract trades around 15 cents. When mills cannot earn a positive margin, they stop replanting cane. Instead of renewing fields after harvest, they extend the life of existing cane by allowing it to ratoon for another cycle. This boosts current crush volumes but degrades the age profile of the cane stock. Older cane produces lower yields per hectare. The result is that today's supply abundance is directly creating tomorrow's supply deficit.

The data supports this logic. The report's estimates show that while Brazil's 2025/26 sugar production is expected to remain elevated at roughly 40 million tons, the 2026/27 estimate has been revised down meaningfully, with production now forecast at 42.9 million tons versus a prior estimate of 44.1 million. That is a 3% downward revision, concentrated entirely in the medium term. The market is not yet pricing this. The futures curve shows a gradual upward slope, but it remains well below the levels that would be consistent with the tightening the report projects.

The implication is that the current price weakness is a timing issue, not a change in the cycle call. Investors who extrapolate near-term softness into their medium-term assumptions are making a structural error. The supply response to low prices is already underway, and it will take time to show up in the data. By the time it does, prices will have moved.

## Ethanol Policy Creates a Hidden Floor and a Potential Catalyst

The ethanol market is the second leg of the asymmetry argument, and it is more complex than most observers appreciate. Ethanol prices in Brazil have declined sharply since March, even as global oil prices have risen. This divergence is striking and points to a market that is being compressed by local dynamics, not global ones.

The key driver is inventory. Ethanol inventories in the Center-South have built rapidly, reflecting both strong early supply and weaker-than-expected demand. At the same time, fuel price policy in Brazil has become a binding constraint. Despite higher oil prices, Petrobras has kept gasoline prices unchanged, and the political environment makes any significant increase unlikely in the near term. This has effectively capped gasoline prices and delayed the recovery in ethanol prices, as ethanol competes directly with gasoline at the pump.

But this dynamic is also self-limiting. The report notes that ethanol inventories are expected to normalize over the coming months, and the structural drivers of ethanol demand remain intact. Brazil's ethanol blend mandate is a policy tool that can be adjusted, and the political calculus around fuel prices could shift after the election cycle. If ethanol blend mandates are strengthened—a scenario that is not priced—the demand impact would be significant. Higher ethanol demand would divert cane away from sugar production, tightening the sugar balance at the margin.

The more immediate point is that the ethanol floor is lower than the market thinks, but it is not zero. At current ethanol prices, the incentive for mills to shift from ethanol to sugar is already fully reflected. Any recovery in ethanol prices would quickly reverse that incentive, reducing sugar supply and supporting prices. The risk is asymmetric: ethanol prices are more likely to rise than fall from here, given the inventory normalization and policy backdrop. That means the sugar mix is more likely to shift back toward ethanol than to continue moving toward sugar.

## The Medium-Term Tightening Is Driven by Three Factors the Market Is Ignoring

The report's medium-term price target of 17 cents per pound may seem modest relative to the near-term weakness, but it represents a significant re-rating from where the futures curve currently trades. More importantly, the risks to that forecast are skewed to the upside, not the downside. Three factors support this view.

First, El Niño risk is not being priced. The report explicitly notes that current market expectations embed only a modest tightening and likely exclude upside risks from any outsized El Niño implications. The impact of El Niño on sugar production in India, Thailand, and Europe can be severe. India's production is already being revised down sharply: the report shows India's 2026/27 production estimate at 27.3 million tons, down 12% from a prior estimate of 31 million tons. That is a massive revision, and it is not yet reflected in market pricing. Thailand and Europe face similar risks. If El Niño materializes with any strength, the supply impact could be far larger than the consensus expects.

Second, policy-driven ethanol demand is underappreciated. The report highlights that potential strengthening of ethanol blend mandates could tighten balances beyond what is currently priced. This is not a speculative scenario. Governments around the world are looking for ways to reduce carbon intensity in transportation, and ethanol is one of the most scalable options. Brazil, in particular, has room to increase its blend mandate. If that happens, the impact on sugar supply would be direct and immediate.

Third, the supply response to low prices is already embedded in the data, but the market is not connecting the dots. The report's supply and demand estimates show a global surplus of 2.7 million tons in 2025/26, flipping to a deficit of 3.1 million tons in 2026/27. That is a swing of nearly 6 million tons in one year. The stock-to-use ratio is projected to decline from 26% to 25%, moving toward historical lows. The market is focused on the surplus year. It is ignoring the deficit that follows.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Magnitude and Timing of the El Niño Effect

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves one critical question open: how large and how fast will the El Niño impact be? The report acknowledges that El Niño is likely and that its strength is the swing factor, but it does not provide a specific quantification of the potential supply loss. This is not a flaw in the analysis; it is a reflection of genuine uncertainty. El Niño events vary widely in their intensity and geographic distribution, and the impact on sugar production depends on timing, rainfall patterns, and regional resilience.

What the report does make clear is that the market is not pricing any significant El Niño risk. The consensus view is for a modest tightening. If El Niño proves stronger than expected, the supply deficit in 2026/27 could be far larger than the 3.1 million tons the report projects. That would push prices well above 17 cents per pound. Conversely, if El Niño is weaker than expected, the deficit could be smaller, and prices could remain range-bound. The asymmetry is clear: the upside from an El Niño shock is larger than the downside from a benign outcome.

This is the kind of uncertainty that creates opportunity for investors who are willing to take a contrarian position. The market is pricing a benign scenario. The report's analysis suggests that the benign scenario is the less likely outcome.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position for the Timing Mismatch

The analytical challenge is not whether the sugar cycle will turn, but how to position for the timing mismatch between near-term weakness and medium-term tightening. A useful framework has three dimensions.

First, assess your time horizon. If you are trading the next three to six months, the near-term pressure from Brazil is real, and you should be cautious. The data supports lower prices in the near term, and fighting that trend is costly. But if your horizon is twelve to eighteen months, the risk-reward shifts dramatically. The supply response to low prices, the potential for El Niño, and the policy-driven ethanol demand all point to higher prices. The optimal position is to use near-term weakness to build exposure.

Second, identify the best instrument to express the view. The report identifies a specific listed company as the cleanest way to play the asymmetric risk-reward. The logic is straightforward: a low-cost asset base provides a margin cushion during weak prices, high operating leverage means that any price recovery flows directly to the bottom line, and an underappreciated corn ethanol business adds a second growth engine. The valuation does not yet reflect a cycle turn, which means there is upside if the thesis plays out.

Third, monitor the key catalysts. The most important near-term data point is the pace of ethanol inventory normalization in Brazil. If inventories begin to decline faster than expected, ethanol prices will recover, and the sugar mix will shift. The second catalyst is the El Niño outlook. If weather models begin to show a stronger event, the market will start to price the risk. The third catalyst is policy. Any announcement of higher ethanol blend mandates would be a significant positive for sugar prices.

The report's analysis suggests that the market is currently focused on the wrong signals. The consensus sees the near-term surplus and extrapolates it. The strategic investor sees the medium-term deficit and positions for it. The timing mismatch creates the opportunity.

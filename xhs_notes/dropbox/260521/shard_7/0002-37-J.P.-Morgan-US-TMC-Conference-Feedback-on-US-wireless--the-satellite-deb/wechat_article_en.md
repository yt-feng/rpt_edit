# The US Wireless Market Is No Longer Defensive — It Is Becoming a Structural Growth Story, and Investors Are Underpricing the Shift

For years, the dominant narrative around US telecoms has been one of maturity: saturated penetration, price competition, and diminishing returns to scale. That narrative is now outdated. Based on fresh feedback from the ongoing JPM TMC conference in Boston, a different picture is emerging — one in which the three largest US wireless operators are acting not like defensive utilities, but like growth-oriented platforms. They are expanding into adjacent markets, leveraging artificial intelligence to reshape cost structures and customer economics, and demonstrating a collective confidence that the market has not yet fully priced in.

The conference feedback, drawn from conversations with the CEOs of T-Mobile, Verizon, and AT&T, as well as key executives from Twilio, American Tower, SBA Communications, Uniti, and AST SpaceMobile, points to a structural inflection. The big three US mobile network operators are no longer fighting over the same pool of subscribers. They are expanding total addressable markets — through fixed wireless access, fibre buildouts, and convergence strategies — while simultaneously using AI to reduce churn and improve unit economics. The result is a growth profile that looks increasingly sustainable, and that has direct implications for European telecom investors who have been conditioned to think of US wireless as a low-growth benchmark.

The argument here is not that the sector has become a high-growth tech story. It is that the sector's growth is more durable, more diversified, and more margin-accretive than current valuations suggest. For investors who have been rotating out of European telecoms into US exposure, or who are evaluating Deutsche Telekom's outsized US exposure, the conference provides a wealth of confirmatory signals. But it also raises unresolved questions about the timing of the next competitive cycle, the real threat from satellite direct-to-device services, and whether the AI-driven cost savings are sustainable or one-time.

## The Big Three Are No Longer Defending Share — They Are Expanding the Market, and That Changes the Investment Calculus

The most important takeaway from the conference is not any single data point. It is the collective mindset shift among the CEOs of T-Mobile, Verizon, and AT&T. All three expressed confidence in their growth outlooks, but the source of that confidence has changed. It is no longer about taking share from each other in a zero-sum game. It is about expanding into new total addressable markets — fixed wireless access, fibre, and converged services — and using AI to improve customer retention and cost efficiency.

T-Mobile's CEO reiterated the company's guidance for 15 million FWA customers by 2030, underpinned by fallow spectrum capacity and without even assuming upside from new spectrum wins or technology improvements. That is a statement of abundance, not scarcity. Verizon's CEO, meanwhile, framed his company's cost-cutting program — already on track to cut $5 billion in opex and $4 billion in capex — as an output of a broader transformation, not an end in itself. The goal is to "delight customers," and the cost savings are a byproduct of becoming more efficient and more customer-centric. AT&T's CEO emphasized that fibre growth is coming from competitive wins, not base transition, because there is no remaining DSL base to harvest. That means the company is genuinely taking share from cable, not just migrating its own legacy customers.

For investors, the implication is clear: the US wireless market is becoming a growth market again, not because of population growth or device proliferation, but because the operators are creating new revenue pools and defending them with better economics. This is not a cyclical uptick. It is a structural shift in how the big three think about their business models.

## AI Is Not a Hype Theme Here — It Is Already Reshaping Customer Economics and Cost Structures in Measurable Ways

One of the most striking themes from the conference was the degree to which AI is already embedded in the operations of US telecom operators and their ecosystem partners. This is not a future opportunity. It is a present-day driver of financial metrics.

Verizon's CEO noted that churn fell from 95 basis points in Q4 to 85 basis points by the end of Q1, and that over 50% of the company's net adds improvement this year will come from churn reductions. That is a direct result of AI-driven improvements in customer experience. Verizon is working with Anthropic, becoming "AI-native," and driving improvements to every layer of the customer journey. The cost savings are not just about layoffs or network rationalization. They are about reducing the cost per gross add and retention costs through better targeting, personalization, and automation.

Twilio's CFO provided a window into what is technically possible. The company has embedded AI capabilities that allow it to maintain a continuous customer conversation across all channels, remember context, and turn conversations into actionable intelligence. While Twilio's product stack is more advanced than what Sinch currently offers, the CFO's comments make clear that the technology exists and is already driving acceleration in usage-based revenue. For telecom operators, the implication is that AI can reduce churn, improve upsell, and lower service costs simultaneously.

American Tower's CFO added a complementary layer. AI workloads are increasingly tipping out of large language models and hyperscale facilities into edge facilities like CoreSite, which American Tower owns. The convergence of mobile network densification and AI-driven edge compute demand is creating a new source of upside for tower companies. Future wireless network evolution will require uplink and downlink symmetry, improved latency, more antennas, and compute power paired directly at tower sites. That is a multi-year investment cycle that benefits the entire tower ecosystem.

For investors, the so-what is straightforward: AI is not a vague tailwind. It is already showing up in churn metrics, cost structures, and capital allocation decisions. Companies that are early adopters are gaining a compounding advantage in customer economics.

## Satellite Direct-to-Device Is Real, but the Big Three Have Already Neutralized the Threat Through Cooperation and Standardization

A recurring concern among telecom investors has been the potential disruption from satellite direct-to-device services. The fear is that satellite operators like AST SpaceMobile could bypass the traditional wireless carriers and offer direct connectivity to consumers, undermining the carriers' pricing power and network investment returns.

The conference feedback should put that concern to rest. All three US mobile network operators were emphatic that they have no appetite to offer satellite operators an MVNO arrangement. They see satellite as complementary, not competitive. T-Mobile noted that satellite usage today accounts for just 0.0002% of its total network traffic — a rounding error. The big three have formed a joint venture to implement satellite as a standard feature on premium rate plans, drive a uniform device ecosystem, and standardize spectrum. AT&T expects a seamless direct-to-device product in the second half of 2026.

AST SpaceMobile's president confirmed that 45+ satellites in orbit is the threshold for full commercial service in priority markets, with broad integration expected from 2027 onward. The company's revenue guidance of $150-200 million in 2026, split roughly 50/50 between government and commercial, is real but small relative to the scale of the US wireless market.

The strategic insight here is that the incumbents have successfully co-opted the satellite threat. By standardizing spectrum and device ecosystems through a joint venture, they have ensured that satellite becomes a feature of their own premium plans, not a competing service. The economics are clear: satellite fills dead zones and adds a premium feature, but it does not challenge the carriers' urban capacity advantage or their control over the customer relationship. For investors, this means the satellite risk premium should be reduced, not eliminated, but significantly narrowed.

## The Report Leaves an Open Question: Will Verizon's Q2 Tariff Refresh Trigger a New Competitive Cycle, or Is the Market Maturing Past Price Wars?

Despite the overwhelmingly constructive tone of the conference, one unresolved question hangs over the near-term outlook. Verizon's CEO confirmed a targeted Q2 "valuation proposition refresh" that has been in development for some time. He provided no clear colour on what it will entail, but he suggested it will involve changing "lots of small things" and being "fiscally responsible." He also expressed a desire to move away from clumsy "free handset" offers toward tools that create tangible improvements in customer experience.

This is the kind of language that could mean anything from a modest price increase to a more aggressive competitive move. The market is understandably nervous. Verizon's CEO insisted that his FY net adds guide is conservative, but that could simply mean he expects to hold share, not gain it aggressively. T-Mobile's CEO, meanwhile, believes there are 20 million-plus "network seekers" who are addressable, suggesting that T-Mobile expects to continue taking share regardless of what Verizon does.

The open question is whether the industry has genuinely moved past the era of price-driven competition, or whether the current period of stability is a lull before the next skirmish. The conference feedback suggests the former is more likely, but the evidence is not yet conclusive. Investors should watch the Q2 refresh closely, not for the specific pricing changes, but for the reaction of T-Mobile and AT&T. If they hold their pricing discipline, it will confirm that the industry has matured. If they respond with aggressive offers, the near-term outlook will become more volatile.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Whether the US Wireless Thesis Applies to Your Portfolio

For investors trying to translate the conference feedback into actionable decisions, the following framework may help. It is not a checklist. It is a set of diagnostic questions that determine whether the US wireless growth story is relevant to your portfolio and, if so, how to position.

First, do you believe that AI-driven churn reduction is sustainable, or is it a one-time improvement? The conference feedback suggests that AI is improving customer experience at multiple layers, from call centre interactions to network optimization. But churn reductions cannot compound indefinitely. At some point, the base rate of churn reaches a floor. The question is whether that floor is 80 basis points or 60. If it is the former, the current improvement cycle is largely priced in. If it is the latter, there is meaningful upside to subscriber lifetime value across the sector.

Second, is the fibre and FWA expansion creating genuine new revenue pools, or is it simply shifting revenue from one part of the industry to another? T-Mobile's FWA guidance is clear, and AT&T's fibre wins are coming from cable, not from other telcos. But the total addressable market for fixed broadband is not growing rapidly. The question is whether the big three can sustain their share gains without triggering a price war with cable operators. The conference feedback suggests they are confident, but cable is not standing still.

Third, how should European investors think about Deutsche Telekom in this context? The report argues that Deutsche Telekom's shares screened oversold before the conference and that the US feedback should support a rebound. But the report also notes that European consolidation plays offer the most compelling risk-reward. For investors with a European mandate, the question is whether to increase exposure to Deutsche Telekom as a proxy for US wireless growth, or to focus on pure-play European consolidation stories. The answer depends on conviction in the US thesis and tolerance for the regulatory and competitive risks that remain unresolved.

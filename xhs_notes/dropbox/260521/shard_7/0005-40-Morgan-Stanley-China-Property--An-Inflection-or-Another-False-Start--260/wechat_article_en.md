# The China Property Rebound Is a False Start, Not an Inflection

The debate over whether China's property market has reached a genuine inflection point has resurfaced with urgency. Secondary home sales in 25 major cities have rebounded unexpectedly since March 2026, sparking hopes that the multi-year downturn may finally be bottoming out. This narrative is seductive but dangerously premature. A careful examination of the data reveals that the recent sales pickup reflects pent-up demand released after a weak fourth quarter of 2025, not an organic recovery in household fundamentals. The divergence between secondary and primary markets, the continued fragility of resident sentiment, and the structural headwinds facing developer profitability all point to a market that is experiencing a temporary reprieve, not a durable turnaround. Investors who treat this as an inflection risk buying into a valuation recovery that has already priced in continued sales growth, leaving them exposed when the next leg of weakness arrives.

Why now matters more than the surface data suggests. The March-April rebound occurred without notable policy support, which the market has interpreted as evidence of an organic recovery taking hold. But this interpretation conflates timing with causality. The weak fourth quarter of 2025 created a backlog of demand that was always likely to release after the Chinese New Year holiday. When adjusted for the CNY calendar, the cumulative sales trajectory looks far less impressive. The real question is not whether sales can spike for a few weeks, but whether the underlying drivers of housing demand--household income, leverage capacity, and confidence--are improving. On all three fronts, the evidence points in the opposite direction.

The stakes are high. China's property sector has historically been the most powerful transmission mechanism for credit creation, local government revenue, and household wealth. A genuine recovery would have profound implications for the broader economy and for equity markets. A false start, by contrast, would trap capital in a sector that is structurally shrinking, with developers facing permanently lower profit margins and investors chasing a beta that no longer exists. The distinction between an inflection and a false start is not academic; it is the single most important judgment call for anyone allocating capital to China today.

This article draws on a detailed research report from a global investment bank to dissect what the data actually says, why the market narrative is misreading it, and what framework investors should use to navigate the uncertainty. The analysis is structured around four core questions: What caused the secondary sales rebound? Why is it unlikely to sustain? What does the divergence between new and secondary homes tell us about market structure? And finally, what remains unknown?

## The Secondary Sales Rebound Is Driven by Pent-Up Demand, Not a Structural Recovery

The most important chart in the report is the CNY-adjusted weekly secondary home sales data for 25 major cities. When viewed on a calendar-adjusted basis, the pattern is clear: the fourth quarter of 2025 was exceptionally weak, with cumulative sales running well below the previous three years. The post-CNY catch-up in early 2026 brought sales back to levels that are consistent with the 2023-2024 range, but not above it. This is not a breakout; it is a normalization after an abnormal trough.

The market's error is to interpret the sequential improvement as a trend change. Sequential data is inherently noisy in a sector as seasonal as real estate. The correct analytical approach is to compare cumulative sales trajectories across years, not week-over-week changes. When you do that, the recent rebound looks like a release of deferred transactions from buyers who had delayed decisions during the uncertainty of late 2025. Once that backlog is cleared, the underlying pace of demand will reassert itself.

The "so what" here is critical. If the rebound is pent-up demand, it is inherently finite. It does not signal a change in buyer behavior or confidence. It does not imply that new buyers are entering the market. It simply means that existing demand that was temporarily suppressed has now been executed. The sustainability of sales depends entirely on whether new demand is being generated. The evidence on that front is negative.

## Macro and Housing Indicators Remain Weak, Undermining the Case for Sustained Demand

The report identifies three structural drags on housing demand that have not improved despite the sales rebound. First, household income growth remains under pressure. The employment PMI for non-manufacturing sectors continues to signal contraction, and this is where the majority of urban employment is concentrated. Manufacturing PMI is also weak. Without income growth, households cannot increase their housing expenditure.

Second, household leverage remains elevated at approximately 63% of GDP, despite home sales having cumulatively shrunk by roughly 40% from peak levels. This is a striking statistic. Normally, a sharp decline in sales would be accompanied by deleveraging as households pay down debt. That is not happening. It suggests that households are using their available financial capacity to service existing debt rather than take on new mortgage obligations. The capacity for additional leverage is limited.

Third, home ownership ratios are already high. China's urban home ownership rate reached approximately 78% in 2020, among the highest in the world. This means that the pool of potential first-time buyers is structurally smaller than in previous cycles. The demand that drove the property boom of the past two decades was driven by urbanization and rising ownership. Those tailwinds have largely played out.

The market narrative tends to focus on price adjustments as a solution: lower prices will attract buyers back into the market. But this logic ignores the balance sheet constraint. If households are already highly leveraged and income growth is weak, lower prices may not stimulate demand because buyers cannot qualify for mortgages or are unwilling to take on additional risk. The data on housing affordability shows that while Tier 1 city price-to-income ratios have dropped back to 2014-2015 levels, they remain much higher than comparable global metropolitan areas. Affordability is improving from extreme levels, but it is still stretched.

## The Divergence Between New and Secondary Homes Reveals a Market in Structural Transition

One of the most telling findings in the report is the growing divergence between the primary and secondary markets. Secondary home sales have rebounded, but primary sales have not followed. This is not a typical pattern. In a healthy recovery, strength in the secondary market eventually pulls up primary sales as home sellers use their proceeds to upgrade. That is not happening here.

The reason is pricing. Secondary home sellers are eager to dispose of properties. The report shows that over 60% of homeowners with sales plans are willing to accept losses, including 13% who would accept losses of more than 20%. This creates a supply of distressed inventory that competes directly with new homes. Developers cannot match these discounts because their cost structures are higher. The result is that secondary sales are cannibalizing primary sales, not complementing them.

This has profound implications for developer profitability. If primary sales volumes remain weak, developers cannot generate the revenue needed to cover their fixed costs and debt service. The market is pricing developers based on a beta recovery--a belief that all boats will rise with the tide. But the tide is not rising for everyone. The structural shift toward secondary market dominance means that developers with high-quality land banks and strong execution capabilities may survive, but the sector as a whole faces a permanently lower profit pool.

The data on rental rates reinforces this cautionary picture. Rental rates have cumulatively dropped approximately 15% since July 2021 and began to re-weaken again in April 2026. Falling rents are a leading indicator of housing demand weakness because they reflect the balance between supply and demand for housing services. When rents fall, it signals that the population of potential tenants is not growing fast enough to absorb the available housing stock. This is inconsistent with a narrative of improving housing demand.

## The Report Leaves Critical Questions Unanswered About the Path to Equilibrium

No research report can answer every question, and the most valuable ones are those that identify what remains unknown. This report is honest about its limitations, but there are several questions that deserve deeper exploration.

First, what is the policy pathway? The report notes that the recent rebound occurred without notable policy support. But this raises the question of whether policy intervention could change the trajectory. If the government were to implement large-scale fiscal transfers to households, or directly purchase distressed inventory from developers, could that create a floor under prices? The report does not model this scenario. It implicitly assumes that policy will remain incremental. That assumption may be correct, but it is not tested.

Second, what is the regional dispersion? The report focuses on 25 major cities, but China's property market is highly heterogeneous. Tier 3 and Tier 4 cities have been in a prolonged downturn for years. The recovery in high-tier cities may not be representative of the national picture. The report acknowledges this implicitly by focusing on high-tier cities, but it does not quantify how much of the national market these cities represent or what the implications are for the broader economy.

Third, what is the timeline for developer earnings recovery? The report argues that reduced primary market size, constrained leverage, homogeneous landbanking strategies, and continued drag from legacy inventory would limit medium-term earnings rebound potential. This is a structural argument, not a cyclical one. But it does not provide a specific timeline. Is this a three-year problem or a ten-year problem? The distinction matters for valuation.

These open questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are invitations for further analysis. Investors who want to form a complete view need to engage with these uncertainties rather than assume they will resolve favorably.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguishing Beta from Alpha in a Shrinking Market

The report's most valuable contribution may be its implicit framework for stock selection. The market is pricing developers based on pure beta--the belief that the sector will recover broadly. The report's authors argue for a different approach: Beta plus Alpha. In other words, even if the sector does not recover broadly, individual companies with strong execution, high-quality land banks, and healthy balance sheets can outperform.

This framework is directly applicable to investment decisions. The first question an investor should ask is not whether the property market will recover, but whether the specific developer they are considering has a viable business model in a market that is structurally smaller and less profitable. The report identifies two types of developers: those that benefit from pure beta (sector-wide recovery) and those that have idiosyncratic advantages (alpha). The market is treating all developers as beta plays. The report suggests this is a mistake.

The second question is about valuation. The report notes that 2028E P/E and ex-IP EV/pre-sales have already exceeded bull-market levels. This means that the market is pricing in a recovery that has not yet materialized. If the recovery does not happen, or if it is weaker than expected, these valuations will correct. The margin of safety is thin.

The third question is about the time horizon. The report's analysis suggests that the medium-term outlook for developer earnings is constrained. This implies that investors need to be patient and selective. The easy money from a sector-wide re-rating has already been made. The next phase requires stock-specific analysis and a willingness to hold through continued volatility.

# The Cyber Defense Window Is Closing: AI Has Collapsed the Gap Between Threat Discovery and Exploitation

The most significant strategic shift in enterprise cybersecurity is not that threats are increasing. It is that the economics of offense have inverted. A single autonomous AI system can now discover vulnerabilities that have evaded human experts for decades, develop working exploits overnight, and execute attacks at a speed and scale that no human team can match. The cost of this capability has collapsed by a factor of 500 to 1,000x, making sophisticated offensive operations accessible to any organization with model access and compute. Defenders are no longer racing against skilled human adversaries. They are racing against machines that never sleep, never fatigue, and improve with each iteration.

This is not a future scenario. In September 2025, a Chinese state-sponsored group used a commercially available AI model to autonomously conduct cyber espionage against approximately 30 entities, including government agencies and financial institutions. The AI performed 80 to 90 percent of tactical operations independently, at rates physically impossible for human operators. The model used was less capable than models now publicly available as open-source. The window for proactive defense is measured in months, not years.

The investment implications are equally profound. A global investment bank report has identified a structural overweight opportunity in cybersecurity platforms, arguing that the accelerating threat landscape will drive security budgets to exceed 10 percent of total IT spend, with spending directed at AI systems running into the billions. But not all security vendors are positioned equally. The report's overweight list favors platform vendors with deep telemetry, proprietary data, and established partnerships with foundation model companies. The key insight is that the market is not rewarding all security companies equally. It is rewarding those that can operationalize AI defense at scale.

The argument of this article is straightforward: the cybersecurity investment thesis has shifted from incremental growth to structural acceleration. The collapse of the time-to-exploit metric, the emergence of autonomous offensive AI, and the proliferation of open-source models with near-frontier capability have created a permanent step-change in demand for defensive platforms. The question for investors is not whether security spending will increase, but which vendors will capture the disproportionate share.

## The Collapse of Mean Time to Exploit Has Made Human-Only Defense Obsolete

The most underappreciated metric in cybersecurity is not the number of vulnerabilities discovered. It is the time between discovery and exploitation. In 2018, the mean time-to-exploit was 63 days, meaning defenders had roughly two months to patch before adversaries could weaponize a vulnerability. By 2023, that window had collapsed to five days. In 2025, it turned negative. The mean time-to-exploit is now negative seven days, meaning adversaries are exploiting vulnerabilities before patches exist. Twenty-nine percent of known exploited vulnerabilities were weaponized on or before the day of public disclosure.

This shift is not incremental. It represents a structural change in the nature of the threat. When exploit windows were measured in weeks, human security teams could prioritize, patch, and manage risk. When windows collapse to days or hours, the manual remediation model breaks entirely. The report notes that breakout time, the period from initial access to lateral movement, is now measured in minutes, with the fastest recorded instance at 27 seconds. Ransomware-to-data-exfiltration has dropped from over eight hours in 2022 to 22 seconds in 2025.

The implication is clear. Security operations centers staffed by human analysts cannot respond at machine speed. The only viable defense is autonomous detection and response systems that operate at the same cadence as the attack. This is not a feature enhancement for existing security tools. It is a fundamental requirement that favors platform vendors with integrated telemetry across endpoint, network, identity, and data estates. Vendors that rely on manual triage or siloed point solutions will struggle to keep pace.

## Autonomous Offensive AI Has Crossed a Critical Threshold That Changes the Nature of the Game

The report's most striking data point concerns Anthropic's Mythos Preview, a restricted-access AI model that can autonomously discover zero-day vulnerabilities, develop working exploits, and complete full network penetration tests without human intervention. In controlled testing, Mythos discovered critical vulnerabilities that had survived decades of automated scanning. One vulnerability had remained undetected for 27 years. Another for 17 years. A third for 16 years. These are not edge cases. They are proof that existing security tools are inadequate against AI-capable adversaries.

The economics are equally transformative. The report estimates that scanning an entire operating system for security flaws costs less than $50 with AI, compared to weeks of expert team time under legacy approaches. Developing a complete attack that gains full control of a computer costs under $1,000 and half a day, compared to $500,000 to $2 million on the exploit black market. Running 1,000 automated security scans costs under $20,000, compared to dedicated research teams. The cost reduction is not incremental. It is two to three orders of magnitude.

The report also reveals that Anthropic deliberately reduced the cyber capability of its public release model, Claude Opus 4.7, decoupling coding performance from offensive security capability. Open-source models do not have these constraints. Kimi K2.6, an open-source Chinese model, already matches the coding performance level at which real-world exploit capability was demonstrated. Anthropic's own offensive cyber lead estimates that competing systems will reach Mythos-comparable capability within months. The current access restrictions on Mythos are temporary. The underlying capability is proliferating.

For investors, the implication is that the defensive security market is about to experience a demand shock. Enterprises that previously considered their security posture adequate will be forced to upgrade as the cost of attack plummets and the sophistication of attacks rises. The vendors best positioned to capture this spending are those that have already begun deploying autonomous defensive agents in production, including the platform leaders identified in the report's overweight list.

## Platform Vendors Have an Evolutionary Advantage That Point Solutions Cannot Match

The report's overweight list is not random. It favors platform vendors with established partnerships with foundation model companies, deep proprietary telemetry, and decades of domain expertise. CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Check Point, Zscaler, and others are identified as having the infrastructure to operationalize AI defense at scale. The report notes that these vendors have been actively involved with foundation model companies, and some may be limited by agreements that prevent them from disclosing the full extent of their partnerships.

The logic is straightforward. AI-enabled threats require AI-enabled defense. But not all AI is equal. A security vendor that trains a model on its own proprietary data, drawn from millions of endpoints, networks, and identities, has a structural advantage over a vendor that relies on generic AI models or third-party data. The report highlights Anthropic's Glasswing coalition, which grants 52 vetted organizations access to Mythos for defensive purposes. The founding partners include CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, AWS, Apple, Broadcom, Cisco, Google, JPM Chase, Microsoft, and NVIDIA. These are not random selections. They are the organizations best positioned to translate frontier AI capability into operational defense.

The report also notes that partnerships with foundation model companies offer a lens into leadership. Anthropic's Glasswing, OpenAI's Trusted Access for Cyber, and CrowdStrike's Quiltworks illustrate the critical role security platforms will play in addressing the incremental threats that foundation models introduce. The vendors leading these partnerships have an evolutionary competitive advantage. They have access to capabilities that are not available to the broader market, and they are building the integration layer that will define the next generation of enterprise security.

For investors, the key question is not which vendor has the best marketing. It is which vendor has the deepest data, the strongest partnerships, and the most integrated platform. The report's overweight list provides a starting point, but the real insight is that the market is likely to concentrate around a small number of platform winners. Point solution vendors that cannot integrate into the broader security stack will face increasing headwinds.

## The Report Leaves Critical Questions Unanswered About Remediation Capacity and Budget Timing

The report is comprehensive, but it leaves several meaningful open questions that investors should consider. The most important is remediation capacity. The report notes that less than one percent of the vulnerabilities discovered by Mythos have been repaired to date. The disclosure pipeline will pressure enterprise patching capacity for months, if not years. But the report does not fully address how enterprises will scale their remediation capabilities to match the speed of AI-enabled discovery. If patching remains a bottleneck, the value of vulnerability discovery may be limited until the remediation ecosystem catches up.

A second open question concerns budget timing. The report argues that security budgets can account for over 10 percent of total IT spend and that spending directed at AI systems could run into the billions. But it does not provide a clear timeline for when this spending will materialize. Enterprises are still in the early stages of AI adoption, and budget cycles are slow. The demand shock may be real, but it may take two to three years to fully manifest in vendor revenue. Investors should be prepared for a gradual acceleration rather than an immediate spike.

A third question concerns the competitive dynamics among platform vendors. The report identifies CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks as obvious beneficiaries, but it also notes that others within coverage have been actively involved with foundation model companies, and some may be limited by agreements they have. The competitive landscape is still forming, and it is not yet clear which vendors will emerge as the dominant platforms. The report's overweight list should be viewed as a starting point for analysis, not a final verdict.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Filters for Evaluating Cybersecurity Vendors

The report provides extensive data on the threat landscape, but investors need a framework for translating that data into investment decisions. Based on the report's logic, I propose three filters for evaluating cybersecurity vendors.

First, evaluate data moat. The most valuable asset in the AI era is proprietary telemetry. Vendors with access to millions of endpoints, networks, and identities have a structural advantage in training defensive AI models. They see more attacks, they learn faster, and they can detect patterns that smaller vendors cannot. The report's overweight list favors vendors with deep data moats, and investors should apply this filter rigorously.

Second, evaluate partnership depth. The report makes clear that partnerships with foundation model companies are a leading indicator of competitive advantage. Vendors that are part of the Glasswing coalition or similar initiatives have access to capabilities that are not available to the broader market. They are also building the integration layer that will define the next generation of security. Investors should look for evidence of deep, strategic partnerships rather than superficial marketing relationships.

Third, evaluate platform integration. The threat landscape is evolving too fast for point solutions. Enterprises need integrated platforms that can correlate data across endpoint, network, identity, and cloud. Vendors that offer siloed products will struggle to keep pace with AI-enabled attacks. The report's overweight list favors platform vendors, and investors should be skeptical of vendors that cannot demonstrate cross-product integration.

These three filters are not exhaustive, but they provide a structured approach to evaluating the opportunities identified in the report. The cybersecurity market is entering a period of structural acceleration, and the winners will be those vendors that can combine data, partnerships, and platform integration to deliver AI-enabled defense at scale.

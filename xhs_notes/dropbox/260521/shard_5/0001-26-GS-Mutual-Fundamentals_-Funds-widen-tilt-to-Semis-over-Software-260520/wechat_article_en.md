# Active Managers Are Betting Against Software and Into Semiconductors With a Conviction Not Seen in Over a Decade

The first quarter of 2026 has revealed a decisive and historically significant shift in the positioning of America's largest active mutual fund managers. After years of cautious navigation, these professional allocators have made a bet that is both clear and aggressive: they are rotating out of Software and into Semiconductors at a pace and magnitude that has created the widest tilt between these two technology sub-sectors since at least 2012. This is not a marginal adjustment. It represents a fundamental re-evaluation of where value creation is occurring in the AI-driven economy.

This matters now because the stakes have never been higher for active management. Only 29% of large-cap mutual funds are outperforming their benchmarks year-to-date, well below the historical average of 37%. As inflows to US equity funds accelerate to $166 billion year-to-date, and as passive vehicles continue to capture the bulk of new capital, active managers are under immense pressure to prove their stock-picking prowess. The Semis-versus-Software trade is their most visible attempt to do so. Understanding the logic, the risks, and the second-order implications of this rotation is essential for any serious investor trying to anticipate where institutional capital will flow next.

The data, drawn from the quarter-end positioning of 509 large-cap active mutual funds with a combined $3.9 trillion in equity assets, paints a picture of conviction that borders on unanimity. Excluding the mega-caps, the average large-cap mutual fund lifted its Semiconductors overweight by 25 basis points to plus 49 basis points. Simultaneously, funds deepened their underweight in Software, excluding Microsoft, by 12 basis points to minus 36 basis points. This is not a subtle drift. It is a structural reallocation.

## The Rotation from Software to Semiconductors Reflects a Conviction That AI Infrastructure Spending Will Outpace Application Monetization

The underlying logic of this trade is straightforward but its implications are profound. Mutual funds are essentially betting that the hardware layer of the AI revolution will capture more value, sooner, than the software application layer. This view has been reinforced by upgrades to hyperscaler capital expenditure outlooks, which point to sustained investment in data centers, networking equipment, and memory chips. At the same time, concerns about AI-driven disruption to traditional software business models have weighed on the sector. The result is a performance divergence that has intensified year-to-date, and active managers have leaned into it.

Six of the 20 most popular mutual fund stock increases in the first quarter were constituents of the AI Data Centers basket. These include names like Arista Networks, Corning, SanDisk, and Coherent. The message is clear: managers want direct exposure to the physical infrastructure enabling AI, not the software platforms that may or may not successfully monetize it. This is a bet on tangible assets and recurring capital cycles over intangible intellectual property with uncertain adoption curves.

Yet the trade is not without tension. The strong performance of some of these semiconductor and infrastructure names has created a paradoxical problem for active managers. As stock prices rise, benchmark weights increase even faster. Several stocks, including SanDisk and Corning, have become new constituents of the most underweight basket, meaning that despite buying them, funds still hold less than their benchmark weight. This is a classic active management challenge: conviction in a name does not guarantee the ability to keep pace with a rising benchmark weight driven by price momentum.

## The Magnificent Seven Underweight Has Stabilized, But Individual Stock Positions Reveal a Fragmented View of Megacap Risk

The aggregate underweight to the Magnificent Seven has widened modestly to 723 basis points from 710 basis points in the prior quarter. On the surface, this suggests continuity. The diversification restrictions that constrain mutual funds from holding oversized positions in any single name continue to play a significant role in these tilts. But beneath the aggregate number lies a more interesting story of dispersion and conviction.

On a net shares basis, mutual funds reduced ownership in each of the Magnificent Seven, with the largest reduction in Alphabet. This is a meaningful signal. It suggests that managers are not simply maintaining underweights due to regulatory constraints; they are actively choosing to reduce exposure. The dispersion in performance across the group amplifies the importance of this decision. Apple, Nvidia, Alphabet, and Amazon have outperformed year-to-date, while Microsoft, Tesla, and Meta have lagged. The average fund is underweight all seven, but the performance impact of that underweight varies dramatically depending on which names are most heavily avoided.

This raises an uncomfortable question for active managers: if the Mag Seven continue to drive index returns, can any underweight be sustained without destroying relative performance? The data suggests that the answer, so far, has been no. The 29% outperformance rate is evidence that the Mag Seven underweight is a significant drag. Yet managers are doubling down, not capitulating. This is either a contrarian bet of exceptional conviction or a structural constraint that will continue to erode active returns.

## Sector Tilts Show Unusual Stability, Suggesting Managers Are Making Fewer Cross-Sector Bets and Focusing on Intra-Sector Differentiation

The magnitude of changes across mutual fund sector tilts in the first quarter ranked in just the 4th percentile since 2012. This is a remarkable statistic. It means that net sector-level positioning changed less than it has in 96% of quarters over the past 14 years. At a time when capital is flowing rapidly into equities and the AI narrative is reshaping entire industries, active managers are making surprisingly few large-scale sector rotation bets.

This stability is itself a strategic signal. It suggests that managers are not trying to outguess the market on broad sector direction. Instead, they are focusing on stock selection within sectors. The Semis versus Software trade is the exception that proves the rule. It is a sub-sector rotation within Technology, not a broad sector shift. Outside of Technology, the most overweight sectors are Industrials at plus 197 basis points and Financials at plus 190 basis points. These tilts are near their 10-year highs, indicating a long-standing conviction that has not been disrupted by recent market moves.

The most underweight sector remains Information Technology at minus 478 basis points. This seems contradictory given the enthusiasm for Semiconductors, but it is not. The Technology sector includes Software, IT Services, and Hardware, all of which are underweight. The Semis overweight is not large enough to offset the underweights in other Technology sub-sectors. This reinforces the point that the Semis trade is a specific, concentrated bet, not a broad endorsement of Technology as a whole.

## The Data Leaves Open the Question of What Happens When the AI Infrastructure Trade Reaches Peak Saturation

For all its clarity, the report does not fully answer a critical question: how much further can this rotation go before it becomes crowded? The mutual fund tilt in Semiconductors versus Software is already at its widest since 2012. The overweight in Semis, while significant, remains modestly below the peaks reached in the first quarter of 2023 and the second quarter of 2024. This suggests there is still room to run, but the margin for error is shrinking.

The risk is that the trade becomes a consensus positioning that leaves managers exposed to a reversal. If hyperscaler capital expenditure guidance disappoints, or if AI software monetization begins to accelerate, the Semis overweight could become a source of significant underperformance. The report highlights the most overweight Software stocks and most underweight Semis stocks as potential opportunities for buying and selling if the trade continues. But it does not address the scenario where the trade reverses. That is the missing analysis.

Furthermore, the report notes that mutual fund cash balances have risen year-to-date to 1.4% of assets, remaining low relative to history but moving in the direction of caution. This cash buildup, combined with the acceleration of inflows to US equity funds, creates a backdrop where managers have dry powder to deploy. But into what? If the Semis trade is already well-owned, the next marginal buyer may need to find a new theme. The report does not identify what that theme might be.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating Active Manager Positioning

For readers trying to make sense of this data and apply it to their own portfolios, a structured decision framework is essential. The first question to ask is whether you believe the AI infrastructure buildout has further to run. If the answer is yes, then following the mutual fund rotation into Semiconductors and data center infrastructure makes sense, but with a critical caveat: you must be willing to own these positions through the volatility that comes with consensus crowding.

The second question is whether you believe the Software underweight has become excessive. The report shows that mutual fund exposure to Software, excluding Microsoft, is at its lowest since at least 2012. If you believe that AI will eventually create significant software application value, then the current underweight represents a contrarian opportunity. The report lists the most overweight Software stocks among mutual funds, including Cadence Design Systems, Intuit, and ServiceNow. These are the names that active managers still like within a sector they broadly dislike. That concentration of ownership is itself a risk.

The third question is about the Mag Seven. The underweight is structural and likely to persist due to diversification rules. But the dispersion of performance within the group means that not all underweights are created equal. If you must be underweight, the data suggests that the largest reductions have been in Alphabet, which has outperformed. This is a painful combination. A more nuanced approach would be to differentiate between Mag Seven names based on their exposure to the AI infrastructure theme versus their exposure to consumer or advertising cyclicality.

Finally, the stability of sector tilts suggests that broad sector rotation is not currently a source of alpha. The action is in sub-sector and stock selection. Any strategy that relies on predicting the next sector rotation is swimming against the tide of manager behavior. The smart money is focused on intra-sector differentiation, and your framework should reflect that.

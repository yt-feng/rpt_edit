# China’s Housing Market Is Stabilising, But the Credit Engine Has Not Yet Turned Over

The recent 4.5% decline in Chinese developer equities on 18 May was not a signal of renewed fundamental distress. It was a healthy profit-taking pause in a market that had rallied sharply on improving data. The Hang Seng Index fell only 1.1% that day, confirming the move was sector-specific and likely driven by macro headwinds—lower expectations for US and China rate cuts and rising US bond yields—rather than a deterioration in property fundamentals. For investors willing to look past short-term sentiment noise, the underlying picture is constructive: tier-1 primary home sales area rose 7% year-on-year month-to-date in May, secondary transactions in 10 cities increased 22% year-on-year, and home prices in tier-1 cities are posting month-on-month gains for both primary and secondary markets. The market is moving toward a natural equilibrium without additional policy stimulus.

But this is where the strategic question becomes urgent. The valuation recovery has been sharp, and further re-rating now requires firmer evidence of a sustained recovery. The sector is no longer pricing in distress; it is pricing in hope. The next leg of the bull case depends not on further policy easing, but on transmission from housing market stabilisation to credit expansion. That transmission has not yet occurred. In April, household medium- to long-term lending—a proxy for mortgage activity—fell by RMB 341 billion, a record low. This sharp net contraction suggests rising early mortgage prepayments and a weaker willingness to use leverage for home purchases. The housing market may be healing, but the household balance sheet is still in repair mode.

This creates a two-phase investment thesis. Phase one, which is largely behind us, was driven by valuation mean-reversion and the removal of tail risks. Phase two, which lies ahead, requires a visible improvement in household credit behaviour and a broader wealth effect from stabilising home prices. The transition between these phases is where the market now sits. And it is precisely this transition that makes the current pull-back a strategic opportunity for disciplined investors to re-engage with the sector—but only with a clear framework for distinguishing between developers that are positioned for the next cycle and those that are merely surviving.

## The Onshore Funding Environment Is Unambiguously Supportive, but Offshore Refinancing Risks Are Concentrated and Growing

The most underappreciated structural support for Chinese developers right now is the onshore funding environment. Commercial mortgage rates at 3.1% and housing provident fund rates at 2.6% are at historical lows. The 10-year government bond yield continues to trend downward, and with economists expecting the policy rate to remain stable through 2026, mortgage rates should stay low for the foreseeable future. This is a powerful tailwind for demand, particularly in tier-1 cities where price stabilisation is already visible. The combination of low rates and improving price momentum should support transaction volumes through the June peak pre-sale season, which is typically the strongest period for new home sales.

Yet this supportive onshore picture contrasts sharply with the offshore refinancing landscape. Rising US bond yields are pressuring equity valuations through a higher risk-free rate, but the direct impact on property fundamentals is limited. The real risk is concentrated in specific companies with USD-denominated maturities in 2027. Longfor has a USD 250 million bond maturing in April 2027 with a coupon of 3.375%. Vanke faces a much larger USD 1 billion maturity in November 2027 at a coupon of 3.975%. In a higher-yield environment, refinancing these bonds will likely push borrowing costs significantly higher. This is not a systemic risk—these are isolated, manageable maturities—but it is a stock-specific risk that investors must assess carefully.

The implication is clear: the low-rate onshore environment is a tailwind for the sector as a whole, but the offshore refinancing burden creates a divergence between developers with strong onshore funding access and those that are more exposed to international capital markets. Investors should favour developers with diversified funding sources, strong state-owned enterprise backing, and limited near-term offshore maturity walls.

## The Credit Transmission Mechanism Has Broken Down, and the Recovery Depends on a Wealth Effect That Has Not Yet Materialised

The most concerning data point in the report is the record RMB 341 billion net contraction in household medium- to long-term lending in April. This is not a seasonal fluctuation. It represents a fundamental breakdown in the transmission mechanism from housing market stabilisation to credit expansion. Households are not only refraining from taking on new mortgage debt; they are actively prepaying existing mortgages at an accelerating pace. This behaviour is consistent with a household sector that remains risk-averse, focused on deleveraging, and sceptical about the sustainability of any housing recovery.

The report notes that the reset in household housing wealth is likely to complete in 2026. This is a critical timeline for investors to understand. The wealth effect from rising home prices—where households feel wealthier and therefore more willing to spend and borrow—is the engine that drives a self-reinforcing recovery. But that engine cannot turn over until households are convinced that home prices have bottomed and will continue to appreciate. The data suggests we are not there yet. While tier-1 primary and secondary prices are showing month-on-month gains, year-on-year comparisons are still negative. Tier-1 secondary prices were down 6.8% year-on-year in April. The wealth effect requires sustained price appreciation, not just stabilisation.

This creates a paradox. The market is pricing in a recovery based on volume and price stabilisation. But the household sector is behaving as if it is still in crisis mode. Until the wealth effect materialises and credit expansion resumes, the recovery will remain fragile and dependent on continued low rates and policy support. The transmission from stabilisation to expansion will take time, and investors should not expect a linear, smooth recovery. The path will be choppy, with periodic pull-backs like the one on 18 May serving as reminders that the market is pricing hope ahead of fundamentals.

## The June Peak Season and Tier-1 Price Leadership Are the Key Catalysts for the Next Leg of Re-Rating

The report points to the June peak pre-sale season as a potential catalyst for supporting valuations on forward earnings. This is the most important near-term event for the sector. Historically, June is the strongest month for new home sales in China, and a strong performance this year would provide tangible evidence that the recovery is broadening beyond tier-1 cities. The month-to-date May data is encouraging: tier-1 new home sales area rose 7% year-on-year, and secondary transactions in 10 cities increased 22% year-on-year. But the total for 30 cities was down 3% year-on-year, indicating that the recovery is still concentrated in top-tier markets.

The dispersion is even more striking at the city level. Xiamen saw a 42% year-on-year increase in secondary transactions in May, followed by Shanghai at 33%, Foshan at 32%, Suzhou at 25%, and Shenzhen at 24%. Meanwhile, Hangzhou posted a 6% decline. This pattern suggests that the recovery is being driven by specific structural factors—population inflows, economic dynamism, and limited supply—rather than a broad-based improvement in sentiment. Investors should focus on developers with high exposure to these outperforming cities, particularly those with rich pipelines of high-end projects that cater to the premium segment.

The report highlights that CR Land and C&D International are the preferred picks among developers, with their rich pipelines of high-end projects in the coming months strengthening their earnings recovery from 2027 onwards. CR Land is also expected to benefit from the investment property revaluation theme as the C-REITs market expands. C&D still has room to catch up in terms of year-to-date share price performance. These are not just stock picks; they are a reflection of a broader strategic insight. In a recovery that is concentrated in tier-1 cities and driven by premium demand, developers with strong land banks in top-tier markets and a focus on high-end residential projects are best positioned to capture the next wave of earnings growth.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Structural Shift in Household Behaviour and Its Implications for Developer Business Models

The report provides a comprehensive assessment of near-term fundamentals and valuation, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most critical is whether the breakdown in credit transmission is a cyclical phenomenon that will reverse as home prices stabilise, or a structural shift in household behaviour that will permanently alter the demand landscape. The record prepayment rate suggests that households are not just waiting for prices to recover; they are actively reducing their leverage. This could be a rational response to a decade of excessive debt accumulation, stagnant income growth, and a diminished belief in real estate as a wealth-building asset.

If this is structural, then the recovery in transaction volumes may not translate into a recovery in developer profitability. Developers have historically relied on high leverage and rapid turnover to generate returns. If households are no longer willing to borrow, developers will need to compete on price to generate volume, compressing margins. The report’s preference for high-end developers like CR Land and C&D makes sense in this context, because premium projects are less dependent on leverage-driven demand. But the broader question of whether the Chinese housing market can return to its historical growth trajectory remains open.

A second unanswered question is the role of the C-REITs market in providing an alternative funding source and valuation anchor for investment properties. The report notes that CR Land could benefit from this theme, but it does not quantify the potential impact. If the C-REITs market continues to expand and provide liquidity for commercial real estate assets, it could fundamentally change the valuation framework for developers with large investment property portfolios. This is a second-order implication that deserves deeper analysis.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Transition Phase

For investors considering re-engagement with the Chinese property sector, the current environment demands a structured decision framework rather than a binary bet on the sector as a whole. The following framework is designed to help investors distinguish between developers that are positioned for the next cycle and those that are merely riding the valuation recovery.

First, assess onshore funding access and offshore maturity exposure. Developers with strong state-owned enterprise backing and limited near-term offshore maturities are better insulated from rising US bond yields and offshore refinancing risks. Those with large USD maturities in 2027, like Vanke and Longfor, face potential margin compression from higher refinancing costs.

Second, evaluate land bank quality and project pipeline. The recovery is concentrated in tier-1 cities and premium segments. Developers with rich pipelines of high-end projects in Shanghai, Shenzhen, and other outperforming cities are best positioned to capture demand. Those with exposure to tier-3 cities, where sales are still declining sharply, face headwinds.

Third, monitor the credit transmission mechanism. The key leading indicator is not transaction volumes but household medium- to long-term lending. A sustained recovery in mortgage lending would signal that the wealth effect is beginning to take hold. Until that happens, the recovery remains fragile and dependent on low rates.

Fourth, consider the structural shift in household behaviour. Even if credit transmission resumes, it may not return to historical levels. Developers that can generate returns without relying on high leverage and rapid turnover are better positioned for a lower-growth, higher-margin environment.

Fifth, evaluate the C-REITs opportunity. Developers with large investment property portfolios that can be monetised through REIT listings have an additional source of value creation that is not captured in traditional NAV-based valuations.

This framework is not exhaustive, but it provides a systematic way to evaluate the opportunities and risks in a sector that is no longer in crisis but has not yet entered a self-sustaining recovery.

## The Next Catalyst Requires Patience, but the Reward for Disciplined Positioning Could Be Significant

The Chinese property sector is at an inflection point. The macro headwinds that triggered the 18 May pull-back are real but manageable. The onshore funding environment is supportive. Tier-1 cities are showing genuine improvement in both volumes and prices. The June peak season could provide the next catalyst for re-rating. But the transmission to credit expansion has not yet occurred, and the household sector remains in deleveraging mode.

For investors with a medium-term horizon, the current pull-back represents an opportunity to build positions in developers with strong fundamentals, premium project pipelines, and limited offshore refinancing risk. The report’s preference for CR Land and C&D International is consistent with this framework. But the broader lesson is that the sector is no longer a binary trade on survival. It is a stock-pickers market where the dispersion between winners and losers will widen as the recovery matures.

# South Korea’s May Export Data Reveals a Memory Supercycle That Is Still Accelerating, Not Peaking

The narrative that the memory cycle is maturing is not supported by the latest South Korean trade data. In May 2026, total semiconductor exports surged 169% year-on-year, with memory alone posting a staggering 255% growth, marking the fourth consecutive month of 200%-plus expansion. This is not a cycle reaching its apex; it is a cycle that continues to compound. For investors who have been waiting for a pullback to enter, the data suggests that the structural demand drivers from AI infrastructure buildout are still intensifying.

The most striking figure is DRAM export growth of 370% year-on-year, the highest since tracking began in January 2008. This is not a base-effect artifact. The absolute export value reached $18.6 billion in a single month, a level that would have been unthinkable two years ago. The implication is clear: the AI-driven demand for high-bandwidth memory and conventional DRAM is overwhelming supply, and Korean memory manufacturers are the primary beneficiaries.

The question is no longer whether this cycle is real. The question is how much further it can run, and which segments of the supply chain will capture the most value. The May data provides a rich set of signals to answer that question, but it also raises new ones that demand deeper analysis.

## South Korea’s Memory Export Data Is a Leading Indicator for Global AI Infrastructure Demand, Not Just a Local Metric

Many investors treat Korean export data as a lagging indicator of semiconductor industry health. The May figures invert that logic. Because Korean memory makers supply the vast majority of the world’s DRAM and NAND, and because AI server demand is the primary driver of memory consumption growth, the Korean export data has become a real-time proxy for global AI infrastructure spending.

The 370% year-on-year growth in DRAM exports is not solely about volume. It reflects a significant shift in product mix toward higher-value memory, particularly HBM and advanced DDR5. The average selling price for memory has risen dramatically as the industry has shifted production capacity away from legacy products. This is a structural change, not a cyclical one. The AI data center buildout requires memory that is faster, denser, and more power-efficient, and Korean manufacturers are the only ones producing it at scale.

The implication for global investors is straightforward: as long as South Korean memory exports maintain this trajectory, the AI infrastructure investment thesis remains intact. Any slowdown in these numbers would be the first genuine warning signal for the broader AI trade.

## The Correlation Between Japanese Material Imports and HBM Shipments Creates a Powerful Leading Signal for Samsung and SK Hynix

One of the most valuable insights from the May data is the continued strength in HBM-related material imports. The report tracks two specific proxies: epoxide resins imported from Japan to Icheon and Cheongju, where SK Hynix produces HBM, and plastic film imported to Hwaseong and Pyeongtaek, where Samsung Electronics produces HBM. In April, epoxide resin imports rose 57% year-on-year, and plastic film imports rose 96% year-on-year.

These materials are not generic inputs. Epoxide resins are used in SK Hynix’s MR-MUF process, and plastic film is used in Samsung’s TC-NCF process. The historical correlation between these import values and actual HBM shipments is strong, as shown in the report’s exhibits. When imports accelerate, shipments follow with a lag of one to two quarters.

This is a critical forward-looking indicator. The April data suggests that HBM shipments for both companies are set to accelerate further in the second half of 2026. The report projects that SK Hynix’s second-quarter 2026 revenue will increase 271% year-on-year, while Samsung’s memory revenue will increase 452% year-on-year. The differential reflects Samsung’s higher exposure to conventional memory and a lower base in HBM revenue, but both trajectories are extraordinary.

The open question is whether the material import data can also serve as a warning signal. If imports plateau or decline, it would suggest that HBM production is hitting capacity constraints. The April data shows no such signal yet.

## The WFE Equipment Import Surge Signals That Memory Makers Are Still Adding Capacity, Not Slowing Down

Wafer fabrication equipment imports into South Korea rose 99% year-on-year in May, while WFE exports rose 66% year-on-year. This is a powerful signal that memory manufacturers are still in the midst of a major capacity expansion cycle. The report attributes the strong import growth to increasing capital expenditure by memory suppliers in an effort to alleviate supply tightness.

This is a crucial point for understanding the cycle’s duration. In previous memory upcycles, capacity additions would eventually overshoot demand and trigger a downturn. The current cycle is different because the demand is coming from AI workloads that require specialized memory products, not just generic DRAM. The capacity being added is primarily for HBM and advanced nodes, which take longer to bring online and are harder to repurpose for other applications.

The WFE data suggests that the memory makers believe the demand is structural and are willing to commit significant capital to meet it. The risk is that if AI demand growth slows unexpectedly, the industry could be left with excess capacity. But for now, the equipment import data is a vote of confidence in the cycle’s longevity.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Vulnerability of the Battery and Display Recovery

While the memory data is overwhelmingly positive, the recovery in other segments of South Korea’s tech exports is more fragile and less well understood. Lithium-ion battery exports rebounded 53% year-on-year in May after a 10% decline in April. The report attributes this to increased EV and ESS battery exports to the U.S. and China, along with ASP hikes from elevated mineral prices.

The volatility in battery exports is concerning. The April decline was sharp, and the May recovery, while strong, has not yet established a sustained trend. The battery market faces structural uncertainties that the memory market does not: policy changes in the U.S. and Europe regarding EV subsidies, competition from Chinese battery makers, and the potential for technological disruption in solid-state batteries. The report does not provide a framework for assessing whether the May rebound is a genuine recovery or a temporary bounce.

Similarly, display exports showed a high single-digit rebound, with OLED up 3% year-on-year and LCD up 30%. The report notes that new OLED mobile products contributed to the increase, despite contraction in end-market demand due to higher semiconductor costs. This is a revealing comment: higher memory costs are actually cannibalizing demand for other components. The question is whether this is a temporary squeeze or a structural shift in how device makers allocate their bill of materials.

Investors in display and battery stocks need a more granular framework for assessing these risks. The memory data is a clear signal; the non-memory data is more ambiguous.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Use South Korean Export Data to Position in the AI Supply Chain

The May export data provides a practical framework for making investment decisions across the AI supply chain. The framework rests on three pillars: demand confirmation, capacity signaling, and margin visibility.

First, demand confirmation. The memory export data is the most direct and timely indicator of AI-related demand. When DRAM exports grow at 370% year-on-year, the demand thesis is confirmed. Investors should use the monthly export data as their primary check on whether the AI narrative is intact. If memory growth decelerates to below 100% year-on-year, that would be the first sign of normalization.

Second, capacity signaling. The WFE import data tells investors whether the supply side is expanding to meet demand. Strong WFE imports mean that memory makers are investing for growth, which supports the equipment and materials suppliers in the supply chain. The 99% year-on-year growth in WFE imports is a positive signal for companies that provide semiconductor manufacturing equipment, advanced materials, and testing services.

Third, margin visibility. The HBM material import data provides a leading indicator for the profitability of SK Hynix and Samsung. When epoxide resin and plastic film imports are rising, it suggests that HBM production is ramping, which typically comes with higher margins than conventional memory. The April data supports the view that HBM margins will remain strong through the second half of 2026.

For investors who want to go deeper, the open questions are worth exploring. How sustainable is the battery recovery? Will the display segment continue to be squeezed by memory costs? And what happens when the memory cycle eventually normalizes? The full report provides the detailed charts and data needed to answer these questions.

EN_A: I came across a research report on the European steel industry with a really interesting title: “Old Playbook, New Framework.” What does that actually mean?
EN_B: It captures the core shift. The old playbook for European steel was all about economic cycles and demand. But now, a new framework is forming, and the main driver is policy and regulation, not just demand.
EN_A: So what is the one big question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It is asking a very practical question: Are European steel companies actually a good investment right now? And importantly, it is not approaching this from the old angle of a demand recovery. It is looking at changes on the supply side and the regulatory side.
EN_A: Changes on the supply and regulatory side. What exactly do you mean?
EN_B: Two big things. First, the EU’s new steel safeguard measures. Starting July 2026, import quotas will be cut by about 47%, and the duty for imports outside the quota will jump to 50%. Second, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, or CBAM, also kicks into its definitive phase in 2026.
EN_A: What does that actually mean for domestic steel mills in Europe?
EN_B: It means the cost of imported steel goes up significantly. Import parity gets lifted, and that opens up pricing power for local European mills. The report argues the steel profit pool is shifting back from importers to domestic producers.
EN_A: So this is a policy-driven profit transfer. Does the report name any companies that benefit the most from this?
EN_B: Yes. In carbon steel, their top picks are ArcelorMittal and SSAB. ArcelorMittal has local assets and high operating leverage to wider price spreads. For SSAB, they think the market is not fully pricing in its strong US plate momentum and its resilient special steels business.
EN_A: That sounds like a structural change. But what about demand? European manufacturing and construction don’t look that strong.
EN_B: That is a key point. The report acknowledges that demand is not booming. Indicators like German factory orders and building permits are still weak. So the investment thesis here is not built on a demand surge. It is built on this new framework of constrained supply, higher costs, and policy protection.
EN_A: That is really interesting. What about the stainless steel side? How does the report see that?
EN_B: The logic is similar, but it relies more on imports falling due to policy. They favor Aperam and Acerinox. Aperam is the leader in Europe, and its capacity utilization is only around 65% right now. If European demand shows even a small recovery, its operating leverage would be enormous.
EN_A: So does the report leave any big questions unanswered? What are the risks to this new framework?
EN_B: Definitely. For example, how durable is this policy framework? What if global trade tensions escalate and European steel exports face retaliation? Also, some companies mentioned, like thyssenkrupp, have big strategic restructuring plans. Whether those succeed is still an open question.
EN_A: So this “new framework” is not a risk-free conclusion.
EN_B: Exactly. The report gives a clear directional view, but the real investment decision requires reading the full report to understand the detailed risk-reward breakdown for each company. For example, why they think some stocks still have room to re-rate, while others have already priced in the good news.

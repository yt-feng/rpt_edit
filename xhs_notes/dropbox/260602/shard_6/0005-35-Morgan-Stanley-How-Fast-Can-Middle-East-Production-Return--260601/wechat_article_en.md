# The Oil Market Is Pricing a Clean Recovery That History Suggests Will Not Arrive

The most striking feature of the current oil market is not the scale of the supply disruption, which is the largest in history, but the price it has produced. Inflation-adjusted Brent crude now sits precisely at its 20-year median, at roughly $92 per barrel. This is the most ordinary price of the last two decades, generated by the most extraordinary supply shock. The market has priced in a clean, near-term resolution, and it has become more confident in that view even as weeks pass without a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz.

This confidence is visible across the futures curve. The front-week Brent CFD, the clearest measure of prompt physical tightness, has more than halved in three weeks. The M1-M2 spread has collapsed from nearly $4 per barrel to under $1. The market is not merely expecting a resolution; it is increasingly pricing a swift and complete return of supply across the entire curve. From current levels, this optimism appears misplaced. The mechanisms that have absorbed the shock so far are showing signs of strain, and the timeline for production recovery is likely to be longer and more backloaded than the market assumes.

Our modelling now shifts the assumed inflection point for Strait of Hormuz oil exports to late July, from the end of May previously. We also adjust our estimate for production recovery after reopening, from 70% recovery over three months to 75% recovery over four months. The combined effect of these changes is a Brent market that remains broadly as tight in the third quarter as it was in the second quarter, with further inventory draws ahead. We hold our Brent forecast at $100 for the third quarter but raise our fourth-quarter estimate from $90 to $95 and our first-quarter 2027 estimate from $80 to $85. With spot at $92, an unchanged near-term forecast is itself a constructive call. From here, risks to oil prices have a positive skew.

## The Strait of Hormuz Will Reopen Later Than the Market Assumes, and Each Delay Compounds the Recovery Challenge

The pattern is now familiar. An early-April ceasefire announcement, mid-April peace-talk headlines, a May escort proposal, and a late-May report of a deal "largely negotiated" each made an imminent reopening look plausible. Each proved premature. Over the last three weeks, the price has not risen; it has fallen, from roughly $104 to $92. The market has grown more sanguine about a resolution, not less, despite the absence of any concrete agreement.

The delay in our modelling assumption from end-May to late July reflects two distinct components. First, at the time of writing, no agreement or memorandum of understanding exists between the US and Iran on peace negotiations. How much longer this takes is uncertain, but for modelling purposes we assume a few more weeks. Second, even after a political agreement, several weeks are required for mine clearing and for commercial confidence among ship owners to rebuild. That puts a meaningful pick-up in flows only from the second half of July. This is the central case of an admittedly wide distribution, but the skew is clearly to the downside for supply timing.

The implication is straightforward: the longer the Strait remains closed, the more complex the recovery becomes. Each additional week of shut-in deepens the operational challenges that will constrain the pace of production restoration once flows resume. The market is not pricing this compounding effect.

## Production Recovery Will Be Slow and Backloaded Because the Bottlenecks Are Operational, Not Just Political

Data from Rystad Energy indicates that roughly 11 million barrels per day of Gulf crude remains offline, close to half the region's pre-conflict output. Crucially, nearly all of this is because storage facilities have filled up, not because capacity has been destroyed. Restarting production is not technically impossible, but it takes time, and the bottlenecks are numerous and sequential.

The first hurdle is security and insurance. Shipowners and insurance providers need evidence that the Strait is genuinely safe to navigate again. A ceasefire announcement alone is not sufficient. The area must be cleared of sea mines, a process that likely takes weeks to several months. As long as mines are believed to be in place in traditional shipping lanes, the capacity of the Strait is much reduced, even if it is technically open.

Second, tankers are in the wrong place. Over the last few months, idle ships that could not find work in the region have ballasted to the US Gulf, West Africa, and Brazil, often creating an oversupply there. As the CEO of Aramco noted on the company's first-quarter earnings call, this is likely "the biggest issue." Even in the most optimistic scenario, energy and commodity supply chains will need several months to return to their pre-conflict traffic patterns.

Third, storage and ullage inside the Gulf constrain the restart. Oilfields cannot restart as long as export storage is full. This is especially critical for Iraq and Kuwait, whose seaborne exports have practically fallen to zero. For these countries, the number of empty tankers transiting into the Gulf is essential to the restart of oil production.

Fourth, field restarts become more difficult with each passing week. Rystad estimates that roughly 10,000 of the approximately 36,000 wells that were active before the closure across the six Gulf producers are currently offline. After a shut-in of roughly four and a half to five months, some 4,000 to 5,000 wells face restart constraints. When injection is halted, reservoir pressure bleeds away. Wellbores cool, precipitating wax and asphaltenes that plug tubing. Corrosion and artificial-lift failures set in after extended idle periods. Flowlines must be cleaned, integrity-tested, and phased back into service. These effects are largely reversible, but they cost time and money.

Finally, oilfield service companies have demobilized staff, suspended travel, and rerouted logistics. Bringing back crews and equipment takes time. Expats have largely withdrawn from Kuwait and Iraq, which rely most heavily on international providers for drilling, completions, and artificial lift.

The cumulative effect of these bottlenecks is a recovery that is likely to be backloaded, with the bulk of production restoration occurring in the fourth quarter of 2026 and the first quarter of 2027, rather than in the immediate aftermath of a political agreement.

## The Buffers That Have Absorbed the Shock Are Fraying, and the Mechanisms May Not Last Through the Third Quarter

The oil market has absorbed the largest supply disruption in history with remarkably little price impact, and this has been possible because of three buffers. First, the market entered the conflict with substantial inventories. Second, the market has persistently expected an imminent reopening of the Strait, which has kept forward prices anchored. Third, large US oil exports and low imports into China have shielded the rest of the oil market from the full force of the supply loss.

These buffers are now under strain. US exports cannot grow indefinitely, and Chinese imports are unlikely to remain depressed throughout the third quarter. The inventory cushion that existed at the start of the conflict has been drawn down. And the market's expectation of an imminent reopening has been repeatedly disappointed, yet the price has continued to fall.

The question that the report does not fully answer is whether the market's current pricing reflects a rational assessment of the probability distribution of outcomes, or whether it reflects a systematic underestimation of tail risks. The distribution of possible reopening dates is wide, and the consequences of a further delay are asymmetric. If the Strait reopens tomorrow, prices fall, but the downside is limited by the fact that production recovery will still take months. If the Strait remains closed through the end of the third quarter, the upside could be substantial, as the buffers that have absorbed the shock become exhausted. The market appears to be pricing the modal outcome, not the expected value across the full distribution.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in an Oil Market Where the Skew Is to the Upside

For readers trying to make sense of this market, the analytical challenge is to distinguish between the base case and the range of plausible outcomes. Our base case is that the Strait reopens in late July, production recovers at a rate of 75% over four months, and Brent trades in the $95 to $100 range through the end of the third quarter before converging toward $80 in the second half of 2027. This is a constructive call relative to spot, but it is not a bullish call.

The more interesting question is how to think about the tails. The distribution of outcomes around this base case is asymmetric. On the downside, an earlier reopening would likely push prices lower, but the magnitude of the decline is capped by the operational constraints on production recovery. Even in the best case, it will take months for supply to return to pre-conflict levels. On the upside, a further delay in reopening, or a slower-than-expected recovery, could push prices significantly higher as inventories are drawn down and the buffers that have shielded the market become exhausted.

The framework we find most useful is to think in terms of scenarios rather than point forecasts. In a scenario where the Strait reopens in late July and recovery proceeds as we model, Brent is likely to trade in the $90 to $100 range through the end of the third quarter. In a scenario where reopening is delayed until September, Brent could trade above $120. In a scenario where reopening occurs earlier but recovery is slower than expected, Brent could remain elevated for longer than the curve currently implies.

The key variable to watch is not the headline price of Brent, but the shape of the futures curve and the behavior of physical premiums. A widening of the front-month spread would be the first sign that the market is beginning to price in a tighter physical balance. A narrowing of the spread, as we have seen over the last three weeks, suggests that the market remains in denial about the timeline for supply restoration.

## The Report Raises More Questions Than It Answers, and Those Questions Are the Reason to Read the Full Analysis

This analysis leaves several open questions that are worth considering. First, how much of the current price weakness is due to financial positioning rather than physical fundamentals? If speculative positions have been reduced, a reversal could amplify any price move that results from a change in the physical outlook. Second, what is the role of strategic petroleum reserves? The US and other OECD countries have released significant volumes from strategic reserves, but the capacity for further releases is limited. Third, how will Chinese demand evolve in the second half of the year? Chinese imports have been low, but this reflects a combination of destocking and demand weakness. A recovery in Chinese demand would put additional pressure on an already tight market.

These are not idle questions. They are the analytical gaps that the full report addresses with data, charts, and scenario analysis.

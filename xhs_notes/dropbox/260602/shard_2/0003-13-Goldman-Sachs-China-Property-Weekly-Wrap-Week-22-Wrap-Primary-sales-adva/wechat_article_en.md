# China's Property Market Is Rebalancing, Not Recovering: The Real Story Behind the Week 22 Data

The narrative that China's property market is staging a broad-based recovery is incomplete. What the Week 22 data reveals is something more nuanced and strategically important: the market is undergoing a structural rebalancing, where primary sales are stabilizing on policy support while secondary markets show genuine demand-side resilience. This divergence is not a sign of weakness. It is the signature of a market transitioning from developer-led speculation to end-user-driven liquidity.

Primary sales volumes advanced 13% week-on-week, reaching a new high since the Qingming Festival and closing May at roughly flat year-on-year. That is a meaningful milestone. After months of contraction, the primary market is holding its ground. But the secondary market tells a different story. While transactions dipped 2% week-on-week, they remain 20% higher year-on-year. More importantly, sentiment indicators show a widening gap: agents' price expectations remain intact, but home-sellers' asking prices are edging lower. This gap is the key to understanding where the market is headed.

The policy layer adds critical texture. Guangzhou launched a city-wide buyback program to facilitate trade-ins, targeting central district units priced below RMB 3 million with floor area under 70 square meters. The city also expanded the conversion of commercial mortgages into Housing Provident Fund loans, raising the maximum loan-to-value ratio from 70% to 80%, and broadening borrower eligibility to include multi-home owners, previous HPF loan recipients, and contributors from the Greater Bay Area. This is not a one-off stimulus. It is a deliberate attempt to create a liquidity bridge between the primary and secondary markets.

What makes this moment strategically significant is that the market is responding to policy in a differentiated way. The primary market's recovery is policy-dependent and fragile. The secondary market's strength is more organic, driven by genuine end-user demand. Investors who treat these as the same signal will misread the trajectory. The real question is not whether the market is recovering, but how the two segments will converge or diverge in the months ahead.

## The Primary Market Recovery Is Real but Narrowly Based on Policy Catalysts

The 13% week-on-week increase in primary sales volumes is the headline number that will grab attention. But the details beneath it matter more. Search activities for new homes remained flat week-on-week, suggesting that the volume increase was not driven by a surge in new buyer interest but by conversion of existing interest into transactions. That is a positive signal for developers with inventory, but it also means the recovery lacks a self-sustaining demand base.

Year-to-date, primary GFA sold is still down 14% year-on-year. Relative to 2024 levels, the decline is 15%, and relative to 2023, it is a staggering 47%. The recovery is from a very low base. The policy catalysts are clear: Guangzhou's buyback program and HPF loan expansion, combined with similar easing measures in other cities, are creating a window of opportunity for transactions. But this window is not open indefinitely. The buyback program is targeted at specific price points and unit sizes. The HPF loan expansion relies on eligibility criteria that not all buyers can meet.

The implication for investors is straightforward: primary market exposure should be seen as a tactical play on policy timing, not a structural bet on demand recovery. Developers with strong execution in cities implementing buyback programs will outperform. But the broader primary market will remain constrained until the supply-side issues of inventory and new starts are resolved. The data on new starts is telling: a low-twenties percent year-on-year decline in May, based on land sales trends and cement shipment ratios. Developers are not building. They are selling what they have.

## Secondary Market Resilience Reveals a Structural Shift in Buyer Behavior

The secondary market is where the real story lies. Transactions dipped only 2% week-on-week, maintaining a 20% year-on-year gain. Year-to-date, secondary GFA sold is flat year-on-year but up 23% versus 2024 and 7% versus 2023. This is not a blip. It is a pattern that has been building for months.

The divergence between agents' price expectations and sellers' asking prices is the critical signal. The Centraline Salesman Index, which measures agents' views on property prices, rose 0.2 points week-on-week and is 3.7 points higher year-on-year. Agents see price stability or appreciation ahead. But the Centraline Seller Asking Index fell 0.5 points week-on-week and is 3.6 points lower year-on-year. Sellers are becoming more realistic about pricing.

This gap means the market is clearing. Sellers who adjust expectations are finding buyers. The volume is there because price discovery is happening. In a healthy market, this is exactly what you want to see. The secondary market is functioning as a price-setting mechanism, and the volume data confirms that buyers are willing to transact at the right price.

The strategic implication is that secondary market exposure offers a more durable demand signal than primary market data. Investors focused on property-related equities or credit should pay more attention to secondary volume trends than to primary sales headlines. The secondary market is where end-user demand reveals itself, and that demand is proving to be more resilient than many expected.

## Guangzhou's Policy Innovation Is a Template, Not an Outlier

Guangzhou's city-wide buyback program is worth examining in detail because it represents a new policy approach that other cities are likely to follow. The program targets central district units priced below RMB 3 million with floor area under 70 square meters. The government will acquire these units and convert them into social and talent housing. This is not just about stimulating demand. It is about creating a dual-purpose mechanism that addresses both the inventory overhang and the housing affordability challenge.

The expansion of HPF loan conversion is equally significant. By raising the maximum LTV to 80%, broadening borrower eligibility to include multi-home owners and previous HPF recipients, and extending coverage to GBA contributors, Guangzhou is effectively creating a larger pool of qualified buyers. The old restrictions excluded exactly the demographic that has the highest propensity to trade up: existing homeowners with equity. By bringing them back into the market, Guangzhou is trying to restart the housing ladder.

The question is whether this model can scale. Other tier-1 and tier-2 cities have similar inventory challenges and similar housing affordability pressures. If Guangzhou's program succeeds in clearing inventory and stabilizing prices, it will become a template. If it fails, the policy toolkit will need to be recalibrated. Either way, the next six months will be a live experiment in state-facilitated housing market intervention.

## The Report's Blind Spot: What Happens When Policy Support Fades?

The data and analysis in the report are thorough, but there is one question it does not fully answer: what happens when the current wave of policy support fades? The primary market recovery is policy-dependent. The secondary market resilience is more organic, but it is also vulnerable to macro headwinds. The report does not model a scenario where policy support is withdrawn or where macro conditions deteriorate.

Consider the inventory picture. Inventory months stand at 27.6, down from an average of 29.3 in April but still elevated. Inventory balance is down 0.5% week-on-week and 4.4% from end-2025 levels. Progress is being made, but the pace is slow. At the current rate of absorption, it will take years to reach equilibrium. The report's completion data, based on the GSPC tracker, shows a high-teens percent year-on-year decline in May and a projected 1% decline for the full year 2026. New completions are falling, which will eventually help inventory, but the lag is long.

The valuation data adds another layer of uncertainty. Covered stronger SOE developers trade at an average 24% discount to end-2026E NAV for offshore and 20% for onshore. Price-to-book multiples are at 0.6x and 0.5x respectively. These are trough-like valuations, but they are not necessarily a buy signal. Trough valuations can persist for extended periods if the fundamental drivers of recovery remain uncertain. The report compares current valuations to previous troughs in 2008, 2011, and 2014, but those troughs occurred in different macro environments. The current environment features a structural slowdown in housing demand, not a cyclical downturn.

The open question is whether the market can transition from policy-supported stabilization to self-sustaining recovery. The answer will depend on income growth, employment conditions, and household confidence. The report does not address these macro variables in depth, leaving investors to fill the gap with their own analysis.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Divergence

The primary-secondary market divergence creates a clear decision framework for investors. The framework has three dimensions: market segment, time horizon, and risk tolerance.

For investors with a short-term horizon and higher risk tolerance, the primary market offers tactical opportunities tied to policy catalysts. Cities implementing buyback programs or HPF loan expansions will see transaction spikes. Developers with concentrated exposure to these cities will benefit. But the trade-off is timing risk. Policy windows can close quickly, and the underlying demand may not sustain volumes once the catalyst fades.

For investors with a medium-term horizon, the secondary market offers a more reliable signal. The volume trend is positive, price discovery is happening, and the demand base is end-user driven. The risk is that macro conditions deteriorate, causing sellers to become even more aggressive in cutting prices, which could lead to a downward price spiral. But so far, the data suggests that sellers are adjusting gradually, not panicking.

For long-term investors, the structural shift in the market is the most important consideration. The era of developer-led speculation is over. The market is becoming more transaction-driven and less investment-driven. This has implications for asset allocation. Direct property exposure may offer lower returns but more liquidity. Developer equity may offer deep value but with higher risk. The right strategy depends on the investor's ability to hold through cycles and their conviction about the Chinese economy's long-term trajectory.

The key metric to watch is the gap between agents' price expectations and sellers' asking prices. If that gap narrows, it means the market is approaching equilibrium. If it widens, it means sellers are losing confidence, and the recovery could stall. This single indicator captures more information than any volume or price headline.

## Why This Moment Demands Deeper Analysis

The Week 22 data is a snapshot of a market in transition. The primary market is stabilizing on policy life support. The secondary market is showing genuine resilience. The policy toolkit is evolving, with Guangzhou's buyback program offering a potential template. But the report leaves important questions unanswered, and those questions will determine the market's trajectory over the next 12 to 18 months.

Investors who rely on headlines will miss the nuance. The market is not recovering in a uniform way. It is rebalancing, and the rebalancing process creates both opportunities and risks. Understanding the divergence between primary and secondary markets, the limitations of policy support, and the structural shift in buyer behavior is essential for making informed decisions.

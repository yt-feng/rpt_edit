# China Software Growth Is Slowing, but the Real Story Is the Shift From Volume to Value

The headline numbers for China's software industry in April 2026 tell a straightforward story of deceleration. Aggregate revenue growth slipped to 8.9% year-on-year, down from 11.6% in March and 11.5% in April 2025. The four-month cumulative figure of 10.9% barely exceeds the 10.8% recorded in the same period last year. For investors accustomed to double-digit expansion, this looks like a sector losing momentum. That reading, however, misses the more consequential transformation underway. The slowdown is not a signal of weakening demand so much as it is a reflection of how enterprise spending is being reallocated—away from generic IT services and toward foundational AI models, customized AI solutions, and agent-based platforms. The revenue composition is changing faster than the top-line growth rate suggests, and that shift carries profound implications for which companies will win and which will be left behind.

The April data, drawn from official MIIT figures and analyzed in a recent global investment bank report, reveals a market at an inflection point. Net margins improved to 12.0% in April from 9.0% in March, pushing the four-month net margin to 11.4% versus 11.2% in the first quarter. This margin expansion, occurring against a backdrop of slower revenue growth, is the first clue that the industry is moving from a volume-driven model to a value-driven one. Companies are not simply selling more seats or licenses; they are delivering higher-value solutions that command better pricing and generate stronger profitability. The question for investors is whether this margin improvement is sustainable or merely a quarterly artifact.

The more telling signal comes from the segment-level data. Semiconductor design software grew 18.5% year-on-year in the first four months, far outpacing the industry average. Cloud computing and big data followed at 12.3%, while IT services—the largest segment at 67% of total revenue—grew 11.9%. The laggards are telling: basic software and industrial software both grew at just 8.5%, cybersecurity at 7.5%, and e-commerce IT services at 7.9%. This is not a broad-based slowdown. It is a rotation. Enterprises are prioritizing investments that directly enhance productivity, automate complex workflows, and embed intelligence into core operations. They are deprioritizing perimeter security, basic software licenses, and transactional e-commerce platforms.

The SMB PMI reading of 48.5 in May, down from 50.1 in April and 49.3 in March, adds a layer of caution. Small and medium businesses, which form the backbone of China's software consumption, are clearly under pressure. When the PMI dips below 50, it signals contraction in business activity, which typically leads to tighter IT budgets. Yet even here, the data carries a nuance. The volatility in the SMB PMI over recent months suggests that the traditional correlation between PMI and software spending may be weakening. Companies are not cutting software budgets uniformly; they are rebalancing them. They are canceling or deferring legacy maintenance contracts while accelerating spending on AI agents, generative AI tools, and platforms that promise measurable returns on efficiency.

This is the central argument that most market commentary misses: the China software industry is not entering a downturn. It is entering a restructuring. The growth deceleration in April is a statistical reflection of the transition, not its cause. The real strategic question is how to position for the next phase of the cycle.

## AI Agent Monetization Is Accelerating Faster Than the Aggregate Data Suggests

The most important development buried in the April report is the accelerating monetization of AI agents. The report explicitly notes that AI agents are being deployed to retain experienced employees' know-how in-house, to diversify production sites with higher efficiency, and to serve as personal assistants for daily tasks such as file search and chat. This is not speculative. Companies like Meitu are hosting major product launch events focused on AI agents and generative AI products. Glodon has released AI products including PMLead and PMSmart, built around a platform strategy to better integrate data.

What makes this significant is the economic logic. AI agents represent a shift from selling software as a tool to selling software as a substitute for labor. When a manufacturing company deploys an AI agent to capture the tacit knowledge of senior engineers and apply it across multiple production sites, the value proposition is no longer about cost savings on software licenses. It is about scaling scarce expertise without hiring. This is a fundamentally different pricing model, one that supports higher per-user revenue and more sticky customer relationships.

The aggregate industry data cannot yet capture this shift because the revenue base is still dominated by traditional IT services. But the margin improvement in April is a leading indicator. Companies that are early movers in AI agent deployment are likely enjoying better margins because they are selling higher-value solutions rather than competing on price for commoditized services. The risk is that the market is still pricing most software companies based on historical multiples that reflect the old revenue mix. As more companies report earnings that highlight AI agent revenue, the valuation gap between leaders and laggards will widen sharply.

## The IT Services Dominance Masks a Structural Decline in Traditional Outsourcing

IT services account for 67% of total software industry revenue, and the segment grew 11.9% in the first four months. On the surface, this looks healthy. But the composition within IT services matters enormously. The report notes that IT services growth was mainly driven by cloud computing and big data. The traditional IT outsourcing and system integration business, which has been the backbone of China's software industry for two decades, is likely growing much more slowly or even contracting.

This distinction is critical for investors. Companies that derive the majority of their revenue from legacy IT services face a double squeeze: their clients are redirecting budgets toward AI and cloud, while their own cost structures are burdened by labor-intensive delivery models. The margin compression in traditional IT services is likely to accelerate, even as the headline IT services number appears stable. The winners in this segment will be those that have successfully pivoted their service offerings toward cloud migration, AI implementation, and data engineering. The losers will be those that continue to sell bodies and billable hours.

The data on overseas exposure adds another dimension. The export ratio for China's software companies rose to 3.1% in April from 2.8% in March. This is still a small number, but the direction is positive. Chinese software companies are increasingly targeting non-China markets, particularly in Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Africa, where demand for digital transformation is strong and competition from Western vendors is less intense. The ability to serve these markets with AI-enabled platforms rather than labor-intensive services will determine whether this export trend becomes a meaningful growth driver or remains a marginal contributor.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Quickly AI Investment Will Translate Into Sustained Revenue Growth

For all its granularity, the April report leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important is the conversion rate between AI investment and revenue. The report documents that enterprise spending is being allocated to AI foundational models and customized AI projects, but it does not provide data on how much of this spending is experimental versus production-ready. In the early stages of any technology cycle, a significant portion of investment goes to proof-of-concept projects that never scale. If the current AI spending wave is heavily weighted toward pilots, the revenue impact for software vendors may remain lumpy and unpredictable for several more quarters.

A second unresolved question concerns pricing power. The report notes improving net margins, but it does not distinguish between margin improvement driven by operational efficiency and margin improvement driven by pricing. If companies are simply cutting costs to maintain profitability in a slower-growth environment, the margin expansion is not sustainable. If, however, companies are successfully raising prices for AI-enabled products, the margin story has legs. The data on net margins by segment would be invaluable here, but the report aggregates at the industry level.

A third open question is the competitive dynamics within the AI agent space. The report mentions Meitu and Glodon as examples, but it does not address how many companies are entering this market and whether the competitive intensity will compress margins over time. In the early phase of any new technology market, first movers can capture disproportionate share. But if the barriers to entry are low, the market could quickly become crowded, and pricing discipline could erode. The report's silence on this point leaves investors to make their own judgments about the sustainability of AI agent margins.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the China Software Transition

For investors and strategists trying to position for the next 12 to 18 months, the April data suggests a three-part decision framework. First, segment exposure matters more than sector exposure. The old approach of buying a basket of China software companies and expecting the rising tide to lift all boats is no longer valid. Investors need to distinguish between companies that are benefiting from the AI and cloud rotation and those that are being disrupted by it. The segment growth data in the report provides a clear ranking: semiconductor design software, cloud computing and big data, and IT services with a cloud focus are the high-growth areas. Basic software, industrial software, and cybersecurity are the laggards.

Second, margin trajectory is a better signal than revenue growth. The April margin improvement is encouraging, but it needs to be confirmed by subsequent months. Investors should focus on companies that can demonstrate both revenue growth and margin expansion, as this combination indicates pricing power and operational leverage. Companies that grow revenue but compress margins are likely competing on price in commoditized segments. Companies that maintain margins but fail to grow revenue are harvesting legacy businesses without investing in the future.

Third, AI agent monetization is the highest-conviction theme, but it requires patience. The report's examples of AI agent use cases are compelling, but the revenue contribution from these products is likely still small for most companies. Investors should look for companies that are reporting AI agent revenue as a separate line item or providing disclosure on the number of enterprise customers deploying agents. The absence of such disclosure should be treated as a red flag. The market will eventually reward companies that can show tangible AI monetization, but the timing of that reward is uncertain.

The April software industry data is not a signal to reduce exposure to China software. It is a signal to become more discriminating. The deceleration in top-line growth is the price the industry is paying for its transition to a higher-value, AI-driven model. The companies that navigate this transition successfully will emerge stronger, with better margins, stickier customer relationships, and a more defensible competitive position. The companies that fail to adapt will see their revenue growth stall and their margins compress, even if the aggregate industry data continues to show modest expansion.

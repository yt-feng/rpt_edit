# AI Is Not Just Driving Semiconductor Demand—It Is Reshaping the Industry's Structural Growth Model

The semiconductor industry has entered a phase where the traditional cyclical narrative no longer fully explains what is happening. For years, investors have been conditioned to expect boom-and-bust patterns driven by inventory corrections, end-market saturation, and commodity pricing pressure. That framework is becoming obsolete. Based on conversations with five CEOs at a major industry conference in mid-2026, a different story is emerging: artificial intelligence is not merely adding a new demand vector to an existing cycle. It is fundamentally altering the visibility, duration, and composition of semiconductor capital spending. The implications for investors are not incremental. They are structural.

What makes this moment different is not just the scale of AI-driven demand, but the breadth. AI is simultaneously pulling demand across leading-edge logic, memory, advanced packaging, and even analog and power semiconductors. The CEOs of Lam Research, Applied Materials, Qualcomm, Texas Instruments, and Entegris each described a landscape where their customers are placing orders with visibility extending multiple years into the future—something that would have been unthinkable in previous cycles. This is not a typical upswing. It is a regime change.

The key question for investors is whether current valuations already reflect this structural shift or whether the market is still pricing semiconductors on a cyclical basis. The evidence from these CEO conversations suggests that the latter is more likely. If that is correct, there is meaningful upside for companies that are positioned to capture the multi-year AI-driven buildout, and significant risk for those that are not.

This article synthesizes the strategic insights from those five CEO discussions, identifies the patterns that matter most for long-term investors, and highlights the analytical gaps that the full report addresses in greater depth.

## The Industry Has Entered a Period of Unprecedented Demand Visibility That Breaks the Historical Cyclical Pattern

The single most striking theme across all five CEO conversations was the length and clarity of demand visibility. This is not a cyclical upswing driven by inventory restocking or short-term end-market strength. It is a structural buildout driven by AI infrastructure requirements that span every major device category.

Lam Research's CEO described a WFE (wafer fab equipment) environment where CY26 guidance of $140 billion has upward bias and CY27 is expected to grow further. More tellingly, he noted that the largest constraint on growth is not demand but clean room space—a physical capacity limitation that speaks to the intensity of current investment. Applied Materials' CEO echoed this, stating that customers are providing rolling eight-quarter forecasts, a level of forward visibility that is historically unprecedented. Even Texas Instruments, a company known for its conservative outlook and low visibility, acknowledged that its datacenter segment grew 90% year-over-year in Q1 and now represents a $2 billion run-rate business.

The so-what for investors is clear: the traditional semiconductor cycle, where peaks are followed by sharp corrections, may not apply to this phase. Companies are building capacity not for next quarter but for the early 2030s. Lam Research is already qualifying products that will not ramp for another four to five years. This suggests that the current spending wave has a longer tail than most market participants assume. Valuations based on peak-cycle earnings multiples may significantly understate the duration of the opportunity.

## AI Is Driving Demand Across Every Device Type, Not Just Leading-Edge Compute

A common misconception is that AI semiconductor demand is concentrated in a narrow set of advanced logic chips used for training large models. The CEO conversations paint a very different picture. AI is pulling demand across the entire semiconductor ecosystem, and each segment has its own growth trajectory.

Lam Research's CEO described AI as requiring "more of everything"—leading-edge compute, storage, memory, advanced packaging, and new materials for device architectures. He noted that the first wave of AI demand was driven by training, which benefited advanced foundry and logic. But the second wave, driven by inference, is now boosting NAND demand and CPU demand. This is not a single-threaded story.

Applied Materials' CEO quantified this more precisely. He stated that AI-driven demand in advanced logic, DRAM (including HBM), and advanced packaging will account for roughly 80% of incremental WFE spending in 2026. Advanced packaging alone is expected to grow more than 50% this year. This is not a niche opportunity. It is the primary driver of industry growth.

For investors, this means that a narrow focus on GPU-related semiconductor plays may miss the broader opportunity. Companies with exposure to memory, packaging, and materials are also benefiting from AI-driven demand. Entegris, for example, is seeing materials content increase with every node migration and NAND layer increase. The AI buildout is not just about compute. It is about the entire infrastructure required to support it.

## The Profit Pool Is Expanding, and Pricing Power Is Returning to Equipment and Materials Suppliers

One of the most important strategic shifts described by the CEOs is the changing relationship between semiconductor equipment suppliers and their customers. For years, the industry narrative was that equipment companies faced pricing pressure as chipmakers consolidated and gained bargaining power. That narrative is being inverted.

Applied Materials' CEO stated bluntly that the industry profit pool is "larger than ever" and that the company expects to capture its fair share of the value it provides. Pricing is growing on both existing and new tools because the innovations they deliver are extremely valuable to customers. Lam Research's CEO similarly noted that gross margins are now in the "50s neighborhood" and have expanded despite lower China exposure—a clear sign that pricing power is improving.

This is a structural change, not a cyclical one. As device architectures become more complex—with gate-all-around transistors, backside power delivery, and new memory architectures like 3DDRAM—the equipment and materials required become more specialized and harder to replicate. Suppliers that are deeply embedded in customer roadmaps and co-innovating on future architectures have pricing leverage that did not exist in previous cycles.

The implication for investors is that revenue growth in semiconductor capital equipment may be accompanied by margin expansion, creating a powerful compounding effect on earnings. Companies like Applied Materials and Lam Research are not just riding a volume wave. They are capturing a larger share of a growing profit pool.

## The China Question Is More Nuanced Than Headlines Suggest

China exposure has been a source of anxiety for semiconductor investors, particularly after the significant tightening of export restrictions in 2025. The CEO conversations provide a more nuanced picture than the binary narrative of "China is a risk" or "China is an opportunity."

Applied Materials' CEO noted that China is now in the mid-20s percent of revenue, primarily composed of its ICAPS (IoT, Communications, Automotive, Power, and Sensors) business, and that China revenue is expected to be flat to slightly up. Lam Research's CEO said China exposure is expected to be flat to up slightly, but only on lagging-edge nodes where the company can compete. Entegris' CEO made an especially interesting point: China restrictions are not an issue for his company because regulators have focused on larger-ticket items like WFE tools, while Entegris delivers over 1,000 SKUs of materials and consumables.

The strategic insight here is that China is not a monolithic exposure. Companies with leading-edge technology that is restricted in China are seeing their China business stabilize at lower levels but maintain pricing power elsewhere. Companies with materials and consumables that are harder to restrict are actually increasing their China supply capabilities. The risk is real but manageable, and the full report provides detailed breakdowns of each company's China exposure and strategy.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Which Semiconductor Companies Will Win

Based on the patterns that emerged from these five CEO conversations, investors can use a simple three-question framework to evaluate semiconductor companies in the current environment.

First, does the company have exposure to AI-driven demand across multiple device types, or is it reliant on a single end market? The companies that are best positioned are those like Applied Materials and Lam Research, which benefit from AI demand in logic, memory, and packaging simultaneously. Companies with narrow exposure to a single segment face higher risk if that segment's growth trajectory shifts.

Second, does the company have pricing power and margin expansion potential, or is it a volume-driven story? The companies that are winning in this cycle are those that can capture a larger share of the profit pool, not just more revenue. Pricing power is a function of technological differentiation and customer embeddedness. Companies that are co-innovating on future architectures with their customers have structural advantages.

Third, is the company's valuation based on cyclical or structural assumptions? If the market is still pricing semiconductor companies on peak-cycle multiples that assume a sharp downturn in two to three years, there may be significant upside for companies that can sustain growth for five to seven years. The CEO conversations suggest that the latter scenario is more likely for well-positioned firms.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer Yet

While the CEO conversations provide remarkable clarity on the industry's trajectory, several important questions remain unresolved. The full report explores these in greater depth, but they are worth highlighting here.

First, how sustainable is the pricing power that equipment and materials suppliers are currently enjoying? If chipmakers eventually succeed in bringing more manufacturing in-house or developing alternative architectures that reduce reliance on specialized equipment, the current pricing dynamics could reverse. The CEOs are confident in their positions, but the long-term competitive landscape is still evolving.

Second, what happens when the clean room capacity constraints that Lam Research and others have identified are eventually resolved? If the current bottleneck is physical infrastructure, then the current spending wave could accelerate once that constraint is lifted, or it could peak sooner if demand is being artificially pulled forward by capacity limitations.

Third, how will the memory cycle evolve? Qualcomm's CEO highlighted that memory availability is artificially constraining smartphone builds, and Texas Instruments noted that industrial is still 15% below prior peak. If memory supply normalizes faster than expected, it could create a temporary headwind for some segments even as others continue to grow.

These are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are the natural questions that arise when an industry is undergoing structural change. The full report provides additional data and context to help investors form their own views on these questions.

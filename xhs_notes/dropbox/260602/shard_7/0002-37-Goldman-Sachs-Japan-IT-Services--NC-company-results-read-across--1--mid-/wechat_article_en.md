# Network Infrastructure Demand Is Reshaping Japan’s IT Services Landscape, and Mid-Tier Players Are the Leading Indicators

The Japanese IT services sector is undergoing a structural transformation that is visible not in the headline numbers of the largest firms, but in the order books and segment-level performance of mid-tier system integrators and network integrators. Two companies not typically covered by institutional investors—Internet Initiative Japan (IIJ) and Dentsu Soken—have delivered earnings results that contain powerful read-across signals for the entire sector. The core thesis is this: demand for network infrastructure construction is accelerating on a structural basis, and this demand is creating a tailwind that will lift multiple players, but the distribution of benefits will be uneven. Companies with high exposure to government and financial sector network projects, proprietary software capabilities, and pricing power in system integration will outperform. Meanwhile, the automotive-facing segments of the IT services market are showing signs of strain that may deepen before they improve.

The timing of this insight matters because the market is currently focused on aggregate earnings growth and margin trajectories. The conventional narrative is that Japanese IT services firms are benefiting from digital transformation spending and a tight labor market that supports pricing. This is true but incomplete. The real story is about where demand is concentrated and which business models are positioned to capture it. IIJ and Dentsu Soken, despite their differences in focus, both point to the same conclusion: the network layer of the economy is being rebuilt, and the companies that build and maintain it are entering a multi-year upcycle.

This article draws on a recent research report that analyzes these two companies in detail. It does not summarize the report. Instead, it extracts the strategic logic, extends it into second-order implications, and identifies the analytical gaps that investors should focus on. The goal is to provide a decision framework, not a data dump.

## IIJ’s Order Surge in System Integration Confirms That Network Infrastructure Construction Is Entering a Structural Growth Phase, Not a Cyclical One

IIJ’s system integration segment, which focuses on network infrastructure, reported a 51% year-on-year increase in orders in the fourth quarter of its fiscal year ending March 2026. This is not a one-off spike. The order backlog at the end of the fiscal year stood at ¥158.9 billion, up 37.7% year-on-year. The company itself attributes this to structural demand from government agencies and the financial sector, and it expects the trend to continue into fiscal 2027.

The significance of this data point extends far beyond IIJ. Network infrastructure construction is a bellwether for broader IT investment because it precedes and enables virtually every other form of digital transformation. Before a company can deploy cloud applications, implement AI workloads, or modernize its data architecture, it must have the underlying network capacity and security architecture in place. The fact that IIJ is seeing this level of demand suggests that the Japanese economy is in the early stages of a network modernization cycle that will sustain IT services spending for several years.

The read-across for larger players is direct. BIPROGY, which has a high sales weighting in network infrastructure, is the most obvious beneficiary. NEC and Fujitsu, both of which have significant network and communications businesses, should also see tailwinds. But the implication is not simply that these companies will see higher revenue. The more important point is about margin structure. As IIJ noted, the gross margin in its system integration business is expected to improve as the weighting of maintenance business increases. This is a classic software-like transition: upfront construction work generates recurring maintenance revenue over time. Companies that can capture both the construction and the maintenance phases will see improving margin profiles.

This raises an open question: how much of this demand is being driven by government digitalization initiatives versus private sector modernization? The report notes that IIJ’s orders include brisk inquiries from government agencies, but it does not break down the proportion. If the bulk is government-driven, the sustainability of the cycle depends on fiscal policy continuity. If private sector demand is equally strong, the cycle is more resilient.

## Dentsu Soken’s Strong Start in Financial Solutions and Proprietary Software Shows That Diversification Away from Automotive Is Paying Off, but the Auto Weakness Is a Warning Signal for the Sector

Dentsu Soken reported a 14% year-on-year increase in operating profit in the first quarter of its fiscal year ending December 2026, with orders up 20%. The strength came from financial solutions, where megabank development projects are driving demand, and from proprietary software products such as STRAVIS and POSITIVE, which are gaining traction with trading companies and electric power companies.

The contrast with the manufacturing solutions business is stark. Dentsu Soken explicitly noted that investment appetite among automotive customers lacks strength due to reciprocal tariffs and poor business performance. The company is shifting its focus from automotive to semiconductors. This is a strategic adaptation, but it also serves as a warning for the broader IT services sector. If a mid-tier player with a relatively diversified customer base is seeing weakness in automotive, the exposure of larger players to the automotive sector deserves scrutiny.

The proprietary software angle is particularly important. Dentsu Soken’s gross margin improved on growth in custom system development and proprietary software. This is the holy grail for IT services companies: moving from low-margin body shopping to higher-margin productized services. The report does not specify the exact margin differential, but the implication is clear. Companies that can develop and sell proprietary software alongside their system integration services will command higher valuations and more predictable earnings.

The open question here is whether Dentsu Soken’s success with STRAVIS and POSITIVE is replicable. Proprietary software is notoriously difficult to scale in the IT services industry because it requires a combination of domain expertise, long development cycles, and customer trust. The report does not address whether these products are gaining market share or simply benefiting from a few large contracts. Investors should watch the renewal rates and customer concentration of these software products closely.

## The Pricing Power Dynamic Differs Sharply Between System Integration and Network Services, Creating a Divergence in Margin Trajectories

One of the most analytically useful distinctions in the report is the contrast between IIJ’s system integration business and its network services business. In system integration, passing on cost increases through price hikes is relatively straightforward. In network services, it takes time due to contract timing. This is not a minor operational detail. It is a structural difference that determines margin behavior.

IIJ’s network services business has been under pressure, with gross margins declining. The company expects profitability to bottom out in fiscal 2027, supported by price hikes for corporate customers and cost controls. But the key constraint is that the MVNO and MVNE businesses, which are price-sensitive and competitive, cannot support price increases. IIJ is gaining share in these segments on the back of low prices, which is a double-edged sword: volume growth at the expense of margin.

The implication for the sector is that companies with a high proportion of network services revenue will face margin headwinds that are not easily resolved. Companies with a high proportion of system integration revenue, particularly in network infrastructure, will see margin tailwinds. This is a sector-level divergence that stock pickers can exploit.

The report also highlights a secondary but important factor: rising memory prices. IIJ noted that components for servers and network equipment have become somewhat difficult to procure, and it started front-loading procurement in January. This is a supply chain signal that could affect the entire sector. If component shortages persist, project timelines may slip, and margins may be squeezed for companies that cannot pass on costs. The report does not quantify this risk, but it is a variable worth monitoring.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Order Backlog and the Risk of a Demand Pull-Forward

The most significant analytical gap in the report is the question of sustainability. IIJ’s order backlog is growing at nearly 38% year-on-year, and Dentsu Soken’s backlog is up 33%. These are impressive numbers, but they raise a critical question: is this a genuine structural increase in demand, or is it a pull-forward of projects that would have been ordered over the next two to three years?

The report does not provide data on the average duration of projects in the backlog or the cancellation rate. It also does not address whether customers are ordering early due to fears of component shortages or capacity constraints. If the latter is true, the backlog may normalize once supply conditions improve, leading to a slowdown in new orders.

Another unresolved question is the impact of macroeconomic uncertainty. The report notes that Dentsu Soken has not seen any signs of a decline in customer investment appetite due to the situation in the Middle East. But it does not address the potential impact of a global recession, a sharp yen appreciation, or a prolonged trade dispute. The automotive weakness that Dentsu Soken cited is partly tariff-driven, and tariffs are inherently unpredictable.

Investors who rely solely on the order backlog as a leading indicator may be overestimating the durability of the current cycle. The report provides a snapshot of strong demand, but it does not provide a framework for stress-testing that demand under different macroeconomic scenarios.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Focus on Three Variables to Differentiate Winners from Losers

Based on the insights from this report, investors should evaluate Japanese IT services companies along three dimensions:

First, exposure to network infrastructure construction. Companies with a high and growing share of revenue from network-related system integration are likely to benefit from the structural demand that IIJ’s order book confirms. This includes not only pure-play network integrators but also larger players with significant network divisions. The key metric to watch is the order backlog growth rate in the network segment, not the company-wide backlog.

Second, the mix of proprietary software versus custom development. Dentsu Soken’s margin improvement is directly tied to its proprietary software products. Companies that can demonstrate a growing contribution from software products or platforms will likely see margin expansion and valuation re-rating. The risk is that many IT services companies claim to have proprietary software, but few have products with meaningful market share.

Third, the ability to pass on cost increases. The report makes clear that system integration businesses have more pricing power than network services businesses. Investors should segment a company’s revenue by the ease of cost pass-through. Companies with high exposure to long-term network services contracts may face margin compression even if revenue grows.

These three variables are not exhaustive, but they are mutually exclusive and collectively cover the most important drivers of earnings quality in the current environment. Investors who apply this framework will be better positioned to distinguish between companies that are riding a cyclical wave and those that are building structural competitive advantage.

# Inflation Is No Longer Transitory: Three Structural Forces Are Reshaping the Price Landscape Through 2027

The April 2026 inflation data marks a critical inflection point that most market participants have not yet fully priced in. Core PCE inflation accelerated to 3.29% year over year, while headline PCE hit 3.77% — levels that would have triggered aggressive policy responses in any other cycle. But the composition of this inflation tells a far more troubling story than the headline numbers suggest. This is not a temporary overshoot driven by base effects or seasonal adjustments. Three structural forces — the Iran war's commodity shock, AI-related demand for electronics and energy, and the lingering passthrough of tariff costs — are converging to create a persistent inflation regime that will outlast any single policy intervention.

The critical insight that most observers miss is this: the traditional decomposition of inflation into cyclical versus transitory components has broken down. What we are witnessing is the simultaneous arrival of multiple supply-side shocks that interact with each other in ways that standard macroeconomic models cannot capture. The war in Iran has raised oil prices by roughly 50% since February, but the secondary effects on fertilizer, aluminum, and chemical prices are propagating through supply chains in ways that will take 12 to 18 months to fully materialize in consumer prices. Meanwhile, the AI investment cycle is creating entirely new inflation channels — from memory chip prices to electricity demand — that have no historical precedent. And tariffs, which many assumed would fade as an inflation driver, have achieved a 90% passthrough rate to consumer prices after 13 months, embedding themselves permanently into the price structure.

The timing matters enormously. The Federal Reserve has been signaling a path toward rate cuts throughout 2026, but the April data makes that trajectory untenable. Core PCE inflation net of tariff effects stands at roughly 2.4% — still above the 2% target even when you strip out the most obvious policy-driven price increases. When you add back the tariff effects, the war effects, and the AI-related effects, you get an inflation picture that is not just sticky but accelerating. The question is no longer whether the Fed will cut rates in 2026, but whether they will need to raise them.

## The Iran War Has Created a Multi-Layered Commodity Shock That Will Persist Through 2027, Not Just an Oil Price Spike

The conventional wisdom treats the Iran war as an oil story, and oil prices have indeed surged from $71 per barrel in February to $117 in April, settling around $92 today. But this framing misses the more dangerous transmission mechanism. The war has disrupted production and shipping of a much broader set of Gulf exports, including sulfur (up 103% since the war began), ammonia (up 66%), methanol (up 39%), and aluminum (up 19%). These are not niche commodities — they are critical inputs into fertilizers, industrial chemicals, and manufacturing processes that touch every sector of the economy.

The fertilizer channel is particularly concerning because it creates a delayed inflation effect that most forecasters are underestimating. Higher fertilizer costs will boost food prices by roughly 1.0% this year, which translates to about 0.07 percentage points on headline inflation. But that is just the direct effect. The secondary effect flows through restaurant prices, which are captured in core PCE inflation. The report estimates this will add 0.05 to 0.10 percentage points to core inflation. When you compound these effects over multiple quarters, the total contribution from the war to core PCE inflation this year reaches roughly 0.35 percentage points from energy alone, plus additional contributions from food and industrial commodities.

The critical question that the report leaves open is how persistent these effects will be. Oil strategists expect Brent crude to decline to $90 per barrel by the fourth quarter of 2026, but they explicitly note that risks are two-sided. If supply losses prove more persistent than expected, oil prices could remain elevated well into 2027. More importantly, the non-oil commodity channels — fertilizers, chemicals, metals — may take longer to normalize because they involve different production and shipping infrastructure. The war's inflation effects may peak in 2026, but they will not disappear in 2027.

## AI Infrastructure Demand Is Creating a New and Poorly Measured Inflation Channel That Disproportionately Affects PCE

The AI-related inflation story is the most analytically interesting and the most misunderstood. It operates through three distinct channels, each with different measurement implications and different policy relevance. First, strong demand for AI infrastructure has driven up prices for key electronics inputs, particularly digital memory and batteries. The producer price index for electronic components and accessories increased by 20% year over year in April. This is not a speculative bubble — it reflects genuine physical demand for the hardware required to build and operate AI data centers.

Second, AI-related spending has put upward pressure on software prices. Several companies have raised software prices in recent months, and the report identifies a critical measurement problem: software and accessories are not quality-adjusted in either PCE or CPI. This means that when software companies add AI features and raise prices, the statistical agencies capture the entire increase as pure price inflation rather than as a quality improvement. The measured inflation is real, but it may overstate the true cost increase for consumers who are getting more functionality for their money.

Third, data center electricity demand is boosting power prices. The report estimates this will add 0.1 to 0.2 percentage points to headline PCE inflation over the next few years. This channel is particularly important because it creates a self-reinforcing cycle: higher electricity prices make AI operations more expensive, which may slow investment, but in the near term, the demand pressure is unambiguous.

The most striking finding in this section is the asymmetric impact on PCE versus CPI. The software and accessories component receives roughly 30 times the weight in PCE compared to CPI. This means that the same price increases in software show up as a much larger inflation signal in the Fed's preferred PCE measure. The report suspects this reflects an overstatement of software sales to retail customers in the PCE source data, but regardless of the cause, the implication is clear: the Fed's preferred inflation gauge is being disproportionately affected by AI-related price increases that may not be fully real.

The combined effect is substantial. The report expects higher electronics and digital input prices to boost year-over-year core PCE inflation by another 0.3 percentage points, making a peak contribution of 0.6 percentage points in the third quarter of 2026. For core CPI, the effect is only about 0.1 percentage points. This divergence between PCE and CPI creates a strategic communication challenge for the Fed, which targets PCE but communicates to a public that experiences CPI.

## Tariff Passthrough Has Reached 90% After 13 Months, Embedding a Permanent Layer of Core Goods Inflation

The tariff story has evolved from a temporary policy shock into a structural feature of the inflation landscape. The report estimates that tariffs have boosted current year-over-year core PCE inflation by 0.9 percentage points and will contribute 0.1 percentage points at the end of 2026. The passthrough rate of 90% after 13 months is remarkably high — it suggests that companies have been able to pass on almost all of their tariff costs to consumers, which implies pricing power that many economists did not expect.

The implication for the inflation outlook is profound. Core goods prices increased by 0.30% in April and 2.8% over the prior year — the fastest year-over-year pace since December 2022. To put this in perspective, in 2018 when overall core PCE inflation was running at roughly 2%, core goods prices were declining at an average annual pace of -0.5%. The structural shift from goods deflation to goods inflation represents a permanent change in the inflation regime.

The report expects core goods inflation to decelerate from 2.8% year over year in April to 1.8% by December 2026 and 0.5% by December 2027, as tariff cost passthrough comes to an end. But this forecast assumes no new tariffs and no escalation of trade tensions. Given the current geopolitical environment, that assumption is far from certain. If tariffs are expanded or increased, the goods inflation channel could reignite.

The more important structural question is whether the tariff effects will ever fully unwind. Even if tariff passthrough ends, the price level has been permanently raised. Consumers are paying 0.9 percentage points more for core goods than they would have without tariffs. This does not show up in year-over-year inflation comparisons after the first year, but it represents a permanent reduction in purchasing power. The transition from goods deflation to goods inflation also changes the dynamics of the broader inflation cycle, because goods prices have historically been the main source of disinflationary pressure in the economy.

## Inflation Expectations Are Breaking Anchoring at the Long End, Creating a Self-Fulfilling Dynamic That Policy Cannot Easily Reverse

The most concerning data in the report may not be the actual inflation numbers but the inflation expectations data. Short-term inflation expectations have increased notably since the start of the Iran war, with the University of Michigan one-year-ahead measure rising to 4.8% and the New York Fed measure rising to 3.6%. These are elevated but not alarming — short-term expectations are always more volatile and respond to actual inflation prints.

The alarming data is at the long end. The University of Michigan measure of long-term inflation expectations over the next 5 to 10 years increased by 0.4 percentage points to 3.9%. This is a critical threshold. When long-term expectations become unanchored, they create a self-fulfilling dynamic: workers demand higher wages, companies raise prices in anticipation of higher costs, and inflation becomes embedded in the economic structure. The Fed's credibility is built on its ability to keep long-term expectations anchored, and 3.9% is dangerously close to the level where that credibility begins to erode.

The New York Fed measure tells a different story, declining slightly to 3.0% over the five-year horizon. This divergence between the two surveys creates analytical uncertainty. Which one is right? The University of Michigan survey tends to be more sensitive to actual inflation experiences, while the New York Fed survey is more influenced by financial market conditions. The truth may lie somewhere in between, but even the lower New York Fed number is above the 2.5% level that the Fed would consider consistent with its 2% target.

Financial market-implied expectations for annualized headline CPI inflation over the next few years have increased sharply since the start of the war. This matters because market-implied expectations incorporate forward-looking information that surveys cannot capture. If bond markets are pricing in higher long-term inflation, that will feed into higher borrowing costs for consumers and businesses, creating a tightening of financial conditions that acts as an additional drag on economic growth.

## The Report's Forecast Leaves Unanswered Questions About the Path of Services Inflation and the Durability of Disinflation

The report's central forecast calls for core PCE inflation to decline to 2.8% by December 2026 and core CPI to decline to 2.5%. This forecast is based on the assumption that tariff effects fade, energy prices moderate, and AI-related effects peak in the third quarter of 2026. But within this forecast, there are several unresolved analytical questions that the report does not fully address.

The first is the path of services inflation. Core services prices increased by 0.22% in April, down from 0.32% in March, but the year-over-year pace remains at 3.4%. This compares to an average pace of 2.5% in 2018, when overall core PCE was roughly 2%. The gap between current services inflation and its pre-pandemic trend is 0.9 percentage points. Closing that gap requires either a significant slowdown in wage growth or a productivity acceleration that is not visible in the data. The report does not provide a clear mechanism for how services inflation will return to trend.

The second unresolved question is the role of housing. The April data included two months' worth of rent and owners' equivalent rent inflation that went unmeasured in October 2025 because of the federal government shutdown. This created a temporary boost to the April numbers, but the underlying trend in housing inflation remains unclear. Rent inflation has been decelerating in the private sector data, but the official measures have been slow to capture this. If housing inflation continues to moderate, it could provide significant disinflationary momentum. If it stabilizes at current levels, the path to 2% becomes much harder.

The third question is the interaction between the three structural forces. The report treats the war effects, AI effects, and tariff effects as additive. But they may be multiplicative. Higher energy prices increase the cost of manufacturing electronics, which amplifies the AI-related inflation channel. Higher commodity prices increase the cost of imported goods, which amplifies the tariff passthrough effect. The total inflation impact may be larger than the sum of its parts.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the New Inflation Regime

For investors and business leaders, the April inflation data requires a fundamental reassessment of how to position portfolios and make strategic decisions. The old framework — in which inflation is a cyclical phenomenon driven by the output gap and labor market tightness — is no longer sufficient. A new framework must account for structural supply-side shocks that are persistent, interacting, and poorly measured.

The first principle of the new framework is to distinguish between inflation that is reversible and inflation that is permanent. Tariff effects are permanent in the sense that they represent a one-time price level shift. They will drop out of year-over-year comparisons after 12 months, but the price level will not decline. War effects are potentially reversible if supply normalizes, but the timing is highly uncertain. AI effects are the most ambiguous — they may represent genuine price increases or they may reflect measurement problems that will be corrected over time.

The second principle is to focus on the divergence between PCE and CPI. The Fed targets PCE, but the economy and financial markets respond to CPI. When these two measures diverge significantly, as they are doing now, the Fed faces a communication challenge that can create policy errors. Investors should monitor both measures and pay particular attention to the software and accessories component of PCE, which is driving much of the divergence.

The third principle is to recognize that the inflation outlook is path-dependent. The war could escalate or de-escalate. Tariffs could expand or contract. AI investment could accelerate or slow. Each of these scenarios has dramatically different implications for the inflation path. The correct response is not to bet on a single scenario but to build portfolios that are resilient across multiple scenarios. This means maintaining exposure to real assets, inflation-linked bonds, and commodities while being underweight nominal fixed income.

The fourth principle is to watch the long-term inflation expectations data as the most important leading indicator. If the University of Michigan measure remains above 3.5% for another quarter, the Fed will be forced to respond, regardless of what the actual inflation data shows. The cost of unanchored expectations is higher than the cost of over-tightening, and the Fed knows this.

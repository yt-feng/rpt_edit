# The AI Supply Chain Is Rewriting the Rules of China’s Property Recovery, and Investors Must Focus on Tier 1 Cities

For nearly five years, the China property sector has been trapped in a negative feedback loop: falling home prices weakened industrial enterprises, which led to layoffs and lower incomes, which further depressed housing demand. Each attempted recovery was met with a fresh wave of disappointment. That pattern is now breaking, but not because of policy stimulus or a broad-based rebound. The catalyst is something structurally different: the rapid expansion of the AI supply chain is generating concentrated industrial profits and wealth effects that are flowing directly into tier 1 city housing markets.

The argument is not that the entire property sector is turning around. It is that a narrow, powerful set of economic forces is creating a self-reinforcing recovery in Beijing, Shanghai, and a handful of other Cities, while the rest of the market remains under pressure. For investors, the strategic question is no longer whether to buy property stocks, but which ones are positioned to capture this geographically specific, profit-driven demand. The evidence from industrial profit data, corporate headquarters mapping, and vacancy rate trends suggests that the recovery this time is real, but it is also highly selective.

This is not a repeat of past cycles driven by mortgage rate cuts or purchase restrictions. It is a recovery rooted in the balance sheets of China’s most productive companies, and it demands a fundamentally different investment framework.

## The Negative Feedback Loop Is Unwinding Because Rising Industrial Profits Are Driving Housing Demand, Not Policy

The historical relationship between industrial enterprise profits and tier 1 city property prices is not a statistical curiosity. It is a structural link that reflects the deep interdependence between corporate profitability and housing demand in China’s most economically dynamic Cities. When companies make money, they hire more people, pay higher salaries, and generate the confidence that leads to home purchases. When they struggle, the reverse happens.

For the past several years, that relationship worked in the wrong direction. Declining property prices hurt industrial enterprises through the long supply chain connected to real estate, leading to layoffs and lower income expectations, which further suppressed housing demand. The correlation coefficient of 0.63 between industrial profits and tier 1 property prices was a measure of how tightly the two were locked in a downward spiral.

What has changed is the source of profit growth. In the first quarter of 2026, industrial enterprise profits grew by 15% year-over-year, driven overwhelmingly by AI-related sectors. The manufacture of computers, communication equipment, and other electronic equipment alone contributed 8.2 percentage points of that total growth, or more than half. Non-ferrous metals and chemicals added another 8 percentage points combined. This is not a broad cyclical upturn. It is a concentrated surge in profitability from the sectors that are most integrated into the global AI supply chain.

The mechanism is straightforward. Positive producer price inflation, which turned positive in March 2026 for the first time since September 2022, signals that pricing power is returning to industrial companies. Export growth, particularly in technology products, accelerated to 14% year-over-year in April 2026. These are not temporary factors. They reflect China’s deepening role in the AI supply chain, a structural shift that will persist regardless of short-term policy adjustments.

For property investors, the implication is clear. The demand driver has shifted from policy stimulus to corporate profitability. That makes the recovery more sustainable in the Cities where profitable companies are concentrated, but it also means that Cities without exposure to these sectors will continue to lag.

## The Geographic Concentration of AI Wealth Means Only a Handful of Cities Will Drive the Recovery

The profit data tells only half the story. The other half is where the money is going. By mapping the headquarters of companies that have outperformed in the stock market over the past 12 months, a clear geographic pattern emerges. There are 514 listed companies with a market capitalization above 10 billion renminbi whose share prices have increased by more than 50% in the past year. The vast majority are headquartered in just five Cities: Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Suzhou, and Dongguan.

Beijing alone accounts for 43 such companies, Shanghai for 50, and Shenzhen for 48. The market capitalization of these high-growth stocks in Beijing exceeds 8.9 trillion renminbi. In Shanghai, it is over 4.6 trillion. These are not small companies with speculative valuations. They are large, profitable enterprises that are generating real wealth for their employees, executives, and shareholders.

The wealth effect from rising share prices and profitability is not evenly distributed across China. It is concentrated in the Cities where these companies are based. And the data on secondary housing transactions confirms the connection. Cities with the highest concentration of high-growth companies also show the strongest growth in secondary transaction volumes year-to-date. Beijing and Shanghai lead, followed by Suzhou and Dongguan. Cities like Guangzhou, Hangzhou, and Chengdu, which have less exposure to AI-related sectors, show much weaker or even negative transaction growth.

This is the most important insight for investors. The recovery is not going to lift all boats. It is going to lift the boats that are docked in the ports where the AI supply chain is anchored. Tier 1 Cities, particularly Beijing and Shanghai, are the primary beneficiaries. Tier 2 Cities with strong tech exposure, like Suzhou, will also participate. But the broader market, especially lower-tier Cities without industrial profit growth, will remain under pressure.

## Falling Vacancy Rates in Suburban Districts Confirm That Shadow Inventory Is Being Digested

One of the most persistent risks to the property market over the past three years has been the overhang of shadow inventory, vacant properties that were completed during the boom years and slowly leaked into the rental and secondary markets, suppressing prices. This inventory was especially concentrated in the suburban districts of tier 1 Cities, where speculative demand and new home completions were highest.

The market has consistently underestimated this supply overhang because it is not captured in official data. Vacant properties do not appear in new home sales figures, but they become visible when they are listed for rent or resale. The result was a steady drip of supply that kept downward pressure on prices even as new home sales slowed.

That dynamic is now reversing. Using the percentage of bare shell listings on property portals as a proxy for vacancy rates, the data shows a decline of 0.6 to 10.1 percentage points in suburban district vacancy rates across all four tier 1 Cities and Hangzhou between June 2023 and April 2026. This is not a marginal improvement. It is a meaningful reduction in the supply overhang that has been weighing on the market.

Two factors explain this improvement. First, new home completions in these districts have declined significantly over the past one to two years, reducing the flow of new vacant units. Second, the existing stock of vacant properties has been gradually absorbed through rental and secondary market transactions. The properties did not disappear. They were occupied, either by renters or by buyers who purchased them from the secondary market.

Looking ahead, the supply outlook is even more favorable. The sUBStantial decline in new home starts and land sales over the past three years means that the pipeline of future supply is constrained. Government social housing construction is also being scaled back. The combination of lower new supply and ongoing absorption of existing inventory suggests that the supply-demand balance in tier 1 Cities will continue to improve.

## The Valuation Case for Tier 1 City Exposed Developers Is Compelling, but Requires a Selective Approach

The sector has already outperformed, but the valuation argument is not exhausted. The current price-to-book value for the sector is 0.66 times, compared to the 15-year average of 0.85 times. A reversion to that average implies approximately 30% upside from current levels. But that is a sector-level average, and the dispersion between companies is wide.

The key differentiator is exposure to tier 1 Cities. Developers with high tier 1 exposure, defined as approximately 41% of their net asset value in tier 1 Cities, are positioned to capture the profit-driven demand that is emerging. Companies with exposure below 25% will benefit less, and may continue to face headwinds from weaker markets in lower-tier Cities.

The report upgrades several developers to Buy, with price target increases ranging from 22% to 81%. The largest increase is for a developer with a 56% implied upside from current levels, driven by its high tier 1 exposure and the potential for asset securitization to unlock value. Another key upgrade is for a real estate brokerage platform that benefits from increased secondary transaction volumes in tier 1 Cities, with a 20% upside to the new price target.

The earnings revisions are not uniform. Some developers see significant upward revisions to 2027 earnings estimates, while others face downward revisions in 2026. This reflects the uneven nature of the recovery. Companies with high tier 1 exposure are seeing improving fundamentals, while those with exposure to weaker markets are still working through legacy issues.

For investors, the decision framework is clear. The recovery is real but narrow. The investment case rests on identifying which developers have the highest concentration of assets and sales in the Cities where AI-driven wealth is accumulating. A simple screen for tier 1 city exposure is a good starting point, but it is not sufficient. Investors also need to assess the quality of those assets, the balance sheet strength of the developer, and the potential for asset recycling through securitization or other mechanisms.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: How Durable Is the AI Profit Cycle, and Can It Spread Beyond Tier 1 Cities?

The report makes a compelling case that the current recovery is different from previous false starts, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most critical is the durability of the AI-driven profit cycle. The current surge in industrial profits is being driven by a combination of factors: AI investment, commodity price increases, capacity reduction, and export growth. Some of these factors are structural, but others may be cyclical or even temporary.

The AI supply chain is undoubtedly a long-term growth story, but it is also volatile. Semiconductor cycles, trade policy shifts, and technology sUBStitution could all affect the profitability of Chinese AI-related companies. If profit growth slows or reverses, the property recovery in tier 1 Cities would lose its primary driver. The report acknowledges the correlation between industrial profits and property prices, but it does not model the sensitivity of property prices to different profit growth scenarios.

A second unanswered question is whether the recovery can spread beyond tier 1 Cities. The report is explicit that tier 2 city prices are expected to decline by 5% in 2026 and only stabilize in 2027. But it does not explore the conditions under which the recovery could broaden. Would a sustained period of tier 1 price increases eventually pull up tier 2 markets through relocation or spillover effects? Or are the structural differences between tier 1 and tier 2 Cities too large to bridge?

Third, the report does not fully address the role of policy. It argues that the current recovery is profit-driven rather than policy-driven, but policy still matters. The government has tools to support or suppress housing demand, including mortgage rates, purchase restrictions, and social housing supply. If the recovery in tier 1 Cities accelerates too quickly, policymakers may intervene to cool the market. The report does not discuss this risk.

These are not criticisms of the analysis. They are the natural limitations of any forward-looking research. But they highlight the importance of monitoring the underlying drivers of the recovery rather than simply extrapolating current trends.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Focus on Profit Exposure, Not Policy Exposure

The report provides the building blocks for a new investment framework for China property. The old framework was based on policy cycles: buy when the government eases, sell when it tightens. That framework has failed repeatedly because it ignored the underlying economic reality that policy alone cannot create sustainable demand.

The new framework should be based on profit exposure. The starting point is identifying the Cities where industrial profits are growing, driven by structural factors like AI supply chain integration. The next step is identifying the developers with the highest concentration of assets and sales in those Cities. The final step is assessing the quality of those assets and the financial health of the developer.

Within this framework, the key metrics to track are not policy announcements but industrial profit data, PPI trends, export growth in technology products, and vacancy rates in suburban districts. These are the leading indicators that will signal whether the recovery is broadening or narrowing.

The report identifies several developers that score well on this framework, but investors should conduct their own analysis. The geographic concentration of assets is a starting point, but it is not the only factor. Balance sheet strength, land bank quality, and the ability to generate cash flow from asset recycling are all important considerations.

The most important insight from the report is that the recovery is not going to be uniform. It is going to be concentrated in the Cities and developers that are connected to the most dynamic part of the Chinese economy. Investors who understand this will be positioned to capture the upside. Those who continue to treat China property as a single, policy-driven sector will miss the opportunity.

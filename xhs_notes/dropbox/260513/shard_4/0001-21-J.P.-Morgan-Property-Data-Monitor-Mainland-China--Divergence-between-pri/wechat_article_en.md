# China's Property Market Is No Longer a Single Story: The Divergence Between Primary and Secondary Sales Demands a Two-Track Investment Strategy

The most important data point in the latest weekly property monitor is not the headline number. It is the divergence. In mainland China, primary sales registrations across 60 Cities dropped 7% year-on-year last week, while secondary sales in 12 major Cities surged 16% year-on-year. This is not a statistical anomaly. It is a structural signal that the market is repricing risk and preference in real time. Investors who treat "Chinese real estate" as a single asset class are making a category error.

The argument of this article is straightforward: the Chinese property market has entered a two-track equilibrium where primary and secondary markets are driven by fundamentally different forces. The primary market remains constrained by developer balance sheet concerns, policy uncertainty, and a buyer preference for completed stock. The secondary market, by contrast, is being lifted by genuine end-user demand, price discovery, and a gradual restoration of confidence in tier-1 Cities. Hong Kong, meanwhile, presents a third and more coherent story: a synchronized recovery in both primary and secondary markets, supported by aggressive developer pricing and a clear policy backdrop.

Why now matters is because the data is moving past the noise of seasonal distortions and policy announcements. The Golden Week effect has been absorbed. The policy easing in late April is now reflected in asking prices. What remains is a cleaner read on underlying demand. And that read tells us that the recovery is real but uneven, that it is concentrated in specific Cities and asset types, and that the investment implications are far more nuanced than a simple "buy the sector" trade.

## The Secondary Market Is Leading the Recovery, But the Primary Market Is Trapped by Its Own Structure

The most telling leading indicator in the report is the Centraline tier-1 Cities' secondary asking price index, which rose from 19.0 to 19.3 week-on-week. Shenzhen led the increase, jumping 1.0 point to 28.1, a direct response to the late-April policy easing. This is not a speculative spike. The Centaline manager confidence index held steady at 57, a level that historically correlates with stable to rising transaction volumes. The combination of rising asking prices and stable manager sentiment suggests that the secondary market is experiencing genuine demand, not just a policy-driven sugar rush.

The secondary sales data supports this interpretation. Twelve-city secondary sales registrations rose 16% year-on-year, and year-to-date they are up 3%. Shanghai is up 9%, Beijing 4%, and Shenzhen 1%. These are not explosive numbers, but they are consistent and broad-based. They indicate that end-users are transacting. They are buying existing homes, not new ones. This is a rational response to a market where developers are still under financial pressure and where the risk of delayed delivery or quality issues remains elevated.

The primary market tells a different story. The 7% year-on-year decline in 60-city primary sales is not just a blip. It follows a week of +1% growth, suggesting that momentum is fragile. Tier-1 and tier-2 Cities outperformed with only a 3% decline, but that is cold comfort. The year-to-date figure for 20 Cities shows a 10% drop. Developers are struggling to convert policy interest into signed contracts. The reason is structural: buyers in the primary market are making a bet on a developer's ability to deliver. In the secondary market, the product is visible, the title is clear, and the transaction is immediate.

The so-what for investors is clear. The secondary market is the better proxy for genuine demand. It is where price discovery is happening. It is where the recovery is real. The primary market, by contrast, is a lagging indicator that will only improve when developer balance sheets are visibly repaired and when buyers no longer fear that their deposit will be tied up in a stalled project. That day is not here yet.

## Hong Kong Is the Cleanest Recovery Story in the Region, But It Carries Its Own Risks of Overshoot

Hong Kong's property market is outperforming mainland China on almost every metric, and the data is unambiguous. The home price index rose 0.2% week-on-week and is up 8.0% year-to-date. It is now just 2% away from the 2026 forecast of 10-15% appreciation. The Centa Valuation Index rose to 89.3, well above the 60 threshold that signals banks are revising up property valuations. The Centa Salesman Index remains elevated at 69.7, above the 50 level that historically predicts rising prices.

But the most striking data point is the primary market activity. Over the weekend, 560 primary transactions were recorded, the highest in 2.5 years. Three new launches achieved 100% sell-through rates. SHKP's Lime Spark in Tseun Wan sold 154 units at an average selling price 14% above comparable secondary units. Henderson's Highwood Phase 2 in To Kwa Wan sold 150 units at a 14% premium. One Victoria Cove Phase 3 sold 130 units at a 20% premium. Even PORTO in Ap Lei Chau, which sold 51% of its first batch, achieved a 31% premium over secondary.

These are not fire sales. Developers are pricing above secondary levels and still achieving full absorption. This tells us that demand is not just recovering; it is robust. The premium over secondary is a signal that buyers see value in new supply, either because of quality, location, or the absence of the delivery risk that plagues the mainland primary market. The 100% sell-through rates suggest that developers have priced accurately and that the market has cleared.

The risk, however, is that the market is moving too fast. The home price index is already close to the full-year forecast, and the leading indicators are all pointing upward. If sentiment continues to improve, prices could overshoot. The report notes that secondary transactions in the top 35 estates fell 24% week-on-week, even though they rose 22% year-on-year. This weekly decline could be noise, or it could be an early sign that affordability constraints are starting to bite. Investors should watch the secondary transaction volumes closely. If they start to decline while prices continue to rise, that is a classic divergence that precedes a correction.

## The Credit Market Is Picking Winners Based on Asset Quality, Not Developer Size

The credit view section of the report offers a different lens through which to understand the recovery. The JACI China HY Property Index rose 1.7% last week, outperforming the broader China HY index by 100 basis points. Year-to-date, the property index is up 1.3%. This is not a broad-based rally. It is a selective one.

The report highlights two specific credit stories that are instructive. First, Swire Properties' 1Q26 data showed Hong Kong retail tenant sales growth accelerating from 6% year-on-year in 4Q25 to 13% in 1Q26. For Hong Kong office, Swire expects negative rental reversion to narrow at Pacific Place in the second half of 2026, driven by increased leasing activity and stabilizing office rents in Central. The credit analyst prefers Hysan's 7.2% perpetual bond as the best way to play the Hong Kong commercial property recovery.

Second, China Resources Land is planning to inject RMB 10-15 billion of shopping mall assets into public REITs in 2026, with a target of issuing RMB 60 billion in public REITs by 2030. Approximately 60% of the proceeds will be used for construction and maintenance, and 30% for M&A. This is a strategic pivot that transforms CR Land from a pure developer into an asset manager. The REIT channel provides a new source of funding that is not dependent on the primary sales market. For developers with high-quality investment properties, this is a structural advantage.

The implication is that credit investors are no longer buying the sector. They are buying specific stories: high-quality commercial assets in Hong Kong, and developers with the scale and asset base to execute a REIT strategy. The market is rewarding asset quality and operational capability, not balance sheet size or historical reputation. This is a mature market behavior, and it suggests that the recovery will be uneven even within the same sector.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Secondary Market Recovery and the Role of Policy

For all its richness, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved. The first is the sustainability of the secondary market recovery in mainland China. The data shows that secondary sales are up 16% year-on-year, but the base effect matters. Last year's numbers were depressed. The year-to-date growth of 3% is more modest. The question is whether the current volume can be maintained as the year progresses, or whether it will fade as the policy tailwinds diminish.

The second question is the role of policy. The report notes that the late-April policy easing boosted Shenzhen's asking price index, but it does not specify what the policy was or whether it can be replicated in other Cities. The market is now pricing in further easing, but the central government has been careful to avoid a repeat of the 2015-2016 stimulus that led to a speculative bubble. The policy path is uncertain, and the market's sensitivity to policy announcements is high.

The third question is the interaction between the primary and secondary markets. If secondary prices continue to rise, will that eventually pull primary buyers back into the market? Or will the gap between primary and secondary prices widen, creating a two-tier market that persists for years? The report does not provide a clear answer, and the data is not yet conclusive.

These are not gaps in the analysis. They are genuine uncertainties that any investor must grapple with. The report provides the data and the framework, but the judgment call on sustainability and policy is left to the reader. That is as it should be.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask Before Allocating to Chinese Property

Based on the report's data and logic, a disciplined investor should ask three questions before making any allocation decision.

First, which market are you investing in? Mainland China and Hong Kong are not the same story. Hong Kong is in a synchronized recovery with clear price momentum. Mainland China is a two-track market where the secondary market is recovering and the primary market is still struggling. The investment thesis for Hong Kong is straightforward: buy developers with strong execution and high-quality land banks. The thesis for mainland China is more nuanced: focus on developers with exposure to tier-1 secondary markets and those with REIT-capable asset bases.

Second, what is your time horizon? The leading indicators in the report are short-term: weekly sales, weekly asking prices, and manager sentiment. These are useful for tactical positioning but dangerous for long-term allocation. The year-to-date data is more stable but still inconclusive. If you are a long-term investor, you need to see at least two consecutive quarters of consistent improvement before concluding that the recovery is structural. The current data supports a cautious optimism, not a conviction trade.

Third, what is your risk tolerance? The credit market is already differentiating between winners and losers. The equity market is doing the same: the sector rose 14% last week, but the dispersion was wide. Longfor rose 22%, while Country Garden fell 2%. The outperformers were developers with strong balance sheets and clear strategies. The underperformers were those still struggling with legacy issues. If you are risk-averse, buy the credit of high-quality commercial landlords. If you are risk-tolerant, buy the equity of developers that are leading the recovery. But do not buy the sector indiscriminately.

# The Smart Money in Hong Kong Is No Longer a Single Cohort: Why Combining Southbound and Global Investor Signals Now Outperforms Any Single Group Alone

The Hong Kong equity market has long been defined by a simple narrative: large-cap concentration, thin liquidity in smaller names, and a clear divide between domestic Chinese capital and global institutional flows. That narrative is breaking down. Since the fourth quarter of 2024, a structural shift has taken hold. Liquidity is spreading beyond the mega-caps into a broader set of stocks, return dispersion is rising, and the traditional distinction between "smart money" and "retail flow" has become far more complex. The key insight from a recent quantitative study is this: the most consistent alpha now comes not from tracking any single investor cohort, but from systematically combining signals from both Southbound investors (excluding passive ETF flows) and the best-performing global participants in the Hong Kong market.

This is not a trivial observation. It reflects a fundamental change in who is driving returns and how. Southbound flows have accelerated, but their composition has shifted from purely active trading toward a growing passive component via cross-border ETFs. This means that aggregate Southbound positioning data has become increasingly noisy for stock selection. The raw signal of "Southbound is buying this stock" no longer carries the same edge it once did. However, when one strips out the passive ETF flows, the remaining active Southbound positions still contain genuine idiosyncratic insight. Meanwhile, global participants—particularly the top-performing brokers and custodian banks on a dynamic rolling basis—continue to exhibit distinct preferences and timing. The two groups have low correlation with each other, and their combined signal produces a strategy with an annualized excess return of 12% since 2019, a risk-adjusted return of 1.7, and a maximum drawdown of only 6%.

The timing of this shift matters. The improvement in market breadth and liquidity in Hong Kong has been dramatic. The number of stocks with average daily value traded above USD 5 million has more than doubled, from around 200 in 2024 to approximately 500 by early 2026. This is not a temporary spike. It is a structural expansion of the investable universe, driven by rising trading activity within the Southbound eligible names. For systematic and capacity-sensitive investors, this changes the opportunity set entirely. The market is no longer just a large-cap game. The alpha opportunity has broadened alongside the liquidity.

But this raises a difficult strategic question. If the composition of flows is shifting, and if the efficacy of any single signal is fading, how should an investor construct a robust Hong Kong equity strategy? The answer lies in understanding the distinct behavioral signatures of each investor cohort, removing the distorting effects of passive flows, and combining signals in a way that maximizes diversification while minimizing turnover and drawdown. The remainder of this article unpacks the evidence behind this argument, explores what the report does not yet fully explain, and offers a decision framework for investors looking to act on these insights.

## The Expansion of Hong Kong's Investable Universe Has Been Underestimated, and It Is Reshaping Alpha Opportunities

For years, the dominant characteristic of the Hong Kong market was extreme concentration. The top 10% of large caps by market capitalization accounted for over 70% of total market cap within the Southbound universe, and the top 10% most liquid names represented over 60% of market turnover. In the non-Southbound universe, concentration was even more extreme, with the top decile capturing over 80% of market cap and over 90% of turnover. This created a market where only a handful of names were genuinely tradable for institutional-scale capital, and where alpha was largely a function of getting the mega-cap story right.

That structural constraint has loosened. Starting in Q4 2024, daily market turnover in the Southbound universe surged from roughly USD 12 billion to over USD 30 billion by 2025. The non-Southbound universe remained relatively stagnant, meaning the entire expansion was driven by activity within Southbound-eligible names. More importantly, this liquidity did not remain concentrated in the top decile. The number of stocks meeting the USD 5 million average daily value threshold expanded from approximately 200 to 500. This is not merely a cyclical uptick. It represents a structural broadening of the market's capacity to absorb systematic strategies.

The consequence for alpha is direct. Return dispersion has picked up meaningfully. Traditional single factors such as size, value, and low-risk have generated excess returns exceeding 20% per annum since late 2024. When liquidity improves, the pricing of idiosyncratic risk becomes more efficient, but also more varied. Stocks that were previously too illiquid to trade now offer genuine differentiation. For stock pickers and quant strategies alike, the opportunity set has expanded in both depth and breadth.

The so-what is clear: any investor who continues to treat Hong Kong as a concentrated large-cap market is leaving significant alpha on the table. The market has changed, and the strategies that worked in the past—or the excuses that were used to avoid the market—need to be reconsidered. The question is not whether alpha exists, but whether your signal can capture it across a broader universe.

## The Rise of Passive Southbound Flows Has Degraded the Aggregate Stock-Picking Signal, but Active Positioning Still Contains Alpha

Southbound investors have become an increasingly dominant force in the Hong Kong market. Their market share has grown steadily, and by early 2026, ETF holdings accounted for more than 10% of all Southbound shareholdings. This shift from active to passive is not neutral for alpha generation. When a significant portion of inflows is channeled through passive cross-border ETFs, the aggregate positioning data becomes a blend of genuine stock selection and mechanical index replication. The signal-to-noise ratio deteriorates.

The evidence is clear: since 2025, the aggregate stock-picking efficacy of Southbound investors has been fading. The simple strategy of following all Southbound flows has lost its edge. This is not because Chinese retail investors have become less skilled. It is because the composition of the flow has changed. Passive flows do not reflect conviction; they reflect allocation. Following them is akin to trading on index rebalancing rather than on insight.

However, the report demonstrates that when passive fund flows are removed from the data, the remaining actively managed Southbound positions still generate consistent alpha. The idiosyncratic stock ideas embedded in active Southbound trading remain valuable. The key is to isolate the active component. This requires granular data that distinguishes between ETF-related holdings and direct stock purchases. Without that separation, the signal is contaminated.

The implication for practitioners is straightforward: do not use aggregate Southbound positioning as a single factor. Disaggregate it. If you cannot separate active from passive flows, you are likely trading on noise. The alpha is in the active residual, not in the total.

## Combining Low-Correlation Investor Cohorts Produces a Strategy with Superior Risk-Adjusted Returns and Lower Drawdowns

The most powerful finding in the report is not about any single group, but about the combination of groups. Southbound investors (ex-ETF) and global participants exhibit strikingly different behavioral profiles. Southbound investors from onshore China have generally been overweight in high-volatility names, particularly in information technology. Global participants, on the other hand, tend to favor value stocks with high dividend yields in utilities and communication services. The correlation between these two cohorts is low.

The report constructs a composite ownership factor that combines three sources: Southbound ex-ETF positioning, the top 20 best-performing participants (selected dynamically on a rolling 52-week basis), and a proprietary crowding factor that quantifies global institutional investors' positions. The result is a strategy that has generated an annualized excess return of 12% since 2019, with a risk-adjusted return of 1.7 and a maximum drawdown of only 6%. For context, the Southbound ex-ETF strategy alone produced a risk-adjusted return of 1.3 and a drawdown of 8%. The top 20 participants alone achieved 1.5 and a drawdown of 12%. The composite improves on both dimensions.

The mechanism is diversification. Each cohort has blind spots. Southbound investors may overpay for growth and momentum. Global participants may be too slow to adjust to regime changes. The crowding factor captures the risk of excessive consensus positioning. By combining them, the strategy smooths out the volatility of any single signal and reduces the tail risk of being on the wrong side of a crowded trade.

This is not a theoretical result. The weekly turnover of the composite strategy is 35%, lower than any of its components, indicating that the signals are reinforcing rather than contradicting each other. The average liquidity of the stocks selected is also higher, at USD 56.9 million, suggesting that the strategy is genuinely implementable at scale.

## What the Report Does Not Yet Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Liquidity Expansion and the Role of Regime Change

While the evidence is compelling, several open questions remain. The first concerns the sustainability of the liquidity expansion. The surge in trading activity since Q4 2024 is striking, but it is not yet clear whether this is a permanent structural shift or a cyclical phenomenon driven by a specific set of market conditions. If liquidity were to contract, the alpha opportunities in smaller-cap names would likely compress, and the broader investable universe would shrink. The report's backtest spans a period that includes this expansion, but it does not fully stress-test the strategy against a prolonged liquidity drought.

The second open question is about regime dependency. The composite strategy's performance since 2019 has been impressive, but the period includes multiple distinct regimes: the post-COVID recovery, the rate hiking cycle, the China regulatory crackdown, and the current AI-driven tech rally. Each regime likely favored different components of the composite. The report does not fully decompose which component contributed most in which regime. An investor needs to understand whether the composite's robustness comes from genuine diversification or from a specific set of conditions that may not persist.

The third question is about the stability of the top 20 participants signal. The selection is dynamic and based on a rolling 52-week performance window. This introduces look-ahead bias concerns, albeit mitigated by the rolling nature. More importantly, it raises the question of whether the top performers persist. If the identity of the best-performing participants changes rapidly, the signal may be less reliable out of sample. The report does not provide evidence on the turnover of the top 20 list itself.

These are not weaknesses of the analysis; they are natural boundaries. Every quantitative strategy has regime dependencies and data limitations. The responsible investor will not take the backtest at face value but will probe these open questions before committing capital.

## A Decision Framework for Acting on These Insights

For investors looking to translate this analysis into action, a structured decision framework is essential. The following steps can help determine whether and how to incorporate these insights into a Hong Kong equity strategy.

First, assess your signal infrastructure. Do you have access to granular Southbound positioning data that distinguishes between ETF holdings and direct stock positions? If not, the aggregate signal is likely too noisy to use effectively. The first prerequisite for alpha is clean data.

Second, evaluate your capacity constraints. The composite strategy selects stocks with an average liquidity of USD 56.9 million, which is manageable for most institutional investors. But if your fund is large enough that even these names create market impact, you may need to adjust the weighting or accept a higher tracking error. The strategy's low turnover of 35% per week helps, but capacity is not unlimited.

Third, decide on your conviction in the diversification thesis. The composite strategy works because the three signals are lowly correlated and each captures a different aspect of market behavior. If you believe that correlation will rise in a stress scenario, you may want to overweight the component with the lowest drawdown or the most robust historical performance. The report does not prescribe this; it is a judgment call based on your risk appetite.

Fourth, monitor regime shifts. The composite's performance is not guaranteed in all environments. Pay attention to changes in Southbound flow composition, shifts in global risk appetite, and signs of liquidity contraction. If the market begins to concentrate again, the broader universe may shrink, and the alpha opportunity may revert to large caps. Have a contingency plan for such a scenario.

Finally, do not treat the backtest as a promise. The 12% annualized excess return and 1.7 risk-adjusted return are historical. They reflect a specific set of market conditions and data sources. Use them as a benchmark for expectations, not as a guarantee. The real value of the analysis is in the framework, not in the precise numbers.

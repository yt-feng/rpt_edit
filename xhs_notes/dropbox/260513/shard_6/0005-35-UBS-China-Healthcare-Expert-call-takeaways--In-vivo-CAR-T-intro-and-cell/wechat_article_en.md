# In Vivo CAR-T Will Not Disrupt Cell Therapy Markets Until It Answers Three Fundamental Questions

The next generation of cell therapy is being built inside the patient's body, not in a cleanroom. In vivo CAR-T technology promises to eliminate the most expensive, time-consuming, and logistically complex step in current CAR-T therapy: the ex vivo manufacturing process that extracts, engineers, and re-infuses a patient's own T cells. If this technology works at scale, it could reduce per-dose manufacturing costs from hundreds of thousands of dollars to hundreds of dollars, fundamentally reshaping the economics of one of medicine's most promising but prohibitively expensive treatment modalities. That is the promise. The reality is more complicated, and the path from laboratory innovation to commercial reality is strewn with unresolved scientific, clinical, and business development questions that investors must understand before placing bets.

Based on a recent expert call with a director of clinical operations at a Chinese cell therapy company, the report underlying this analysis provides a detailed look at where in vivo CAR-T stands today, where it is heading, and what remains unknown. The picture that emerges is one of genuine technical progress constrained by incomplete data, unresolved safety questions, and a structural mismatch between how Chinese innovators generate clinical evidence and how global pharmaceutical companies evaluate licensing opportunities. For investors, the critical insight is not whether in vivo CAR-T will eventually work, but which specific milestones will determine whether it works soon enough to matter for current portfolio decisions.

## In Vivo CAR-T's Cost Advantage Is Real, But Its Clinical Advantage Remains Unproven at Any Scale That Matters

The fundamental economic case for in vivo CAR-T is straightforward and compelling. Traditional ex vivo CAR-T requires apheresis, transportation of patient cells to a centralized manufacturing facility, genetic modification using viral vectors, quality control testing, cryopreservation, and shipment back to the treatment center. This process takes weeks, costs hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient, and is difficult to scale because each batch is patient-specific. In vivo CAR-T bypasses this entire supply chain by delivering the genetic engineering machinery directly into the patient's body, where it modifies T cells in situ.

The expert cited in the report estimates that large-scale industrial production could bring manufacturing costs down to hundreds of US dollars per vial. To put that in perspective, current approved CAR-T therapies in China are priced at roughly RMB 1 million per treatment before insurance, and even after inclusion in the commercial insurance drug list at approximately 30% price reductions, the cost remains prohibitive for the vast majority of patients. A shift from patient-specific manufacturing to an off-the-shelf, injectable product would represent a cost reduction of three orders of magnitude.

But cost is only half the equation. The critical question is whether in vivo CAR-T can match, let alone exceed, the efficacy and safety profile of its ex vivo predecessor. And on this front, the evidence base is thin. The report notes that most in vivo CAR-T programs in China are currently conducted under investigator-initiated trials, which are early-stage, small-sample studies that lack the rigorous controls and statistical power of late-stage clinical trials. Early exploratory studies using viral vector delivery systems have reported objective response rates exceeding 80%, but these numbers come from limited sample sizes that make any comparative assessment unreliable.

The so what here is that investors face a classic early-stage technology dilemma: the cost thesis is strong, but the clinical thesis is incomplete. The market is pricing in the cost advantage without fully discounting the clinical risk. Until we see robust Phase 2 or Phase 3 data from adequately powered trials, the efficacy of in vivo CAR-T remains a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

## The Two Delivery Systems Present a Risk-Return Tradeoff That Investors Must Evaluate Separately

The report identifies two mainstream delivery systems for in vivo CAR-T: viral vectors and LNP-mRNA. These are not interchangeable technologies, and conflating them obscures a critical divergence in risk profiles.

Viral vector delivery systems, which use modified viruses to deliver the CAR gene into T cells, have produced the most impressive early efficacy signals, with ORR exceeding 80% in small exploratory studies. However, they also carry a significant safety burden, with higher incidences of grade 3 or higher cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity. These are not trivial side effects. Grade 3+ CRS can be life-threatening and requires intensive care management, which limits the addressable patient population and increases the total cost of treatment even if the drug itself becomes cheaper. The question for viral vector in vivo CAR-T is whether the safety profile can be improved through better dosing regimens, patient selection, or adjunctive therapies, or whether the inherent biology of systemic viral delivery will always carry this risk.

LNP-mRNA delivery systems, by contrast, offer a more favorable safety profile with lower CRS incidence and more manageable side effects. This is the same technology platform that enabled the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, and its safety characteristics are relatively well understood. The tradeoff is that LNP-mRNA systems have not yet demonstrated the duration and depth of response that viral vectors have shown. mRNA is transient by design, which is advantageous for safety but potentially limiting for therapeutic durability. The question for LNP-mRNA in vivo CAR-T is whether the response is deep enough and long-lasting enough to achieve meaningful clinical outcomes, or whether patients will require repeat dosing that erodes the cost advantage.

This creates a clear framework for investors. Viral vector in vivo CAR-T offers higher potential efficacy at higher safety risk. LNP-mRNA offers lower safety risk but unproven durability. These are fundamentally different investment propositions, and the market is currently pricing them as if they are the same. They are not. Any investor evaluating in vivo CAR-T companies must distinguish which delivery platform the company is pursuing and assess the specific risk profile accordingly.

## The China BD Opportunity Is Real, But Mismatched Evidence Standards Create a Structural Bottleneck

The report highlights that recent landmark transactions and early clinical data disclosures have significantly increased market interest in in vivo CAR-T. This is consistent with what we see across the biotech landscape: large pharmaceutical companies are hungry for platform technologies that can address the cost and scalability limitations of current cell therapies. The potential for Chinese innovators to capture value through business development deals with multinational corporations is a key part of the investment thesis for the sector.

However, the report also identifies a critical structural bottleneck. Chinese companies can rapidly generate human efficacy data through investigator-initiated trials, which are faster and less expensive than formal clinical trials. This gives them a speed advantage in generating early proof-of-concept data. But MNCs typically require robust preclinical datasets to support business development transactions, not early human data from small, uncontrolled studies. The expert notes that this is precisely why we have not yet seen a deal between MNCs and leading in vivo CAR-T companies in China.

This mismatch matters. It means that the early human data Chinese companies are generating, while scientifically interesting, may not be sufficient to catalyze the kind of licensing deals that would validate the technology and provide capital for further development. The path to a BD transaction for a Chinese in vivo CAR-T company may require completing formal clinical trials to the standards expected by global regulators, which takes time and money. The early-stage data that generates media headlines and investor enthusiasm may not translate directly into deal flow.

The strategic implication is that investors should not assume that early positive data from IITs will automatically lead to licensing revenues. The BD timeline for Chinese in vivo CAR-T companies may be longer and more uncertain than the market currently prices in. The real catalyst will be the completion of well-designed clinical trials that meet global regulatory standards, not the publication of additional small-sample studies.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Commercial Viability of In Vivo CAR-T in Autoimmune Disease

The report notes that current development of in vivo CAR-T is concentrated in oncology and autoimmune diseases, focusing on validated targets such as CD19 and BCMA. The oncology application is well understood, but the autoimmune indication represents a potentially larger market opportunity that the report does not fully develop.

The logic is straightforward. CAR-T therapy works by eliminating B cells that express specific surface markers. In oncology, this is a treatment strategy for B-cell malignancies. In autoimmune diseases such as lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis, the same mechanism can reset the immune system by eliminating autoreactive B cells. This is a potentially transformative approach to autoimmune disease treatment, offering the possibility of durable remission from a single treatment rather than chronic immunosuppression.

But the autoimmune application also raises questions that the report does not answer. What is the appropriate risk-benefit ratio for a therapy like CAR-T in autoimmune disease, where patients are not facing an immediately life-threatening condition? The safety concerns that are acceptable in advanced cancer may not be acceptable in lupus. How will in vivo CAR-T's safety profile, particularly the risk of CRS and neurotoxicity, be evaluated in this context? And what is the appropriate pricing and reimbursement model for a one-time curative therapy in a chronic disease population that currently generates recurring revenue for drug companies through maintenance therapies?

These are not minor questions. They will determine whether in vivo CAR-T in autoimmune disease is a multibillion-dollar opportunity or a niche application limited to the most severe, treatment-refractory patients. Investors need to watch for clinical data in autoimmune indications specifically, not just extrapolate from oncology results.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Evaluating In Vivo CAR-T Opportunities

For investors trying to translate this analysis into portfolio decisions, the following framework may be useful. It is designed to identify the specific milestones that will separate winners from losers in the in vivo CAR-T space.

First, evaluate the delivery platform. Viral vector or LNP-mRNA? Each has a fundamentally different risk profile. Viral vector companies need to demonstrate that they can manage the safety burden through improved clinical management or engineering modifications. LNP-mRNA companies need to demonstrate durable responses. A company that is pursuing both platforms simultaneously is likely spreading its resources too thin and may not achieve sufficient depth in either.

Second, assess the clinical evidence standard. Is the company generating data through IITs or formal clinical trials? IIT data is useful for hypothesis generation but should not be given the same weight as registrational-quality data. Companies that have initiated or completed formal Phase 1/2 trials should be valued differently from those that have only IIT data, even if the IIT response rates look impressive.

Third, evaluate the BD readiness. Does the company have the preclinical datasets that MNCs require for deal evaluation? The expert call suggests that MNCs want robust preclinical data, not early human data from IITs. Companies that have invested in comprehensive preclinical characterization may be better positioned for licensing deals than those that have rushed to generate human data without building the underlying preclinical foundation.

Fourth, consider the autoimmune opportunity separately from oncology. The autoimmune market is potentially larger but carries different risk-benefit considerations. Companies that are developing in vivo CAR-T for autoimmune indications need to demonstrate not just efficacy but an acceptable safety profile in a non-life-threatening disease. This is a higher bar than oncology.

Fifth, monitor the manufacturing cost trajectory. The report notes that per-unit manufacturing costs for traditional CAR-T could decline to approximately RMB 200,000 if key raw materials are domestically sUBStituted. This means the cost gap between in vivo and ex vivo CAR-T may narrow over time, reducing the urgency of the in vivo approach. Investors should not assume that the current cost differential will persist indefinitely.

## The Unresolved Question That Will Define the Next Phase

The most important question that emerges from this analysis is not whether in vivo CAR-T will work, but when we will know whether it works. The technology is real, the cost thesis is compelling, and early clinical signals are encouraging. But the data is not yet sufficient to support the valuations that some companies in this space are commanding. The market is pricing in a high probability of success based on small-sample studies and mechanistic plausibility, without adequately discounting the risk of clinical failure or the timeline required to generate definitive evidence.

For investors, the right response is not to ignore the opportunity, but to be precise about what would constitute validation. A single well-designed Phase 2 trial in a defined patient population, with adequate sample size and rigorous endpoints, would be worth more than a dozen small IITs with eye-popping response rates. A licensing deal with a major pharmaceutical company that includes meaningful upfront payments and a clear clinical development plan would be a stronger signal than any amount of early-stage data. And a clear demonstration of durable responses with acceptable safety in autoimmune disease would open a market opportunity that is currently not priced into any valuation.

These milestones are coming. The question is which companies will reach them first, and whether the market has the patience to wait for real answers.

# China's Two-Speed Economy Is Not a Transition – It Is the New Steady State

The dominant narrative about China's economy has long been one of transition: from export-led growth to domestic consumption, from investment-heavy to innovation-driven, from a property-centric model to one powered by services and technology. That narrative is now misleading. What is emerging is not a transition but a permanent two-speed structure. On one side, exports, AI capex, and green energy investment are accelerating, supported by supply chain resilience and global demand shifts. On the other side, domestic consumption remains constrained by a soft labor market, a property sector still in adjustment, and a policy framework that shows no intention of shifting toward broad-based demand support. The result is an economy where aggregate growth looks stable but internal dynamics diverge sharply. A global investment bank report has revised its 2026 real GDP growth forecast upward by 0.1 percentage point to 4.8 percent, and its GDP deflator forecast by 0.3 points to 0.5 percent. These are not trivial adjustments. But they come with a critical caveat: the reflation is narrow, driven by energy costs and AI-related demand, not by a recovery in household spending. The economy is running on its exports engine, and that engine, while powerful, does not generate the same breadth of income and employment growth it once did.

This matters now because the policy response is effectively on cruise control. The report removes its earlier calls for fiscal top-ups and additional monetary easing. The augmented fiscal deficit is expected to remain flat. The People's Bank of China is seen relying on liquidity operations and targeted credit tools, not rate cuts. This is a deliberate choice, not a failure of imagination. Strong exports reduce the urgency for stimulus, and the policy framework remains supply-centric, focused on energy security and tech self-sufficiency. For investors and business leaders, the implication is straightforward: the structural divergence between export-oriented and domestic-demand-oriented sectors is not a temporary phase that policy will correct. It is the new operating environment. The question is not whether rebalancing will happen, but how to navigate an economy where growth is increasingly concentrated in specific pockets while the broader domestic demand picture remains subdued.

The report makes a compelling case that China's export strength is both real and structural. But it also identifies a paradox that is often overlooked: the very factors that make exports resilient also limit their spillover to the broader economy. This article unpacks that paradox, explores the implications for inflation, investment, and employment, and identifies the critical uncertainties that the report leaves open. It then translates the analysis into a decision framework for readers, and concludes with the key analytical questions that make the full report essential reading.

## Exports Are the Dominant Growth Engine, but Their Transmission to Jobs and Domestic Demand Is Structurally Weakening

The report forecasts China's goods export growth at 10 percent in nominal terms and 9 percent in real terms in 2026, with net exports contributing approximately 1.3 percentage points to real GDP growth. This is down slightly from 1.6 points in 2025, owing to a terms-of-trade shock from higher commodity import prices, but exports remain the key growth driver. The sources of this strength are well identified: market share gains as China's supply chain proves more resilient to the oil shock than competitors, the global AI super-cycle boosting demand for semiconductors and electronics, and the acceleration of the green transition as the Middle East conflict pushes countries to diversify energy sources. The April PMI new export orders index rebounded to 50.3, the highest in recent years, reflecting strong sUBStitution demand.

The critical insight, however, is not that exports are strong. It is that the transmission from export strength to the broader economy has structurally weakened. The report identifies three reasons. First, the employment content of today's export dollar is lower than in previous cycles. China's export mix has shifted decisively up the value chain toward EVs, ships, semiconductors, and power equipment. These are capital-intensive, highly automated sectors. Each incremental dollar of export growth generates fewer jobs than it used to. Second, overcapacity across much of the manufacturing base means that firms respond to rising orders by running existing lines harder, not by expanding headcount or capex. The anti-involution policy guidance reinforces this behavior. Job gains at the extensive margin only materialize once capacity utilization tightens meaningfully, which requires a sustained export cycle beyond a few quarters. Third, the hole that exports need to fill is exceptionally large. Housing and related sectors are still a drag on nominal GDP of roughly 2 percentage points this year. With exports at approximately 15 percent of GDP on a value-added basis, fully offsetting this drag alone would require sustained nominal export growth of 14 to 15 percent, a bar China has rarely cleared.

The bottom line is that exports are helping, but the labor market arithmetic has structurally changed. Wage growth in select high-value export sectors may accelerate, supporting aggregate income in a narrow, segmented way. But this is not the broad-based income recovery that would move the needle on aggregate consumption. The report's forecast of private consumption growth at 3.7 percent in 2026, down from 4.5 percent in 2025 and below headline GDP growth, is a direct consequence of this structural weakening.

## Inflation Is Rising, but Reflation Is Narrow and Policy Is on Cruise Control

The report raises its GDP deflator forecast by 0.3 percentage points to 0.5 percent for 2026. Headline CPI is expected to average 0.8 percent year-on-year, with core CPI at 1.1 percent on a shallow moderating path. PPI is forecast to reach 1.5 percent. These are meaningful revisions, but they do not signal a broad-based reflation. The drivers are specific: higher energy costs from the oil shock, and demand tied to AI and the green transition lifting producer prices in those sectors. The pass-through to core consumer prices remains limited because weak domestic demand and a soft labor market constrain pricing power.

This narrow reflation has a direct implication for policy. Strong exports reduce the urgency for stimulus, and the report explicitly removes its earlier calls for fiscal top-ups and additional monetary easing. The augmented fiscal deficit is expected to remain flat at 11.7 percent of GDP. The PBoC is seen holding the seven-day reverse repo rate at 1.40 percent, relying on liquidity operations and targeted credit tools rather than rate cuts. This is a deliberate policy posture: supply-side priorities on energy security and tech self-sufficiency take precedence over demand-side support. The Middle East conflict, if anything, reinforces this orientation by highlighting the strategic importance of energy independence.

The report's revised USD/CNY forecast to 6.75 by year-end from 7.00, with a near-term shift toward 6.70 possible, is consistent with this policy stance. Growth outperformance and a softer USD as risk aversion fades support a stronger renminbi. But the report is careful to note that the PBoC remains mindful of soft domestic prices and is unlikely to use currency strength to address growth imbalances. The currency is not being deployed as a policy tool; it is being allowed to reflect the external surplus.

The key question that the report leaves open is whether this policy posture is sustainable. If export strength begins to fade, either because global demand softens or because trade tensions escalate, the absence of fiscal and monetary buffers would become a significant vulnerability. The report acknowledges that risks hinge on external demand and domestic policy direction. Stronger global growth would further boost exports, giving room for economic rebalancing policies. Conversely, a demand-destructive oil shock would hit mainly via weaker exports, compressing margins, lowering capacity utilization, and renewing deflationary pressures. In that scenario, cyclical easing may step up, but the policy mix would remain supply-centric. The question is whether the supply-centric framework is flexible enough to respond to a demand shock, or whether it would amplify the downturn by failing to support consumption.

## The Investment Picture Is Mixed, with AI Capex and Infrastructure Offsetting Property and Manufacturing Overcapacity

The report forecasts real growth of gross fixed capital formation to rebound to 3.5 percent in 2026 from 2 percent in 2025. This is a meaningful acceleration, but the composition matters enormously. The drivers are concentrated: booming AI capex, policy support for infrastructure, and export-driven capex in select manufacturing sectors. The drags are equally concentrated: property investment remains sluggish, and overall manufacturing capacity consolidation continues.

Infrastructure capex is expected to accelerate modestly, supported by newly planned 800 billion renminbi in policy-based financing instruments and 500 billion renminbi carried over from the second half of 2025. The front-loading of key national strategic projects under the 15th Five-Year Plan helps alleviate the project pipeline bottleneck that constrained infrastructure spending last year. This is a deliberate policy choice to use infrastructure as a counterweight, but it is not a broad-based stimulus. It is targeted and supply-centric.

Manufacturing investment presents a sharp divergence. Sectors with severe excess capacity, such as solar, may see capacity utilization improve but are unlikely to add new investment. Anti-involution policies cap the upside. Sectors where supply and demand are more balanced or tight, particularly those tied to AI and the green transition such as power equipment, could see investment accelerate. The report's forecast of 4.5 percent manufacturing investment growth in 2026, up from 0.6 percent in 2025, masks this internal divergence.

Property investment remains the largest structural drag. New starts fell another 22 percent year-on-year, and land sale revenues declined 24 percent. The construction pipeline is depleted. While secondary home sales have shown signs of recovery in select Cities, the improvement is localized and narrow. The report expects broader stabilization across Tier 1 and 2 Cities by 2027, but until then, property investment will likely remain sluggish, though incrementally less bad. The forecast of minus 11.5 percent property investment growth in 2026, compared to minus 17.5 percent in 2025, is a moderation, not a recovery.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Export-Driven Model Under Trade and Technology Uncertainty

The report provides a rigorous and data-rich analysis of China's current growth dynamics. But it leaves several critical questions open, and these are the questions that make the full report essential reading for any serious investor or strategist.

First, the report assumes that China's export market share gains will continue at the upper end of its estimated 0.3 to 0.9 percentage point range. This is a reasonable baseline, but it depends on a specific set of conditions: that global trade does not contract sharply, that trade tensions do not escalate to the point of significant tariff or non-tariff barriers on Chinese goods, and that China's supply chain resilience is not disrupted by further geopolitical shocks. Each of these conditions is contestable. The report does not model a scenario in which export market share gains are reversed or slowed by policy responses from trading partners.

Second, the report identifies AI and the green transition as structural drivers of export demand, but it does not fully explore the feedback loop between these drivers and China's domestic policy priorities. If AI capex accelerates, it could further widen the gap between capital-intensive export sectors and labor-intensive domestic sectors, exacerbating the structural labor market weakness the report identifies. The skill-biased technical change that displaces entry-level cognitive work is not an external shock; it is a direct consequence of the policy emphasis on tech self-sufficiency. The report notes that AI diffusion adds a medium-term headwind to youth employment, but it does not quantify this or explore the policy implications.

Third, the report's policy outlook assumes that the current supply-centric framework will persist. But it does not address the political economy of this choice. A two-speed economy generates concentrated winners and diffuse losers. The winners are in export-oriented, capital-intensive sectors and the financial and technology hUBS that serve them. The losers are in domestic services, construction, and the broader labor market. Over time, this divergence could generate pressure for a policy pivot toward demand-side support, particularly if social stability concerns rise. The report does not model the conditions under which such a pivot might occur.

These open questions are not criticisms of the report. They are the natural boundaries of any analytical framework, and they point directly to the scenarios that investors should stress-test. The full report, with its detailed charts and scenario analysis, provides the data to run those stress tests.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating China's Two-Speed Economy

For readers who need to translate this analysis into strategic decisions, the following framework captures the key dimensions.

The first dimension is sector exposure. The report makes clear that the growth is concentrated in exports, AI-related capex, green energy, and infrastructure. These sectors are likely to outperform, but they are also more exposed to external demand and policy risks. Domestic consumption, property, and labor-intensive services are likely to underperform. Any investment or business strategy that assumes a broad-based recovery in domestic demand is not supported by the data.

The second dimension is the transmission mechanism. The report shows that the traditional channels from export strength to employment and domestic income have weakened. This means that even if export data looks strong, the spillover to the broader economy will be slower and narrower than in previous cycles. Investors should not extrapolate from export strength to a general economic improvement. The two-speed structure is persistent.

The third dimension is the policy stance. The report argues that policy is on cruise control, with no additional easing expected. This implies that the current divergence is not going to be corrected by fiscal or monetary stimulus. Any strategy that relies on a policy pivot toward demand-side support is betting against the report's base case. The risk is that if external demand weakens, the absence of policy buffers could amplify the downturn.

The fourth dimension is the currency. The report's revised USD/CNY forecast to 6.75 reflects growth outperformance and a softer USD. But the PBoC is unlikely to use currency strength to address growth imbalances. This means that the renminbi appreciation is a reflection of the external surplus, not a policy tool. For investors with cross-border exposure, the currency outlook is supportive, but it should not be read as a signal of broader economic strength.

The fifth dimension is the risk scenario. The report identifies two key risks: stronger global growth that further boosts exports, and a demand-destructive oil shock that hits exports and renews deflationary pressures. The former is a positive scenario for the export-led sectors, but it does not resolve the structural domestic weaknesses. The latter is a negative scenario that would test the resilience of the supply-centric policy framework. Investors should have a clear view on which scenario they are betting on, and the report provides the data to make that assessment.

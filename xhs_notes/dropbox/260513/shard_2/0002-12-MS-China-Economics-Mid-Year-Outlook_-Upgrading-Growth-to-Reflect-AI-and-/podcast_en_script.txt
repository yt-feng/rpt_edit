EN_A: The “employment content” of exports? That’s a fascinating concept. Can you unpack that?
EN_B: Sure. In the past, exporting a t-shirt required a lot of labor. Today, China exports a ship, a semiconductor, or an EV battery. These are high-value products, but they are also highly automated. So, for every hundred billion dollars in export growth, you generate far fewer jobs than you used to. The report has a really telling math problem.
EN_A: What math?
EN_B: It estimates that the property sector is still a drag on nominal GDP of about 2 percentage points. Exports, on a value-added basis, are about 15% of GDP. To fully offset that property drag just with exports, you’d need sustained nominal export growth of 14 to 15 percent. That’s a bar China has rarely cleared.
EN_A: So that explains why we see strong export data but a still-tough job market, especially for young graduates. What about inflation? We’ve been talking about deflation, but this report seems to say inflation is coming back.
EN_B: Yes, and this is another key insight. The report sees PPI, the producer price index, turning positive in 2026, driven by higher energy costs and demand from AI and green capex. But they use a very specific term: “narrow reflation.”
EN_A: “Narrow reflation”? What does that mean exactly?
EN_B: It means prices will go up, but only in the production and export sectors. Think chips and power equipment. But the pass-through to core consumer prices, what you and I pay at the store, will be very limited. Because domestic demand is weak, companies have no pricing power. So you get this weird situation where factory gate prices rise, but the stuff on the shelf stays cheap.
EN_A: So, production side is hot, consumption side is cold. Given the strong exports, does the government feel it can just coast and not do any more stimulus? What’s the report’s view on policy?
EN_B: The report uses the term “cruise control.” Because the export engine is doing the heavy lifting, there’s no urgency for more stimulus. It removes its calls for additional fiscal top-ups and rate cuts. The central bank will rely on liquidity tools, not broad-based easing. The policy focus remains supply-side, on energy security and tech self-sufficiency.

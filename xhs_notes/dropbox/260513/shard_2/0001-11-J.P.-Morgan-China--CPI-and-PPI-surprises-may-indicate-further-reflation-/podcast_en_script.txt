EN_A: So we just got China’s April inflation numbers, and both CPI and PPI came in above expectations. Is this the big turnaround everyone’s been waiting for?
EN_B: It’s a surprise, for sure. CPI hit 1.2 percent year on year, PPI jumped to 2.8 percent. But I’d call this more of an “imported price shock” than a genuine demand recovery.
EN_A: Imported shock? What’s driving it?
EN_B: Energy, mainly. The Strait of Hormuz is still effectively closed, so global oil prices stay elevated. Gasoline prices in China rose 19.3 percent year on year, adding 0.56 percentage points to CPI alone. On the PPI side, oil and gas extraction prices surged 18.5 percent month on month.
EN_A: So it’s not consumers spending more, it’s global costs feeding through?
EN_B: Mostly, but not entirely. Travel services jumped 3.7 percent thanks to the Qingming and Labor Day holidays. And the anti-involution campaign is starting to show—lithium battery prices are up, and new energy vehicle prices are falling less.
EN_A: But those holiday effects fade, right? What’s sustainable here?
EN_B: That’s the key question. The report does say some of the seasonality carries into May. But the bigger issue is whether upstream price hikes can pass through to consumer goods. Right now, consumer goods PPI is still negative, at minus 1.0 percent. So the pipeline is clogged.
EN_A: Sounds like inflation with a weak foundation.
EN_B: Exactly. The bank actually raised its full-year CPI forecast to 1.1 percent and PPI to 2.5 percent. But it also warns that domestic demand is still soft, and PPI could even slip back into deflation.
EN_A: So what should we watch going forward?
EN_B: Two things. First, whether the Strait of Hormuz actually reopens—that depends on US-Iran talks and the upcoming Trump-Xi summit. Second, whether domestic demand stabilizes. Pork prices are still falling 15 percent, which is a big drag on food CPI.
EN_A: That pork drag sounds huge. Any idea when it bottoms?
EN_B: The report doesn’t fully answer that yet. And that’s one of the things the full report dives deeper into—along with how the anti-involution policy impacts different industries, and the lag in pass-through from producer to consumer prices.
EN_A: So there’s a lot more beneath the surface. Thanks for unpacking the key signals for us.
EN_B: My pleasure. If you want the full picture, check out the complete report. We’ll pick up the rest next time.

# The Factor Rotation Is Not a Pivot: Why the US Risk-On Rally Demands a More Selective Strategy Than the Broad-Based Recovery Narrative Suggests

The April 2026 equity rally was not a broad-based recovery. It was a concentrated, factor-specific rotation driven by a single catalyst: the easing of Iran-related energy fears. In the United States, the S&P 500 surged over 9%, Big Tech rallied by more than 16%, and factor performance diverged dramatically. Growth and Momentum exploded higher. Value sold off 6.7%. Yield declined 12%. Quality went nowhere. The pattern is unmistakable: this was not a market that simply turned bullish. It was a market that re-priced the probability of a tail risk receding, and it rewarded the factors most exposed to that re-pricing.

The report from a global investment bank makes this clear. But the implications extend far beyond the monthly performance table. The question every serious investor must now confront is whether this rotation has staying power or whether it is a temporary repricing of a risk premium that could just as easily re-emerge. The bank upgrades Growth and Momentum in the US to Positive, downgrades Value to Neutral, and downgrades Quality to Negative. These are not neutral observations. They are directional bets on a specific macroeconomic and geopolitical scenario.

The problem is that the scenario is fragile. The same report notes that the energy shock that drove the initial sell-off is now five weeks old, not eight, because inventories were large at the onset of the conflict. But those buffers are shrinking. Crude and refined product inventories are declining. The market expects only one Fed cut, penciled in for March 2027. Elevated energy prices are expected to persist for the rest of the year. The very conditions that made the risk-on rally possible are eroding in real time.

This creates a strategic tension. The factor views are correct for the current regime. But the regime itself may be short-lived. The most important analytical task is not to accept the factor tilts at face value but to stress-test them against the scenarios that the report itself identifies as medium-term headwinds. That is the work this article undertakes.

## The US Factor Rotation Is a Bet on Geopolitical De-escalation and AI Earnings Momentum, Not a Secular Shift in Market Regime

The April factor performance tells a coherent story. The long-short High-over-Low Volatility pair gained 18.9%, the strongest monthly performance among all factors. Momentum rose 7.0%. Growth rebounded 5.1%. Value fell 6.7%. Yield dropped 12.0%. Quality was flat. The correlation between Momentum and Volatility, which had been elevated during the conflict, moderated as the energy risk receded.

This is not a random dispersion. It is a textbook unwinding of a geopolitical risk premium. During the Iran conflict, investors rotated into defensive, low-volatility, high-quality, and value-oriented exposures. When tensions eased, they reversed those positions and re-entered the growth and momentum trades that had been the market leaders before the shock.

The bank's factor views reflect this logic. Upgrading Growth and Momentum is a bet that the pre-conflict leadership will resume. Downgrading Value to Neutral acknowledges that its anti-AI, consumer-heavy, defensive profile is less attractive in a risk-on environment. Downgrading Quality to Negative recognizes that its defensive appeal has eroded as investors pivot toward speculative AI exposures and semiconductors.

But there is a critical distinction between a factor view that is correct for the current quarter and one that is robust across multiple scenarios. The current view is highly conditional on continued geopolitical calm, sustained AI earnings momentum, and no further deterioration in consumer sentiment. Each of these conditions is contestable.

## European Factor Dynamics Tell a Different Story: Value Is the Cleanest Beneficiary of a Broader Rotation, but the Trade Is Already Crowded in Momentum

The European factor picture is structurally different from the US, and the report's views reflect that divergence. In Europe, the bank is Positive on Value, Neutral on Growth, Neutral on Momentum, and Negative on Low Volatility. This is not a mirror image of the US positioning. It is a recognition that European markets have different sector compositions, different earnings drivers, and different sensitivity to rates and inflation.

European Value has been supported by strong earnings delivery. EPS growth has been lifted to 7%, the highest since the first quarter of 2023. Earnings momentum is improving. The bank views Value as the cleanest beneficiary of any broadening beyond Momentum, which is now looking crowded. The structural negative correlation between Value and Momentum in Europe means that a rotation away from crowded momentum trades would directly benefit Value.

This is a more nuanced argument than the US case. It does not depend on a single geopolitical catalyst. It depends on mean reversion in factor crowding and on the sustainability of European earnings momentum. The report is Neutral on European Growth because earnings momentum is improving but renewed rates volatility weighs on long-duration support. It is Neutral on Momentum because the trade is increasingly crowded, arguing for managing exposure rather than adding.

The implication is that European factor investing requires a different decision framework than US factor investing. In the US, the dominant variable is the interplay between AI earnings momentum and geopolitical risk. In Europe, the dominant variables are factor crowding, rates volatility, and the sustainability of value-oriented earnings growth. An investor who applies the US framework to Europe will make systematic errors.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How to Position for a Resumption of Geopolitical Risk or a Stagflationary Surprise

The report is explicit about its base case: risk-on is back, AI earnings are strong, and geopolitical tensions have eased. But it also flags the medium-term headwinds: elevated energy prices, shrinking inventory buffers, and only one expected Fed cut through early 2027. These headwinds are not incorporated into the factor views because the views are based on the current regime, not on a scenario analysis.

This creates a gap. The report does not address how the factor views would change if energy prices remain elevated through year-end, if consumer sentiment deteriorates further, or if the geopolitical risk premium re-emerges. These are not tail risks. They are central to the macro outlook that the report itself describes.

Consider the following scenario: energy prices stay high, the Fed remains on hold, and consumer spending weakens. In that environment, Growth and Momentum would likely underperform. Value and Yield would benefit from their defensive and income-oriented characteristics. Quality, which the report downgraded to Negative, would regain its appeal as investors seek earnings stability. The current factor views would be exactly wrong.

The report does not explore this scenario. It does not provide a framework for adjusting factor tilts as macro conditions evolve. It offers a snapshot, not a dynamic strategy. This is a meaningful limitation for any investor who wants to use factor investing as a tactical tool rather than a static allocation.

## A Decision Framework for Factor Investing in a Regime of Uncertainty: Use the Report's Views as a Starting Point, Not a Destination

The most valuable contribution of the report is not its specific factor ratings. It is the analytical structure that connects macro conditions to factor performance. Any investor can replicate this structure and apply it to their own scenarios. The following decision framework translates the report's logic into actionable questions.

First, identify the dominant macro variable. In the US, the dominant variable is the interplay between AI earnings momentum and geopolitical risk. In Europe, it is factor crowding and rates volatility. Do not apply the same framework to both markets.

Second, stress-test the factor views against alternative scenarios. The report's base case is geopolitical de-escalation and sustained AI earnings. What if that base case is wrong? What if energy inventories continue to shrink and the Fed is forced to react? What if AI earnings momentum slows? For each scenario, determine which factors would benefit and which would suffer.

Third, monitor the leading indicators that the report identifies but does not fully integrate. Energy inventory levels, consumer sentiment data, Fed rate expectations, and factor crowding metrics are all observable in real time. When these indicators move, the factor views should move with them.

Fourth, do not treat factor ratings as binary signals. The report uses a five-point scale: Positive, Neutral, Negative. But within each rating, there are degrees of conviction. The US Growth upgrade is more confident than the European Value upgrade because the US case is supported by stronger earnings data and clearer flow dynamics. The US Quality downgrade is more confident than the European Quality neutral because the US has a clearer risk-on rotation. Use the conviction level, not just the rating, to size positions.

Finally, recognize that factor investing is a relative game. The report's long-short factor baskets are designed to capture premiums while controlling for stock and sector weights. But most investors cannot implement long-short strategies at scale. The practical implication is to overweight the factors with the highest conviction and underweight the factors with the lowest conviction, while maintaining a diversified core.

## The Most Important Unresolved Question Is Whether the Market Is Correctly Pricing the Sustainability of AI-Led Earnings Growth

The report's US factor views rest on a single empirical observation: AI and Tech earnings have been exceptional. Big Tech EPS surprises are tracking at plus 15.6% versus a long-term median of plus 7.2%. EPS growth of plus 35.8% year-over-year marks the fastest pace since the second quarter of 2024. Earnings for all four quarters have been revised upward since the first quarter season began.

This is the foundation of the Growth and Momentum upgrades. But the report does not ask whether these earnings are sustainable. It does not analyze the source of the earnings beat. Is it revenue growth, margin expansion, or cost cutting? Are the earnings driven by genuine demand or by inventory restocking and one-time factors? How much of the earnings surprise is concentrated in a few names versus broadly distributed?

These questions matter because factor performance is not static. If AI earnings momentum slows, the entire factor framework shifts. Growth and Momentum would lose their primary support. Value and Quality would regain relevance. The report's current views would need to be reversed.

The report does not answer these questions. It provides the data but not the interpretation. That is the gap that the full report fills.

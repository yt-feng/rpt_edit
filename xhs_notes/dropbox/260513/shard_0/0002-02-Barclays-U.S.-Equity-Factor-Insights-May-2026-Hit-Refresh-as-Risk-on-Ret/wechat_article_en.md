# The Risk-On Regime Has Returned, But the Real Question Is Whether the Market Is Rotating Into a New Cycle or Simply Re-rating the Old One

The first quarter of 2026 was a period of strategic confusion. Geopolitical risk from the Iran conflict, sticky inflation, and a sharp drawdown in Big Tech had forced factor rotation into defensive and value-oriented exposures. By late April, that narrative had inverted. The S&P 500 rallied over 9% in a single month. Big Tech surged more than 16%. Momentum exploded higher by 18.6% in the long-short pair. And a global investment bank's factor model, which had been cautious on growth and momentum just weeks earlier, flipped decisively positive.

This is not simply a relief rally. It is a structural reassertion of the growth-and-momentum complex, supported by earnings that have exceeded expectations by a wide margin and a macroeconomic backdrop that, while still uncertain, has shifted in favor of duration-sensitive assets. The key insight from the latest factor research is that the market is not broadening out. It is concentrating again, and doing so on the back of fundamentals that are genuinely strong, not just speculative froth.

But beneath the surface, there are tensions that deserve scrutiny. The same report that upgrades Growth and Momentum downgrades Quality and Value, and remains neutral on Yield. The conviction on Large-over-Small is unchanged, despite small caps rallying sharply. And while the earnings picture is robust, the medium-term headwinds from energy prices, rate expectations, and inventory dynamics are far from resolved. The question for serious investors is not whether risk-on is back, but whether this rotation has legs, or whether it is a temporary repricing that will fade as macro constraints reassert themselves.

## The Earnings Engine Is Running Hot, and It Is Powering Growth and Momentum, Not Quality or Value

The most important data point in the entire report is not the factor returns, but the earnings surprise numbers. For the first quarter of 2026, the S&P 500 posted an EPS surprise of 18.0%, more than three times the long-term median of 5.2%. Within Big Tech, the surprise was 40.7%, versus a median of 7.2%. The beat rate for Big Tech was 100%. These are not incremental beats. They are outliers by any historical standard.

What makes this earnings cycle different is that it is being driven by operating leverage, not just revenue growth. Sales growth for the S&P 500 was 8.6% year-over-year, but EPS growth was 18.1%. Net margins expanded by 1.2 percentage points. For the Tech-ex-S&P 500 universe, the divergence was even starker: sales growth of 24.0% translated into EPS growth of 48.9%, with margins expanding by 4.0 percentage points. This is the kind of earnings quality that justifies multiple expansion, and the market has responded accordingly.

The factor implications are clear. Momentum, which had been under pressure during the geopolitical shock, now has the strongest earnings profile across all factors. Its next-twelve-months EPS growth is above 100% year-to-date, and forward earnings are at the 93rd percentile versus history. Growth, meanwhile, benefits from the duration tailwind of easing inflation expectations, which improves the relative attractiveness of longer-duration assets. Value, by contrast, is structurally disadvantaged in this environment. Its consumer-heavy exposure is facing weak sentiment, and its anti-AI profile means it misses the primary earnings driver of the current cycle.

This is not a market where all factors can work simultaneously. The earnings data is telling a clear story: the marginal dollar of capital is flowing toward companies that are delivering earnings acceleration, not earnings stability. Quality, which had been a safe haven during the first quarter's turmoil, is now seeing its defensive attributes become a liability. Its sales and earnings growth characteristics are historically subdued relative to other styles, and despite recent price declines, valuations remain elevated. The report's downgrade of Quality to Negative is a direct reflection of this fundamental reality.

## The Large-Cap Advantage Is Not Just About Size; It Is About Operating Leverage and Earnings Visibility That Small Caps Cannot Match

The report maintains a Positive stance on Large-over-Small, and this is one of the most strategically significant calls in the document. On the surface, it may seem counterintuitive. Small caps rallied sharply in late March and April, and their valuations are cheaper on a headline basis. But the report's analysis goes deeper, and it reveals a structural gap that is unlikely to close quickly.

The fundamental advantage of large caps is not simply that they are bigger. It is that they have materially higher operating leverage, better margin dynamics, and stronger earnings revisions. The data in the report makes this explicit. For the S&P 500, net margins stand at 14.4%. For mid-caps, they are 8.0%. For small caps, they are 5.6%. The year-over-year margin expansion for large caps was 1.2 percentage points, versus 0.7 for mid-caps and just 0.2 for small caps. This is a structural difference, not a cyclical one.

Moreover, the recent gains in small-cap industries have been driven more by multiple expansion than by earnings growth. This is a classic warning sign. When prices rise without earnings backing them, the rally is vulnerable to sentiment shifts. In contrast, large-cap gains, particularly in the hyperscaler-led AI infrastructure space, are underpinned by earnings visibility that small caps simply do not have. The report notes that large caps continue to show clear fundamental advantages, and that conviction remains strongest in mega caps.

The medium-term risk for small caps is compounded by the rate environment. With only one Fed cut now expected through 2027, and with small-cap leverage still elevated, the cost of capital remains a headwind. Large caps, with their stronger balance sheets and more diversified revenue streams, are better positioned to absorb higher-for-longer rates. The report's continued Positive stance on Large-over-Small is therefore not a tactical call. It is a strategic conviction based on a structural earnings advantage.

## The High-Volatility Rebound Is Real, but the Conviction Is Tempered by Valuation and Sector Composition

One of the most dramatic factor moves in April was the rebound in High-over-Low Volatility, which surged roughly 31% from its year-to-date trough. The report upgrades this factor to Neutral, which is a meaningful shift from its prior negative stance. But the upgrade is cautious, and the reasoning reveals important nuances.

The rebound was supported by two developments. First, easing Iran-related energy concerns removed a key source of geopolitical tail risk for low-volatility stocks. Second, the strong earnings season and sustained AI infrastructure spending reignited risk appetite, which historically benefits high-volatility names. The report notes that fundamentals have improved meaningfully, with next-twelve-months EPS growth around 90% year-to-date, and earnings finally catching up with prices.

However, the upgrade is to Neutral, not Positive, and the report explicitly states that a mixed sector composition tempers conviction. High-volatility baskets tend to be heavily weighted toward cyclical and speculative names, which can be vulnerable if the macro environment deteriorates. Moreover, valuation is now stretched, with forward price-to-earnings at the 71st percentile versus history. The report is less bearish on this factor, but not constructive enough to turn positive.

This is a classic example of a factor that has recovered on sentiment and technicals, but where the fundamental case is not yet fully compelling. The open question is whether the earnings improvement is sustainable, or whether it is a one-time catch-up effect. For investors considering an allocation to high-volatility, the report suggests that the easy money has likely been made, and that further gains will require continued earnings momentum.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Earnings Cycle and the Risk of Energy-Driven Reversal

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several meaningful questions unresolved. The most important of these is the sustainability of the current earnings cycle. The first quarter of 2026 saw an extraordinary earnings surprise, but the report itself notes that medium-term headwinds are building. Energy prices remain elevated, crude and refined product inventories are shrinking, and the expectation of only one Fed cut through 2027 creates a restrictive monetary backdrop.

The report acknowledges that the resilience of U.S. equities so far can be partly explained by the energy shock being closer to five weeks old than eight weeks, due to sizeable inventories at the beginning of the Iran conflict. But as those inventories are drawn down, the buffer shrinks. If energy prices remain elevated through the rest of the year, the headwinds for risk assets could become more pronounced. The report does not model this scenario in detail, nor does it specify the conditions under which the current factor rotation would reverse.

A second unresolved question is the concentration risk within the Growth and Momentum factors. The report notes that leadership in Momentum is driven by AI- and Tech-exposed names, and that Growth's gains are concentrated in communications, electrical equipment, and semiconductors. This is not a broad-based rally. It is a narrow one, and narrow rallies are inherently fragile. If a single large-cap Tech name misses earnings or guides lower, the entire factor complex could suffer. The report does not address how investors should size their exposure to account for this concentration risk.

Finally, the report's stance on Quality raises a deeper strategic question. Quality is being downgraded because its defensive attributes are out of favor in a risk-on market. But if the macro headwinds materialize, Quality could become attractive again very quickly. The report does not provide a clear trigger for when Quality would be upgraded, leaving investors to guess at the conditions that would justify a rotation back into defensive exposures.

## A Decision Framework for Factor Allocation in the Current Regime

For investors who want to translate the report's analysis into actionable decisions, the following framework may be useful. It is not a prescription, but a way of thinking about factor allocation in a regime that is simultaneously risk-on and macro-constrained.

The first decision is about conviction. The report's strongest signals are on Growth and Momentum, where the combination of earnings momentum, supportive positioning, and favorable seasonality creates a compelling case. These are the factors where the fundamental and technical data align most clearly. For investors with a high-conviction mandate, these should be the core overweight positions.

The second decision is about hedging. The downgrade of Quality to Negative and the neutral stance on Yield suggest that the report sees limited value in defensive exposures. But this does not mean that investors should ignore them entirely. Given the unresolved macro risks, a small allocation to Quality or Yield could serve as a hedge against a reversal in risk appetite. The report's own data shows that Quality has offered little downside protection in the recent risk-on environment, but that is precisely because the environment has been risk-on. If the environment changes, the hedge value could re-emerge.

The third decision is about size. The report's positive stance on Large-over-Small is one of its most durable convictions. For investors who are overweight small caps, the data suggests that this is a tactical trade, not a strategic allocation. The structural advantages of large caps in terms of operating leverage, margin dynamics, and earnings visibility are unlikely to erode quickly. Any small-cap exposure should be sized accordingly, with an understanding that the fundamental gap is wide.

The fourth decision is about timing. The upgrade to High-over-Low Volatility is cautious, and the report explicitly notes that valuation is stretched. For investors who missed the rebound, chasing it now carries significant risk. The better approach may be to wait for a pullback, or to use options-based strategies to express a view on volatility without taking outright directional exposure.

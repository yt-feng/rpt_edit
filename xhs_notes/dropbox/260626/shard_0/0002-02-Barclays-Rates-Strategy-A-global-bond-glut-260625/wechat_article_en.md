# The Global Bond Glut Has Replaced the Savings Glut: Long-Term Yields Are Structurally Higher, and Tactical Issuance Management Will Not Reverse This

The narrative that has dominated fixed income markets for two decades—a global savings glut compressing yields—has given way to a far less comfortable reality. A global bond glut is now the defining structural force, and it points to persistently higher long-term yields across developed markets. This is not a cyclical repricing that will reverse when central banks cut rates. It is a regime change driven by the collision of sustained fiscal deficits with a fundamentally more price-sensitive buyer base. The long end of the curve is clearing at a higher term premium, and that premium is unlikely to compress without a material macro shock.

Long-end yields across the G7 have repriced to multi-decade highs, with the weighted average 30-year yield approaching 5 percent. But the composition of those yields tells a different story than the last time yields were at these levels. In 2007, elevated yields were driven by high expected short-term rates. Today, the expected path of short rates is significantly lower—roughly 3.1 percent nominally, compared to 4.4 percent in 2007. The difference is being made up by term premia. Investors are now demanding greater compensation for bearing duration risk, and that compensation comes from two distinct sources: a rate-driven term premium and a fiscal risk premium. This structural shift in how duration risk is priced is the single most important development for sovereign bond markets.

The implications for portfolio construction, liability management, and fiscal strategy are profound. A sustained rally in long-end yields would require either a material growth shock, a decisive return to policy easing or balance-sheet support, or a meaningful restoration of fiscal credibility. Absent one of these, the bias remains toward structurally higher long-end yields. Tactical measures—shortening issuance tenors, adjusting auction calendars, or altering remits—can slow the pace of cheapening, but they cannot change the direction of travel.

## The Composition of Long-End Yields Has Fundamentally Shifted, and the Fiscal Risk Premium Is Now the Dominant New Variable

The most striking change in the decomposition of 30-year Treasury yields is the emergence of a large and persistent fiscal risk premium. In the mid-1990s and early 2000s, Treasury yields traded at a discount to swap rates—meaning investors were willing to pay a premium for the perceived safety and liquidity of government debt. In 2000, 30-year Treasuries traded at roughly 70 basis points below swaps. Today, they trade at approximately 70 basis points above swaps. That swing of 140 basis points represents a fundamental repricing of the fiscal credibility of the United States.

This fiscal premium is not simply a function of current deficit levels. It reflects the market's assessment of the trajectory of fiscal policy. A 1 percentage point increase in the average deficit-to-GDP ratio pushes 30-year Treasury yields roughly 15 basis points higher relative to swap rates. The market is currently discounting an average 10-year budget deficit of 6 to 6.5 percent of GDP, broadly in line with the Congressional Budget Office's latest forecast. But that forecast may already be optimistic. Tariff revenues have fallen short of assumptions, and higher interest costs are compounding the deficit dynamic. Every 10 basis point increase in yields adds approximately $380 billion to 10-year cumulative deficits. With 5-year yields having risen about 50 basis points year-to-date, the implied additional fiscal burden is nearly $1.9 trillion over a decade.

The critical question is whether the fiscal risk premium has further room to expand. The evidence suggests it does. If the budget deficit were to average 7 percent of GDP instead of 6 percent, 30-year swap spreads would tighten to approximately negative 85 basis points—implying even higher Treasury yields relative to swaps. The Treasury's current issuance strategy, which skews heavily toward the front end, is likely suppressing the fiscal premium for now. But that strategy is not sustainable. As the Treasury signals its intention to increase auction sizes in longer-dated maturities, the fiscal risk premium should reassert itself.

## The Buyer Base Has Become Structurally More Price-Sensitive, Eliminating the Inelastic Demand That Once Suppressed Term Premia

The era of inelastic demand for duration is over. Foreign official holdings of U.S. Treasuries have steadily declined over the past decade. The Federal Reserve's holdings have unwound significantly from their COVID-era peak. The result is that private investors—both foreign and domestic—now own a much larger share of the outstanding stock of government debt. These investors are fundamentally different from the central banks and official institutions that once absorbed duration without regard to price. Private investors demand compensation for risk, and they are getting it.

The implications extend beyond the United States. Across Europe, the United Kingdom, and Japan, the same pattern is visible. Central bank and official sector support has weakened, and the buyer base has become more price-sensitive. In Europe, this vulnerability to fiscal stress re-emerges under weaker macro conditions. In the United Kingdom, the market's sensitivity to fiscal narratives has increased markedly. In Japan, fiscal expansion is feeding into inflation risk premia. The common thread is that sovereign curves now need to clear at higher term premia because the marginal buyer demands it.

The diminished perception of bonds as reliable diversifiers compounds this dynamic. During the post-GFC to pre-COVID era, the negative correlation between equities and long-duration bonds provided a powerful source of insurance value, which manifested itself as depressed term premia. That correlation has now reverted to positive territory, as inflation has become the primary macro concern. Bonds no longer provide the same diversification benefit, and investors require higher compensation to hold them. Were growth to dominate the macro narrative again, the term premium might fall. But that is not the current trajectory.

## Neutral Rate Estimates Are Too Anchored to the Post-GFC Era, and the Saving-Investment Imbalance Points Higher

The expected path of short rates embedded in long-term yields is likely too low. The consensus estimate of the nominal neutral rate—around 3.1 percent—is anchored to the post-GFC, pre-COVID era of subdued growth and low inflation. That era is over. The U.S. economy has grown approximately 2.5 percent in the first half of the year. Job gains have picked up. Core PCE inflation has risen to 3.4 percent year-over-year. The saving-investment imbalance is shifting toward excess investment, which points to a higher neutral rate for a given estimate of trend growth.

The evidence is visible in the data. Hyperscaler investments have risen to approximately 2.2 percent of GDP and are expected to reach 3 percent by 2028. Meanwhile, the personal savings rate has fallen dramatically, to about 2.5 percent, as household net worth has grown. These trends point to a rising neutral rate. Model-based estimates of the nominal neutral rate average approximately 3.5 percent, with the higher end nearing 4 percent. The lower end, which includes the Federal Reserve's long-run dot, is likely lagging economic performance. The market should be discounting a nominal neutral rate near 3.5 percent, with the risk of skewing toward 4 percent. All else equal, this should push long-term yields even higher.

The rate-driven term premium—the compensation for bearing duration risk beyond the expected path of short rates—has already normalized to levels above pre-COVID and likely above pre-GFC norms. But there is scope for it to move higher. Rate volatility remains below pre-GFC levels, and the Federal Reserve's shift toward a more data-reactive policy framework could increase volatility. The Fed's balance sheet is also a source of upside risk. While the Fed's Treasury holdings as a share of outstanding debt are below pre-GFC levels, the portfolio is much longer in duration. Approximately 40 percent of Fed holdings have maturities greater than 10 years, compared to 10 percent in 2006. As the portfolio shortens passively through reinvestment, it could add approximately 10 basis points per year to the term premium.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Threshold for a Regime Change and the Interaction Between Fiscal and Monetary Credibility

The report makes a compelling case for structurally higher long-end yields, but it leaves several important questions unresolved. The first is the threshold at which higher yields themselves become the catalyst for a macro regime change. At what level do long-end yields trigger a material growth shock that forces central banks back into easing? The report acknowledges that a sustained rally would require a material growth shock, but it does not specify the yield level at which such a shock becomes probable. This is a critical gap for investors trying to position for tail risks.

The second unresolved question is the interaction between fiscal and monetary credibility. The report decomposes the fiscal risk premium as the spread between Treasury yields and swap rates. But this spread is not purely fiscal. It also reflects liquidity premia, regulatory factors, and the relative supply of hedging instruments. More importantly, the fiscal risk premium is not independent of monetary policy. If the Federal Reserve were to signal a willingness to accommodate fiscal expansion through yield curve control or quantitative easing, the fiscal risk premium could compress significantly. The report assumes that such a scenario is unlikely, but it does not fully explore the conditions under which it could materialize.

The third question is the asymmetry of the adjustment. The report focuses on the upward pressure on yields from the bond glut. But what about the downward pressure from private investors who see current yield levels as attractive? The 5 percent threshold on 30-year yields has already drawn demand from investors seeking to lock in long-duration yields. The report notes this but does not fully reconcile it with the structural bearish thesis. The answer may lie in the distinction between tactical positioning and structural flow, but the report does not provide a framework for distinguishing the two.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Your Duration Positioning

For investors trying to navigate this environment, the report implies a clear decision framework organized around three questions. First, what is your view on the neutral rate? If you believe the consensus estimate of 3.1 percent is correct, then current long-end yields already embed a significant term premium, and you may be tempted to add duration. If you believe the neutral rate is closer to 3.5 or 4 percent, then long-end yields still have room to rise, and you should remain underweight duration.

Second, what is your view on fiscal credibility? The fiscal risk premium is the most variable component of long-end yields. If you believe that fiscal discipline will be restored—through spending cuts, tax increases, or faster growth—then the fiscal premium could compress, and long-end yields could rally. If you believe the deficit trajectory will worsen, then the fiscal premium has further to expand, and long-end yields will continue to cheapen.

Third, what is your view on the correlation between bonds and equities? If you believe inflation will remain the dominant macro concern, then bonds will not provide diversification, and the term premium will remain elevated. If you believe growth concerns will re-emerge, then the negative correlation could return, and the term premium could compress. Your answer to this question determines whether you view current term premia as fair compensation or as a structural headwind.

These three questions are not independent. They interact in ways that the report does not fully explore. But they provide a framework for disciplined decision-making in an environment where the old rules no longer apply.

# The Data Centre Supply Chain Has Flipped: Infrastructure No Longer Dictates Deployment — NVIDIA Does

The global data centre industry has reached an inflection point where the traditional hierarchy of infrastructure planning has been inverted. For decades, the physical plant — power systems, cooling architecture, and building design — determined what computing equipment could be deployed inside a facility. That era is over. The report from a recent expert call with a senior executive at the world's largest power and cooling supplier for data centres reveals a structural shift: NVIDIA's architecture and equipment now dictate the entire ecosystem infrastructure, from power delivery to cooling methodology to deployment timelines. This reversal has profound implications for which companies will capture value in the next wave of data centre buildout, and which will be left supplying commoditized components.

The strategic stakes could not be higher. The pipeline of data centre projects globally is now outstripping production capacity, especially on the infrastructure side. Lead times for critical components such as silicon carbide transistors have ballooned from 10-20 weeks to 40 weeks. Global uninterruptible power supply solutions remain tight, with the only manufacturer that produces its own batteries already sold out for the next 18 months. These are not temporary bottlenecks — they represent a structural constraint that will reshape competitive dynamics across the data centre value chain. The companies that locked in supply relationships and forward orders before the crunch will have a decisive advantage.

What makes this moment different from previous cycles is the source of demand. The buildout is no longer driven by general enterprise IT expansion or even cloud adoption. It is driven by artificial intelligence workloads that require radically different power densities, cooling approaches, and deployment speeds. The report indicates that the market for pre-fabricated data centre systems — which reduce deployment time by 50-60% — is seeing the strongest demand the supplier has ever experienced. The company plans to grow its modular data centre capacity fourfold over the next six months. This is not a cyclical uptick; it is a structural transformation of how data centres are built and operated.

## The Supply Chain Bottleneck Has Shifted from Capacity to Components, and the Most Strategic Players Are Those with Vertically Integrated Power Solutions

The report makes clear that not all bottlenecks are created equal. The most severe constraint is not in data centre shells or even in transformers, but in the semiconductor components that enable power conversion and management. Silicon carbide transistors, which are critical for efficient power conversion in high-density AI racks, have seen lead times quadruple. This is not a general semiconductor shortage — it is a specific pinch point in a narrow but essential part of the power train.

The implications for competitive positioning are stark. Companies that manufacture their own power components, particularly batteries and power electronics, have an inherent advantage. The report notes that the supplier in question is the only global provider of uninterruptible power supply systems that manufactures its own batteries. That vertical integration has become a strategic asset rather than merely a cost optimization. With battery systems sold out for the next 18 months, any data centre operator or colocation provider that did not secure forward supply is now facing a multi-quarter delay in commissioning new capacity.

The strategic lesson is clear: in a supply-constrained environment, the companies that control their own critical components will be the ones that can deliver. This is particularly true for the power train, which represents approximately 8-10% of total data centre build cost. Within that, the uninterruptible power supply and battery system represents only 0.5% of total cost, yet it is one of the longest-lead-time items. This mismatch between cost share and supply criticality creates a trap for operators who optimized for lowest upfront cost rather than supply assurance.

## The Cooling Technology Transition Is Accelerating, and the Winners Will Be Those That Have Already Shifted from Retrofits to New Builds

The report reveals a decisive shift in cooling technology that many market participants may be underestimating. The supplier has nearly stopped all air-cooling deployments for new projects, aside from a few self-build hyperscalers with existing pipeline commitments. From colocation providers, there are almost no new air-cooled deployments. The expectation is a 5% to 10% reduction in air-cooled projects through 2028-2030.

This is not simply a preference for liquid cooling — it is a recognition that AI workloads have fundamentally changed the thermal profile of data centres. The report distinguishes between liquid-to-air cooling systems, which are typical in retrofit scenarios, and direct liquid cooling for new builds. The expectation is that liquid-to-air cooling will decline as operators shift from retrofitting existing facilities to building new ones designed from the ground up for liquid cooling.

The geographic dimension adds another layer of strategic nuance. Air cooling with evaporative systems remains more efficient in warmer, drier climates, while water-based cooling suits cooler, more humid environments. This means that the optimal cooling strategy varies by region, and operators with a global footprint cannot simply standardize on one approach. The report highlights the benefits of closed-loop cooling systems that offset additional energy requirements with renewable sources, suggesting that the next frontier of competitive differentiation will be in cooling system design that integrates with broader energy and sustainability strategies.

For investors and operators, the key question is which cooling technologies will become commoditized and which will remain sources of differentiation. The report suggests that the supplier's liquid-to-air cooling systems are becoming less strategically important, while its next-generation high-voltage direct current power and cooling systems, developed in close partnership with NVIDIA, represent a more proprietary and defensible position.

## Pre-fabricated and Modular Data Centres Are Reshaping Deployment Economics, Creating a Clear Divide Between Hyperscaler Strategies and Enterprise Approaches

The most significant operational insight from the report concerns the growing divergence in data centre deployment strategies. The supplier's pre-fabricated data centre systems come in two types: power train systems, which provide pure grey space (power only), and modular data centres, which combine white and grey space (computing and power). The newly launched AI modular solution, which reduces deployment time by 50-60%, is seeing unprecedented demand.

The report reveals a fascinating strategic split among major players. AirTrunk, a leading Asia-Pacific data centre operator, follows the modular approach. In contrast, NXT and MAQ, which serve a more enterprise-skewed customer base, mostly use traditional construction methods. Among hyperscalers, Microsoft and AWS skew toward traditional methods because they have standardized global design blueprints that are difficult to adapt to modular approaches. This is why hyperscalers use colocation for regional deployments but self-build in the United States, which represents approximately 90% of their total capacity.

This strategic divide has important implications. Hyperscalers with standardized global blueprints are effectively trading deployment speed for consistency and global manageability. But in a world where AI infrastructure is evolving rapidly, that trade-off may become increasingly costly. The modular approach allows for faster iteration, easier scaling, and potentially lower capital at risk. The report suggests that the best-positioned colocation providers will be those with the strongest supply relationships and forward orders locked in — which is precisely the strategy that modular builders can execute more effectively.

The open question is whether the hyperscalers' preference for traditional methods will persist as AI workloads continue to drive demand for faster deployment. If the competitive pressure to bring AI capacity online accelerates, even Microsoft and AWS may be forced to adopt modular approaches for their non-US deployments. This would represent a significant shift in the colocation market and could benefit operators that have already invested in modular capabilities.

## The Next Technology Leap — 800-Volt Direct Current Power — Will Reshape Rack Economics, But Adoption Will Lag in Key Markets by 12 to 18 Months

Perhaps the most forward-looking insight from the report concerns the next-generation high-voltage direct current power and cooling system developed in close partnership with NVIDIA. This 800-volt direct current system is designed specifically for AI data centres where space within racks is the primary constraint. The technology directs more power into racks using less copper and fewer cables — two cables instead of the current four or five.

The economics are compelling. With higher voltage reducing current, total system cost could fall by 30% to 40%. However, the electronics would need to be strengthened to handle the higher voltage, potentially increasing the power electronics cost from 0.5% of total build cost to 1.5% to 2.0%. This is a classic trade-off: higher upfront component costs for significantly lower total system costs.

The report notes that the latest technology typically lags the Australian market by 12 to 18 months. The expectation is that 800-volt direct current will be deployed in International Electrotechnical Commission countries — including Australia, Europe, and Southeast Asia — by the first half of 2027. This timeline has strategic implications for operators planning new builds today. If they lock into current-generation power distribution, they may face a competitive disadvantage within two years when new facilities come online with 800-volt direct current systems.

The partnership with NVIDIA is particularly significant. It suggests that the power architecture of next-generation AI data centres is being co-designed with the chipmaker, not independently specified by the data centre operator or the power equipment supplier. This reinforces the central thesis of the report: NVIDIA is now the driving force in data centre infrastructure decisions, not the other way around.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Regulatory Wild Card and the Feasibility of Compliance

The report raises a critical regulatory question that it does not fully resolve. It discusses a proposed regulation from the Australian Energy Market Operator that would require a battery energy storage system at the front of any data centre facility larger than 100 megawatts. The cost implication is significant — more than double the current cost structure for power delivery. The report notes that currently no uninterruptible power supply system globally complies with this requirement.

This is a potentially transformative regulatory development that could reshape the economics of large-scale data centre deployment in Australia and possibly set a precedent for other markets. The report does not provide clarity on whether the regulation is likely to be ratified, what the timeline might be, or whether technology solutions are in development to meet the requirement. It also does not address whether the regulation would apply to existing facilities or only new builds.

For operators and investors, this regulatory uncertainty creates a strategic dilemma. Investing in large-scale Australian data centre capacity today carries the risk that a future regulatory requirement could fundamentally alter the cost structure. Conversely, waiting for regulatory clarity could mean missing the current wave of AI-driven demand. The report leaves this tension unresolved, which is appropriate given the genuine uncertainty.

## A Decision Framework for Data Centre Investors and Operators

Based on the insights from the report, a structured decision framework emerges for evaluating data centre investments and operational strategies. The framework has four dimensions.

First, supply chain positioning: assess whether the operator or supplier has secured forward orders for critical components, particularly power electronics, batteries, and silicon carbide transistors. The companies that locked in supply before the current crunch will have a multi-year advantage. Those that did not face significant execution risk.

Second, cooling technology trajectory: evaluate the operator's cooling strategy against the expected shift from retrofits to new builds. Operators still deploying air cooling for new facilities may be building in technological obsolescence. Those investing in liquid cooling designed for new builds are better positioned for the AI workload environment.

Third, deployment methodology: determine whether the operator uses modular or traditional construction. Modular approaches offer faster deployment, lower capital at risk, and greater flexibility. Traditional approaches offer consistency and global standardization but may become increasingly costly as deployment speed becomes a competitive differentiator.

Fourth, technology adoption timeline: assess exposure to the 800-volt direct current transition. Operators planning facilities today should consider whether they will be competitively disadvantaged when next-generation power distribution becomes available in 2027. The partnership between the power supplier and NVIDIA suggests that 800-volt direct current is not a speculative technology but a confirmed product in development.

# The European Industrial Capex Story Is Not Over. It Is Rotating.

The dominant narrative in European capital goods for the past two years has been a simple one: invest in companies riding the electrification and data-center megatrend, and avoid everything else. That thesis has produced strong returns, but it has also created a valuation dispersion that is now extreme. The stocks tied to grid, power, and hyperscaler capex trade at significant premiums to their own history and to the broader market, while a wide swath of industrial companies have been left behind, de-rated on fears of a global growth slowdown. The critical question for the second half of 2026 is whether that dispersion is about to narrow.

A global investment bank report published in late June 2026 makes a compelling case that it is. The report argues that the risk of an industrial demand collapse has diminished substantially, that capex "green shoots" are emerging in the United States and Asia, and that the market may be underestimating the resilience of short-cycle industrial demand. At the same time, the report identifies a new and underappreciated risk: cost inflation is back, and this time it is hitting electrical companies harder than their mechanical peers. The implication is clear. The next leg of the cycle may not be about avoiding risk. It may be about selective broadening out.

This argument matters now because the macro backdrop has shifted. The Iran conflict introduced a layer of geopolitical uncertainty that punished cyclical industrial stocks indiscriminately. Mechanical companies saw their valuations de-rate by 10 to 25 percent, regardless of underlying business quality. Electrical companies, by contrast, saw upgrades and multiple expansion, reinforcing the thematic trade. But the report suggests that the indiscriminate sell-off in mechanicals may have gone too far. If the data on US durable goods orders, Japan machine tool orders, and Asian capital goods imports is any guide, the industrial recovery is real, and it is broadening beyond the narrow cluster of data-center beneficiaries.

The report does not claim that a broad-based boom is imminent. It does not dismiss the risks in European auto, O&G, pharma, or chemicals capex. What it does is present a more nuanced picture of a market that is becoming increasingly two-tiered, and it offers a framework for navigating that divide.

## The Risk of an Industrial Demand Collapse Has Receded, and the Recovery Is Broadening Beyond Data Centers

The most important structural insight in the report is that the industrial demand base is more resilient than many investors assume. This is not a forecast of accelerating growth across the board. It is an argument that the downside is limited because the base is already low. For many short-cycle companies, volumes have not grown relative to 2019 levels. The European industrial production recession of 2023 and 2024 already happened. The question now is not whether the cycle will turn down further, but whether the nascent recovery can sustain itself.

The evidence for this view is strongest in the United States and Asia. US Dodge manufacturing starts, a leading indicator of machinery investment, are beginning to grow again after years of headlines about reshoring that produced little tangible activity. This is reminiscent of the 2021-2022 cycle, when manufacturing starts preceded a significant upturn in capital goods orders. Similarly, Asia ex-China capital goods imports have picked up, which historically leads machinery gross fixed capital formation. The report's Asia economists see an emerging capex cycle in the region, driven by investments in battery, electronics, and general machinery.

The short-cycle data reinforces this picture. US durable goods orders have continued to accelerate into the second quarter of 2026, suggesting that any pre-buying effect from tariff or price-rise concerns has not yet snapped back. Japan machine tool orders, a bellwether for global factory automation demand, continue to deliver attractive growth rates. Fanuc's factory automation orders saw a strong year-on-year increase in the first calendar quarter of 2026. Airtac's sales growth in Asia remained above 20 percent year-on-year in April. These are not data points that suggest a pending industrial collapse.

The report does acknowledge a real risk: the pre-buying debate. Several companies, including Metso, Kion, Sandvik, and Carrier, have explicitly cited pull-forward effects from price increases or geopolitical uncertainty. This raises the question of whether the current demand resilience is borrowed from future quarters. But the report's interpretation is measured. It notes that while pre-buying is visible, there are also early signs of improved underlying sentiment. The pattern is not uniform, and the risk is worth monitoring, but it does not yet justify a bearish stance on the sector as a whole.

## Cost Inflation Is the Underappreciated Risk for 2026, and It Disproportionately Hits Electricals

If the demand picture is more resilient than feared, the cost picture is more challenging than priced in. This is the report's most contrarian insight. The consensus view has been that margin expansion is on track for capital goods companies, supported by volume recovery and pricing power. The report challenges that assumption by highlighting a sharp re-acceleration in input costs.

The data is striking. Raw material costs for industrial companies are expected to increase by 27 percent year-on-year in 2026, compared to just 2 percent in 2025. Electricity costs are forecast to rise by 25 percent. Wages and component costs are both trending higher. The report estimates that capital goods companies will need to raise prices by approximately 4 percent in 2026 just to offset these headwinds. That is a non-trivial ask in an environment where end-market demand is recovering but not booming.

The distribution of this cost pressure is uneven. Electrical companies are more exposed than mechanical companies. This is a reversal of the pattern seen in 2022, when backlog-driven mechanical companies faced margin pressure from raw material and freight inflation. This time, the cost pressure is concentrated in electricals, where raw materials and components represent a higher share of cost of goods sold. Companies like Signify, Vestas, Weir, and Rexel are flagged as having particular exposure.

This insight has direct portfolio implications. The market has rewarded electrical companies for their structural growth exposure to electrification and data centers. But if cost inflation erodes margins in 2026, those earnings upgrades may stall. The report's preferred electrical names, such as Schneider and Legrand, are selected partly because they offer the best balance of growth versus valuation and have the pricing power or self-help programs to offset cost pressure. But the broader message is clear: the electrical trade is no longer as clean as it was.

## The Valuation Dispersion Between Electricals and Mechanicals Creates an Opportunity for Selective Re-Rating

The report's analysis of valuation dispersion is where the strategic argument becomes most actionable. Since the start of the Iran conflict, electrical companies have mostly seen small upgrades to consensus expectations and, in many cases, multiple expansion. Prysmian, Signify, and ABB led the re-rating. Mechanicals, by contrast, have seen 93 percent of companies in the coverage universe de-rate, with the majority falling by 10 to 22 percent.

This divergence has created a valuation landscape that is unusually polarized. Thematic stocks tied to grid, power, and data centers are all relatively expensive versus their own ten-year averages. At the same time, a significant number of mechanical and process-oriented companies are trading at levels that discount a much weaker macro environment than the data currently supports.

The report does not argue for a wholesale rotation out of electricals and into mechanicals. It is more precise than that. It identifies specific names where the risk-reward has become attractive: Atlas Copco, Knorr Bremse, and Spirax are highlighted as stocks for a broadening out. Siemens is cited as having a more positive risk-reward into 2027. Epiroc is preferred over Sandvik in upstream mining, with the view that consensus margin downgrades are done and a multi-year order growth story is intact.

The key decision framework here is not about sector allocation in the abstract. It is about distinguishing between companies that are de-rated for structural reasons and those that are de-rated for cyclical reasons that are likely to reverse. The report implies that many mechanicals fall into the latter category. Their de-rating reflects a generalized fear of global growth that the hard data in the US and Asia does not support. If that fear fades, the re-rating could be significant.

## The Report Leaves Two Critical Questions Unanswered, and They Define the Risk

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves two questions unresolved. The first is the durability of the US industrial recovery. The data on durable goods orders, ISM new orders, and Fastenal daily sales is encouraging. But the report also notes that US ISM new orders moderated in March before recovering in May. The path is not linear, and the risk of a second-half slowdown cannot be dismissed. The pre-buying debate adds another layer of uncertainty. If a significant portion of current demand is pulled forward, the second half of 2026 could see a demand vacuum that the report's constructive thesis does not fully account for.

The second unresolved question is the trajectory of European industrial demand. The report is candid about the challenges. German infrastructure spending is falling behind expectations even after the fiscal plan. European auto OEMs continue to lose market share globally, depressing a major source of European industrial capex. The German IFO business expectations have deteriorated, and while they are not yet at 2022 lows, the trend is concerning. The report prefers US exposure over European exposure at this point in the cycle. But that preference implicitly assumes that the US recovery is real and sustained. If it is not, the European discount may be a value trap rather than an opportunity.

These are not weaknesses in the report. They are honest acknowledgments of the limits of forecasting. The report's value lies not in pretending to know the answers, but in providing the framework to navigate the uncertainty. Investors who understand these open questions can position themselves to benefit from the broadening out thesis while hedging against the downside scenarios.

## The Decision Framework for Investors Is a Three-Layer Filter

The report's analytical structure can be translated into a practical decision framework for any investor in European capital goods. The framework has three layers, each of which narrows the opportunity set.

The first layer is geographic exposure. The report's data clearly favors North America and Asia over Europe. US durable goods orders, Japan machine tool orders, and Asian capital goods imports are all showing momentum. European industrial production is moving sideways at best. The first filter, therefore, is to favor companies with significant revenue exposure to the US and Asia, and to be cautious on names that are heavily dependent on European end markets, particularly European auto and German infrastructure.

The second layer is end-market exposure. Within the US and Asia, not all end markets are equal. Hyperscaler and semiconductor capex remain the strongest drivers. Process industries like O&G, pharma, and chemicals face higher risk. The report's analysis of order growth trends shows that electrification order growth has likely peaked, while process and construction are in no-man's land. The second filter is to favor companies exposed to data centers, factory automation, and mining, and to avoid or underweight names tied to European auto, European construction, and process industries where capex is under pressure.

The third layer is cost exposure. This is the layer most investors are likely to underweight. The report's cost inflation analysis shows that electrical companies face a sharper headwind than mechanicals in 2026. Within electricals, the winners will be those with pricing power, self-help programs, or favorable mix shifts that can offset input cost inflation. Schneider and Legrand are cited as examples. Within mechanicals, the winners will be those that have already de-rated to attractive levels and have end-market tailwinds that can support volume recovery and margin expansion. Atlas Copco, Knorr Bremse, and Spirax fit this profile.

An investor applying this three-layer framework would end up with a portfolio that is overweight US and Asia-exposed mechanicals with selective exposure to electricals that have pricing power, and underweight European-exposed names, process-oriented companies, and electricals that lack the ability to pass through cost increases.

## The Real Opportunity May Be in Names the Market Has Left Behind

The report's most valuable contribution is to challenge the consensus that the only game in town is the electrification and data-center trade. That trade has worked, and it may continue to work for companies with the best execution and valuation. But the report's data suggests that the opportunity set is broader. The mechanical companies that have been de-rated by 10 to 22 percent are not all value traps. Some of them are exposed to end markets that are recovering, and their valuations now reflect a level of pessimism that the hard data does not justify.

The broadening out thesis is not without risk. The pre-buying debate, the European macro weakness, and the potential for cost inflation to erode margins are all real concerns. But the report's framework allows investors to navigate these risks selectively. The names that pass all three layers of the filter offer a compelling risk-reward, particularly if the US and Asian industrial recoveries continue to gain traction.

The question that remains open is whether the market will reward this broadening out. The report suggests that it should, but conviction requires more than data. It requires seeing the data in the context of a cycle that is still in its early stages. The full report provides the charts and the detailed company-level analysis that can turn this framework into a specific portfolio.

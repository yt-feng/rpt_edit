# The Market Is Already Pricing a 2026 Oil Glut, and the Security Premium Is Eroding Faster Than Most Investors Realize

Spot Brent crude fell 8% last week. That sharp decline was not triggered by a single headline, but by a cascade of signals: reported progress in US-Iran negotiations, a US waiver authorizing Iranian oil sales, a rapid recovery in Persian Gulf exports, and a continuing liquidation of crude positioning. The market is not merely reacting to the news flow; it is extrapolating a trajectory. It is fast-forwarding through the current supply disruption and pricing a future where the disruption is resolved, supply is abundant, and the security premium that has underpinned long-dated oil prices is unwinding.

The speed of this repricing should concern any investor who has assumed that geopolitical risk will keep oil structurally supported. The data shows that Gulf exports have already returned to 63% of normal levels. The visible flow through the Strait of Hormuz is recovering faster than many models predicted. Empty tanker capacity in the Persian Gulf continues to rise, with 52 million barrels of additional empty tanker capacity entering the Gulf last week alone, against 53 million barrels of loadings leaving. The market sees the bottlenecks loosening, and it is acting on that view before the bottlenecks are fully cleared.

This is not a market in shock. It is a market in anticipation. The question is whether that anticipation is correct, or whether it is discounting risks that remain very real. The answer has profound implications for portfolio positioning across commodities, equities, and currencies over the next 12 to 18 months.

## The Recovery in Gulf Flows Is Real, and the Market Is Discounting a Full Normalization

The most striking data point in the report is that total Gulf exports have rebounded to 63% of normal levels on a 7-day moving average basis. That is a dramatic recovery from the initial disruption. The visible flow through the Strait of Hormuz has increased significantly, partly because more ships are crossing and partly because a larger share of those crossings are now "visible" as vessels have reactivated their Automatic Identification Systems.

The market is treating this recovery as the beginning of a trend, not a temporary blip. The logic is straightforward: if the system can restore nearly two-thirds of normal flows within weeks, the remaining one-third may be restored even faster. The absence of any reported attacks on ships in or around Hormuz since June 11 reinforces this narrative. The security environment is stabilizing, and the market is pricing that stabilization as durable.

But the recovery is not uniform. The UAE is reportedly already at 85% of pre-war export levels, while Iraq has halted production at the West Qurna 2 oil field due to a shortage of empty tankers. The shipping constraint is real, even if it is not binding at the aggregate level. The report notes that empty tanker capacity continues to rise, but the distribution of that capacity matters. If the available tankers are in the wrong locations or are not suitable for the specific grades being produced, the recovery can stall at the margin.

The market is implicitly betting that these frictions are temporary and that the logistics will sort themselves out. That is a reasonable base case, but it is not a certainty. The risk is that the market has already priced a full normalization that may take longer to achieve than the current data suggests.

## The US Waiver on Iranian Oil Is a Game Changer for Supply Expectations, But the Real Story Is What It Reveals About Sanctions Effectiveness

The US Treasury issued a 60-day waiver authorizing sales of Iranian oil, including oil transported on previously sanctioned vessels. The report estimates this waiver may unlock up to around 60 million barrels of Iranian oil currently sitting on water. That is a significant overhang, and the market is treating it as additional supply that will arrive relatively quickly.

The deeper implication, however, is more structural. The report notes that Iran grew its production by 1.3 million barrels per day, or 59%, between 2020 and 2025, even under the US "maximum pressure" campaign. This suggests that US sanctions are not a very tight constraint on Iranian production growth. The waiver is not a one-time event; it is a signal that the enforcement regime has limits.

If the market concludes that Iranian oil will find its way to market regardless of sanctions, then the effective supply capacity of the global oil system is higher than the headline numbers suggest. This erodes the scarcity premium that has been embedded in oil prices. It also raises questions about the effectiveness of sanctions as a policy tool for influencing oil markets. The report does not answer these questions definitively, but the data points are clear: Iran has demonstrated an ability to grow production under sanctions, and the waiver provides a legal channel for that oil to reach buyers.

The demand side of the Iranian equation is also important. Asia, and China in particular, will likely remain the main buyers of Iranian crude. The EU and UK sanctions on Iranian oil and vessels remain in place, so the waiver does not open up European demand. But Chinese demand has been softening, with crude imports down 4.5 million barrels per day year-over-year despite the moderation in prices. The availability of Iranian oil may not find eager buyers at current volumes if Chinese demand continues to weaken.

## Demand Scarring Is Becoming Visible, and It May Be More Persistent Than the Market Expects

The report provides several data points that suggest demand is not just temporarily disrupted but structurally changing. Global visible stock draws have slowed sharply in June to -1.8 million barrels per day from -5.5 million barrels per day in May. Oil in transit has increased by 137 million barrels since late March, offsetting nearly 60% of the initial drop. Some of this is a normalization of supply chains, but some of it reflects genuine demand softening.

The most concerning signal is from China. Chinese crude imports continue to decline and are now down 4.5 million barrels per day year-over-year, despite the moderation in prices. This is not a price-driven response; it is a structural shift. Chinese refinery runs have declined by 1.5 million barrels per day from a year ago. The EV penetration rate in China has accelerated by 11.4 percentage points since the beginning of the Hormuz shock. Globally, EV penetration is up 3.4 percentage points.

The report describes this as potential "demand scarring." The argument is that the supply shock and resulting price spike may have permanently accelerated demand destruction in certain sectors and regions. Consumers and businesses that were already considering alternatives to oil may have used the disruption as a catalyst to make the switch. Once those decisions are made, they are not easily reversed even if prices come back down.

The market is pricing a return to normal demand patterns, but the data suggests that some of the demand losses may prove sticky. This is particularly true in Asia, where the combination of economic slowdown, EV adoption, and structural shifts in industrial activity is reducing oil intensity. If the market is wrong about demand, then even a full supply recovery will not lead to balanced markets. It will lead to surplus.

## The Security Premium Is Unwinding, and Long-Dated Prices May Be Repriced Downward

Perhaps the most important insight in the report is not about spot prices but about the structure of the forward curve. The report notes that the market is increasingly challenging its prior assumption that long-dated prices need to incorporate a sticky security premium. This is a fundamental shift in how investors are valuing oil.

The security premium was the idea that oil prices needed to be high enough to compensate for the risk of supply disruptions. It was a risk premium embedded in the term structure. But the market has now observed something remarkable: the system absorbed the sharpest oil supply shock ever without a complete breakdown. The elasticity of demand and the flexibility of Mideast supply channels proved to be greater than expected.

The report makes this point explicitly: "the ability of the oil market (via elasticity of demand and flexibility of Mideast supply channels) to respond to the sharpest oil supply shock ever has been a key surprise that is likely eroding this security premium." This is not just an academic observation. It has real implications for portfolio construction. If the security premium is eroding, then long-dated oil prices should be lower than previously assumed. The market is already reflecting this, with Dubai prompt timespreads flipping into contango.

The report also highlights a downside scenario where Brent trades around $60 per barrel by December 2026. That is a scenario that the market is assigning increasing probability to. It is not the base case, but it is no longer a tail risk. It is a scenario that investors need to take seriously.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Supply Recovery Speed and Demand Elasticity

The report provides extensive data on supply recovery and demand trends, but it does not fully resolve a critical question: how fast can supply recover relative to the speed at which demand is changing? The answer to this question determines whether the market is correctly pricing a surplus or is overcorrecting.

The supply recovery is happening quickly, but it is not complete. The report estimates that normalization in Gulf exports to pre-war levels would require an 8.5 million barrel per day increase in Hormuz flows from current levels. That is a large number. The market is assuming it will happen, but the logistical constraints are real. The shipping constraint, while not binding at the aggregate level, is binding for specific producers like Iraq. The availability of empty tankers in the right locations at the right time is not guaranteed.

On the demand side, the stickiness of demand losses is uncertain. The report provides evidence of demand scarring in China and in EV adoption, but it does not quantify how much of the demand loss is permanent versus temporary. The distinction matters enormously for the balance outlook. If half of the demand loss is permanent, then the market is right to price a surplus. If most of it is temporary, then the current selloff is overdone.

The report leaves this question open, and it is the most important analytical question for investors to resolve. The answer will depend on data that is not yet available: how quickly Chinese imports recover, whether EV adoption continues at the accelerated pace, and whether industrial activity in Asia rebounds.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios and the Signals to Watch

For investors trying to navigate this environment, the report provides enough data to construct a decision framework. The key variables are the speed of supply recovery and the persistence of demand losses. These two variables define three scenarios.

The first scenario is a rapid supply recovery combined with sticky demand losses. This is the scenario the market is currently pricing. In this scenario, Brent drifts toward $60 by late 2026, the contango deepens, and the security premium continues to erode. The investment implication is to underweight oil exposure and to favor assets that benefit from lower input costs, such as downstream refiners and transportation stocks.

The second scenario is a rapid supply recovery combined with a demand rebound. In this scenario, the market has overcorrected. Prices stabilize and then rise as demand recovers faster than supply can adjust. The investment implication is to overweight oil and energy equities, particularly those with low production costs and exposure to the spot market.

The third scenario is a slow supply recovery combined with sticky demand losses. This is the most challenging scenario for investors. Prices remain volatile, the term structure is unstable, and the risk of sharp moves in either direction is high. The investment implication is to reduce directional exposure and focus on relative value trades, such as calendar spreads or crack spreads.

The signals to watch are clear. On the supply side, watch the weekly data on Gulf exports and Hormuz flows. If the recovery stalls or reverses, the market will need to reprice. On the demand side, watch Chinese crude imports and EV penetration data. If Chinese imports stabilize or rebound, the demand scarring narrative weakens. If they continue to decline, the bear case strengthens.

The report provides the data to monitor these signals. The charts on Gulf exports, oil in transit, and visible stock draws are updated weekly. The demand data on Chinese imports and EV penetration is available with a lag but is directionally informative.

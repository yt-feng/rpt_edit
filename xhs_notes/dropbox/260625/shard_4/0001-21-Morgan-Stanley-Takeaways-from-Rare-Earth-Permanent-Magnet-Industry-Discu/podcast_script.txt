ZH_A: 你最近有没有关注一个特别有意思的趋势？稀土永磁体，特别是中国以外的产能建设，好像突然成了热点。

ZH_B: 对，我们刚看完一份某外资投行的深度研报，里面请了一位有35年行业经验的专家来聊。结论很直接：美国到2028年的钕铁硼产能规划，如果都能落地，可能完全覆盖它现在的进口量。

ZH_A: 等等，这个“如果都能落地”听起来就很有悬念。美国现在每年进口多少？

ZH_B: 大概8500吨。而他们规划的产能，保守估计是每年28000吨，甚至可能到35000吨。所以数字上看，是够的。但问题是，能不能真的造出来。

ZH_A: 那瓶颈在哪里？是缺矿还是缺技术？

ZH_B: 都不是。专家说，最关键的卡脖子环节，是“金属和合金供应”。你看，从稀土氧化物变成金属，再到做成薄带铸锭的合金，这一块在美国几乎还是空白。而且，磁铁制造本身是个工艺活，需要自动化、质量管控、熟练工人，还有客户认证。

ZH_A: 所以就是有工厂，不一定能产出合格的产品。那要打通这个链条，需要多久？

ZH_B: 对有经验的公司，从设计到满产，专家估计要三年。但对新入行的公司，可能六年都打不住。这就是第一个关键洞察：产能数字很漂亮，但执行风险非常大。

ZH_A: 那既然要搞自给自足，有没有可能在材料本身下手，比如减少稀土用量，或者干脆不用稀土？

ZH_B: 这就是第二个有意思的地方了。专家说，过去二十年，钕铁硼磁铁里的总稀土含量，只从32%降到了30.5%，几乎没变。真正的降幅，发生在重稀土上，比如镝和铽。

ZH_A: 哦？所以降重稀土比较容易？

ZH_B: 对，通过晶界扩散、晶界工程这些技术，在某些应用里重稀土用量能降50%以上。但核心的钕和镨，很难替代。它们就是磁铁化学结构的基石。

ZH_A: 那无稀土磁铁呢？比如铁氮化合物，听起来很酷。

ZH_B: 专家认为，不太可能出现大规模替代。铁氧体、铝镍钴这些老牌磁铁，还有铁氮化合物，都有自己的特定应用场景。但钕铁硼在价格和性能上的平衡，目前还是最好的。更可能的结果是，不同磁铁类型按照不同的速度增长，而不是谁把谁完全取代。

ZH_A: 所以稀土磁铁的地位，短期内还是很稳固。那中国在这个链条里的角色呢？我们一直在说供应链风险。

ZH_B: 专家明确说，中国的稀土管控已经是一个国家安全议题了。虽然国防用的磁铁量很小，可能一年不到500吨，但它会跟民用工业应用混在一起，导致出口审批变得非常复杂。

ZH_A: 而且中国磁铁的价格优势还是很大，对吧？

ZH_B: 没错。早年间，中国磁铁的售价只有美国的75%左右。现在，海外生产的钕铁硼磁铁，可能还是比中国货贵50%。专家说，西方企业可以在三年内追平质量，但成本差距，短期内几乎不可能抹平。

ZH_A: 那这对下游企业意味着什么？比如汽车厂商和风电公司。

ZH_B: 意味着他们已经开始行动了。报告里提到，客户已经在转向非中国供应商，进行认证和采购。比如在电动汽车里，一块钕铁硼磁铁大概重2公斤多，这是刚性需求。在风电里，虽然美国只有1%到2%的风机用钕铁硼，但中国可能高达50%。如果这些需求都要找中国以外的替代，压力会非常大。

ZH_A: 听起来，整个产业链的重构才刚刚开始。那这份报告里，还有什么问题是没有完全讲清楚的？

ZH_B: 有，至少两个。第一，这些规划的产能，到底有多少能通过客户认证，真正变成出货量？报告没有给出一个确定的数字。第二，西方企业到底能不能在成本上找到突破口？是依靠自动化，还是靠新的工艺？这些在报告里也是留了悬念的。

ZH_A: 明白了。所以想了解更完整的推演和具体的时间表，还是得去看报告全文。

ZH_B: 没错。我们只是把最核心的框架和悬念提了出来。如果大家对完整的逻辑和后续的行业推演感兴趣，欢迎加入我们的社群，或者直接阅读这份报告的完整版。今天我们就先聊到这里。

EN_A: You know, I’ve been hearing a lot about the rare earth magnet supply chain lately, especially the push to build capacity outside of China. What’s the real story here?

EN_B: We just looked at a deep dive from a global investment bank. They brought in an expert with 35 years in the industry. The headline is that US neodymium magnet capacity could reach 28,000 tons per year by 2028. That’s more than enough to cover the current US imports of about 8,500 tons.

EN_A: That sounds like a done deal. So the US is about to be self-sufficient?

EN_B: Not so fast. The key word is “if executed.” The report makes it clear that having a plant on paper is very different from actually producing a qualified magnet. The biggest bottleneck isn’t the factory itself, it’s the metal and alloy supply chain.

EN_A: What do you mean by that?

EN_B: The US is missing the step where you turn rare earth oxide into metal, and then into a strip-cast alloy. That’s a huge gap. Plus, magnet manufacturing requires serious process control, automation, and customer qualification. For an experienced company, the expert says it takes about three years from design to full output. For a startup, it could be six years.

EN_A: So the numbers look great, but the execution risk is real. What about the magnets themselves? Can we just use less rare earth material, or find a substitute?

EN_B: That’s the second big insight. The total rare earth content in a neodymium magnet has only dropped from 32% to 30.5% over twenty years. Almost no change. The real savings have come from heavy rare earths like dysprosium and terbium, which have been cut by more than 50% in some applications through grain boundary engineering.

EN_A: So we can reduce the expensive heavy stuff, but the core neodymium and praseodymium are still essential?

EN_B: Exactly. The expert says neodymium and praseodymium are structurally embedded in the magnet chemistry. Substitution is very limited. And as for rare-earth-free magnets, like iron-nitride, they are still application-specific. They fill a niche, but neodymium magnets still have the best price-performance position.

EN_A: So the supply chain risk is real, and the material itself is hard to replace. That puts China’s export controls in a different light.

EN_B: Absolutely. The expert views China’s controls as a national security issue. Even though defense demand is tiny, maybe under 500 tons a year, it gets mixed in with commercial applications. That complicates all export approvals.

EN_A: And the cost gap is still massive, right? Chinese magnets are cheaper.

EN_B: Yes. Historically, Chinese prices were about 75% of US prices. Today, non-Chinese magnets are still about 50% more expensive. The expert believes Western producers can close the quality gap in about three years, but the cost gap is much harder to bridge. China has decades of process learning and lower costs across every manufacturing stage.

EN_A: So what does this mean for car makers and wind turbine companies? Are they already changing their behavior?

EN_B: They are. The report says customers are already moving toward non-Chinese producers for qualification and immediate supply. For example, in an EV, a neodymium magnet weighs about two kilograms. That’s a hard requirement. In wind, while only 1-2% of US turbines use these magnets, in China it’s 30-50%. If those customers want to diversify, the pressure on non-Chinese supply is enormous.

EN_A: So the whole industry is in the middle of a major shift. But the report doesn’t answer everything, right?

EN_B: Right. There are at least two big open questions. One, how much of that planned capacity will actually pass customer qualification and become real output? The report doesn’t have a firm number. Two, can Western producers ever find a way to close the cost gap? Through automation or new processes? That’s left as a cliffhanger.

# ByteDance's Soda Music Reveals a Strategic Contradiction: Sacrificing Music Revenue to Build an AI Advantage

The most revealing detail in the recent expert call on ByteDance's Soda Music is not the 50 million daily active users or the 50% growth deceleration. It is the revenue structure: 70% from advertising, only 30% from subscriptions. This is the inverse of what a mature music-streaming business looks like. Tencent Music Entertainment derives 60-70% of its online music revenue from subscriptions. Soda Music is not trying to be a better music company. It is building a different kind of asset entirely—one that serves ByteDance's broader ecosystem ambitions, not the music industry's traditional profit model.

This matters now because the music-streaming landscape in China is approaching a strategic inflection point. The era of pure user acquisition through traffic diversion is ending. The convertible user pool from Douyin is nearly saturated. The next phase of competition will be defined not by who has the largest catalog, but by who can leverage data, artificial intelligence, and ecosystem integration to create defensible advantages that traditional music companies cannot replicate. Soda Music's current strategy—constrained copyright budgets, advertising-first monetization, and internal competition with sibling platforms—looks like a weakness only if you measure it against conventional music industry metrics. If you measure it against ByteDance's broader strategic objectives, it begins to look like a calculated bet on a very different future.

The expert call, hosted by a global investment bank's China internet team, provides the raw data points. But the strategic narrative that emerges from these numbers tells a story that is both more interesting and more unsettling for incumbent players. Soda Music is not trying to win the music wars. It is trying to make music irrelevant as a standalone profit center and turn it into a data engine for AI-driven content creation.

## Soda Music's advertising-first model is not a monetization failure but a strategic choice to serve as an ecosystem moat, not a profit center

The conventional wisdom in music streaming has long been that subscription revenue is the holy grail. It is recurring, predictable, and high-margin. Advertising revenue, by contrast, is volatile, lower-margin, and dependent on macroeconomic cycles. By this logic, Soda Music's 70% advertising mix looks like a weakness. But the expert's commentary reveals that Soda Music is operating under a deliberate mandate from ByteDance: maintain break-even operations, not maximize profit. This is a fundamentally different objective function.

Soda Music's role within the ByteDance ecosystem is defensive. It exists to absorb excess advertising inventory from Douyin, satisfying users' listening needs without forcing them to leave the ByteDance universe. This is a moat strategy, not a profit strategy. The music platform is a retention tool for the broader advertising ecosystem. Every minute a user spends on Soda Music is a minute they are not spending on a competitor's platform, and every ad impression served on Soda Music is one that would otherwise go unsold on Douyin.

This logic explains the strict copyright budget discipline. Why spend heavily on exclusive premium content to drive subscription conversion when the platform's primary purpose is not to generate subscription revenue but to keep users within the ecosystem? The expert notes that Soda Music's catalog covers only 75% of nationally consumed songs, well below TME's 90-95%. This gap is intentional. Exclusive content drives subscription conversion, but subscription conversion is not the goal. The goal is to maintain a sufficient music experience to prevent user churn while keeping costs low enough to achieve break-even.

The strategic implication is clear: Soda Music is not competing with TME on TME's terms. It is competing on ecosystem terms, where music is a feature of a larger platform, not a standalone product. This is a model that traditional music companies cannot replicate without building their own social media ecosystems, which is unlikely.

## The 60-70% user overlap between Soda Music and Tomato Music reveals that ByteDance is running a deliberate multi-brand experiment, not suffering from internal inefficiency

Internal competition is usually viewed as a sign of organizational dysfunction. When two products under the same parent company compete for the same users, it seems wasteful. But the expert's description of Soda Music and Tomato Music—separate teams, separate P&Ls, separate copyright portfolios, all drawing from the same Douyin traffic pool—suggests something more deliberate.

ByteDance is running a controlled experiment in music platform differentiation. Soda Music targets younger, male audiences with an interest-based recommendation algorithm. Tomato Music targets older, female audiences aligned with web novels and romance dramas, leveraging the broader Tomato ecosystem that includes novels, audio content, and short dramas. The 60-70% user overlap is not evidence of inefficiency; it is evidence that both platforms are successfully capturing the same core Douyin audience while testing different content strategies and monetization approaches.

The absence of a near-term merger plan, as confirmed by the expert, reinforces this interpretation. ByteDance is gathering data on two distinct approaches to music streaming within its ecosystem. Over time, the winning strategy will likely be scaled, and the losing strategy will be folded or retired. But for now, the company is willing to tolerate the duplication of costs to generate proprietary insights about user behavior, content preferences, and monetization pathways that no single-platform strategy could provide.

This multi-brand approach also creates a hedge against regulatory risk. If one platform attracts regulatory scrutiny—for example, around copyright infringement or fan economy practices—the other platform can continue operating without disruption. The expert noted that Soda Music is unlikely to pursue large-scale fan economy ventures due to regulatory pressure, but Tomato Music's older, female demographic may be less exposed to the same regulatory risks. The portfolio approach allows ByteDance to pursue different regulatory strategies simultaneously.

## The AI music opportunity is Soda Music's hidden strategic asset, but the report leaves critical questions about monetization and copyright unresolved

The expert's most provocative claim is that Soda Music may have an advantage in AI music development. The logic is compelling: ByteDance's substantial AI investments, proximity to internal AI teams, and Douyin's vast data ecosystem create a unique capability to understand user preferences and discover trends. Traditional music companies have data on what users listen to. ByteDance has data on what users watch, share, comment on, and create. This is a fundamentally richer dataset for training AI music models.

But the report also reveals a tension that the expert call did not fully resolve. Soda Music is already grappling with AI-generated remixes that circumvent paywalls, particularly appealing to lower-tier users unwilling to pay for subscriptions. The expert described a "cat-and-mouse dynamic" where rights holders lack the enforcement resources to address all infringing content. This is both a threat and an opportunity.

The threat is obvious: if AI-generated content cannibalizes licensed content revenue, the already constrained copyright budget becomes even harder to justify. The opportunity is less obvious but potentially more significant: Soda Music could become the platform where AI-generated music is not just tolerated but actively curated and monetized. If ByteDance can develop AI music tools that generate high-quality, original content that does not infringe on existing copyrights, it could bypass the traditional licensing model entirely. This would be a structural disruption to the music industry.

The report does not answer several critical questions. How will ByteDance navigate the copyright implications of AI-generated music? Will the company invest in developing proprietary AI music models, or will it focus on curating user-generated AI content? Can AI music generate sufficient advertising revenue to justify the investment, or will it require a subscription model that Soda Music has so far avoided? These are the unanswered questions that make the full report worth reading.

## The decision framework for investors: evaluate music-streaming platforms on ecosystem integration, AI capability, and cost structure, not just user metrics

Traditional music-streaming valuation relies on metrics like monthly active users, average revenue per user, subscription penetration, and content catalog size. The Soda Music case suggests that these metrics are becoming less relevant for platforms that operate within larger ecosystems. A new decision framework is needed.

First, assess ecosystem integration. How deeply is the music platform embedded in the parent company's broader user experience? Soda Music's value to ByteDance is not measured by its standalone revenue but by its contribution to Douyin's ad inventory utilization and user retention. A platform that is tightly integrated into a larger ecosystem can afford lower standalone profitability because it creates value elsewhere.

Second, evaluate AI capability. The music industry's next competitive battleground will be AI-generated content. Platforms with access to large, diverse datasets and strong AI research teams have a structural advantage. ByteDance's AI investments give Soda Music a potential edge that is not captured in traditional music industry analysis. Investors should ask: does the platform's parent company have a credible AI strategy, and how does music fit into that strategy?

Third, analyze cost structure. The ability to maintain break-even operations with a constrained copyright budget is a strategic asset, not a weakness. Platforms that can deliver a sufficient user experience without spending heavily on exclusive content have more flexibility to invest in AI and ecosystem development. Soda Music's cost discipline is a form of strategic optionality that traditional metrics undervalue.

Fourth, consider the regulatory landscape. Platforms that avoid deep involvement in fan economy ventures and large-scale offline events face lower regulatory risk. Soda Music's focus on small-scale, sponsor-funded offline activities in lower-tier cities is a prudent strategy that reduces exposure to regulatory crackdowns on fan culture.

## The most important question the report leaves open is whether ByteDance will eventually merge its music platforms and what that would mean for the competitive landscape

The expert confirmed that no near-term merger is planned, but the logic of the multi-brand experiment suggests that a merger is inevitable at some point. When ByteDance has collected sufficient data to determine which platform strategy is superior, the weaker platform will likely be absorbed or shut down. The question is not whether a merger will happen, but when and on what terms.

A merger would create a single music platform with a combined user base that could challenge TME's dominance. But it would also concentrate ByteDance's music assets in a way that could attract regulatory attention. The current structure, with two separate platforms operating independently, may be a deliberate strategy to avoid creating a single entity large enough to trigger antitrust concerns.

The report also leaves open the question of how Soda Music will evolve its subscription revenue mix. The expert noted that subscription revenue is only 30% of total revenue, but did not indicate whether ByteDance has plans to increase this share. If Soda Music can develop a compelling AI music offering that drives subscription conversion, the revenue mix could shift significantly. But if the platform remains advertising-first, its long-term profitability will depend on the health of the broader digital advertising market.

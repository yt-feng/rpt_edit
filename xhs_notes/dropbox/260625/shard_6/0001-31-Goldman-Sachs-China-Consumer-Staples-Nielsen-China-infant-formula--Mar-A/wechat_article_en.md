# China’s Infant Formula Market Is Not Simply Declining—It Is Restructuring, and the Winners Will Be Those Who Master Channel-Specific Strategy

The Chinese infant milk formula market has entered a phase that looks like a downturn but functions as a selective reset. Offline sales contracted by 8.2% year-on-year in March-April 2026, a slight acceleration in decline from the 7.7% drop in January-February. Online sales followed an even steeper trajectory, falling 19% year-on-year in May, dragged primarily by a 26% plunge on JD.com against a difficult comparable base. At first glance, these numbers suggest a market in uniform retreat. That interpretation is misleading.

What is actually unfolding is a market that is fragmenting by channel, by brand tier, and by pricing strategy. The aggregate data masks a critical divergence: some players are losing share rapidly while others are quietly consolidating their positions. The volume decline of 9% year-on-year in offline channels tells only half the story. The other half is that average selling prices grew by 0.9% year-on-year in the same period, suggesting that the remaining demand is shifting toward higher-value products. This is not a collapse. It is a structural rebalancing driven by demographic headwinds, shifting consumer trust, and a competitive landscape that rewards operational precision over broad market presence.

For investors and strategists tracking China’s consumer staples sector, the key question is no longer whether the market is shrinking. It is shrinking. The real question is which brands are positioned to capture disproportionate share of the remaining, more discerning demand. The data from the latest tracking period offers several clear signals, but also leaves important questions unresolved. This article unpacks those signals, interprets their strategic implications, and identifies the analytical gaps that will determine the next phase of competition.

## The Offline Channel Is Splitting into Two Distinct Battlegrounds, and Brands Must Choose Where to Fight

The offline market is not a single channel. It is two: modern trade, which includes hypermarkets and supermarkets, and mom-and-baby stores, which remain the primary touchpoint for many Chinese parents. In March-April 2026, modern trade sales fell by 20% year-on-year, while mom-and-baby stores declined by only 7%. This is not a new gap, but it is widening. In January-February, the respective declines were 21% and 6.7%. The modern trade channel is deteriorating faster, and the trend is accelerating.

This divergence matters because the two channels serve different consumer segments and require different go-to-market strategies. Modern trade is a high-traffic, low-engagement channel where price competition is fierce and brand loyalty is thinner. Mom-and-baby stores, by contrast, rely on personal relationships, in-store education, and trust. A brand that treats both channels with the same strategy will underperform in both.

The data supports this interpretation. Yili, the domestic giant, posted 1.9% year-on-year sales growth in the offline channel in March-April, reversing a 0.9% decline in January-February. The recovery was driven by a 2.8% year-on-year increase in mom-and-baby store sales. Yili is winning where trust and relationship-building matter most. Meanwhile, Feihe, the market share leader, saw its offline sales decline narrow from 15% to 12% year-on-year, but it still lagged the market average of 8.2%. Feihe’s strength in modern trade is being eroded by the channel’s structural decline, and its recovery in mom-and-baby stores has been insufficient to offset it.

The strategic implication is clear: brands must treat offline not as one market but as two, and allocate resources accordingly. Those that continue to invest in modern trade without a differentiated value proposition will face diminishing returns. Those that double down on mom-and-baby stores with localized marketing, retailer partnerships, and service-oriented sales models will be better positioned to capture the higher-margin, more loyal customer base that remains.

## Online Sales Are Not Just Slowing—They Are Revealing a Divide Between Platform Strategy and Brand Strength

The online channel presents an even more complex picture. Total online infant formula sales on Tmall, Taobao, and JD combined fell 19% year-on-year in May, a sharp deterioration from the 14% decline in April. But the aggregate number conceals a dramatic platform-level divergence. On Tmall and Taobao, sales declined only 2% year-on-year in May. On JD, they fell 26%. The gap is not random. It reflects different consumer behaviors, different promotional calendars, and different brand positions on each platform.

JD’s decline is particularly striking because it is the platform most associated with trusted, premium, and authentic products. A 26% year-on-year drop suggests that even the most confident consumers are pulling back, or that the platform’s heavy reliance on the 618 shopping festival last year created a base effect that is now punishing current comparisons. But this explanation is incomplete. If the base effect were the sole driver, we would expect a more uniform decline across brands on JD. Instead, the data shows significant variation.

Aptamil, the Danone-owned brand, grew 6% year-on-year on the combined Tmall, Taobao, and JD platform in May, despite the overall market contraction. Bellamy’s grew 19%. These are not small brands. They are established players with strong online presence. Meanwhile, Feihe fell 31%, A2 fell 25%, and Wyeth fell 45%. The divergence is too large to be explained by base effects alone. It suggests that online market share is being reallocated based on brand trust, product differentiation, and the ability to navigate platform-specific dynamics such as algorithm changes, promotional requirements, and logistics partnerships.

For strategists, the takeaway is that the online channel is no longer a single growth engine. It is a fragmented ecosystem where platform selection, promotional timing, and brand equity interact in ways that are not always predictable. Brands that succeed online will need to develop platform-specific strategies rather than a one-size-fits-all e-commerce approach. The days of simply listing a product on all three platforms and expecting uniform results are over.

## Market Share Gains Are Concentrated Among a Few Players, and the Pattern Suggests a Winner-Take-Most Dynamic Is Emerging

When we look at market share movements in the offline channel, a clear pattern emerges. Large domestic companies collectively held approximately 62% of the offline market in March-April, roughly flat versus January-February. But within that group, the distribution shifted. Yili and Feihe gained share, while Mengniu and Biostime lost ground. Among multinational brands, the leading group increased its collective share by 0.3 percentage points to 23.8%, driven by gains from Danone and A2, while Wyeth and Nestle lost share.

This is not a market where everyone is losing equally. It is a market where share is concentrating among a handful of players that have demonstrated the ability to maintain or grow sales despite the overall contraction. Yili’s return to positive offline growth, A2’s steady share gains in the mom-and-baby channel, and Danone’s resilience across both online and offline are all signals of competitive strength.

The concentration dynamic has second-order implications for the entire value chain. Retailers, who are themselves under pressure, will increasingly allocate shelf space and promotional support to brands that can generate consistent foot traffic and higher margins. This creates a virtuous cycle for the winners and a death spiral for the losers. Brands that lose share will find it harder to regain retailer attention, which will accelerate their decline. The market is not just shrinking; it is consolidating, and the consolidation is happening faster than the aggregate numbers suggest.

For investors, this means that the relevant metric is not total market growth, but individual brand trajectory. A brand that is gaining share in a declining market is demonstrating real competitive advantage. A brand that is losing share in the same market is signaling structural weakness that may not be reversible.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer Why Some Brands Are Resisting the Downturn While Others Are Not

The tracking data provides a detailed picture of what is happening, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. Why is Yili able to return to positive offline growth while Feihe, the market share leader, continues to decline faster than the market? Why is Aptamil growing online while A2, another premium multinational, is declining? Why is Biostime, which posted 22.4% year-on-year offline growth in March-April, losing market share sequentially?

These questions point to deeper strategic factors that the current data cannot fully explain. Product formulation, marketing spend, distributor relationships, and regulatory positioning all play roles that are not captured in sales growth and market share tables. For example, the report notes that A2’s online sales fell 25% year-on-year in May, potentially due to a supply cut since April. But supply cuts are a symptom, not a cause. The underlying question is why A2 chose or was forced to cut supply. Was it a deliberate inventory management decision, or a sign of weaker demand that the company is trying to manage?

Similarly, the report highlights that Feihe’s omni-channel tracked sales declined 24% year-on-year in March-April, worse than its reported financial performance. This discrepancy between tracked data and reported results is a puzzle that deserves deeper investigation. Is Feihe losing share in the tracked channels but gaining in untracked ones? Or is the tracked data overstating the decline due to methodological differences?

These unresolved questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are natural limits of any tracking-based analysis. But they are also the most important questions for investors and strategists to pursue. The answers will determine whether current market share movements are temporary or structural.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Market: Focus on Channel Fit, Share Momentum, and Platform Strategy

For readers trying to translate this analysis into actionable strategy, a three-part decision framework is more useful than a list of data points. The first dimension is channel fit. Brands must assess whether their product, pricing, and go-to-market model are aligned with the channels that are growing or stabilizing, not the ones that are declining. For most premium brands, this means prioritizing mom-and-baby stores over modern trade, and Tmall over JD, at least in the current environment.

The second dimension is share momentum. In a declining market, absolute sales growth is less important than relative share movement. A brand that is losing share is fighting a losing battle, regardless of its sales level. A brand that is gaining share, even if its absolute sales are flat or slightly down, is building a stronger competitive position that will pay off when the market stabilizes.

The third dimension is platform strategy. The online channel is not a monolith, and the divergence between Tmall and JD is likely to persist or widen. Brands need to understand the specific dynamics of each platform, including the impact of promotional events, algorithm changes, and competitor activity. A strategy that works on Tmall may fail on JD, and vice versa.

Applying this framework to the current data yields a clear set of watchpoints. Yili and A2 are showing positive share momentum in the offline channel, particularly in mom-and-baby stores, and deserve close attention. Danone’s Aptamil is demonstrating resilience online, suggesting strong brand equity and effective platform management. Feihe, despite its market-leading position, is showing warning signs: declining share year-on-year, a slower recovery than the market, and a significant gap between tracked and reported performance. These patterns do not guarantee future outcomes, but they provide a structured way to evaluate risk and opportunity.

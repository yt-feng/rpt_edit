# The China Property Recovery Is Real but Narrow: Why the K-Shape Demands a New Investment Framework

The headline numbers from last week's China property data look troubling. Secondary sales in the Iceberg 10-city index dropped to near-zero year-over-year growth. Primary sales registrations fell 23% year-over-year. The sector's share prices tumbled 13%, sharply underperforming the Hang Seng Index. Any investor looking at this snapshot might conclude the long-awaited recovery has already stalled. That conclusion would be wrong—and dangerously misleading for portfolio positioning.

The real story is not about a recovery that is failing. It is about a recovery that is succeeding exactly where it matters most, and failing everywhere else. The Dragon Boat Festival holiday distorted the weekly data, masking what is actually a strengthening trend in tier-1 cities. When adjusted for the holiday calendar, 10-city and tier-1 secondary sales were up 15% and 10% year-over-year respectively. More importantly, secondary listings in tier-1 cities have now fallen 2.6% from their March peak, with Shanghai's inventory dropping another 1.1% week-over-week. This is the supply-side tightening that ultimately drives price stabilization.

The reason this matters now is that the market is at a critical inflection point. Hong Kong home prices have already hit the full-year target range of 10-15% appreciation, rising 10.4% year-to-date. Mainland China's tier-1 cities have now reported three consecutive months of positive month-over-month secondary price increases, according to the May NBS data. Yet the broader 70-city index continues to deteriorate, with month-over-month declines marginally widening from -0.23% to -0.26%. The divergence is not a temporary anomaly. It is the structural pattern that will define China property investing for the next 12 to 18 months.

## The Holiday Distortion Confirms the Trend, It Does Not Invalidate It

The most common mistake in interpreting weekly property data is treating holiday-affected readings as signals of underlying momentum. The week ending June 21 saw the Dragon Boat Festival fall on a Thursday and Friday, when sales registrations are systematically lower. The result was a sharp year-over-year decline in both primary and secondary transactions. But this is a mechanical effect, not a demand collapse.

The correct analytical approach is to compare the holiday week against the same holiday week in the prior year. When that adjustment is made, the picture reverses entirely. Primary sales registrations across 60 cities were up 60% year-over-year against the comparable Dragon Boat Festival period in 2025. Secondary sales in the Iceberg 10-city index, which had been showing 13-22% year-over-year growth in the preceding four weeks, would have shown 15% growth in tier-1 cities after holiday adjustment.

The more important signal is what happened to listings. Secondary inventory in tier-1 cities has been consistently declining for weeks, with Shanghai showing the most aggressive drawdown at -1.1% week-over-week. This is not a small fluctuation. A persistent decline in listings at a time when transaction volumes remain elevated creates the classic conditions for price stabilization: fewer sellers meeting steady demand. The Centraline asking price index, while dipping slightly from 17.8 to 17.3, remains at levels consistent with a market that is finding its floor, not falling through it.

The implication is clear: the recovery in tier-1 secondary markets is real, it is broad-based across Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen, and it is being driven by the supply side as much as the demand side. Investors who dismiss the weekly data as noise risk missing the structural improvement that is already underway in the highest-quality markets.

## The K-Shape Is Not a Risk, It Is the Investment Thesis

The May NBS data tells a story that every serious property investor must internalize: China's housing market is no longer a single market. It is a K-shaped bifurcation between tier-1 cities and the rest of the country. All four tier-1 cities reported positive month-over-month secondary price increases for the third consecutive month. Meanwhile, the broader 70-city average continued to decline, and tier-3 and tier-4 cities showed no signs of stabilization.

This K-shape is not a temporary divergence that will eventually converge. It is the new equilibrium. The reasons are structural and self-reinforcing. Population flows continue to concentrate in tier-1 and leading tier-2 cities. Policy support has been disproportionately targeted at these markets through relaxed purchase restrictions, lower mortgage rates, and down payment reductions. The supply of new housing in tier-1 cities remains constrained by land availability, while lower-tier cities face a multi-year overhang of unsold inventory.

The investment implications are profound. For equity investors, the K-shape means that a rising tide will not lift all boats. The report's top picks—COLI, CR Land, Jinmao, and CR Mixc—are all developers with concentrated exposure to tier-1 and quality tier-2 markets. The underperformers in last week's selloff, such as Longfor at -17% and A-Living at -20%, reflect market concerns about their exposure to weaker markets. This is not a sector where beta strategies work. It is a sector where stock selection based on geographic exposure is the primary driver of returns.

For credit investors, the K-shape creates a similar dynamic. The JACI China HY Property index rose 0.3% last week, resuming its uptrend after a brief correction. But this index masks enormous dispersion. The report's credit recommendations—LNGFOR '29s at 9.9% yield-to-maturity and SHUION '29s at 9.1%—are names with what the report calls "IP transformation stories, solid debt servicing ability, and decent valuations." In a K-shaped market, the ability to service debt depends not just on a developer's balance sheet but on the quality of its asset base. Developers with prime tier-1 assets have refinancing options—such as Seazen's planned RMB 2 billion private REIT tap—that are simply unavailable to lower-tier players.

## Hong Kong Has Already Priced in Its Recovery, Raising Questions for the Second Half

Hong Kong's residential market presents a different but equally important analytical challenge. The home price index rose another 0.6% week-over-week, bringing year-to-date appreciation to 10.4%. This already hits the full-year target range of 10-15% that was set at the beginning of the year. The question is no longer whether Hong Kong property will recover. It is whether the recovery has further to run.

The leading indicators are sending mixed signals. The Centa Salesman Index (CSI) moderated from 69.6 to 68.2, still above the 50 threshold that signals positive sentiment and likely price increases. The Centaline Valuation Index (CVI) actually rose from 82.7 to 86.2, suggesting that valuers are becoming more optimistic. But secondary transactions in the top 35 estates fell to just 43 units, the lowest since Chinese New Year and down 47% year-over-year. Weather was a factor, but this level of transaction volume is not consistent with a market that is about to accelerate.

The report's outlook for the second half is for "slower price growth of less than 5%." This seems reasonable given where valuations now stand. But the more interesting question is what drives the next leg of the cycle. The first half recovery was powered by policy relaxation, pent-up demand, and a low base. The second half will require genuine economic momentum, employment growth, and income expectations to sustain price levels. These are harder to engineer than policy changes.

For investors with Hong Kong exposure, the strategic question is whether to take profits on the year-to-date gains or to hold for the remaining upside. The report's top picks—CKA, Sino, Swire Properties, HKL, JM, and CKH—are all high-quality names that should outperform in a slower-growth environment. But the risk-reward has clearly shifted. The easy money in Hong Kong property has been made. The second half will test whether the recovery has fundamental support or was simply a policy-driven bounce.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Transmission Mechanism from Secondary to Primary Markets

The report provides excellent data on secondary market dynamics in tier-1 cities, but it leaves a critical question unresolved: how and when does the secondary market recovery transmit to the primary market? This is not an academic question. It determines whether developers can clear their primary inventory at profitable prices, which in turn determines their cash flow, their ability to service debt, and ultimately their equity valuation.

The data shows that primary sales registrations remain weak, with the 60-city index down 23% year-over-year in the latest week and the 4-week rolling average turning negative at -1%. Even after holiday adjustment, the improvement is modest. Tier-1 primary sales have been positive year-over-year, but the growth rates are far below what secondary market strength would suggest.

There are several possible explanations. First, homebuyers may be favoring the secondary market because it offers immediate occupancy and more negotiable pricing. Second, developers may be holding back new supply to avoid competing with secondary listings. Third, the buyer profile may be shifting toward owner-occupiers rather than investors, and owner-occupiers tend to prefer secondary homes in established neighborhoods.

The report does not resolve this question, and it is a genuine analytical gap. If the secondary-to-primary transmission remains weak, then even a successful secondary market recovery may not translate into developer profitability. This would explain why the sector's share prices fell 13% last week despite fundamentally positive data: the market is pricing in a recovery that benefits homeowners but not developers. Investors need to watch the primary sales data in tier-1 cities over the next 4-8 weeks for signs of transmission. If it does not materialize, the equity thesis for developers becomes significantly weaker.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the K-Shape

For investors trying to position in this environment, a structured decision framework is essential. The K-shape means that generic "long China property" or "short China property" positions are unlikely to work. The dispersion within the sector is too large. The following framework can help investors identify where the opportunity actually lies.

The first dimension is geographic exposure. Investors should segment developers and credits by the percentage of their asset base in tier-1 cities versus lower-tier markets. The threshold should be at least 60% tier-1 exposure to qualify for a positive view. Anything below that is a show-me story that requires evidence of stabilization in the specific markets where the developer operates.

The second dimension is balance sheet strength and refinancing capability. In a K-shaped market, the ability to access capital markets is a competitive advantage. Developers with public REIT platforms, such as Seazen with its private and planned public REITs, have a funding channel that is unavailable to peers. Similarly, developers with investment-grade credit ratings or strong parent company support can refinance at reasonable costs. The credit market is already pricing this divergence, with top-tier names trading at single-digit yields while weaker names remain distressed.

The third dimension is the nature of the asset base. The report emphasizes "IP transformation stories" as a credit selection criteria. This refers to developers who are converting their commercial property portfolios into recurring income streams through REIT listings, asset management, or operational improvements. In a market where development margins are compressed, the ability to generate stable cash flow from existing assets becomes a key valuation differentiator.

The fourth dimension is valuation. The selloff last week created opportunities in names that were punished indiscriminately. Longfor fell 17% and A-Living fell 20%, yet both have significant tier-1 exposure and solid operational platforms. The question is whether the selloff reflects genuine deterioration in their fundamentals or simply a risk-off move in the sector. For investors with a 6-12 month horizon, the latter creates entry points.

## The Questions That Will Define the Next Phase

The data in this report raises several questions that only the coming weeks will answer. Will the Dragon Boat Festival-adjusted sales momentum sustain into July and August, or was it a one-off bounce? Can Shanghai's inventory drawdown continue without a significant increase in new supply? Will Hong Kong's price growth slow to the expected less than 5% range, or will it surprise to the upside or downside? How will the credit market respond to Seazen's private REIT tap, and will other developers follow suit?

These are not questions that can be answered by a single week's data. They require sustained observation of the trends that the report tracks: real-time sales, listing volumes, asking prices, manager confidence, and credit spreads. The report's value lies not in any single data point but in the framework it provides for interpreting the flow of information.

# The Shanxi Accident Has Reshaped the Global Coking Coal Market, and the Structural Shift Is Only Beginning

The coking coal market has entered a new regime. A single mine accident in China's Shanxi province has triggered a cascade of effects that go far beyond the headline production loss of 15 to 20 million tonnes. The conventional framing of this event as a temporary supply disruption misses the deeper story. What we are witnessing is a structural reset in Chinese domestic production capacity, a realignment of global trade flows, and a reordering of pricing power across the seaborne market. The implications for steel mills, miners, and investors are not cyclical. They are strategic.

The accident and its aftermath have already pushed Australian coal to price parity with domestic Chinese coal for the first time in over four years. Spot prices for prime hard coking coal are testing levels near USD 244 per tonne, and the trajectory points toward USD 260 to 280 per tonne in the second half of 2026. But the most consequential development is not the price level itself. It is the growing expectation that 50 to 55 million tonnes of Chinese capacity may never return to full operation. If even a fraction of that capacity is permanently lost, the global coking coal market will need to find new supply, new logistics routes, and new price equilibria.

The market is not simply recovering from a shock. It is being remade.

## The Shanjin Accident Has Created a Structural Supply Deficit, Not a Temporary One

The immediate numbers are stark. Lost production from the Shanxi accident is estimated at 15 to 20 million tonnes through the end of August, representing roughly 5 percent of annual domestic Chinese consumption. But the more important figure is the 50 to 55 million tonnes of capacity that remains offline. The critical question is not when this capacity will restart, but whether it will restart at all.

Feedback from traders and producers at the recent Singapore conferences suggests a growing consensus that a significant portion of this capacity is gone for good. Some mines may return at only 70 to 80 percent of their previous capacity, compared to prior operating rates that in some cases exceeded 110 percent. The reason is not just physical damage or safety inspections. It is a shift in the regulatory and political environment. Officials are under investigation for oversight failures. The cost of non-compliance has risen. The tolerance for risk has fallen.

This matters because the lost supply is not generic coking coal. It is primarily high-strength, low-volatility coal with strong coke strength after reaction characteristics. This is the grade that Mongolian and Russian suppliers cannot easily replicate. The consequence is that Chinese domestic prices have risen by approximately USD 40 per tonne since the accident, and the price gap between Australian and domestic coal has not only closed but inverted. Australian coal is now roughly USD 5 per tonne cheaper than domestic Chinese coal at the portside.

The structural reset in Chinese supply has two immediate effects. First, it forces Chinese buyers to rely more heavily on seaborne imports, particularly from Australia. Second, it raises the domestic cost curve, because the mines that remain offline are generally deeper and more expensive to operate. Some are 1 to 1.5 kilometres deep. Their permanent closure would mean that even when Chinese production recovers, the marginal cost of domestic supply will be higher than before. That is a permanent shift in the cost structure of the global coking coal market.

## Australia's Supply Constraints Are Deepening, Not Easing, and the Cost Curve Is Rising

If Chinese supply is resetting lower, Australian supply is also under structural pressure. The narrative that Australia can simply increase exports to fill the gap is misleading. Australian coking coal exports have already declined from approximately 220 million tonnes to 195 million tonnes over the past decade, and the trend is likely to continue.

The reasons are multiple and compounding. Mine depletion is removing capacity, with assets such as Oaky Creek approaching closure. Even planned restarts at Moranbah North, Grosvenor, and North Goonyella will only partially offset the losses. The net effect is that Australian exports could fall a further 10 million tonnes by 2030. New greenfield projects are barely visible in the pipeline.

Cost inflation is making the problem worse. Queensland operating costs are already 30 to 40 percent above early 2022 levels. Diesel alone accounts for 10 to 20 percent of operating expenditure, and fuel inflation has added USD 8 to 12 per tonne to costs. Labour costs are rising. Stripping ratios are increasing as pits deepen. Reserve quality is declining. The combination pushes Queensland miners higher on the global cost curve, eroding margins and reducing the incentive to invest in new capacity.

The regulatory environment adds another layer of friction. Royalty rates in Queensland are a source of clear frustration among producers. Permitting timelines for mine extensions now exceed five years. The result is a supply base that is not only shrinking but also increasingly inflexible. When prices rise, the response from Australian supply is limited. That is a structural change from earlier cycles, when Australian mines could ramp up relatively quickly to capture higher prices.

The strategic implication is that the seaborne coking coal market is becoming more dependent on a shrinking and higher-cost Australian supply base at the very moment when Chinese demand for imports is rising. That is a recipe for sustained price support.

## India's Growing Appetite for Imports Is Real, but Its Pricing Power Remains Constrained

India is often cited as the next engine of coking coal demand, and the numbers support the case. Imports are currently around 80 million tonnes per year and are expected to exceed 120 million tonnes. The demand structure is highly supportive. Infrastructure and construction account for approximately 64 percent of Indian steel demand, compared to a global average of around 52 percent. With limited scrap availability and insufficient domestic gas, India remains structurally tied to blast furnace and basic oxygen furnace production.

But the idea that India will become the dominant price setter in the seaborne market needs qualification. Several panellists at the conferences argued that India is increasingly central to premium low-volatility price formation, particularly when Chinese portside prices trade below index levels. However, India's influence is constrained by its heavy reliance on term contracts. Spot participation remains limited. Most buyers keep only around 10 percent of their volumes open to spot markets, largely for security of supply reasons. The spot market itself lacks depth. One trader suggested that only about 10 buyers would participate in spot onshore trades in India, compared to over 100 in China.

The infrastructure gap is a structural constraint. India's port, stockyard, and blending ecosystem still lacks the depth of China's coastal market. This limits liquidity and prevents the development of a deeper spot pricing mechanism. The implication is that India can drive episodic price spikes, particularly in the post-monsoon period when restocking occurs. But when a major supply shock hits, pricing power swings back to China. That is exactly what is happening now.

Indian mills are responding to higher prices and supply constraints by shifting toward leaner coal blends. Every mill we spoke with is exploring greater substitution of lower-quality coals, using more sophisticated blending practices to increase the share of Tier 2, semi-soft, and weaker coals. The shift can raise the proportion of domestic and medium coking coals in blends from below 10 percent to around 35 to 55 percent, while still meeting blast furnace coke specifications. The cost savings are significant: USD 10 to 15 per tonne of coal or USD 15 to 20 per tonne of met coke.

This blending shift has second-order implications for the seaborne market. It increases demand for mid-vol and semi-soft coals, which are already tight due to Australian supply challenges. It also creates opportunities for suppliers who can offer consistent quality and reliable logistics. But it does not eliminate India's dependence on seaborne imports. Domestic production of commercial met coal is only around 5 million tonnes, and even ambitious plans to double output would leave India structurally reliant on imports to support steel growth.

## Mongolian and Russian Supply Growth Is a Critical Offset, but It Comes with Its Own Uncertainties

The most important counterweight to tightening supply conditions is the continued growth of Mongolian and Russian coal supply into China. Mongolia is already exporting roughly 80 to 90 million tonnes of raw met coal annually, of which 50 to 55 million tonnes is washed. Several speakers at the conferences expect volumes to exceed this level in the near term.

The upgrade to the Tavantolgoi railway is the key catalyst. Scheduled for completion in 2027, the railway will add approximately 30 million tonnes of annual capacity while significantly reducing logistics costs from USD 30 to 35 per tonne to USD 8 to 12 per tonne. That cost reduction could unlock marginal tonnes that were previously uneconomic to transport. It also improves the competitiveness of Mongolian coal relative to seaborne alternatives.

Russian exports are also rising. Payment mechanisms have normalised, and producers are benefiting from government support. The Elga mine is currently operating at 20 to 25 million tonnes, although it can only wash 8 million tonnes, with the balance sold as unwashed coal to China. The trajectory is upward, but the quality constraints are real. Russian coal cannot replicate the high-strength, low-volatility characteristics of the lost Chinese supply. It can fill volume gaps, but it cannot fully substitute for the premium grades that the market needs.

The strategic question is whether Mongolian and Russian supply growth can fully offset the combined impact of Chinese capacity loss and Australian depletion. The arithmetic suggests it is possible in volume terms, but the quality mismatch creates persistent tightness in the premium segments of the market. That tightness is reflected in the price differentials and is likely to persist.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Tariff Question and the Role of US Supply

One of the most intriguing developments is the recent shipment of US coking coal cargoes to China despite a 28 percent tariff. This suggests that some traders are betting on a reduction or removal of tariffs if seaborne prices remain elevated. The US previously supplied approximately 10 million tonnes per year to China before tariffs were introduced.

A tariff reduction would have significant and complex effects. It would redirect US flows away from India and Brazil, where US coal has been competitive since the tariff was introduced. Indian and Brazilian mills that have been purchasing US coal at discounted prices would face higher costs or need to find alternative supply. Chinese buyers would gain access to a new source of high-quality coal, potentially easing spot activity in the Australian index.

But the full implications are difficult to model. The report does not provide a clear answer on the probability of tariff changes or the timing. It also does not fully explore how US supply would interact with the structural changes in Chinese and Australian production. The question remains open, and it is one of the most important variables for the medium-term outlook.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the New Coking Coal Landscape

For readers who need to make strategic decisions in this market, the analysis points to a framework with four key dimensions.

First, assess supply-side risk by geography. Chinese domestic supply is undergoing a structural reset that will raise the marginal cost of production and increase import dependence. Australian supply is declining and becoming more expensive. Mongolian and Russian supply is growing but cannot fully substitute for premium grades. The net effect is a market that is structurally tighter than headline volume numbers suggest.

Second, evaluate quality premiums. The tightest segment of the market is high-strength, low-volatility coal. This is where the supply loss from Shanxi is concentrated and where substitution is most difficult. Investors and buyers should focus on the premium hard coking coal index as the key price signal, rather than broader averages.

Third, monitor trade flow shifts. The parity between Australian and Chinese domestic prices is a structural change that will redirect Australian coal toward China. This improves price realisation for Australian miners but also increases competition for Indian and other buyers. The seasonal pattern of restocking, particularly post-monsoon in India, will create episodic tightness.

Fourth, watch the tariff and policy environment. US supply is a wild card. A tariff reduction would reshape trade flows and pricing dynamics. Regulatory changes in Queensland and Shanxi will determine the trajectory of supply costs. The market is not just responding to physical supply and demand. It is responding to policy decisions that are still being made.

The coking coal market has entered a period of structural change that rewards deep analysis and punishes superficial assumptions. The Shanxi accident was a catalyst, but the forces it set in motion will continue to play out for years. Understanding those forces is not optional. It is the difference between reacting to events and anticipating them.

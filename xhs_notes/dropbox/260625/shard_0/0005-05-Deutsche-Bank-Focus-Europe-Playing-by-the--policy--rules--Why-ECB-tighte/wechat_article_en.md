# The ECB's Own Projections Justify More Hikes, But the Rules Tell Only Part of the Story

The European Central Bank's June rate hike was described as "robust" but not "forceful." That distinction matters more than most market participants realize. When a central bank president chooses that specific language, it signals a deliberate calibration between doing too little and doing too much. The question every serious investor must now answer is whether the ECB has the conviction to follow through on what its own data demands.

Applying a comprehensive suite of monetary policy rules to the ECB's own staff projections reveals a clear conclusion: the central bank's baseline outlook for inflation and growth warrants further tightening, even after accounting for the recent decline in energy prices following the US-Iran memorandum of understanding. The median policy prescription from these rules, when using inflation measures that exclude volatile energy components, points to a terminal rate near 2.75% by the end of next year. That is consistent with two additional 25-basis-point hikes beyond June's move.

Yet the path from here to there is anything but certain. The same analytical framework that supports further tightening also exposes the limitations of relying on mechanical rules in a world shaped by energy shocks, geopolitical uncertainty, and structural shifts in the labour market. Understanding where the rules are helpful and where they break down is the strategic edge that separates informed positioning from reactive trading.

## Why the ECB's Own Forecasts Demand a Higher Terminal Rate Than Markets Currently Price

The most compelling argument for further tightening comes not from external hawkish commentary but from the ECB's own internal projections. When the central bank's June staff forecasts for inflation, unemployment, and output are fed into a range of standard policy rules, the results are remarkably consistent across different methodological approaches.

The key insight is that the revisions between the March and June baseline projections have systematically boosted the prescribed path for policy rates. Across the various rules, the June revisions add between 25 and 50 basis points to the recommended rate trajectory. This is not a marginal adjustment. It reflects a genuine deterioration in the inflation outlook that the ECB itself acknowledges in its internal numbers.

The rules that matter most for practical policy assessment are those that exclude energy prices from the inflation measure. During an energy supply shock, the central bank cannot directly control the price of oil and gas at the pump. What it can influence is the pass-through of those higher costs into wages, services prices, and broader inflation expectations. When the analysis focuses on HICP excluding energy, the median rule prescription converges on a rate of approximately 2.75% by end-2026. Market pricing at the time of the June meeting implied a terminal rate closer to 2.50%. That gap of roughly 25 basis points represents either a market mispricing or a deliberate discount for downside risks that the rules do not capture.

The critical strategic question is whether that gap will close through higher market rates or lower actual policy rates. The answer depends on how the economy evolves relative to the ECB's scenarios.

## The Milder Scenario Does Not Eliminate the Case for Tightening; It Only Changes the Pace

One of the most important innovations in the ECB's June projections was the introduction of a "milder" scenario that maps out a more favourable energy price trajectory. With oil and gas futures having fallen after the US-Iran MOU announcement, near-term energy prices now track closer to this milder path than to the baseline. This has led some observers to argue that the case for further tightening has weakened.

The data does not support that conclusion. When the policy rules are re-run using the milder scenario projections, the median prescribed rate for the end of this year is approximately 2.50%. That is consistent with exactly one more hike beyond June. Even under the most benign of the ECB's own scenarios, the rules say that current policy rates are too low.

This is a crucial point that many market participants misunderstand. The debate is not whether the ECB should tighten or not. The debate is whether the terminal rate should be 2.50% or 2.75%. Both numbers represent additional tightening. The milder scenario does not justify a pause; it justifies a slower pace.

The difference between one more hike and two more hikes is where the analytical tension lives. Current energy futures suggest a path that lies between the baseline and milder scenarios. If that intermediate outcome materializes, the rules are consistent with either one or two additional moves. The ECB's own language at the June press conference tilted toward the risk of two, but the data leaves room for interpretation.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Feedback Problem and the Limits of Conditional Projections

Every serious analysis of central bank policy rules must confront a fundamental limitation that this report acknowledges but cannot fully resolve. The ECB's staff projections are conditioned on a specific path for monetary policy. In the baseline case, that path assumes three rate hikes. The policy rules, however, prescribe a different path. If the ECB were to follow the rules and hike more aggressively, the future trajectory of inflation and output would change, which would in turn alter what the rules recommend.

This circularity is not a technical curiosity. It is the central challenge of using rules for real-time policy decisions. The rules tell you what to do given the current forecast. But the forecast itself depends on what you do. The feedback loop means that mechanical application of the rules can lead to overshooting if the economy proves more responsive to tightening than the staff models assume, or undershooting if the transmission mechanism is weaker than expected.

The report's alternative scenario analysis partially addresses this by showing how the rules would prescribe different paths under different energy price assumptions. But it cannot resolve the deeper issue of how the interaction between policy and outcomes would evolve in real time. This is why central bankers always emphasize that rules are guides, not scripts. The gap between prescription and actual policy is not necessarily a mistake. It can reflect a deliberate judgment about the uncertainty inherent in the feedback loop.

For investors, this means that the rule-based analysis provides a valuable anchor for understanding the direction of travel, but it cannot deliver precise timing or magnitude. The range between one and two more hikes is not analytical noise. It is the honest reflection of genuine uncertainty about how the economy will respond to the tightening that has already occurred and the tightening that is yet to come.

## How to Translate the Rules Into a Decision Framework for Portfolio Positioning

The strategic value of this analysis lies not in predicting the exact terminal rate but in constructing a decision framework that accounts for the range of plausible outcomes. Four scenarios emerge from the intersection of the policy rules and the ECB's alternative projections.

The first scenario is that the economy follows the milder path. Energy prices remain subdued, inflation moderates more quickly than the baseline assumes, and the ECB delivers one more hike to 2.50% before pausing. In this case, the rules were slightly too hawkish relative to the realized outcome, but the deviation is small. Fixed income markets would likely rally on the expectation that the tightening cycle is complete, and curve steepening would be the dominant trade.

The second scenario is the baseline path. Inflation proves stickier than the milder case but does not re-accelerate. The ECB delivers two more hikes to 2.75% and then holds. This is the most consistent outcome with the median rule prescription excluding energy. In this scenario, front-end rates would need to reprice higher, and the market's current pricing would prove too dovish.

The third scenario is the adverse path. Energy prices rise again, or second-round effects through wages become embedded. The rules prescribe further tightening into 2027. In this case, the ECB would face the difficult choice of either following the rules into restrictive territory or accepting above-target inflation for longer. The market would likely price a more aggressive path, and duration would underperform significantly.

The fourth scenario is the one the rules cannot capture: a structural shift in the economy that changes the neutral rate of interest or the slope of the Phillips curve. If the post-pandemic economy operates with a higher natural rate of unemployment or a lower sensitivity of inflation to slack, the rules based on historical relationships would systematically overstate the required tightening. This is the scenario that keeps dovish central bankers awake at night.

The practical implication for portfolio construction is that the asymmetry of risk favors being positioned for a higher terminal rate than the market currently prices, but with the flexibility to adjust if the data shifts decisively toward the milder scenario. This argues for maintaining some duration underweight but avoiding aggressive bets on further steepening until the September meeting provides clearer signals.

## The Historical Record Shows That the ECB Deviates from Rules When It Matters Most

The report includes a historical comparison that is worth drawing out explicitly because it reveals a pattern that is highly relevant for the current cycle. During the pandemic and the energy shock following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, the policy rules prescribed highly aggressive tightening starting in mid-2021. The ECB did not follow that prescription. Actual rates remained at deeply negative levels until mid-2022, long after the rules said tightening should have begun.

This deviation is often cited as evidence that the ECB was behind the curve. But there is an alternative interpretation. The rules were designed for normal business cycle fluctuations, not for a once-in-a-generation supply shock combined with a pandemic-induced collapse and recovery in demand. The ECB's delay may have reflected a judgment that the inflationary impulse was transitory and that tightening too early would have damaged the recovery unnecessarily. That judgment turned out to be wrong, but it was not irrational given the information available at the time.

The more relevant observation for today is that during the 2024 rate cutting cycle, actual rates broadly followed the prescribed paths early on but then drifted toward the lower end of the prescribed range. This pattern suggests that the ECB is willing to follow the rules when the economic environment is relatively normal but deviates when uncertainty is elevated or when the shocks are structural rather than cyclical.

If that pattern holds, the current environment argues for the rules to have more influence than they did in 2021-2022. The energy shock is receding. The labour market is normalizing. The inflation surprise is becoming less about supply constraints and more about domestic demand. These are conditions under which the rules tend to be more reliable guides. The historical precedent does not guarantee that the ECB will follow the rules this time, but it does suggest that the burden of proof for deviating is higher than it was during the pandemic.

The unresolved analytical question is whether the structural shifts in the European economy from demographics, digitalization, and decarbonization have changed the parameters that the rules take as given. If the neutral rate has risen, the rules would understate the required tightening. If the Phillips curve has flattened, the rules would overstate it. The data over the next six months will begin to answer this question. Until then, the rules provide the best available anchor, but they require constant re-evaluation against the evolving macroeconomic reality.

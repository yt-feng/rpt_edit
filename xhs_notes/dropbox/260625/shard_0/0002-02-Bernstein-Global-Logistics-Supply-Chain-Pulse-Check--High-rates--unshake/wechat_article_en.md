# Supply Chains Are Not Normalizing: The Structural Tightening That Markets Are Underpricing

The prevailing narrative in financial markets holds that supply chain disruptions are a cyclical phenomenon, one that will naturally revert as geopolitical tensions ease and capacity expands. That narrative is wrong. Recent data across ocean freight, air cargo, and U.S. surface transportation tells a different story: rates are not merely elevated; they are structurally supported by a combination of regulatory tightening, capacity discipline, and demand patterns that have proven surprisingly resilient. Ocean freight rates now stand more than 100% above pre-conflict levels, air freight rates are 40-50% higher, and U.S. spot truckload rates excluding fuel have surged 48% year-on-year. These are not temporary spikes. They reflect a supply chain regime that has fundamentally shifted, and the investment implications are far more nuanced than a simple "buy the freight plays" trade.

The critical insight for investors is that the current rate environment is being driven by supply-side constraints that are likely to persist, even as demand shows only modest improvement. This creates a favorable near-term earnings environment for freight forwarders and certain transportation companies, but it also masks significant divergence beneath the surface. Some companies are positioned to benefit from structural tailwinds, while others face existential challenges from overcapacity and strategic missteps. The key is to distinguish between those for whom high rates are a temporary tailwind and those for whom they represent a durable competitive advantage.

Three forces are converging to create this environment. First, regulatory enforcement in U.S. trucking is tightening capacity in ways that are structural, not cyclical. Second, ocean container shipping faces a massive supply wave that has been temporarily masked by the Red Sea crisis, but that will eventually overwhelm demand. Third, air freight is being supported by AI and semiconductor demand that is not going away. Understanding how these forces interact is essential for constructing a coherent investment thesis in logistics today.

## The U.S. Surface Transport Story Is a Supply Story, Not a Demand Story, and That Makes It More Durable

The most misunderstood dynamic in logistics today is the nature of the U.S. trucking recovery. Spot truckload rates excluding fuel have surged 48% year-on-year, accelerating from 34% in May and roughly 20-25% in the prior three months. The temptation is to read this as a demand recovery signal, particularly given multiple consecutive months of positive ISM manufacturing readings and improving shipper sentiment. But the data does not support a demand-led interpretation.

The real driver is supply-side tightening that is structural in nature. Regulatory enforcement around driver language requirements, domicile rules, hours of service, and training standards is reducing the available pool of compliant capacity. These are not temporary measures. They represent a permanent ratcheting up of operating standards that will keep a meaningful portion of fringe capacity off the road. Additionally, higher fuel costs have eliminated financially struggling carriers who cannot absorb the margin compression, further reducing fleet availability. The recent Supreme Court ruling on broker liability will, over time, add another layer of downward pressure on capacity by increasing the legal risk associated with brokerage operations.

Demand, meanwhile, shows only tentative signs of life. While industrial activity related to data center builds has provided some uplift, consumer-oriented demand components remain weak. Housing starts dipped 8.7% year-on-year in May, missing economist expectations for a 10.9% increase. This is not the profile of a broad-based demand recovery. The demand signals that do exist are concentrated in specific industrial verticals, not in the general economy.

The implication is that U.S. surface transport rates have further upside from here, but the driver will remain supply constraints rather than demand acceleration. This is bullish for carriers with disciplined capacity management and bearish for shippers who have been relying on excess capacity to keep rates low. The key question for investors is which companies have the operating leverage to convert these higher rates into margin expansion, and which will see the gains competed away.

## Ocean Freight Faces a Looming Supply Wave That the Red Sea Crisis Has Only Delayed, Not Cancelled

The ocean freight market presents a starkly different picture. Spot rates on the major indices are now more than 100% above pre-conflict levels, driven by the combination of Red Sea rerouting, capacity constraints, and a demand spike into quarter-end. April ocean container volumes were up 4%, in line with the upper end of Maersk's full-year growth guidance of 2-4%. This creates a favorable near-term earnings environment, and there is upside risk to Maersk's Q2 guidance.

But the medium-term outlook is considerably more challenging. The industry-wide container ship orderbook remains at historically high levels, and this capacity is still scheduled to enter the water. The Red Sea crisis has prevented a collapse in freight rates by forcing vessels to take longer routes around Africa, effectively absorbing excess capacity. However, any lasting peace deal in the Middle East would enable a restoration of the Red Sea and Suez Canal route between Asia and Europe, instantly releasing a wave of capacity back into the market. Even without a peace deal, the sheer volume of new vessel deliveries will eventually overwhelm demand growth.

The structural problem for ocean carriers is that container shipping is a commoditized, price-taking business. There is no meaningful differentiation between carriers on service quality or reliability, and barriers to entry are low relative to the capital required. The only sustainable competitive advantage in this industry is cost leadership, and even that is difficult to maintain when the entire industry is adding capacity simultaneously.

This creates a clear divergence in investment implications. For freight forwarders like DSV, which act as intermediaries rather than asset owners, the current environment is unambiguously positive. They benefit from higher rates without bearing the capital risk of vessel ownership. For asset-heavy ocean carriers like Maersk, the near-term earnings boost is real, but it masks a deteriorating structural position. The company's integrator strategy, which aimed to create revenue synergies by combining ocean shipping with logistics services, has failed to deliver on its promises for seven years. The market is pricing in a recovery that may never materialize.

## Air Freight Is the Most Interesting Market Because Structural Demand Tailwinds Are Colliding with Capacity Constraints

Air freight presents the most compelling combination of structural support and cyclical upside. Rates remain 40-50% above pre-conflict levels and have held at these elevated levels even as capacity has recovered through April, May, and June. Both DHL and IATA expect demand to grow by 2-3% in 2026, supported by AI-related shipments, semiconductor logistics, and high-value electronics. This is not cyclical demand. It is structural demand driven by technological change.

The capacity picture is equally supportive. Gulf carriers, which had been adding significant air freight capacity in recent years, are now facing their own constraints. Fuel surcharges are adding another layer of cost support. The result is a market where demand growth is outpacing capacity growth, and where the pricing power is shifting to carriers and forwarders who can offer reliable service.

For freight forwarders, air freight is the most profitable segment of the business. It requires less capital than ocean shipping, offers higher margins, and benefits from the complexity that shippers face in navigating a fragmented market. Companies like DSV and Kuehne+Nagel have built their business models around this complexity, and the current environment plays directly to their strengths.

The key question for air freight is whether the demand growth from AI and semiconductors is sustainable. The bull case is that we are in the early stages of a multi-year investment cycle in data centers and AI infrastructure, which will require ongoing shipments of high-value equipment. The bear case is that this demand is front-loaded and will normalize as the initial build-out phase completes. The data does not yet allow us to distinguish between these scenarios, but the direction of travel is clearly supportive for the next 12-18 months.

## The Report Leaves an Unresolved Question: How Does the Cycle End, and Who Is Left Holding the Risk?

For all its analytical depth, the report leaves one critical question unanswered: what is the catalyst that ends the current favorable environment, and which parts of the logistics ecosystem are most exposed? The answer is not straightforward because different segments face different risk profiles.

For ocean shipping, the catalyst is clear: a peace deal in the Middle East that restores Red Sea transits, or simply the passage of time as new vessel deliveries accumulate. The risk is concentrated in asset-heavy carriers with large orderbooks and limited pricing power. Maersk is the most exposed, and the report's Underperform rating reflects this view.

For U.S. surface transport, the catalyst is less clear. Regulatory tightening is structural, not cyclical, and there is no obvious trigger for a reversal. The risk is that demand deteriorates further, leaving carriers with high rates but declining volumes. This would compress margins despite the favorable rate environment. The report's Outperform ratings on Union Pacific, Norfolk Southern, FedEx, and UPS suggest a belief that these companies have the operating leverage to manage through a demand slowdown.

For air freight, the risk is that capacity catches up with demand more quickly than expected. If Gulf carriers resume their capacity expansion, or if passenger belly cargo capacity increases as travel demand normalizes, the pricing power that forwarders currently enjoy could erode. The report's Market-Perform rating on DHL and Kuehne+Nagel reflects this uncertainty.

The unresolved question is whether the current environment is a new equilibrium or a temporary disequilibrium. If it is a new equilibrium, then valuation multiples should expand to reflect higher structural profitability. If it is temporary, then current earnings are unsustainable and multiples should contract. The report leans toward the latter view for ocean shipping and the former for U.S. surface transport, but the evidence is not yet conclusive.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Exposure

For investors trying to navigate this environment, three questions can serve as a decision framework. First, is the company an asset owner or an asset light intermediary? Asset owners benefit from rate increases in the short term but bear the risk of capacity oversupply in the medium term. Asset light intermediaries benefit from complexity and have more flexible cost structures. The report's top pick in European logistics is DSV, an asset light freight forwarder, which is consistent with this framework.

Second, does the company have pricing power independent of the rate cycle? Companies with unique service offerings, regulatory protection, or oligopolistic market structures can maintain margins even when rates decline. The report's Outperform ratings on FedEx and UPS reflect a belief that the express parcel oligopoly provides pricing stability. Conversely, Maersk's Underperform rating reflects the absence of pricing power in commoditized container shipping.

Third, is the company's cost structure flexible enough to adapt to a downturn? Companies with high fixed costs and limited ability to reduce capacity face the greatest risk if demand deteriorates. Companies with variable cost structures, such as freight forwarders that charter rather than own capacity, can scale down quickly. This is why DSV's asset light model is attractive even as ocean rates remain elevated.

The framework suggests a clear preference for asset light intermediaries with pricing power and flexible cost structures, and a cautious view on asset heavy carriers with limited pricing power and fixed cost bases. The market is not fully pricing this divergence, which creates opportunities for active investors.

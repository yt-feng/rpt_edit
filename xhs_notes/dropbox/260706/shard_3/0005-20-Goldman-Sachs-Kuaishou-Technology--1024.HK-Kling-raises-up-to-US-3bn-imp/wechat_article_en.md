# Kuaishou's Kling Spin-Off Is Not Just a Financing Event — It Is a Strategic Revaluation of China's AI Video Platform

The announcement that Kuaishou has raised up to USD 3 billion for its AI video subsidiary Kling, at a pre-money valuation of USD 15 billion, is far more than a simple capital injection. It represents a deliberate unbundling of a high-growth AI asset from its parent social media platform, creating a cleaner valuation framework for both entities. For observers who have struggled to separate Kuaishou's core advertising and live-streaming business from its speculative AI ambitions, this transaction provides a market-tested price point for the AI piece — and a starkly different picture for what remains.

What makes this deal strategically significant is not the size of the raise, but the structure. Kuaishou will retain 68.3% ownership and continue consolidating Kling's financial results, meaning the subsidiary remains on the balance sheet. Yet external investors — a diverse consortium spanning internet platforms, China-based private equity, and AI-focused funds — have now validated Kling's standalone value at a level that implies the rest of Kuaishou's business is trading at roughly 4x forward earnings. That is a valuation that demands a serious re-examination of how the market has been pricing the parent company.

The timing is also critical. Kling generated approximately RMB 1.1 billion in revenue in 2025, with an annualized run rate reaching USD 500 million by March 2026. That places it among the largest monetized AI-native video applications globally, just two years after the launch of Kling 1.0 in mid-2024. The company is still in a heavy investment phase — net losses expanded from RMB 500 million in 2024 to RMB 1.9 billion in 2025 — but the revenue trajectory suggests a product-market fit that few AI video platforms have achieved.

This article unpacks the strategic logic behind the transaction, examines what it reveals about Kuaishou's sum-of-the-parts valuation, and identifies the critical questions that remain unanswered — including whether Kling's eventual spin-off is a matter of when, not if.

## The financing structure transforms Kling from a cost center into a capitalized growth engine with strategic independence

Before this transaction, Kling was entirely funded by Kuaishou's balance sheet. That arrangement worked when AI model development was experimental, but it becomes unsustainable as competition shifts from algorithm innovation alone toward compute resource availability, infrastructure scale, and global expansion. The capital requirements for frontier model training, GPU procurement, and inference infrastructure are enormous — and they are growing faster than Kuaishou's core advertising revenue can comfortably support.

The USD 3 billion injection changes this dynamic fundamentally. Kling now has its own capital base, its own equity incentive pool (15% of enlarged share capital, with an initial grant of 7.45% already approved), and a diversified investor syndicate that brings more than just money. The equity incentive plan is particularly important. Kuaishou's founder and Kling's CEO Mr. Gai Kun received among the largest grants, signaling that the company is using equity to lock in leadership talent in an intensely competitive AI labor market.

The strategic implication is clear: Kling is no longer a project within Kuaishou. It is a standalone platform with the financial firepower to compete for talent, compute, and customers on its own terms. Kuaishou retains control, but it has effectively shared the future funding burden with external investors while keeping the upside of its 68.3% stake. This is a textbook example of strategic capital allocation — using external capital to fund a high-option-value asset without diluting the parent's core earnings power.

## The implied USD 18 billion post-money valuation creates a clean sum-of-the-parts framework that reveals how the core business is being priced

One of the most persistent debates among Kuaishou observers has been how to value Kling within the broader company. Kuaishou's reported earnings include Kling's losses, making it difficult to assess the profitability of the core advertising and live-streaming platform. The financing changes this by providing a market-tested valuation for Kling.

At USD 18 billion post-money, and with Kuaishou owning 68.33% post-transaction, the implied value of Kuaishou's stake in Kling is approximately USD 12.3 billion. Subtract that from Kuaishou's current market capitalization of roughly USD 23.2 billion, and the implied value of the core platform — excluding Kling — falls to approximately USD 10.9 billion. Against estimated 2026 net income for the core business, that implies a P/E multiple of roughly 4x.

A 4x forward earnings multiple for a platform generating over RMB 145 billion in revenue with improving margins is, by any measure, a notable valuation. The market has been treating Kuaishou as if its AI investments are a value-destroying distraction rather than a strategic asset. The Kling financing provides a mechanism to separate these two narratives and potentially unlock value for those who believe the core business deserves a higher multiple.

This does not mean the core business is without challenges. Revenue growth is expected to decelerate to 1.8% in 2026, and EBITDA is forecast to decline year-over-year. But the margin structure is improving, free cash flow is positive, and the balance sheet is strengthening. The question is whether the market will re-rate the core business now that Kling's losses are ring-fenced and its value is visible.

The strategic question for Kuaishou is whether Kling can capture these enterprise opportunities while remaining integrated with the parent's consumer ecosystem. A full spin-off would create a pure-play AI video company with its own stock price, enabling more direct valuation and potentially unlocking additional value. Management has not committed to this path, and the report notes that progress on a potential spin-off is something to watch. But the financing structure is consistent with a pre-IPO positioning, and observers should expect more clarity on this front in the coming quarters.

Competitors are emerging from large language model providers, social media platforms, and specialized startups.

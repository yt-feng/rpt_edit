## The Supply-Demand Imbalance Is Not Cyclical but Structural, and It Is Widening at an Unprecedented Rate

The most striking data point in the analysis is the projected gap between bit demand growth and supply expansion. For 2027, DRAM bit demand is forecast to grow at 36.2% year-over-year, while supply is expected to increase by only 19.3%. This discrepancy produces a sufficiency ratio that deteriorates to negative 13.6% in 2027, compared to negative 8.1% in 2026. The report explicitly states that both figures represent levels unprecedented over the last 30 years.

This is not a temporary mismatch that can be resolved by a few extra fab lines. The structural nature of the imbalance stems from the fact that demand is being driven by AI workloads that require exponentially greater memory bandwidth and capacity per accelerator. The report's GPU and accelerator unit forecasts show that Nvidia alone is expected to consume HBM-equivalent memory for 8.5 million AI GPU units in 2026 and 11 million in 2027. Google's TPU procurement is forecast to reach 4.2 million units in 2026 and 9.1 million in 2027. The key insight is that these demand drivers are not discretionary. Hyperscalers are building out AI infrastructure as a competitive necessity, not as an option. This means demand is far less elastic to price increases than in previous cycles, which is precisely why pricing can rise so sharply without triggering immediate demand destruction. The risk, however, is that at some point even hyperscalers face capital constraints, and the report flags affordability as the main risk to the upcycle's continuation.

## Long-Term Agreement Negotiations Are Redefining the Industry's Commercial Architecture, and the Terms Set Now Will Lock in Value for Years

The report reveals that revised long-term agreement negotiations are ongoing, and they incorporate more fixed terms for both volumes and pricing. This is not a minor contractual adjustment. It represents a fundamental shift in how memory vendors and their largest customers share risk and reward.

Importantly, the analysis identifies meaningful differences in the LTA structures being pursued by different memory manufacturers. One vendor's approach involves supply capacity agreements with both price floors and ceilings, creating a band within which pricing can fluctuate. Another approach, attributed to Korean memory makers, involves fixing approximately 60-70% of volumes over five-year contracts at fixed pricing, with a variable component for the remainder. Samsung is reportedly progressing toward completing revised LTA agreements with another handful of large customers by the third quarter, with a focus on DDR5, where the target is to have 50-70% of volumes tied up in LTAs.

The strategic implications are significant. For memory vendors, locking in fixed pricing on a majority of volumes provides revenue visibility and reduces exposure to downcycles. For customers, it provides supply certainty in a market where allocation is becoming a critical constraint. But the trade-offs are real. Vendors that lock in fixed pricing now may be leaving money on the table if spot prices continue to rise. Conversely, customers that resist fixed pricing may find themselves unable to secure sufficient supply.

The report does not fully answer which LTA structure will prove more advantageous over the cycle. This remains an open question that depends on the trajectory of pricing and the duration of the upcycle. What is clear is that the negotiations happening now will determine profit distribution for the next five years.

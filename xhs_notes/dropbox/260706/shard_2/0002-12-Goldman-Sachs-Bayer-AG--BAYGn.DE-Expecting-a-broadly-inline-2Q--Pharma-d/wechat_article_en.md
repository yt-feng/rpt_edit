## The Glyphosate Overhang Is Fading, But the August 19 Fairness Hearing Remains a Critical Inflection Point

The litigation narrative has dominated Bayer’s equity story for years, and for good reason. The company has faced tens of thousands of claims related to glyphosate, with billions of dollars in settlements and ongoing uncertainty about future liabilities. The U.S. Supreme Court’s favorable ruling was a watershed moment, but it did not end the story. The fairness hearing for the class settlement, now delayed to August 19, gives Bayer additional time to encourage remaining opt-outs to join the settlement. This delay is not necessarily negative—it provides a window for more plaintiffs to settle, which would reduce the pool of future claims and increase the likelihood that the class settlement achieves its intended finality.

More structurally significant is the creation of Ruveon. By consolidating all glyphosate operations into a separate wholly owned entity, Bayer has created a vehicle that could eventually be spun off or separated. This structure allows the company to ringfence future glyphosate liabilities: the settlement payments and any residual litigation exposure would reside within Ruveon, not Bayer. This is a clever legal and financial engineering solution. It transforms an unquantifiable tail risk into a more contained liability within a discrete entity. For investors, the key question is whether Ruveon will remain a wholly owned subsidiary or whether Bayer will pursue a partial or full separation. A spin-off would effectively remove the glyphosate overhang from Bayer’s valuation entirely, allowing the remaining businesses—Pharma, Consumer Health, and Crop Science—to be valued on their own merits.

The report reduces the WACC from 9.3% to 8.8% specifically due to reduced litigation risk. That 50-basis-point reduction is meaningful. It implies that the market’s required return on Bayer equity is declining as the legal uncertainty recedes. But the WACC reduction is not a one-for-one pass-through to valuation. The report also adjusts the discount applied to the Pharma business, which suggests that litigation risk was not the only factor suppressing the stock. The conglomerate structure itself, and the market’s skepticism about Pharma’s growth prospects, are separate drags that will not automatically resolve with legal clarity.

## The Conglomerate Discount Is Real, But Break-Up Scenarios Face Execution Risks That the Report Only Begins to Explore

The report estimates that Bayer’s conglomerate discount is approximately €10 per share on a sum-of-the-parts basis. This is a substantial discount that implies the market values Bayer’s individual businesses at significantly less than what they would be worth as standalone entities. The logic of a break-up is compelling: separate Pharma, Consumer Health, and Crop Science into independent companies, and the sum of the parts would likely exceed the current market capitalization.

But execution risks are considerable. A break-up of Bayer would be one of the most complex corporate restructurings in European history. The company is deeply integrated across its divisions, with shared R&D, manufacturing, and distribution infrastructure. Tax implications, regulatory approvals, and the need to maintain investment-grade credit ratings would all constrain the options. The report references a potential break-up but does not provide a detailed roadmap. This is not a criticism—the report is focused on the near-term earnings outlook and valuation update. But it leaves investors with an open question: what would a break-up actually look like, and what is the probability that management pursues it?

The creation of Ruveon provides a template. If Bayer can separate glyphosate into a standalone entity, it can do the same for other divisions. But the motivation for a full break-up is not yet clear. Management has not signaled a strong preference for separation, and the board may prefer to retain the conglomerate structure if they believe the synergies are real. The report’s analysis implicitly assumes that the discount will narrow over time, but it does not specify the catalyst. Is it a break-up, or is it simply improved operational performance across all three divisions? The answer matters for the investment thesis.

As data center rack thermal densities continue to rise driven by the rapid scaling of AI workloads and increasing power density of GPUs, air cooling alone is no longer sufficient to manage the heat generated by modern compute architectures. As a result, direct-to-chip (DTC) liquid cooling has become a critical part of the data center ecosystem, with cold plates emerging as a key enabler of the transition (from air cooling to liquid cooling). At the core of the liquid cooling ecosystem, two components drive the thermal loop - CDUs (see our earlier primer) and cold plates. Cold plates perform the most important task in the architecture: physically transferring the heat from the chip surface to the coolant, and are the primary mechanism enabler in the liquid cooling stack.

The importance of cold plates becomes apparent when viewed in the context of modern AI data center racks. For example, a single NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 rack can contain over 100 individual cold plates (one for each GPU / CPU), highlighting the scale at which they are deployed. As rack densities continue to increase with next-gen GPUs expected to reach multi-thousand kW TDP levels, the ability of cold plates to efficiently manage heat flux and thermal resistance becomes increasingly critical to overall system performance. Despite this integral role, we view cold plates through a more nuanced lens than other liquid cooling components; structural tailwinds supporting volumes are offset by commoditization and possibly obsolescence.

On one hand, the near-term outlook is clearly favorable. Adoption of liquid cooling is accelerating, hyperscalers are committing to fully liquid-cooled architectures (NVIDIA announcement for Rubin), and cold plates are effectively a non-optional component within DTC systems. This creates strong volume tailwinds, supported by increasing liquid cooling penetration (from ~30–40% of new additions today towards majority adoption by 2030). On the other hand cold plates (unlike CDUs) are inherently simpler components, with value largely concentrated in design rather than system-level complexity. Service attach is comparatively lower vs. CDUs, and cold plate OEMs largely capture hardware margins which are harder to defend than service margins. This raises the question of how defensible the business model is over time, particularly as designs commoditize, manufacturing scales, and the innovation / scarcity premium erode. And in extreme scenarios, technology such as direct-to-die have the ability to eliminate cold plates entirely.

We size the base case cold plate market to be ~$6 - 7B by 2030 (growing from $2 - 3B today). As with any data center equipment, this is highly dependent on your assumptions around GW added. Reach out to the authors or your Bernstein sales counterpart for the raw model in case you'd like to adjust assumptions.

In this note, we lay out a comprehensive primer on cold plates, including how they work, what drives performance, key design trade-offs, and where innovation is occurring today. We also assess the evolving competitive landscape, examine the economic model (including limited services revenue opportunity), and present a bottom-up view on market sizing and growth. Finally, we outline our perspective on the trajectory of the space, where cold plates remain a critical enabler of AI infrastructure growth in the near-term, but face increasing commoditization and disruption risks over a longer time horizon.

## INTRODUCTION

In the past, a continuous flow of cool air over servers was sufficient to cool data centers. With time, chips and racks have grown increasingly dense, to the point where even freezing cold air blowing at gale force speeds wouldn't do the job. Needless to say, finding an alternative cooling modality became critical. And so, liquid cooling technology, which long sat at the fringe of data center infrastructure, suddenly found itself thrust into the spotlight. Looking ahead, rack densities and chip TDPs seem set to keep growing, forcing liquid cooling solutions to evolve with more rigorous server demands.

The predominant format of liquid cooling today is single-phase DTC (DTC stands for Direct-To-Chip). It involves cold plates (which are aptly named slabs of metal that have reduced temperature due to refrigerant that circulates inside them) coming into contact with GPU / CPU chips to extract heat. Coolant Distribution Units (CDUs) pump low temperature coolant (usually a water - propylene glycol mix) through the plates and recollect the spent liquid once they have extracted heat from the server. The heat is then extracted from the coolant (making it cold again) and the process repeats. It is called single-phase because the coolant stays in a single-phase (liquid) and does not change phases from liquid to gas via evaporation. This loop (i.e., circulating from the CDU through cold plates in the server) is called the TCS or Technology Cooling System. Once spent coolant reaches the CDU, it passes through a heat exchanger where it transfers heat to another cooling loop called the FWS or Facility Water System. The FWS connects to a chiller or a dry cooler to reject this heat outside a data center. However, as rack power densities continue to increase, single-phase DTC is seemingly reaching the theoretical limit of how much heat it can extract from the server rack.

Cold plates and CDUs are the most important to discuss. CDUs (Coolant Distribution Units) are essentially responsible for controlling the distribution, flow, and pressure of coolant across multiple rows and racks. Cold plates receive coolant from CDUs (via a manifold) and absorb heat through their surface before returning the spent coolant to the CDU. Both these components are seeing significant demand (and shortages today) and there has been a flurry of investment activity in the space with larger players making strategic investments and acquisitions (e.g., JCI investing in Accelsius, Eaton acquiring Boyd). In today's note, we focus specifically on cold plates.

## WHAT IS A COLD PLATE?

A cold plate is a machined metal block (copper / aluminum) which is clamped onto the GPU / CPU dies (the slot in which the actual chip architecture sits) on the silicon wafer. A network of minutely thin internal channels or fins (drilled through CNC / precision machining) runs inside the cold plate through which the liquid coolant (usually a mix of water-glycol pumped from the CDU in single-phase) flows. There is a layer of thermally conductive paste or pad between the cold plate and the GPU called the Thermal Interface Material (TIM). TIM serves as the thermal bridge between heat-generating GPUs and the cold plates - filling microscopic air gaps caused by surface roughness and ensuring consistent heat transfer from the GPU to the coolant (and thus the cold plate) through heat conduction. Even high performance liquid cold plates cannot achieve their full cooling potential if this interface is poorly managed, as hotspots can form due to uneven thermal contact. Post-heating, the warm coolant now exits back to the CDU, and thus back to the liquid cooling loop - and the cycle repeats.

In simple terms: the chip gets hot, the cold plate sits on top of it, the coolant flows through the cold plate's internal channels, carries the heat away as warm liquid, and comes back into the loop as cold fluid (post cooling from the loop). The cold plate acts as the "absorber" in this loop - it is the only component of the entire liquid cooling ecosystem that physically touches the chip.

The role of cold plates becomes clearer when viewed within the context of modern high-density AI server racks, such as NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 rack, which consists of 18 compute trays (each tray houses 4 Blackwell GPUs and 2 CPUs for a total of 72 Blackwell GPUs and 36 CPUs). Each GPU and CPU requires its own dedicated cold plate. Cold plates are thus mounted on each GPU / CPU die, resulting in a total of 108 cold plates per rack. Cold plates are inherently a high-volume component. Unlike CDUs (fewer in number and more complex), cold plates need to be deployed at scale, and must balance performance with ease of manufacturing.

## WHAT METRICS MATTER?

First, let's talk about what matters in a cold plate. There are dozens of technical specifications; while all matter in their own unique ways, we specifically call out five to keep an eye on:

1. Thermal capacity: Usually represented in W or kW, thermal capacity is the total rate at which a cold plate can remove heat from a chip (GPU / CPU) under operating conditions. It is influenced by coolant inlet temperature, plate design, thermal resistance, and channel geometry (discussed below). In hyperscaler deployments today, cold plates typically support ~2-5 kW per chip, with next-gen designs reaching 5-10 kW+ (CoolIT recently announced a 15 kW cold plate). As rapidly increasing AI workloads drive even higher power and rack densities, thermal capacity will be increasingly co-optimized with chip architecture going forward.

2. Heat flux: Measures the amount of heat removed from the chip per unit area in W/cm $^{2}$ , making it critical for understanding how well a cold plate can manage localized hotspots on the chip. This is particularly important for modern AI workloads, where heat generation is highly concentrated in small regions of the die where dense tensor cores compute the matrix multiplication for query resolution. Current hyperscaler targets are in the range of 100 - 130+ W/cm $^{2}$ , with leading-edge designs pushing towards 150+ W/cm $^{2}$ .

3. Pressure drop: This represents the amount of fluid resistance the coolant experiences as it flows through the cold plate, directly impacting the pumping power required to operate the system. As a result, lower pressure drop is preferred (it reduces the amount of pumping power) required to operate the system) as it reduces overall energy consumption and improves system efficiency. However, there is a tradeoff: achieving higher heat transfer (higher heat flux) increases resistance due to increased complexity in internal plate geometries (more thinly lined weaving channels). For current deployments, hyperscalers operate around 15-25 psi, with high performance designs reaching 30+ psi.

4. Thermal resistance: Quantifies the efficiency of the heat transfer from the chip to the coolant, expressed as a rise in temperature per unit of heat ( $^{\circ}$ C/W). Lower thermal resistance is desired, as this enables more efficient heat transfer, enabling the chips to run at lower temperatures for the same output. High-performance designs today run at $<0.04\ ^{\circ}C/W$ . As chip power outputs continue to rise, solving for thermal resistance will become more critical, driving innovations in interface materials and surface-level engineering.

5. Channel geometry: Describes the internal structure of the cold plate, specifically the channel / fin density, size, shape, and arrangement of fluid channels, all of which together determine how effectively the coolant interacts with the hot surface of the GPU (a denser array = a higher surface area of contact between coolant and GPU = higher heat transfer). Complex geometries such as microchannels increase surface area and create greater turbulence (enables greater "scraping" of heat from the chip walls), thereby improving heat transfer.

## HOW IS THE COLD PLATE ECOSYSTEM BEING SHAPED?

Cold plates are now a critical part of the liquid cooling ecosystem, as evidenced by recent announcements by NVIDIA and hyperscalers alike. For its next-gen Rubin architecture, NVIDIA has already mandated a "100% liquid cooling ecosystem with no fans anywhere", providing a secular tailwind for manufacturers in the space. By approving specific component vendors for different platforms through the Recommended Vendor List, NVIDIA is exerting soft influence on the evolution of the industry.

Unlike CDUs, there has been a relatively limited push from the industry to specify design configurations for cold plates so far. However, the OCP (Open Compute Project) has established baseline operational performance standards that an operational cold plate must meet (such as permissible pressure drop, leakage rates, operating temperature ranges, permissible amount of stress on the chip package, etc) which allows manufacturers to decide how exactly they want to achieve these performance metrics. We expect this to continue in the near-to-mid term while GPU TDPs continue to rise, enabling cold plate manufacturers to capture an innovation premium in their pricing (as they do with CDUs). However, longer-term, once TDPs stabilize and hyperscalers have a clearer idea of their compute portfolios we anticipate this innovation premium will erode as there is limited need for cold plate evolution. This does impact cold plates more than CDUs (which also see some commoditization risk) because they have limited to no service attach and this essentially puts the margin profile at risk.

## WHY DON'T COLD PLATES HAVE A GREAT SERVICE ATTACH?

Cold plates are largely designed to last for the lifecycle of the GPUs they are cooling. However, there is naturally unexpected failure that takes place in the racks. When this happens, individual cold plates do not get "serviced" and re-attached, they are simply replaced. This is generally done by the server OEM (companies like Dell) which keep an inventory of cold plates and charge for the service of making a swap. This is less intensive work vs. repair or maintenance where pievces of equipment are checked and tested. In addition, for the cold plate manufacturer themselves, they only receive hardware margins (which look set to compress as cold plates themselves have commoditization risk). There are some situations in which the cold plate OEM themselves become responsible for service, but that is more contractual when failure rates exceed acceptable levels and they have a contractual obligation to step in and manage the swaps; terms are not favorable and no OEM would actually want to be in this situation. This is fundamentally different from a CDU where there are multiple components in the unit (pumps, heat exchangers, etc.) that could fail and need maintenance and repair. CDUs also have longer useful lives which allows greater monetization of the install base.

## COMPARISON OF KEY PLAYERS IN THE MARKET

Naturally, as liquid cooling has accelerated, many players have entered the market. The cold plate manufacturing market is characterized by a mix of established industrial players and emerging startups. Large incumbents (such as Boyd, CoolIT, CoolerMaster) bring scale, manufacturing expertise and established hyperscaler relationships, while funded startups focus upon specialized innovations like cold plates for two-phase DTC cooling ecosystem.

Given the opportunity, there has been a spree of M&A activity in the space, with leading manufacturers of electrical and HVAC equipment (JCI, Carrier, Eaton, Schneider) wanting a slice of the AI infrastructure pie. As volumes scale and hyperscalers increasingly consolidate their supplier bases, we expect competitive dynamics to shift towards a balance between manufacturing scalability and technical differentiation.

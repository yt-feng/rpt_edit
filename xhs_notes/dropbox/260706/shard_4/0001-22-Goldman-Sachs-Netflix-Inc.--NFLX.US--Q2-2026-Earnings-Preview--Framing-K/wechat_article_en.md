## The Advertising Business Is Scaling, But Its Profitability Impact Remains an Open Question

Netflix's advertising tier is no longer an experiment. It is a central pillar of the company's revenue growth strategy. The analyst estimates show ad-supported subscribers growing from 35 million at year-end 2024 to 70 million by year-end 2026, with total ad-supported monthly active viewers reaching 280 million. Advertising revenue is expected to roughly double year-over-year in 2026. The May Upfront event signaled that Netflix is serious about building out its ad tech platform, the Netflix Ad Suite, with plans to expand into 15 new countries in 2027.

The strategic logic is sound. Connected TV advertising is growing at a mid-teens compound annual rate globally, and linear TV budgets are migrating to streaming platforms. Netflix has the scale and the premium content library to capture a disproportionate share of that shift. But the near-term financial mechanics are more complicated. The ad-supported tier carries a lower average revenue per user than the ad-free tier, and the analyst notes that this dilutive effect will persist through 2026. The question is how quickly the gap closes. If ad load, pricing power, and targeting efficiency improve faster than expected, the dilution could be temporary. If not, the advertising business could become a drag on overall revenue per user even as it contributes to top-line growth.

The more subtle issue is whether advertisers will pay a premium for Netflix's inventory relative to other streaming platforms. Netflix has historically resisted the kind of aggressive ad loads that characterize free ad-supported TV services. That discipline preserves user experience but limits inventory. The company needs to demonstrate that its ad CPMs can justify the scale it is projecting. The Q2 report may not provide definitive answers, but it should offer clues on whether the advertising trajectory is accelerating or merely tracking expectations.

## Live Events Are Driving Engagement, But They Have Not Yet Solved Netflix's Consistency Problem

The decision to invest in live events—NFL, MLB, WWE, and other tentpole programming—represents a fundamental shift in Netflix's content strategy. Scripted series and films remain the core of the library, but live events serve a different purpose. They create appointment viewing, drive real-time social conversation, and, most importantly, generate advertising inventory that commands higher rates than on-demand content.

The data shows that live events are working in specific moments. Major broadcasts drive spikes in both new user acquisition and engagement. But the analyst commentary is careful to note that the overall global impact has not yet achieved consistent scale quarter in and quarter out. This is the critical distinction. Netflix can point to individual successes, but the question is whether live events can become a steady-state driver of platform metrics rather than episodic boosts.

The implications for the Q2 report are straightforward. If live event programming contributed meaningfully to engagement or subscriber trends in the quarter, it would support the thesis that Netflix is building a durable competitive moat. If the impact was muted, it would suggest that the company is still figuring out how to integrate live events into its broader content calendar without cannibalizing its own on-demand library. The analyst's framing of a "mixture of headwinds" from seasonality and live event impact suggests that the quarter may not resolve this debate.

## Vertical Video Represents a Strategic Hedge, Not a Near-Term Revenue Driver

Netflix's entry into vertical video is the most speculative of the four key themes, but it may be the most strategically important over a three-to-five-year horizon. The format has become dominant among younger demographics, driven by TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts. Netflix is not trying to compete with those platforms on volume. It is trying to capture a share of the attention that is migrating away from traditional long-form video.

The opportunity is twofold. First, vertical video can drive incremental platform engagement, particularly among users who are not currently heavy Netflix consumers. Second, it creates a new inventory category for advertising, potentially opening up formats that are more native to mobile consumption. The risk is that Netflix dilutes its brand identity as a premium, lean-back experience. The company has built its reputation on high-production-value storytelling. Vertical video, by its nature, is more ephemeral and snackable.

The Q2 report is unlikely to provide meaningful data on vertical video performance. This is a long-term initiative, and the company is still in the early stages of product development. But observers should watch for any commentary on user adoption or engagement patterns. If Netflix can demonstrate that vertical video is extending time spent on the platform rather than replacing existing consumption, it would be a positive signal. If the initiative is merely additive to a declining core engagement baseline, the strategic value is less clear.

Netflix has authorized approximately $31.8 billion in remaining share buybacks, and the analyst model assumes a linear application of that authorization over the next 2.5 years, consistent with the historical average of roughly 90% of annual free cash flow. This is a significant capital allocation decision. It signals management's confidence in the company's cash generation and its view that the company is undervalued.

The buyback has a direct impact on earnings per share. The analyst estimates show diluted shares outstanding declining from 4.34 billion in 2025 to 4.01 billion by 2028, a reduction of roughly 8%. Combined with projected earnings growth, this creates a powerful compounding effect. The EPS trajectory—from $2.53 in 2025 to $4.89 by 2028—reflects both operating improvement and financial engineering.

But buybacks are not a substitute for operating momentum. If subscriber growth continues to slow and engagement declines persist, the buyback will mask rather than solve the underlying problem. The analyst's view is that the operating challenges are real and that financial leverage alone cannot offset them. The Q2 report will need to show that the operating business is stabilizing, not just that the buyback is reducing share count.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Netflix's Competitive Position

The analyst report provides a detailed framework for understanding Netflix's near-term challenges and long-term opportunities. But it leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is whether Netflix's competitive position is structurally weakening or merely cyclically soft. The decline in monthly active users and time spent could reflect normal seasonality, content slate timing, and post-pandemic normalization. Or it could signal that competitors—including both traditional studios and tech-backed streaming services—are eroding Netflix's share of viewing time.

The report does not provide a definitive answer because the data is not yet conclusive. The shift to live events and vertical video is a bet that Netflix can create new engagement vectors that competitors cannot easily replicate. But those bets are unproven at scale. The advertising business is growing, but it is still a small fraction of total revenue. The buyback is powerful, but it does not change the competitive dynamics of the streaming market.

Observers should approach the Q2 report with a clear framework. If the company can demonstrate that advertising revenue is accelerating, that live events are driving incremental engagement, and that core subscriber trends are stabilizing, the outlook is compelling. If the quarter confirms the negative trends in MAUs and time spent without offsetting positive signals from the new revenue streams, the company's valuation may remain range-bound until the thesis becomes more visible.

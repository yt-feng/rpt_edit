# Copper's Ex-US Deficit Is the Real Price Driver, Not Tariff Speculation

The copper market is telling a story that many observers are misreading. While headlines focus on tariff threats and geopolitical risks, the real force behind today's elevated prices is a structural tightening outside the United States that is far more severe than most forecasts anticipated. Copper now trades at approximately $13,600 per tonne, up 10% year to date, but the narrative that this rally is driven by speculative froth or policy uncertainty misses the deeper structural shift underway.

The critical insight is this: the ex-US market, where the LME price is determined, is heading into a deficit of 640,000 tonnes in 2026 and 170,000 tonnes in 2027. These are not marginal adjustments. They represent a tenfold increase from earlier deficit estimates of 60,000 and 40,000 tonnes respectively. This is not a temporary imbalance. It is the consequence of supply disruptions that will take years to resolve, combined with demand that is becoming less price-sensitive as it shifts toward strategic infrastructure.

What makes this moment particularly significant is that the price support is coming from two reinforcing mechanisms. The first is physical: lower mine supply and higher US imports are draining metal from the rest of the world. The second is behavioral: speculative positioning, anchored by the long-term structural bull case for copper, is keeping prices above what a pure fundamental model would justify. Together, these forces create a market that is resilient to near-term shocks but vulnerable to a specific set of policy and geopolitical outcomes.

The question for serious investors is not whether copper is expensive. It is whether the current price adequately reflects the risks embedded in the ex-US balance, or whether the market is still underestimating the persistence of supply constraints and the depth of demand from sectors that cannot easily substitute away.

## Persistent Mine Supply Disruptions Have Removed 350,000 Tonnes from 2026 Production, and Full Recovery Will Not Arrive Until 2028

The most consequential revision in the updated outlook is the reduction in global mine supply by approximately 350,000 tonnes for 2026, representing roughly 1.5% of global mine output. This is not a forecast error that will correct itself next quarter. The cuts are concentrated in two major operations: Grasberg in Indonesia and Kamoa-Kakula in the Democratic Republic of Congo, which together account for roughly 200,000 tonnes of the reduction. Neither mine is expected to return to full capacity until 2028.

This matters because it shifts the entire trajectory of the refined market. Lower mine supply means less concentrate available for smelters, which in turn constrains refined production. The report explicitly states that scrap has not provided an offset, with Chinese domestic scrap output down 12% year on year due to stricter VAT invoice compliance. This is a regulatory bottleneck that is unlikely to ease quickly, meaning the refined market will remain dependent on primary production at a time when that production is falling short.

The implication is that the supply side of the copper market is experiencing a multi-year impairment, not a one-off shock. Investors who assume that higher prices will automatically incentivize new supply should note that the two largest contributors to the deficit are existing mines with operational problems, not greenfield projects that could be accelerated. The timeline to 2028 means that for the next two years, the market must operate with a structurally smaller supply base.

## US Import Demand Is Tightening the Ex-US Balance More Than Tariff Policy Itself

The second driver of the ex-US deficit is not mine supply but trade flows. US copper imports in the first half of 2026 have exceeded expectations, tightening the balance for the rest of the world. The report now forecasts US inventory builds of 900,000 tonnes in 2026, up from a prior estimate of 550,000 tonnes. This is happening even under the base case assumption that no copper tariff will be announced this year.

The mechanism is straightforward. The US is importing copper not because of an announced tariff, but because the market is pricing in the risk of a future tariff. Importers are front-loading purchases to hedge against a policy change that may or may not materialize. This creates a self-fulfilling dynamic: the anticipation of a tariff tightens the ex-US market, which supports LME prices, which in turn justifies further imports.

The critical nuance is that this import behavior is not uniform across scenarios. Under a scenario where a tariff is announced in June 2026 to take effect in January 2027, imports would accelerate further in the second half of 2026, pushing prices above $14,000. Under a scenario where no tariff is announced, imports would slow but not stop entirely, because the risk of a future tariff would remain. The report explicitly compares this to the critical minerals tariff decision, which left the door open for future action.

The so-what for investors is that the ex-US deficit is partly policy-driven but not policy-dependent. Even in the absence of a tariff, the market remains tight. The tariff question is a modifier of degree, not a binary switch between surplus and deficit.

## Strategic Demand from Grid, AI, and Defense Is Making Copper Less Sensitive to Economic Slowdowns

One of the most important structural arguments in the report is that copper demand is becoming less cyclical. The analysis notes that more than 60% of copper demand growth by 2030 will come from grid and power infrastructure, driven by AI, data centers, and defense-related energy security investments. These sectors are not only less sensitive to GDP fluctuations than construction or consumer goods, but they are also less sensitive to high copper prices.

The report provides a concrete data point: Chinese apparent copper demand remained resilient at plus 1% year on year in the first quarter of 2026, despite copper prices trading near all-time highs. Strength in grid and power infrastructure demand, which grew 10% year on year, offset weakness in solar and electric vehicle-related demand. This is a pattern that challenges the traditional view that high prices automatically destroy demand.

The implication for the price outlook is significant. If demand is becoming less elastic, then the price required to balance the market must be higher than historical models would suggest. The report's fair value estimate of $12,600 for 2026 and $13,800 for 2027 already incorporates this shift, but the market may still be underestimating how much of demand is now effectively captive to strategic priorities.

This also has implications for the downside scenarios. In the event of a prolonged closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the report estimates that a 1.1 percentage point hit to global GDP would reduce copper demand by roughly 300,000 tonnes. But it also notes that this would be largely offset by supply disruptions caused by sulfur shortages, which would affect production in the DRC and Chile. The net effect on the balance is neutral, meaning that even a severe economic shock may not produce a lasting price collapse.

## The Report Leaves One Critical Question Unanswered: How Much of the Current Price Is Speculative Premium Versus Structural Value

Despite its thoroughness, the report does not fully resolve the tension between its fundamental fair value estimates and the current market price. The 2026 fair value of $12,600 is approximately $1,000 below the current price of $13,600. The report attributes this gap to speculative positioning, noting that a reversal of net managed money positions to March lows could imply 6.6% downside, or roughly $900 per tonne.

But this raises a deeper question that the report only partially addresses. If speculative positioning is the primary reason prices are above fair value, what catalyst would trigger a reversal? The report suggests that a prolonged Strait of Hormuz closure could do it, by reducing risk appetite. But it also acknowledges that in that scenario, the fundamental support level is roughly the same as the fair value, meaning the downside is limited.

The unanswered question is whether the speculative premium is truly unsustainable, or whether it reflects a rational reassessment of the long-term value of copper in a world of constrained supply and strategic demand. If the latter, then the fair value estimates themselves may need to be revised upward. The report does not take that step, leaving it as an open analytical question for readers to consider.

This is precisely the kind of unresolved tension that makes the full report worth reading. The scenarios are laid out clearly, but the probabilities assigned to each are not. Investors must decide for themselves whether the base case is too conservative, or whether the market has already priced in too much optimism.

## A Decision Framework for Copper Exposure: Three Questions Every Investor Should Answer

Given the range of outcomes, a static position in copper is insufficient. Investors need a framework for dynamic decision-making based on how the key variables evolve. The report's scenarios provide the raw material for such a framework, which can be distilled into three questions.

First, what is your view on US copper tariff policy? If you believe a tariff is likely to be announced in the second half of 2026, then the appropriate positioning is to be long copper through that period, with an expectation that prices could exceed $14,000. However, you must also have an exit strategy for 2027, when the imposition of the tariff would likely cause prices to retreat as imports stop.

Second, how do you assess the persistence of mine supply disruptions? If you believe that Grasberg and Kamoa-Kakula will return to capacity sooner than 2028, then the current deficit forecasts are overstated and the market may be overpriced. If you believe the disruptions will persist or worsen, then the fair value estimates are too low and the current price may be justified.

Third, what is your view on the demand elasticity of strategic sectors? If you believe that grid, AI, and defense demand is as price-inelastic as the report suggests, then the traditional relationship between price and demand is broken, and copper should trade at a structural premium to historical norms. If you believe that high prices will eventually trigger substitution or efficiency gains, then the current price is vulnerable.

These three questions map directly onto the report's three alternative scenarios. The Strait of Hormuz scenario tests the demand elasticity question. The tariff scenario tests the policy question. The no-tariff scenario tests the supply question. By assigning probabilities to each, investors can construct a weighted view that goes beyond the base case.

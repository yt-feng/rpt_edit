# The G10 Consumer Is Splitting Into Two Distinct Regimes: Balance Sheet Wealthy but Income Strapped

The most important insight from the latest G10 consumer dashboard is not that consumer health is deteriorating broadly. It is that a structural divergence has opened between household balance sheets and household cash flows. Across the developed world, net-worth-to-income ratios remain near all-time highs in the United States, Japan, and Australia, while real income growth has collapsed to the 5th percentile in the US and the 20th percentile in Canada. This is not a normal cyclical softening. It is a regime in which consumers are simultaneously wealthy on paper and constrained in their wallets.

This matters now because the transmission mechanism from balance sheet strength to spending has broken. In previous cycles, high household net worth acted as a buffer that sustained consumption during income shocks. Today, sentiment has fallen to historic lows in the US—the University of Michigan index reached its lowest level in the survey's history—precisely because consumers are experiencing the erosion of purchasing power in real time. The saving rate in the US has dropped to its lowest level since June 2022, suggesting that households are depleting precautionary buffers rather than increasing discretionary spending.

The data through April 2026 tells a story that is more nuanced than "the consumer is weakening." The consumer is fragmenting along two axes: geography and income source. North American consumers face the sharpest income headwinds, while Euro area and Japanese consumers benefit from labor markets near historical lows in unemployment. Australia presents a paradox: strong employment but spending at the 15th percentile. The UK and Canada are experiencing outright labor market softening.

For investors and strategists, the critical question is whether this divergence will persist or converge. The answer depends on how energy prices, wage dynamics, and fiscal support evolve in the coming months. The dashboard provides the diagnostic. The strategic implications require interpretation.

## Household Balance Sheets Are a Lagging Indicator That Masks Real-Time Spending Stress

The dashboard shows that household net worth as a share of income stands close to all-time highs in the US, Japan, and Australia, at the 90th percentile in Canada and the Euro area, and at the 63rd percentile in the UK. At first glance, this suggests a resilient consumer base. But balance sheets are a stock variable, while spending is driven by flows. The ratio of net worth to income tells us what households own, not what they earn or how they feel about spending.

The saving rate data reveals the tension. In the US, the saving rate has declined to its lowest level since June 2022. In Canada, it has also declined, though it remains elevated by historical standards. Outside Japan, saving rates across the G10 remain elevated, which is typically a sign of precautionary behavior rather than confidence. Households are holding cash not because they are optimistic, but because they are uncertain.

The implication is clear: balance sheet strength is a necessary condition for consumer resilience, but it is not sufficient. If real income growth continues to deteriorate, households will eventually draw down their net worth to maintain consumption, or they will cut spending. The data through April suggests the latter is already happening. Real spending growth stands below historical averages across all developed markets, at the 15th percentile in Australia, the 20th in the US, the 35th in Canada, and the 40th in the Euro area and Japan.

The so-what for investors is that any investment thesis based solely on household wealth is incomplete. The correct framework is to weight balance sheet strength against income momentum, and the dashboard shows that income momentum is deteriorating faster than balance sheets are adjusting.

## Real Income Growth Has Collapsed in North America While Japan Defies the Trend

The most striking data point in the dashboard is the percentile ranking of real income growth. In the US, it stands at the 5th percentile. In Canada, it is at the 20th percentile. In the Euro area, it is at the 95th percentile. In Japan, it is near all-time highs. This is not a minor deviation. It is a chasm.

The US decline partially reflects a technical factor: the expiration of payments from the Farmer Bridge Assistance program, which had boosted personal income in March. But the structural story is more concerning. Real personal spending grew only 0.1% in April, with a 0.2% increase in services spending offset by a 0.1% decline in goods spending. The report anticipates a slowdown in the months ahead as higher energy prices erode spending power. This is not a forecast of a recession, but it is a forecast of a consumption drag.

Japan's outperformance is equally noteworthy. Basic wage growth for all workers slowed slightly to 3.2% in March from 3.4% in February, but it remains above 3%. This is consistent with Japan's long-awaited transition out of deflationary dynamics. The dashboard shows Japan's real income growth at nearly the 100th percentile, which is extraordinary for an economy that has struggled with stagnant wages for decades.

For global investors, this divergence has portfolio implications. If North American consumers are income-constrained while Japanese consumers are income-supported, then exposure to domestic consumption in Japan may be more attractive than in the US or Canada. The dashboard does not make this recommendation, but the data supports the inference.

## Labor Markets Are No Longer a Reliable Support for Consumption in the UK and Canada

The dashboard reveals a clear hierarchy in labor market health. The unemployment rate is close to historical lows in the Euro area at 6.2%, Japan at 2.5%, and Australia at 4.5%. These are tight labor markets that should support wage growth and consumption. In contrast, the US unemployment rate stands at the 65th percentile at 4.3%, which is historically low but rising. The UK at the 35th percentile at 5.0% and Canada at the 20th percentile at 6.9% are notably weaker.

Since the last dashboard update, unemployment ticked down in the Euro area and Japan, moved sideways in the US, and increased in the UK, Canada, and Australia. The trend is clear: labor markets are tightening in Europe and Japan while softening in the English-speaking economies outside the US.

The so-what is that labor market normalization in the US is proceeding in an orderly fashion, but the UK and Canada are experiencing outright deterioration. For the UK, private sector pay growth slowed to 3.0% in March from 3.2% in February, below consensus expectations. This is not a crash, but it is a deceleration that, combined with elevated debt service ratios, creates a fragile consumer environment.

Debt service ratios are instructive here. They remain low by historical standards in the US at the 75th percentile, the Euro area at the 75th percentile, and Japan at the 55th percentile. But they are higher in Canada at the 25th percentile, Australia at the 40th percentile, and the UK at the 45th percentile. Canada, in particular, faces a dual burden: a weak labor market and elevated debt service costs. House prices continue to decline in Canada, which further erodes household wealth and confidence.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Energy Prices Will Reshape Consumer Behavior Across Different Income Brackets

The dashboard identifies higher energy prices as a key headwind to real incomes in the coming months, but it does not disaggregate the impact by income level. This is a meaningful gap because energy price shocks are regressive. Lower-income households spend a larger share of their income on energy and have less buffer from savings or asset wealth.

The report notes that in Australia, nominal household spending declined by 1.1% month-on-month in April, largely driven by a pullback in transport, which the Australian Bureau of Statistics attributed to "widespread impacts and responses to the conflict in the Middle East." This is a direct example of how geopolitical events translate into consumer behavior. But the dashboard does not provide a framework for estimating how much of the spending slowdown is due to energy prices versus other factors.

An open question for readers is whether the pass-through from energy prices to core consumption will be larger in countries with higher debt service ratios, such as Canada and the UK, or in countries with lower saving rates, such as the US. The data suggests both channels are relevant, but the relative importance is unclear.

Another unresolved question is the role of fiscal policy. The US decline in real income growth was partially driven by the expiration of a specific assistance program. If governments in other G10 economies introduce or withdraw fiscal support, the trajectory of consumer health could shift materially. The dashboard does not model fiscal scenarios.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Weight Income Momentum Over Balance Sheet Strength, and Watch the Energy Pass-Through

For readers who need to translate this dashboard into actionable analysis, the following framework is useful.

First, rank G10 economies by income momentum rather than balance sheet strength. Japan and the Euro area score highest on real income growth. The US and Canada score lowest. This ranking should be the primary filter for assessing consumer-related exposures.

Second, overlay labor market health. Japan, the Euro area, and Australia have tight labor markets. The UK and Canada have weakening labor markets. The US is in between. The combination of strong income momentum and tight labor markets is the most favorable regime. Japan and the Euro area fit this description. The UK and Canada do not.

Third, assess the energy price sensitivity of each economy. The dashboard notes that higher energy prices will erode spending power in the next few months. Economies with higher energy intensity in consumption, lower saving rates, and higher debt service ratios will be more vulnerable. Canada and the UK appear most exposed on all three dimensions.

Fourth, monitor the saving rate. The US saving rate has declined to its lowest level since June 2022. This suggests that households are drawing down buffers. If the saving rate continues to fall, spending may hold up temporarily, but at the cost of future resilience. If the saving rate stabilizes or rises, it would signal that households are cutting spending to rebuild buffers.

Finally, watch for divergence within the US consumer. The dashboard provides aggregate data, but the US consumer is not monolithic. The top quintile of households by wealth has benefited from asset price appreciation and has low debt service costs. The bottom quintile is income-constrained and faces higher inflation sensitivity. The aggregate data masks this bifurcation.

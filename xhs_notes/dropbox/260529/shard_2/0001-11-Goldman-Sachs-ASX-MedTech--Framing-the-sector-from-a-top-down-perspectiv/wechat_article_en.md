# ASX MedTech Is Not a Stock-Picker’s Game Right Now: The Sector Signal Overwhelms the Company-Specific Story

The conventional wisdom among ASX healthcare investors is that MedTech is a stock-picker’s universe. The argument is familiar: ResMed’s fate hinges on Philips’ re-entry timeline and GLP-1 drug competition; Cochlear depends on Nexa market share and CI market growth; Fisher & Paykel must execute on hospital capital cycles; Nanosonics lives or dies by Trophon upgrade cadence and CORIS adoption. This narrative is comfortable because it suggests that deep due diligence on individual companies can protect a portfolio from broader market moves. It is also increasingly wrong.

A global investment bank’s recent analysis of the US MedTech sector forces a uncomfortable reframing. Large Cap US MedTech stocks have declined approximately 20% year-to-date through late May 2026. ASX MedTech names have not been spared: ResMed is down 18%, Nanosonics down 19%, and Cochlear has suffered a staggering 62% decline. Only Fisher & Paykel, down 3%, has held relatively firm. The pattern is clear. This is not a collection of independent stock-specific blow-ups. It is a sector-wide de-rating driven by two systemic forces: decelerating organic revenue growth and a sharp contraction in valuation multiples. The company-specific debates that dominate investor calls are necessary conditions for stock selection, but they are no longer sufficient conditions for portfolio performance.

The critical insight is that ASX MedTech investors are now playing a game they may not realize they are in. The sector’s earnings trajectory and its valuation are now the dominant variables. Company-specific catalysts, while real, are operating against a macro headwind that has historically taken years to reverse. Understanding the last cycle is not academic history; it is the most reliable roadmap for what comes next.

## The Revenue Growth Deceleration Is Not Cyclical Noise; It Is a Structural Downdraft That Has Not Fully Played Out

The headline organic revenue growth for US MedTech has decelerated to approximately 6% in 2026 estimates, down from a post-COVID peak of 8% in 2023 and 7.5% in 2024. This looks like a modest normalization. It is not. The bank’s analysis reveals that two specific headwinds have not been adequately captured in CY26 guidance: US Managed Care changes and US Medicaid cuts. These are not one-off adjustments. They represent a structural change in the funding environment for medical devices, particularly for capital equipment and consumables that depend on procedure volumes and hospital budgets.

The historical analogue is instructive. Excluding the COVID period, the last major deceleration in MedTech organic growth ran from approximately 2010 to 2013, a three-year period during which growth slowed from 7.5% to 4%. The current deceleration began in 2024, meaning we may only be halfway through the adjustment. The bank’s data shows that during that prior cycle, valuations bottomed at approximately 11x forward earnings in 2011, compared to the current level of roughly 19x. Even after the recent 700-basis-point contraction from the 20-year average, the sector still trades at a significant premium to that trough.

The so-what is uncomfortable. If the earnings headwinds from payor changes and Medicaid cuts are larger than guidance implies, the current multiple of 19x may not be the floor. It may be a way station. The last cycle required a full rebasing of forward earnings expectations before valuations could recover. That process, by historical precedent, takes time and typically involves downward revisions that are larger and more persistent than most models currently assume.

## Valuation Compression Has Further to Run, and the Historical Recovery Playbook Requires Three Conditions That Are Not Yet Met

The multiple contraction in MedTech has been severe by any measure. The sector currently trades at approximately 16.8x forward earnings, roughly 700 basis points below its 20-year average. This is not a garden-variety correction. It is the kind of de-rating that typically signals a regime change in investor sentiment, not a temporary bout of risk aversion.

The bank’s analysis of the last major valuation cycle is worth studying in detail. Between 2008 and 2011, MedTech multiples compressed from the high teens to a trough of approximately 11x. The recovery from that trough was driven by three factors in sequence: a significant increase in share repurchase activity, a full rebasing of forward earnings expectations to realistic levels, and the emergence of positive growth catalysts such as new product approvals and M&A. All three conditions were necessary. None alone was sufficient.

Apply this framework to the current environment. Share repurchase activity is present but not yet at the levels seen in the 2011-2013 recovery period. ResMed has preserved its approximately USD 700 million buyback program and has supplemented it with the Noctrix acquisition. Fisher & Paykel has several products awaiting FDA approval and has recently stepped up its dividend, signaling confidence in capital management. These are positive signals, but they are not yet at the scale that historically marked the inflection point. The percentage of free cash flow returned to shareholders in the prior recovery cycle reached 72% in 2011, 76% in 2012, and 88% in 2013. Current levels, while improving, are not at those extremes.

The second condition, a full rebasing of earnings expectations, is the most difficult to assess and the most dangerous to ignore. The bank explicitly states that the headwinds from US Managed Care changes and Medicaid cuts have not been adequately captured in CY26 guidance. If this is correct, then the current earnings base is overstated, and the multiple is not as cheap as it appears. A 19x multiple on overstated earnings is not a bargain; it is a value trap until the reset occurs.

The third condition, positive growth catalysts, is the most company-specific and the most hopeful. ResMed is on the cusp of new product launches and has expanded its offering through the Noctrix acquisition. Fisher & Paykel has a pipeline ready for launch pending FDA approval. Nanosonics is rolling out CORIS, though the report notes that the rollout will take time to offset the risk of a slowdown in Trophon upgrades. These catalysts are real, but they are operating against a macro headwind that historically required a multi-year adjustment. The question is whether they are strong enough to pull the sector up before earnings catch down to the multiple.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question It Does Not Fully Answer: How Much of the Current Valuation Reflects a Permanent Re-Rating of MedTech Versus a Cyclical Trough

The bank’s analysis is thorough, but it leaves one central question unresolved. The current forward multiple of approximately 19x is well above the 2011 trough of 11x but well below the 20-year average of approximately 23x. Is this a cyclical low that will revert to the mean, or is it a structural adjustment to a lower growth regime?

The answer depends on whether the current growth deceleration is cyclical or structural. If the headwinds from payor changes and Medicaid cuts are temporary, and if new product cycles and M&A can reaccelerate growth back to 7-8%, then the current multiple is attractive. If, however, the sector is entering a period of structurally slower growth driven by persistent funding pressures, demographic shifts, and pricing constraints, then the 19x multiple may be fair value or even expensive.

The report does not resolve this tension, and readers should be wary of anyone who claims to have the answer. The historical data shows that MedTech growth has been remarkably mean-reverting over long periods, but the post-COVID period was an outlier in both growth and valuation. The question for investors is whether the current de-rating is a return to normal or an overshoot to a new normal. The evidence is not yet clear, and the uncertainty itself is a reason for caution.

## A Decision Framework for ASX MedTech Investors: Three Scenarios and the Signals That Differentiate Them

Given the structural uncertainty, the most useful approach is not to predict the outcome but to define the scenarios and identify the signals that will distinguish them. The following framework translates the report’s analysis into actionable decision rules.

Scenario One: The Cyclical Recovery. In this scenario, the current growth deceleration bottoms in 2026 at approximately 5-6% organic growth, payor headwinds prove temporary, and new product cycles from ResMed and Fisher & Paykel reaccelerate growth in 2027. Valuations recover to the 20-year average of approximately 23x, driven by increased share repurchase activity and a stabilization of earnings expectations. The signal to watch for is a sustained uptick in organic growth guidance from the US MedTech majors, combined with a material increase in buyback announcements. If this occurs, the current levels will prove to have been an attractive entry point.

Scenario Two: The Structural Downshift. In this scenario, payor changes and Medicaid cuts prove persistent, organic growth settles at 4-5% (the 2010-2013 range), and valuations compress further to the 15-16x range before stabilizing. Earnings expectations are revised downward by 10-15% over the next 12-18 months. The signal to watch for is a series of negative pre-announcements or guidance cuts from US MedTech leaders, particularly those with high exposure to US hospital purchasing and managed care. If this occurs, the current multiple is not the floor, and ASX names will likely follow.

Scenario Three: The Divergence. In this scenario, the sector as a whole continues to de-rate, but company-specific catalysts create pockets of outperformance. ResMed’s new product launches and buyback program, Fisher & Paykel’s FDA approvals and capital management, and Nanosonics’ CORIS rollout could each generate idiosyncratic returns that decouple from the sector. The signal to watch for is a clear acceleration in company-specific revenue growth that is not dependent on the macro environment. If this occurs, stock selection becomes the dominant driver of returns, and the sector signal becomes noise.

The report’s authors lean toward Scenario Three, maintaining Buy ratings on ResMed, Fisher & Paykel, and Nanosonics while remaining Neutral on Cochlear. This is a defensible view, but it requires conviction that company-specific catalysts are strong enough to overcome the sector headwind. The historical precedent from 2010-2013 suggests that even the best companies in a de-rating sector tend to underperform until the macro headwind abates.

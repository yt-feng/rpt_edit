# The ABF Substrate Market Is Heading Into a Structural Shortage That Most Investors Are Underestimating

The semiconductor industry is about to confront a bottleneck that few are watching closely. While attention remains fixed on GPU supply constraints and advanced packaging capacity, a less visible but equally critical component is tightening: the ABF substrate. These organic packages form the physical and electrical backbone for high-performance chips, and the data now suggests that demand will outstrip supply in a meaningful and sustained way starting in 2027.

This is not a cyclical upturn. It is a structural shift driven by a fundamental re-acceleration in server CPU volumes, larger chip sizes across successive generations, and robust demand from GPU and ASIC accelerators. A global investment bank report projects total ABF substrate demand will grow at a 22% CAGR from 2025 to 2028, while supply will expand at only a 12% CAGR. The arithmetic is simple and the implication is clear: the market is moving from oversupply to scarcity, and pricing power is shifting decisively to substrate manufacturers.

The timing matters because the market is not yet pricing in this inflection. Capacity expansions have been announced, but they are insufficient relative to the demand trajectory. The window for investors to position ahead of the shortage is narrowing. Understanding the drivers, the supply constraints, and the second-order effects is no longer optional for anyone with exposure to semiconductor supply chains.

## Server CPU Volumes Are Poised to Quadruple by 2030, and That Changes Everything for Substrate Demand

The most powerful driver of ABF demand is not GPUs, it is the renaissance of the server CPU. After two years of weak shipments, server CPU volumes are set to expand dramatically. The report forecasts that server CPU volumes will reach 30 million units by 2030, roughly a fourfold increase from 2026 levels. This expansion is driven by the rise of agentic AI, which requires significantly more general-purpose compute than previous generations of AI workloads.

Why does this matter for substrates? Server CPUs account for approximately 40% of total ABF substrate demand. A quadrupling of volumes translates directly into a massive increase in substrate area requirements. The bank's model shows substrate demand from server CPUs growing at a 37% CAGR, from roughly 205 ksqm per month in 2025 to 529 ksqm per month by 2028. This is not a gradual trend. It is a step-change that reshapes the entire demand curve.

The implication is that even if GPU demand were to moderate, the substrate market would still face significant pressure. Server CPUs are the foundation of the data center, and their expansion is structural, not speculative. Investors who focus only on AI accelerators are missing the larger story.

## Each New Generation of CPUs Requires Meaningfully Larger Substrates, Compounding Volume Growth

Volume growth alone would be enough to strain supply. But the substrate market faces an additional compounding factor: content growth per chip. Each successive generation of server CPUs requires a larger substrate area, driven by higher pin counts, more cores, and more complex architectures.

The data from Intel is illustrative. The substrate size for Intel's Granite Rapids is 61.1 square centimeters. The upcoming Diamond Rapids, expected in mid-2027, will be approximately 82.8 square centimeters, an increase of roughly 36%. Across generations, Intel has consistently increased substrate size by 24% to 36% per generation. AMD follows a similar pattern, with substrate sizes increasing by 12% to 23% per generation.

For Arm-based CPUs, the driver is core count. Amazon's Graviton series demonstrates this clearly: core count has risen from 16 in Graviton 1 to 192 in Graviton 5, a 12x increase across five generations. More cores require more substrate area, and this trend shows no signs of slowing.

The so-what is straightforward. Even if unit volumes remained flat, the substrate market would grow meaningfully due to increasing area per chip. When volume growth and content growth occur simultaneously, the demand trajectory becomes exponential rather than linear. The report estimates that total CPU substrate area demand will grow at a 19% CAGR from 2025 to 2028, with server CPUs alone growing at 37% CAGR. This is the kind of compound growth that supply chains struggle to match.

## Supply Is Growing at Half the Rate of Demand, Making a Shortage Inevitable

The supply side of the equation tells a different story. ABF substrate suppliers have stepped up capacity investment, but the pace is insufficient. The report forecasts industry supply will grow at a 12% CAGR from 2025 to 2028. Demand, by contrast, is growing at 22% CAGR.

This gap is not marginal. It is structural. The report projects that industry utilization will rise from 80-85% in 2025 to above 100% by 2027, with the imbalance worsening further in 2028. In practical terms, this means that by 2027, the market will be unable to fulfill all demand. Lead times will extend. Customers will compete for allocation. Pricing will rise.

Among the major suppliers, Ibiden and Unimicron appear best positioned. Both have the strongest customer exposure and are the most aggressive in expanding capacity, supported in part by customer prepayments. But even their expansions are not enough to close the gap. The report notes that its current demand estimates likely remain conservative, as they do not yet incorporate lower yields associated with larger and more complex substrates. If yields deteriorate as substrate sizes increase, the effective supply shortage could be even more severe.

The critical question is whether Ajinomoto, the sole producer of the ABF film itself, will become a bottleneck. The report argues that it will not. Ajinomoto has been investing ahead of the curve, increasing capacity by roughly 25% in mid-2025 through its Gunma expansion. It has further scope to double output by extending the operating week to seven days and running a second shift daily. A third production site in Gifu is scheduled to come online by 2032, or potentially 2030 if needed. The film supply appears manageable. The constraint is at the substrate manufacturing level, not the raw material level.

## Pricing Power Is Shifting to Substrate Manufacturers, and Current Estimates Likely Understate the Upside

When demand exceeds supply, pricing power shifts. The report explicitly states that any shortage is likely to result in further upside to average selling prices. For Ibiden, the current estimates bake in price increases of 4%, 6%, and 12% across fiscal years 2027 through 2029, but the report notes that this largely reflects product mix shift rather than outright pricing hikes. If a genuine shortage emerges, outright price increases become likely.

For Unimicron, the report estimates that ABF substrate ASP hikes will accelerate from 5% quarter-on-quarter in early 2026 to 7-10% quarter-on-quarter through the rest of the year, driven by a roughly 30% raw material cost hike and a richer high-end mix. This is not a one-time adjustment. It is the beginning of a sustained pricing cycle.

The implication for investors is that revenue and earnings estimates for substrate manufacturers are likely too low. The report raises its price target for Ibiden to ¥23,900, based on 35x fiscal 2029 earnings per share, up from 30x previously. The justification is improved demand visibility and stronger earnings growth. But the report also notes that it would look for more upside in pricing before taking profit on the name. This is a signal that the bull case may not yet be fully reflected in the stock.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Second-Order Effects on End Customers and Alternative Technologies

Every structural shortage creates winners and losers, and the report leaves several important questions open. The first is how end customers will respond. If ABF substrates become scarce and expensive, will chip designers accelerate their adoption of alternative packaging technologies? Glass substrates have been discussed as a potential long-term replacement for organic ABF, but they are not yet commercially viable at scale. The report does not model this substitution risk, and it remains an open question whether the shortage will accelerate R&D timelines for alternatives.

The second open question is about allocation. When supply is constrained, not all customers are treated equally. The report notes that Ibiden and Unimicron have the best customer exposure, supported by prepayments. This suggests that larger, well-funded customers may secure capacity at the expense of smaller players. The report does not explore which segments of the market are most vulnerable to being squeezed out.

The third question is about the sustainability of the demand outlook. The report's demand projections are based on aggressive assumptions about server CPU volumes and agentic AI adoption. If AI workloads shift toward more efficient architectures or if enterprise adoption slows, the demand trajectory could moderate. The report acknowledges this implicitly by noting that its estimates likely remain conservative, but it does not stress-test the downside.

These are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of any forecast. But they are also the questions that investors should be asking as they build their own conviction.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Your Exposure

For investors trying to translate this analysis into action, three questions are essential.

First, do you believe the server CPU volume thesis? The entire substrate demand forecast rests on the assumption that server CPU volumes will quadruple by 2030. If you believe that agentic AI will drive a massive increase in general-purpose compute demand, then the substrate shortage thesis is compelling. If you are skeptical, then the demand-side argument weakens significantly.

Second, how much pricing power do you think substrate manufacturers will have? The report models price increases, but it acknowledges that the current estimates are conservative. If a genuine shortage emerges, pricing could rise substantially more than modeled. The upside scenario is that substrate manufacturers become pricing makers rather than pricing takers for the first time in years. The downside scenario is that capacity additions eventually catch up and pricing reverts.

Third, what is your time horizon? The report projects that the shortage will begin in 2027. For investors with a two-to-three-year horizon, the current window may be the last opportunity to position before the market reprices. For investors with a shorter horizon, the risk is that the shortage narrative takes time to play out and that near-term volatility obscures the structural trend.

These questions do not have easy answers. But they are the right questions to ask.

# The AI ROI Debate Is Misguided: The Real Strategic Question Is Which Software Becomes Indispensable Infrastructure

The market's sharply divergent reactions to Salesforce and Snowflake earnings on May 28 tell a story far deeper than quarterly beats and misses. Salesforce shares slipped in after-hours trade despite beating consensus on both revenue and earnings per share, while Snowflake surged 36 percent on similar outperformance. The difference was not about execution. It was about perceived strategic positioning in the AI era. Investors are beginning to sort software companies into two categories: those that AI will render redundant, and those that AI will render essential. This sorting is only just beginning, and it will determine the winners and losers across Japan's IT services sector for the next decade.

The surface-level narrative is straightforward. Both companies emphasized AI-driven acceleration in software sales and expressed confidence in their ability to coexist with AI platforms. But the market rewarded Snowflake far more generously because its product addresses a problem that is becoming acute: the difficulty of measuring and improving return on investment from AI deployments. Uber's CTO recently stated that productivity gains from AI do not currently justify costs, and the company has reportedly suspended internal use of some AI tools. This admission crystallizes a concern that many enterprises share but few have articulated publicly. AI operates on a pay-per-use model based on token consumption, meaning costs scale with usage and can spiral unpredictably. Without clear mechanisms to improve ROI, enterprise AI adoption faces a real risk of backlash.

This is where the strategic distinction between types of software becomes critical. The report from a global investment bank argues that software replacement by AI will be selective. Systems of record and database tools that provide essential context for AI platforms will not be replaced. Indeed, Snowflake announced a $200 million partnership with OpenAI, and Salesforce stated that Anthropic's use of its products has increased fivefold. These are not coincidental developments. They reflect a fundamental shift in how enterprises will consume AI. The software that survives and thrives will be the software that makes AI more useful, not the software that AI can replace.

## The Market Is Correctly Differentiating Between Software That AI Will Replace and Software That AI Will Require

The divergence between Salesforce and Snowflake's market reactions is not a quirk of quarterly earnings expectations. It reflects a structural reassessment of each company's role in the AI value chain. Salesforce delivered solid results with above-consensus EPS guidance, yet the stock barely moved. Snowflake delivered similar quantitative outperformance and was rewarded with a 36 percent surge. The difference lies in what each company's product does for AI deployments.

Salesforce's core products are customer relationship management tools. These are valuable, but they are also the kind of software that AI could potentially replace or subsume. If an AI agent can directly interact with customers, manage relationships, and update records, the traditional CRM interface becomes less necessary. Salesforce's partnership with Anthropic, while encouraging, suggests that the company is positioning itself as a platform that AI models can use, rather than as an irreplaceable source of data context.

Snowflake, by contrast, provides database and data warehousing infrastructure. This is the software that gives AI models the context they need to produce useful outputs. Without high-quality, well-organized data, AI models generate generic or incorrect responses. Snowflake's databases provide the background information that improves AI ROI. During its earnings call, Snowflake explicitly discussed the need to use different AI models for different purposes, noting that a small model from France's Mistral suffices for note summarization without needing Claude's Opus. Snowflake has built this task-appropriate AI differentiation into its tools. The company is not just coexisting with AI. It is becoming the infrastructure that makes AI cost-effective.

This distinction will only sharpen. Enterprises are beginning to realize that AI's value depends entirely on the quality of the data and context provided to it. The software that provides this context will become indispensable. The software that merely provides interfaces or visualization will face replacement pressure. The report identifies KPI visualization tools as particularly vulnerable. These tools display data but do not create or organize it. AI can generate visualizations directly from databases, rendering standalone visualization software redundant.

## The AI ROI Measurement Problem Creates a Strategic Window for Database and Context Providers

The inability to measure AI-derived ROI is the most underappreciated risk in enterprise AI adoption today. Uber's public acknowledgment of this problem is likely the first of many such admissions. The report notes that it is currently not possible to measure AI-derived ROI because installation is still in its early stages. This measurement gap creates both risk and opportunity.

The risk is that enterprises will begin to pull back on AI investments if they cannot demonstrate returns. The report cites Uber's suspension of some AI tools due to low ROI as a cautionary example. If this becomes a trend, the entire AI ecosystem could face a contraction. Companies that have built their strategies around selling AI tools or AI-enhanced software could see demand evaporate.

The opportunity is for companies that can demonstrably improve AI ROI. Snowflake's entire earnings call argument was that its tools are necessary for improving ROI. This is not marketing hype. The logic is sound. AI models generate better outputs when they receive better inputs. Databases provide the structured, contextualized data that improves model performance. They also enable the task-appropriate model selection that Snowflake discussed, where different tasks use different models to optimize cost and quality.

This creates a strategic window for database and data infrastructure providers. They can position themselves not merely as storage solutions but as ROI enhancement tools. Enterprises that want to continue investing in AI will need to invest in the infrastructure that makes AI work. This is a much stickier value proposition than selling software that AI can eventually replace.

The report emphasizes that improving AI ROI requires clear background and instructions. Providing this background is the role of databases. This is not a trivial function. It requires deep integration with enterprise data systems, robust governance, and the ability to serve data to multiple AI models simultaneously. Companies that have built this capability will be difficult to displace, even as AI models themselves evolve rapidly.

## Stock Selection in Japan IT Services Will Increasingly Depend on Whether a Company's Software Is a Cost Center or an Infrastructure Layer

The report's conclusion that stock selection will remain crucial is an understatement. The entire Japan IT services sector will need to be re-evaluated through the lens of AI indispensability. Companies whose software provides the context and infrastructure for AI will command premium valuations. Companies whose software provides interfaces, visualization, or workflow automation that AI can replace will face compression.

This is not a simple binary. Many companies have product portfolios that span both categories. The key analytical question is which portion of their revenue is defensible against AI replacement and which portion is vulnerable. A company might have a database product that is essential for AI and a visualization product that is replaceable. The market will need to weigh these factors carefully.

The report's analysis of Salesforce and Snowflake provides a framework for this evaluation. Salesforce has some elements of indispensability through its data and partnership with Anthropic, but its core CRM functionality faces replacement risk. Snowflake's core database functionality faces minimal replacement risk and significant demand expansion as AI adoption grows. The market is pricing this divergence correctly.

For Japan IT services companies, the implications are significant. Many Japanese software companies have built strong positions in enterprise systems of record, database management, and business process automation. The systems of record and database products are likely to benefit from AI adoption. The business process automation products face replacement risk. Investors will need to distinguish between these categories within individual company portfolios.

The report does not provide specific company recommendations for Japan, but it establishes a clear analytical framework. Companies that provide the data infrastructure for AI will see demand growth. Companies that provide the application layer that AI can replace will face headwinds. The magnitude of these effects will depend on the pace of AI adoption and the speed at which enterprises solve the ROI measurement problem.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Address: The Timing and Magnitude of Software Replacement

The report makes a compelling case that software replacement by AI will be selective, but it does not fully address the timing and magnitude of this replacement. These are the critical questions for investors. Knowing that some software will be replaced is useful. Knowing when and how much will determine portfolio positioning.

The report identifies KPI visualization tools as vulnerable but does not estimate the size of this market or the timeline for replacement. It notes that Salesforce's stock performance was lackluster despite solid results but does not quantify the revenue at risk. These are meaningful open questions that require additional analysis.

The pace of replacement depends on several factors that the report does not explore in depth. First, enterprise adoption of AI agents that can replace software interfaces is still nascent. Most enterprises are using AI for internal productivity improvements rather than for replacing existing software systems. This suggests that replacement risk may be a medium-term rather than near-term concern. Second, the switching costs for enterprise software are high. Even if AI could theoretically replace a CRM system, the cost and risk of migration may delay actual replacement for years. Third, the regulatory environment for AI in Japan and other major markets is still evolving. Regulatory constraints could slow or accelerate replacement depending on how they are structured.

The report also does not fully address the second-order implications of the AI ROI measurement problem. If enterprises cannot measure ROI, they may reduce AI investment, which would reduce demand for both AI infrastructure and AI-replaceable software. This would create a downward spiral that hurts the entire sector. Alternatively, enterprises may increase investment in data infrastructure specifically to improve AI ROI, which would benefit database providers disproportionately. The report leans toward the latter scenario but does not model the risks of the former.

These open questions create analytical opportunities for investors who want to go deeper. The full report likely contains additional data on market sizes, adoption rates, and company-specific exposures that would help answer these questions. The framework established in this analysis is valuable, but the specific conclusions depend on assumptions about timing and magnitude that require further investigation.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating Japan IT Services Companies in the AI Era

Investors evaluating Japan IT services companies should apply a structured framework based on the insights from this analysis. The framework has three dimensions: data indispensability, AI ROI contribution, and replacement vulnerability.

First, assess data indispensability. Does the company's software create, organize, or provide access to data that AI models need to function effectively? Database providers, data warehouse operators, and enterprise resource planning systems that contain critical business data score highly. Pure interface or visualization tools score poorly. The key question is whether AI could function without the company's software. If the answer is no, the company has a defensible position.

Second, assess AI ROI contribution. Does the company's software demonstrably improve the return on investment from AI deployments? This is a forward-looking assessment that requires understanding how enterprises are using AI and where they are struggling. Companies that help enterprises reduce AI costs, improve output quality, or measure ROI more effectively will benefit from increased AI investment. Companies that add cost without improving outcomes will face pressure.

Third, assess replacement vulnerability. Could an AI agent or AI-enhanced system replace the company's software within the next three to five years? This requires understanding the specific functions the software performs and whether those functions can be automated. Companies whose software performs generic, well-defined tasks that can be replicated by large language models face high vulnerability. Companies whose software performs complex, context-dependent tasks that require deep integration with enterprise systems face low vulnerability.

Applying this framework to Japan IT services companies reveals a spectrum of outcomes. Companies with strong positions in database management, enterprise data warehousing, and core systems of record are likely to benefit. Companies focused on business intelligence visualization, workflow automation, and customer interface software face headwinds. Companies with balanced portfolios will need to be evaluated on a product-by-product basis.

The report's analysis suggests that the market is beginning to make these differentiations but has not fully priced them in. The divergence between Salesforce and Snowflake is an early signal, not the final verdict. As more companies report earnings and provide guidance on AI-related revenue and risk, the market will refine its assessments. Investors who apply this framework now will be ahead of the curve.

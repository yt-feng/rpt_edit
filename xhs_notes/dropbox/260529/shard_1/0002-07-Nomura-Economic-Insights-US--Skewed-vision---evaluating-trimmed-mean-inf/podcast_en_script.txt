EN_A: So, Fed Chair Warsh recently called core PCE inflation a “rough swag.” What’s that all about?
EN_B: It’s a pretty big deal. He’s basically questioning the go-to metric the Fed has used for years, and he’s pushing for alternatives like trimmed-mean inflation.
EN_A: That sounds like a shift. So what’s the main question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: The report asks whether trimmed-mean inflation is actually better at tracking the real trend, or if it has its own blind spots.
EN_A: Let’s start with why Warsh doesn’t like core PCE.
EN_B: Core PCE just strips out food and energy. But trimmed-mean inflation goes further by removing the biggest monthly price swings in both directions. The idea is to filter out noise.
EN_A: That sounds clever. So what’s the catch?
EN_B: The catch is, it’s too slow. During the post-pandemic inflation surge in 2021, trimmed-mean inflation lagged behind the true inflation trend by months. It missed the turning point.
EN_A: So it’s underestimating inflation?
EN_B: Exactly. The report estimates it might be understating core inflation by about 48 basis points annually. The problem is that the components it trims out, like goods prices, were actually the early warning signals.
EN_A: So the metric Warsh likes might be flawed?
EN_B: Yes. It’s designed to avoid false alarms, but at the cost of being late to the party. And recently, the distribution of price changes has become more skewed, so the trimming method may no longer be optimal.
EN_A: Is there a better way to spot those turning points?
EN_B: The report hints that the average of the trimmed components themselves reacted much earlier in 2021. That suggests we might need to rethink what we consider noise.
EN_A: So the report leaves some questions open?
EN_B: Definitely. It doesn’t fully explore whether this bias could cause the Fed to fall behind on policy. And it doesn’t propose a dynamic way to adjust the trimming cutoffs.

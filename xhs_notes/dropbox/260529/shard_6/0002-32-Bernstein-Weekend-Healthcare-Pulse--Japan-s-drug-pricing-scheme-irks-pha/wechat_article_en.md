# Japan's Drug Pricing System Is Quietly Dismantling Its Own Pharmaceutical Market

The global pharmaceutical industry is watching Japan, but not for the reasons the Japanese government might hope. What was once the second-largest drug market in the world, one-third the size of the United States, has now fallen to fourth place, with a market value less than one-tenth of the US. This is not a cyclical downturn. It is the structural consequence of a pricing regime that has made Japan the least attractive developed market for pharmaceutical innovation. The mechanism is not subtle: Japan's drug prices are the lowest among all major developed economies when measured as a percentage of US prices, tied with France at just 22 percent. The numbers tell a story of deliberate policy choices that now threaten patient access to innovation.

The Japanese government has a genuine fiscal problem. Its population is aging faster than any other developed country, with the share of citizens over 65 rising from 22 percent to 29 percent in just 15 years. Healthcare expenditure is climbing, and 38 percent of it is funded by federal and local governments. Drug spending, while only 16 percent of total healthcare costs, was the fastest-growing category until the mid-2010s. It became an obvious target. But the logic of cost containment has gone further than fiscal prudence. It has created a market dynamic where the government controls every lever of drug pricing, from launch to annual adjustments to ad-hoc penalties for commercial success. The result is a system that is rational from the government's short-term budget perspective but strategically damaging to the country's long-term pharmaceutical ecosystem.

The consequences are already visible in the behavior of Japanese pharmaceutical companies. Faced with a domestic market that offers little growth, the major Japanese players have been forced to expand overseas. Takeda, Astellas, Daiichi Sankyo, Eisai, and Shionogi have all seen their overseas revenue grow at compound annual rates significantly higher than their domestic performance. This is not a sign of strength. It is a survival strategy. If the domestic market continues to shrink in relative importance, the question becomes whether Japanese patients will have timely access to the next generation of innovative medicines, or whether the pricing system will have driven those innovations to launch first and preferentially in other markets.

## The Japanese Government Controls Drug Pricing at Every Stage, and That Control Is Becoming More Aggressive

The Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare sets the National Health Insurance price for virtually every drug approved in Japan within 60 days of regulatory approval. There are two primary methods for determining launch prices. The first is comparison with analogous existing drugs, where a new drug receives a premium based on its innovation and usefulness. A drug that meets all three criteria for innovation—clinically meaningful novel mechanism of action, higher efficacy or safety, and objectively proven improvement in treatment approach—can receive a 70 to 120 percent premium over the comparator. A drug meeting one or two of these criteria receives a 5 to 60 percent usefulness premium. The second method is cost-based pricing, applied when no comparable drug exists in the market. This approach is inherently problematic because pharmaceutical companies are reluctant to disclose full cost of goods sold information. The MHLW has reported that for recent years, more than 60 percent of drugs subject to cost-based pricing had less than 50 percent of their COGS information disclosed.

The system also includes several premium categories designed to incentivize certain behaviors. Orphan drugs can receive a 5 to 20 percent market premium. Drugs with pediatric indications receive a similar pediatric premium. Drugs that launch in Japan ahead of other markets receive a 10 to 20 percent frontier premium. These premiums are meant to signal that Japan values innovation and early access. Yet despite these mechanisms, Japan's drug prices remain the lowest among developed peers. This is not an accident. The government also uses the average price from the US, UK, Germany, and France as a reference point to avoid excessive gaps, but that reference has not prevented Japan from consistently pricing at the bottom.

The real story, however, is not just about launch prices. It is about what happens after a drug enters the market. The annual repricing mechanism is designed to capture any margin that drug dispensers might earn from the gap between their purchase price from wholesalers and the NHI reimbursement amount. The government surveys market prices every September and implements adjustments the following April. For fiscal year 2026, the survey revealed a 4.8 percent gap, the lowest in 30 years, resulting in an average 0.9 percent price cut. This annual revision was intensified in 2021 when the government introduced an off-year revision, effectively making price cuts a yearly event.

## Innovative Drugs Are Exempted from Annual Cuts, But the Exemption Creates Its Own Strategic Trap

The Patent-period Price Maintenance Program exempts innovative drugs from annual price cuts until generic entry or 15 years after launch. The criteria for innovation include orphan drug status, receipt of innovation or usefulness premiums, and launch in Japan ahead of other markets. On paper, this program protects the most valuable drugs from the annual repricing cycle. In practice, it creates a cliff effect. When the protection period expires, the drug's price is cut all at once to reflect all the repricing that would have occurred during the exemption period. By that time, generics are usually in the market, so the lower price may not matter commercially. But the structure of the program means that the government is effectively deferring price cuts rather than avoiding them.

The more concerning dynamic is what happens outside the annual repricing cycle. The MHLW has two ad-hoc tools that can significantly alter a drug's pricing trajectory after launch. The first is the Market Expansion Reassessment scheme, which penalizes commercial success. When a pharmaceutical company applies for an NHI price, it must include a peak domestic sales forecast based on the approved indication. If annual sales exceed JPY 100 billion and surpass 1.3 to 1.5 times the initial forecast, the NHI price can be cut by 10 to 50 percent. This is not a rare event. Daiichi Sankyo's Lixiana, an anticoagulant, was cut twice: 28 percent in 2018 and 20 percent in 2026. The problem is structural. Initial peak sales forecasts only reflect the first approved indication, so drugs in oncology and immunology, where multiple indications are the norm, will almost inevitably exceed their forecasts. The government previously applied this price cut not only to the drug that exceeded its forecast but to all drugs with the same mechanism of action. That rule was eliminated in the most recent update, but the core mechanism remains.

The second ad-hoc tool is cost-effectiveness-based price adjustment, fully incorporated into official NHI pricing rules since 2020. This has been applied to a select set of high-cost drugs, mostly in oncology and immunology. The criteria are high innovation and large budget impact. Eisai's Leqembi for Alzheimer's disease received a 15 percent price cut in November 2025 under this scheme. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio analysis suggested a 25 percent adjustment, but the maximum reduction of 15 percent was applied. This sets a precedent that will likely be applied to more drugs as the government gains confidence in its cost-effectiveness methodology.

## The System Creates a Paradox: It Achieves Fiscal Goals but Undermines Patient Access to Innovation

The Japanese government can point to genuine achievements. The country delivers the longest life expectancy among developed nations without spending a higher percentage of GDP on healthcare than its peers. The healthcare system is productive by any measure. But the drug pricing system is creating a divergence between the government's fiscal objectives and patient welfare. When drug prices are the lowest in the developed world, and when commercial success is penalized, pharmaceutical companies have clear incentives to prioritize other markets for launch and development.

The data on Japanese pharmaceutical companies' overseas expansion is stark. Between 2016 and 2022, Chugai's overseas revenue grew at a 29 percent compound annual rate while domestic revenue grew at only 10 percent. Eisai's overseas revenue grew at 11 percent while domestic revenue was flat. Astellas saw overseas growth of 7 percent while domestic revenue declined by 9 percent. These companies are not choosing to expand overseas because they prefer foreign markets. They are responding to a domestic environment where growth is constrained by design.

The implications for patients are serious but not yet fully visible. If Japanese companies continue to shift their focus to overseas markets, and if foreign companies deprioritize Japan for new drug launches, Japanese patients may face delays in accessing innovative treatments. The frontier premium, which rewards launches in Japan ahead of other markets, is supposed to counteract this, but it is a weak incentive when the base price is already the lowest. The government's response has been to tighten the system further, but this only accelerates the cycle.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Limits of Cost Containment and the Unmeasured Costs

The report provides a comprehensive analysis of the pricing mechanisms but leaves several critical questions unresolved. First, there is no quantification of the impact on drug launch delays or abandonment. How many drugs have been launched in the US or Europe but not in Japan? How long are the delays? The report mentions that pharmaceutical companies are "vocal about their discontent," but it does not provide data on actual launch behavior. This is a gap that matters because the ultimate test of the system is not price levels but patient access.

Second, the cost-effectiveness framework is still in its early stages. The report notes that only a small set of high-cost drugs have been subject to cost-effectiveness analysis, but it does not model how this will scale. If cost-effectiveness becomes a routine part of pricing for all new drugs, the implications for innovation incentives are profound. Drugs that offer modest improvements at high cost may simply not be priced to generate returns in Japan. The report acknowledges this risk but does not explore the second-order effects on research and development priorities.

Third, the report does not address the political economy of reform. The Japan Medical Association is a powerful political constituency, and the government has shown no appetite for tackling inpatient and outpatient medical care costs, which account for over 70 percent of healthcare expenditure. Drug pricing is the easy lever. But the question is whether the government can sustain this approach as the market continues to shrink. At some point, the fiscal savings from lower drug prices will be outweighed by the costs of delayed innovation and reduced access. The report does not provide a framework for identifying that tipping point.

## A Decision Framework for Pharmaceutical Executives and Investors

For executives and investors evaluating the Japanese market, the report's analysis supports a clear decision framework with four dimensions. First, assess the vulnerability of your portfolio to the annual repricing mechanism. Drugs without PMP protection are subject to annual cuts, and the trend is toward larger and more frequent adjustments. Second, evaluate the risk of Market Expansion Reassessment for drugs with potential for multiple indications. If your peak sales forecast is based on a single indication, and you expect to expand into additional indications, you are building in a penalty. Third, prepare for cost-effectiveness analysis. The government is building the infrastructure for routine cost-effectiveness evaluation, and drugs with high unit prices and large patient populations will be the first targets. Fourth, consider the strategic implications of the overseas expansion trend. If Japanese companies are increasingly focused on non-Japanese markets, partnerships and licensing deals may need to be structured differently.

The most important strategic question is whether the Japanese market is worth the complexity. For drugs that offer breakthrough innovation, the PMP exemption and potential premiums may still make Japan an attractive market. For drugs that offer incremental improvements, the pricing system may make Japan unattractive relative to other developed markets. The report's data suggests that the market is already voting with its feet. The question is whether the government will notice before the damage becomes irreversible.

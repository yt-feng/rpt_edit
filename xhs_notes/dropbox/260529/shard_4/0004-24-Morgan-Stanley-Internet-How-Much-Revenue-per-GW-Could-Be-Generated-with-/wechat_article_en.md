# The Capacity Math Suggests Hyperscaler Revenue Estimates Are Conservative, and Google Cloud Holds the Most Upside

The most consequential question for cloud investors in 2026 is not whether demand exists. It is whether supply can materialize fast enough to meet it, and at what unit economics. A recent deep-dive from a global investment bank has attempted to answer this question by building a bottom-up framework that maps capital expenditure to physical capacity, and then to revenue. The conclusion is striking: current revenue forecasts for AWS and Google Cloud through 2027 may not just be achievable. They may be conservative.

The analysis begins with a granular model of hyperscaler capacity additions. By tracking chip generations, rack allocations, and cost per gigawatt across nine different silicon types, the report projects that the four largest hyperscalers will bring approximately 14 gigawatts of incremental capacity online in 2026 and 20 gigawatts in 2027. These are not abstract numbers. They represent the physical infrastructure that will determine whether cloud revenue growth accelerates or stalls.

What makes this analysis particularly useful is that it does not stop at capacity. It connects capacity to revenue per gigawatt, a metric that reveals the efficiency and pricing power of each cloud platform. The implied revenue per incremental gigawatt for AWS and Google Cloud in 2027 lands in the range of $11 billion to $14 billion. That range, when compared to alternative benchmarks, suggests that the sell-side estimates for these companies may be understating the potential revenue trajectory.

The implications are not uniform across the two hyperscalers. One platform appears to have more upward bias in its estimates than the other. And that asymmetry creates a specific investment opportunity for those willing to look beyond headline growth rates.

## The Capacity Build-Out Is Larger Than Most Investors Assume, and It Is Accelerating

The first-order insight from the report is the sheer scale of capacity being deployed. The bottom-up model estimates that the four major hyperscalers will add 14 gigawatts in 2026 and 20 gigawatts in 2027. To put that in context, the total incremental capacity added in 2025 was roughly 6.7 gigawatts. The ramp is not linear. It is exponential.

Breaking this down by company reveals the distribution of spending and strategic intent. Google is projected to add 5.6 gigawatts in 2026 and 6.8 gigawatts in 2027. Amazon is expected to add 3.5 gigawatts and 4.7 gigawatts in the same years. Microsoft and Meta are also scaling, but at slightly lower absolute levels. The concentration of capacity at Google and Amazon is notable because these two companies also operate the largest public cloud platforms.

The report makes an important distinction between total capacity additions and capacity allocated to public cloud. Not all new infrastructure serves external customers. Internal workloads, training clusters, and experimental projects consume a meaningful portion. After excluding internal use, the report estimates that roughly 6 gigawatts in 2026 and 8 gigawatts in 2027 will be dedicated to AWS and Google Cloud specifically. This is the capacity that will directly drive cloud revenue growth.

For investors, the key takeaway is that the supply side of the cloud market is entering a period of unprecedented expansion. If demand remains robust, the revenue implications are substantial. But the critical variable is not just the quantity of capacity. It is the revenue that each gigawatt can generate.

## The Implied Revenue Per Gigawatt Suggests Current Estimates Are Achievable and Potentially Conservative

The report calculates the implied incremental revenue per incremental gigawatt for AWS and Google Cloud based on current sell-side forecasts. For AWS, the figure is approximately $14 billion per gigawatt in 2027. For Google Cloud, it is approximately $11 billion per gigawatt. These numbers are derived by dividing the projected incremental cloud revenue by the projected incremental capacity allocated to each platform.

To assess whether these figures are reasonable, the report provides three benchmarks. First, neo-cloud providers that primarily rent bare-metal Nvidia GPUs currently generate roughly $10 of revenue per watt. That translates to approximately $10 billion per gigawatt. Second, industry conversations suggest that current deals for capacity are being negotiated at revenue per gigawatt levels of $20 billion or more. Third, the hyperscalers offer higher-margin software services on top of their infrastructure, which should command a premium over bare-metal rental.

The implied revenue per gigawatt for AWS and Google Cloud sits between the neo-cloud benchmark and the deal-level benchmark. This positioning suggests that current estimates are not aggressive. In fact, they may be conservative. The report explicitly states that the extent to which capacity opens should support the estimated level of growth, and that growth may prove conservative given the trajectory of 2025 results.

This is not a minor point. If the revenue per gigawatt holds or improves, and if capacity comes online as modeled, then the current revenue forecasts for 2027 may be too low. The upside is not speculative. It is grounded in the physical reality of infrastructure deployment.

## Google Cloud in 2027 Appears to Have the Most Upward Bias Among the Hyperscalers

While both AWS and Google Cloud show conservative revenue per gigawatt estimates, the report identifies Google Cloud as having the most potential upside. The reason is the shape of its implied revenue per gigawatt trajectory. For AWS, the figure rises slightly from $12 billion in 2025 to $14 billion in 2027. For Google Cloud, it declines from $15 billion in 2025 to $11 billion in 2027.

A declining revenue per gigawatt could indicate that the market expects Google Cloud to add capacity faster than it can monetize it. But the report suggests an alternative explanation. The decline may be partly due to accounting treatment of Tensor Processing Unit sales. The report explicitly excludes an estimated 3.1 million units of external TPU sales from its Google Cloud revenue calculation. If these sales are included, the implied revenue per gigawatt would be higher.

The report further argues that faster-than-base-case Google Cloud growth could bring the bull case into play. The bull case for Google is raised to $460, implying approximately 28 times 2027 bull-case earnings per share. This valuation is justified, the report argues, by the growing contribution from Google Cloud and the company's leadership in generative AI across its businesses.

For investors, the implication is clear. The consensus estimates for Google Cloud in 2027 may be the most susceptible to upward revision. The combination of capacity deployment, TPU monetization, and AI-driven demand creates a setup where the risk is asymmetrically tilted to the upside.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Elasticity of Revenue Per Gigawatt

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves one critical question open. How elastic is the revenue per gigawatt metric? The analysis assumes that revenue per gigawatt will remain within a certain range based on current forecasts and deal benchmarks. But this assumption may be too static.

The revenue per gigawatt for a cloud platform is not a fixed number. It depends on utilization rates, pricing power, product mix, and competitive dynamics. If capacity comes online faster than demand materializes, pricing could decline. If demand outstrips supply, pricing could rise. The report acknowledges that current deals are being negotiated at $20 billion per gigawatt, but it does not model how the average might shift as more capacity enters the market.

Another open question is the impact of custom silicon. The report tracks nine different chip generations, including custom ASICs from each hyperscaler. But the revenue per gigawatt for custom silicon may differ significantly from that of Nvidia GPUs. Custom chips are typically designed for specific workloads and may have different margin profiles. The report does not disaggregate revenue per gigawatt by chip type, which limits the ability to assess how the mix shift toward custom silicon might affect the aggregate metric.

Finally, the report does not address the potential for capacity to be underutilized. The model assumes that capacity additions translate directly into revenue-generating infrastructure. But in practice, there is always a lag between when capacity is deployed and when it is fully utilized. If utilization rates are lower than assumed, the revenue per gigawatt will be lower as well.

These open questions do not invalidate the analysis. They highlight the areas where further research is needed. For investors, the key is to monitor utilization rates, pricing trends, and product mix changes as capacity comes online.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask Before Positioning

The report provides a useful framework for investors who want to evaluate the cloud revenue opportunity for themselves. Instead of relying solely on top-down estimates, investors can ask three questions that cut to the core of the capacity-to-revenue relationship.

First, what is the implied revenue per gigawatt for each cloud platform based on current estimates, and how does it compare to benchmarks? The report provides the baseline. Investors can track this metric over time to see if it is improving or deteriorating. If the implied revenue per gigawatt is above the neo-cloud benchmark and below the deal-level benchmark, as it currently is, the estimates are likely reasonable. If it falls below the neo-cloud benchmark, that would be a warning sign.

Second, is the capacity being added likely to be fully utilized within a reasonable timeframe? This question requires monitoring both the demand side and the supply side. On the demand side, investors should track cloud revenue growth rates, backlog disclosures, and customer commitments. On the supply side, investors should track capacity deployment timelines and utilization rates reported by the hyperscalers. The report estimates that 6 to 8 gigawatts of incremental capacity will be allocated to public cloud in 2026 and 2027. If demand growth is consistent with historical trends, utilization should be high.

Third, what is the mix of revenue sources within each cloud platform? The report highlights the importance of TPU sales for Google Cloud. But the same logic applies to other revenue streams. Higher-margin software services, such as databases, analytics, and AI platforms, should command a higher revenue per gigawatt than bare-metal compute. If the mix shifts toward higher-margin services, the revenue per gigawatt could rise even if capacity grows rapidly.

Investors who can answer these three questions will have a clearer view of whether the current estimates are conservative, reasonable, or aggressive. The report suggests that for Google Cloud in particular, the answer is likely conservative.

## The Capacity Question Is the Revenue Question

The debate over hyperscaler capital expenditure has often been framed as a question of discipline. Are the companies spending too much? Will returns on invested capital deteriorate? The report reframes the debate. The question is not whether the spending is excessive. It is whether the capacity will generate the revenue that current estimates assume.

The answer, based on the bottom-up analysis, is that current estimates look achievable and potentially conservative. The implied revenue per gigawatt for AWS and Google Cloud is supported by multiple benchmarks. And for Google Cloud, the estimates may have the most upward bias.

The report does not claim to have perfect visibility. The elasticity of revenue per gigawatt, the impact of custom silicon, and the utilization lag all introduce uncertainty. But the direction of the analysis is clear. The capacity is coming. The question is whether the revenue will follow. The evidence suggests it will.

# Digital Health 2.0 Is Not a Repeat of 1.0: Why Hinge Health and Omada Health Deserve a Fresh Look from Skeptical Investors

The scar tissue from the first wave of digital health IPOs runs deep. Investors who watched Livongo, Teladoc, and a dozen other high-flying names crash from triple-digit growth to single-digit stagnation carry a justified skepticism. Short interest in Omada Health sits near 15% of float, and Hinge Health is not far behind at roughly 8%. The market is pricing in a repeat of history. That assumption is wrong.

What separates Hinge Health and Omada Health from the 2020-2021 cohort is not a matter of degree but of kind. Both companies are penetrating total addressable markets at roughly 10%, leaving a decade-plus runway. Both have delivered three consecutive quarters of beating and raising since their IPOs. Both are using proprietary data and artificial intelligence to scale platforms without proportional headcount increases. The structural economics of Digital Health 2.0 are fundamentally different from the unit-economics disaster of Digital Health 1.0. The market has not yet priced this distinction.

The timing matters because the window for building conviction is narrowing. Hinge Health has already begun to re-rate as estimates prove conservative, but Omada Health remains suppressed by lockup overhang and margin skepticism. The next two to four quarters will determine whether both names converge upward or whether the market's residual fear proves self-fulfilling. For investors willing to separate signal from trauma, the setup is unusually asymmetric.

## The Self-Insured Market Is the Engine, but the Fully-Insured Market Is the Accelerator That Estimates Underappreciate

The dominant narrative around both companies focuses on the self-insured employer market, where adoption of digital musculoskeletal and chronic condition programs has reached roughly 14-18% penetration. This is a legitimate growth driver, but it is also the most visible and therefore the most discounted in current estimates. What the consensus underestimates is the velocity of adoption in the fully-insured and Medicare Advantage segments.

Hinge Health's non-self-insured segment grew 135% year-over-year in 2025, from a much smaller base. This growth is not accidental. It reflects a deliberate expansion of health plan partnerships and a growing body of clinical outcomes data that makes the value proposition undeniable for payors who bear the full cost of member care. For Omada, the Medicare Advantage opportunity is barely scratched at roughly 1% penetration, yet the tailwinds are structural: the surge in GLP-1 demand creates an urgent need for companion programs that improve adherence and demonstrate outcomes to justify drug spend. Employers and payors are running out of patience with point solutions that address one condition at a time. Omada's multi-condition platform directly addresses that fatigue.

The strategic implication is clear. If the self-insured market provides the base case, the fully-insured and MA markets provide the upside case that most models do not fully capture. The question is not whether these segments grow, but how quickly the operating leverage kicks in as the fixed-cost platform absorbs a larger member base.

## Margin Expansion Is Not a Hope; It Is a Structural Feature of Platform-Based Digital Health

The most persistent bear argument against Omada Health in particular is that margin expansion and growth are incompatible. The data from the first year of public life tells a different story. Hinge Health posted a first-year EBITDA margin of 21%, well above the median of 7% for the digital health cohort. Omada's first-year margin of 2.5% was below median, but Street estimates for the second year project a move to approximately 4%, with meaningful room for upside.

The more important observation is the trend in gross margin. Hinge Health's gross margin of 83% in year one is projected to rise to 85% in year two. Omada's gross margin of 68% is projected to remain stable, but this stability is itself a signal. In the first wave of digital health, gross margins often deteriorated as companies chased growth by adding lower-quality members or accepting unfavorable contract terms. These companies are doing the opposite. They are improving contract quality, leveraging AI to reduce the cost of clinical support, and using proprietary data to drive member engagement without proportional spending on marketing.

The mechanism is worth understanding. Both companies use AI not as a buzzword but as an operational lever. Hinge Health's platform uses computer vision and sensor data to deliver physical therapy remotely, reducing the need for human coaches per member. Omada uses machine learning to personalize condition management pathways, increasing engagement rates and reducing churn. These are not theoretical capabilities; they are embedded in the unit economics. Every percentage point of gross margin improvement drops disproportionately to the bottom line because the cost structure is increasingly fixed.

## Competitive Moats Are Widening, Not Narrowing, Despite New Entrants

The digital health landscape is crowded, and the bears have a ready list of competitors. American Specialty Health launched a digital MSK solution in January 2026, explicitly marketing 50-70% cost savings versus existing programs. Sidekick Health added MSK to its multi-condition platform in April 2026. Livongo, now part of Teladoc, still reaches 1.2 million chronic care enrollees. These are real competitive pressures, but they are not existential threats to Hinge Health and Omada.

The reason is data moats and distribution advantages. Hinge Health remains roughly two to three times larger than its next-largest competitor based on monthly app downloads, a lead that has persisted even after the Sword-Kaia combination. More importantly, Hinge's win rate in head-to-head competitive deals reached an all-time high in the fourth quarter of 2025. This suggests that the combination of clinical outcomes data, brand recognition, and platform maturity creates a barrier that price-based competitors cannot easily breach.

For Omada, the competitive picture is more nuanced but equally favorable. Livongo's chronic care enrollment growth has moderated to between negative 1% and positive 4% since 2025, a far cry from the triple-digit growth of its pre-merger era. Meanwhile, Omada's app downloads have broken away from the pack, indicating accelerating momentum. The multi-condition platform is becoming a differentiator precisely because employers are tired of managing a portfolio of point solutions. A single platform that addresses diabetes, hypertension, behavioral health, and GLP-1 support is more valuable than the sum of its parts.

## The Report Raises More Questions Than It Answers About the AI Disruption Risk for Doximity and Waystar

The report provides detailed analysis of Hinge Health and Omada Health, but it also touches on two other names that face a different set of challenges: Doximity and Waystar. For Doximity, the concern is that OpenEvidence has emerged as the apparent winner in clinical AI, reaching approximately 1.5 million unique web visitors and 58,000 monthly app downloads. Doximity's own usage metrics remain strong, with unique web visitors at 4.1 million and AI tool adoption growing rapidly. But the growth rate has decelerated to just 5% in the most recent quarter, and the company guided to only 4% year-over-year growth for fiscal 2027.

The unresolved question is whether Doximity's distribution advantage as a workflow platform for physicians can offset the gravitational pull of a pure-play AI competitor. The company's AI Search and Scribe tools have tripled active users since the Pathway acquisition, and users are averaging 31 queries per month. But the market is clearly skeptical that this translates into sustained revenue acceleration. The report is constructive on Doximity's position but acknowledges that stronger growth is needed to dispel concerns. It does not, however, provide a clear framework for what would constitute proof of a turnaround.

For Waystar, the AI debate is even more complex. The company is embedding AI into its revenue cycle platform, but faces threats from two directions. First, foundational large language models are developing agentic capabilities that could automate prior authorization and documentation processes that currently flow through Waystar's clearinghouse. Second, ambient AI scribe technologies are expanding to automate upstream clinical documentation, potentially pulling mid-cycle work into electronic health record-native platforms. Waystar's clearinghouse rails and 5,000 payor connections provide a durable moat, but the report rightly notes that these represent medium to longer-term risks.

The unanswered question for both Doximity and Waystar is whether the AI disruption is a headwind that can be managed through product evolution or a structural shift that erodes the value of their existing distribution advantages. The report leans constructive but leaves the reader wanting a clearer decision framework for when to get more confident or more cautious.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating Digital Health 2.0

For investors trying to translate this analysis into portfolio decisions, three questions should guide the assessment of any digital health company in the current environment.

First, is the company's gross margin trajectory improving or deteriorating? In Digital Health 1.0, gross margin deterioration was the canary in the coal mine, signaling that the company was buying growth with uneconomic contracts. Hinge Health and Omada Health both show stable-to-improving gross margins, which is the single most important signal that the business model is scaling efficiently. Any company that cannot demonstrate this should be treated with extreme caution.

Second, does the company have a proprietary data advantage that compounds over time? The difference between a platform and a feature is the ability to use data from one member to improve outcomes for the next. Hinge Health's sensor-based physical therapy data and Omada's multi-condition longitudinal data create feedback loops that competitors cannot replicate quickly. Companies that rely on third-party data or generic AI models will find their advantages eroding as the technology commoditizes.

Third, is the company's go-to-market strategy diversified across self-insured, fully-insured, and government payor segments? The self-insured market is the beachhead, but the fully-insured and MA markets are where the multi-year growth compound lies. Companies that are single-segment plays face a ceiling that will become visible within two to three years. Companies that are building multi-segment distribution, as Hinge and Omada are doing, have a much longer runway.

EN_A: So the report is not just about oil prices going up. It is about actual shortages of specific industrial inputs?
EN_B: Exactly. The report splits the problem into two. For oil, because it is a global market, higher prices will destroy enough demand to avoid outright stockouts. The hit to GDP from higher energy prices is estimated at about half a percentage point.
EN_A: That sounds manageable. So what is the bigger risk?
EN_B: The bigger risk is with non-oil commodities. These markets are more regional. If you lose supply of something like sulfur or methanol from the Middle East, you cannot just buy it from somewhere else easily. The report models a worst-case scenario where a complete loss of these supplies could knock 1.3 percent off global GDP.
EN_A: 1.3 percent is huge. But I am guessing the report does not just stop at that scary number.
EN_B: Right. It gives three reasons why the actual impact will likely be smaller. First, many of these inputs are tiny fractions of total production costs. Second, companies can reallocate scarce materials from low-value to high-value uses. Third, there is some substitutability between inputs.
EN_A: So the real number is lower?
EN_B: Yes. After accounting for these buffers, the report estimates the direct hit from non-oil shortages is more like 0.4 to 0.5 percent of global GDP. But it warns that downstream supply chain effects could amplify that.
EN_B: The data so far supports this view. Demand destruction is happening mostly in lower-income countries and lower-value industries. And some prices for these constrained products have actually come down in recent weeks.

# China: Apr fiscal spending pullback mirrors FAI contraction

- General budget expenditure slipped into contraction, dragged by infra-outlays despite decent revenue growth.   
- Fund revenue and expenditure both saw steep contractions on lingering housing/land downturn.   
- Latest fiscal figures hit at policy patience, though FAI pullback also reflects thin project supply, debt repayment priority, and slow policy-bank rollout.   
- Fiscal delivery has been less front-loaded than expected, implying a smaller-than-feared fade in fiscal impulse in 2H.   
- If April weakness persists, fiscal delivery may step up. If 2Q materially undershoots $4.5 - 5\%$ target, interim fiscal support in 2H becomes more likely.

The surprise April FAI contraction raised questions about whether fiscal support had been less proactive. April fiscal data confirmed this, with contractions in both general budget spending (most notably infrastructure outlays) and government-managed fund expenditure. After three months of drawdown, fiscal deposits jumped in April, by more than typical seasonality.

Headline general budget revenue growth stayed decent at 6.7% oya in April, lifting ytd growth to 3.5% (vs 2.2% full-year budgetary target). This was still driven mainly by tax revenue (+8.2% oya), while non-tax revenue fell 5.3%. General budget expenditure slipped into -3.2% oya contraction from 1Q's 2.6% growth (vs 4.4% full-year budgetary target). The decline was led by an 18.6% oya contraction in infrastructure outlays, reversing the brief improvement to 2.4% in Jan-Feb. This mirrors the sharp pullback in investment (e.g. -4.5% oya for infrastructure FAI, or -6.3% for public FAI). Despite the increased emphasis on “investing in people” (e.g., education, healthcare, and urban development), livelihood spending also slowed to 1.2% growth (vs 5.3% in 1Q).

Outside the general budget, government-managed funds—now the largest financing source among fund accounts—remain heavily land-sale dependent, with spending tied to land costs and special LGB deployment. In April, fund revenue fell 26.4% oya on a 34.8% drop in land sales, while expenditure declined 20.8%. The renewed acceleration in land-sales contraction (vs -14.7% for 2025 full-year) underscores the ongoing drag from the housing/land downturn on local-government fiscal conditions.

While we began the year constructive on investment given projects are typically front-loaded at the start of the 15th FYP, the latest fiscal figures suggest a degree of policy patience or complacency after strong 1Q GDP growth despite the energy shock. The investment pullback may also reflect a limited pipeline of eligible projects, a greater priority on debt repayment, and slow rollout of policy-bank tools as seed capital for new projects, etc. Fiscal deposits rose 739bn yuan, 2-3 times the usual seasonal increase, to 1200bn yuan, the highest in recent years.

Year-to-date tracking suggests fiscal delivery has been less front-loaded than we initially expected. This helps explain the near-term domestic activity slowdown, but it also implies the feared fade in fiscal policy impulse in 2H may be less pronounced. While central government bond issuance has picked up (especially special CGB, mainly for the “two upgrades” and “two majors”), local government bond issuance, especially special LGB, slowed sharply in April and May. The pullback in April IP and domestic demand shifts 2Q GDP risks to the downside versus our baseline of 4.0%q/q saar or 4.9%oya, which would require more than 1%m/m sa IP rebounds in both May and June. The stronger-than-expected export rebound provides some offset, but the tailwinds appear concentrated. If the April weakness persists, fiscal delivery could accelerate in coming months. As project approvals accelerate, alongside faster government bond issuance and quicker deployment of proceeds, infrastructure and public investment could rebound from April’s sharp contraction. The commitment to steadily advance urban renewal may also provide a mild tailwind to infrastructure and real estate investment. If 2Q growth materially undershoots the full-year target of 4.5-5%, the case for additional fiscal support in 2H would strengthen, although conviction is lower given that the target range offers policymakers more flexibility than in prior years.

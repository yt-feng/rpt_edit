# Japan's Inflation History Is Repeating, But With a Dangerous New Twist: The Bank of Japan Must Act Faster Than It Did in the 1970s or Risk Losing Control

Japan's economy is now confronting an inflationary shock that, in its upstream price pressures, rivals the two great oil crises of the 1970s. The Bank of Japan's own Governor Ueda acknowledged this directly in a late May speech, drawing explicit parallels between today's surge in the Corporate Goods Price Index and the spikes of 1973 and 1980. The April data is stark: domestic corporate goods prices rose 2.3% month-on-month, the largest increase since the second oil shock, excluding consumption tax effects. Chemical product prices recorded their steepest monthly gain since the first oil shock. The question is no longer whether Japan faces a significant inflation test. The question is whether the BoJ will handle it like it did in 1973—with disastrous delay—or like it did in 1980, when a timely policy response contained the damage.

The critical insight from Governor Ueda's speech, and from the data that underpins it, is that identical shocks produce radically different outcomes depending on the economy's "initial conditions." The report we analyze here systematically compares six such conditions across the two historical episodes and the present. The conclusion is uncomfortable for anyone assuming a benign outcome: Japan today is in a more inflationary configuration than it was before the second oil shock, when policy succeeded. The labor market is tighter. The exchange rate is a headwind, not a buffer. And the monetary policy stance, despite two years of rate hikes, remains the most accommodative relative to nominal growth since 1980. The BoJ is running a 1970s-style policy in a 2020s economy, and the window for correction is narrowing.

This article unpacks the analytical framework from the report, translates its findings into a decision framework for investors and policymakers, and identifies the critical questions that the analysis leaves open. The stakes are not merely academic. If the BoJ misjudges the trajectory of unit labor costs or delays normalization further, Japan could find itself in a wage-price spiral that its demographics and debt dynamics can ill afford.

## The Historical Analogy Is Not Comforting: The First Oil Shock Was a Policy Failure That Japan Cannot Afford to Repeat

The report's core analytical move is to compare not just the magnitude of the oil shocks, but the initial conditions that determined how each one propagated through the economy. During the first oil shock of 1973, Japan was already running hot. Wage growth had reached 15-20% year-on-year before the oil price surge. The labor market was in a state of chronic shortage. Inflation expectations were already elevated. And critically, monetary policy was highly accommodative: the policy rate remained below a proxy for the nominal neutral rate throughout the 1970s. The result was a wage-price spiral that sent CPI inflation above 20% and required a painful recession to break.

The second oil shock of 1980 was a different story. The import price shock was of similar magnitude, but the initial conditions were far more favorable. Wages were subdued, restrained by labor-management coordination that had internalized the lessons of the first crisis. Inflation expectations were anchored by that recent traumatic memory. The yen had appreciated significantly, providing a natural buffer against imported inflation. And monetary policy was tightened preemptively: the policy rate was raised above nominal growth, exerting genuine downward pressure on prices. The result was a contained inflation episode that did not spiral out of control.

The report's analysis shows that Japan today occupies a middle ground that is, in several key respects, closer to the 1973 configuration than to 1980. The labor market is tighter than it was during either oil shock: the BoJ Tankan employment conditions DI is at a level that indicates more severe labor shortages than in the 1970s. Unit labor costs are already rising at a pace that exceeds the pre-second-shock period. And the exchange rate is a depreciating headwind, not an appreciating buffer. The monetary policy stance, while in the process of normalization, remains deeply accommodative by historical standards. The degree of accommodation—measured as the gap between nominal GDP growth and the policy rate—is at its highest level since 1980.

The implication is clear: the BoJ cannot afford to assume that today's inflation will prove as benign as the second oil shock episode. The structural conditions are less favorable, and the margin for error is thinner.

## The Wage-Price Spiral Risk Is Low in the Short Term, But 2027 Is the Real Test

One of the report's most valuable contributions is to disaggregate the wage-price spiral risk across two distinct time horizons. In the near term, the risk is limited. The crude oil price surge occurred after the 2026 Shunto spring wage negotiations were largely concluded for major corporations. This means that the direct pass-through of higher energy costs into base salaries will be deferred until next year's negotiations. Summer bonus surveys indicate that bonus growth will be lower than last year. Unit labor costs are unlikely to spike sharply in the coming months.

But this near-term calm is precisely what makes the medium-term risk so dangerous. Against a backdrop of historic labor shortages—the Tankan DI is at levels that suggest employers are finding it harder to hire than at any point since the early 1990s—the conditions for a wage-price spiral are being set. If the current high inflation persists through the autumn and winter, it will form the backdrop for the 2027 Shunto. Workers and unions will have a full year of elevated CPI data to cite in their demands. Employers, facing acute labor shortages, will have strong incentives to concede.

The report's analysis of unit labor costs is instructive here. During the first oil shock, ULC growth surged to over 30% year-on-year as wages chased prices. During the second, ULC growth remained contained. Today, ULC growth is already running at a pace that exceeds the pre-second-shock period, and the labor market is tighter. The question is not whether ULC will rise, but whether it will accelerate. If it does, the BoJ will face a situation where inflation becomes domestically driven—what the report calls "homemade inflation"—rather than merely imported.

This distinction matters enormously for monetary policy. Imported inflation can be waited out; it reverses when commodity prices fall. Domestically driven inflation, once embedded in wage-setting norms and inflation expectations, requires active policy tightening to dislodge. The BoJ's current strategy appears to assume that the current inflation is predominantly the former. The report's analysis suggests this assumption is increasingly risky.

## The Monetary Policy Stance Is Dangerously Accommodative by Historical Standards

The report's most striking quantitative finding is the current degree of monetary accommodation. Using the gap between nominal GDP growth and the policy rate as a measure, the report shows that even after the BoJ's rate hikes since 2024, the current stance is the most accommodative since 1980. This is not a trivial observation. During the first oil shock, when policy was too loose, the policy rate was consistently below nominal growth. During the second, when policy succeeded, the rate was raised above nominal growth. Today, the gap is comparable to the 1970s.

The report notes that nominal growth may temporarily slow in the near term as deteriorating terms of trade squeeze the economy. This is the standard channel through which an import price shock depresses activity: higher costs reduce real incomes and consumption. But the danger is that this slowdown is misinterpreted as evidence that inflation is under control. If the economy then transitions into a phase of domestically driven inflation—as wage growth accelerates and ULC rises—nominal growth will re-accelerate. At that point, the current degree of accommodation will look even more extreme, and the required tightening will be correspondingly larger.

The BoJ's own rhetoric emphasizes data dependence and a careful assessment of the economic outlook. But the report's historical comparison suggests that the risk of delay is asymmetric. The cost of tightening too late is a wage-price spiral that requires a recession to break. The cost of tightening too early is a modest undershoot of the inflation target, which can be reversed. The BoJ's institutional memory is shaped by the deflationary decades that followed the 1990s crash, and this may bias it toward caution. But the current configuration of initial conditions suggests that the greater risk now runs in the opposite direction.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Structural Uncertainty Around Inflation Expectations and Wage-Setting Norms

The report is admirably rigorous in its quantitative analysis of wages and monetary policy. But it also highlights two initial conditions that it cannot fully quantify: inflation expectations and price- and wage-setting norms. These are the soft variables that will determine whether the current inflation episode becomes entrenched.

During the first oil shock, an inflationary norm had been established. Businesses and workers expected prices to keep rising, and they acted accordingly. During the second, a cautious view on inflation was entrenched due to the traumatic experience of the first crisis. Today, Japan's long-standing deflationary norm has begun to change, but it is unclear what is replacing it. Inflation expectations have shifted upward from near zero to the 1.5-2% range, but it is uncertain whether they are firmly anchored at that level or are drifting higher.

The report leaves this question open, and rightly so. There is no historical precedent for a transition from a deflationary norm to a stable, moderate-inflation norm without a period of overshooting. The risk is that the transition overshoots on the upside, and that the new norm becomes one of 3-4% inflation rather than 2%. In that scenario, the BoJ would face a much more difficult policy challenge than it currently anticipates.

The report also does not fully resolve the question of how Japan's demographic structure affects the wage-price dynamics. An aging population and shrinking workforce create structural upward pressure on wages, independent of the business cycle. This is a new factor that has no parallel in the 1970s or 1980s. It may mean that the current degree of labor market tightness is more persistent than in past episodes, and that the BoJ needs to normalize policy faster than historical analogies would suggest.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Policymakers: Three Scenarios and Their Policy Implications

The report's analysis can be synthesized into a decision framework with three scenarios, each with distinct implications for asset prices and policy.

**Scenario 1: Benign Normalization.** The current inflation proves to be predominantly imported and transitory. Commodity prices stabilize or decline. Wage growth remains moderate in the 2027 Shunto. ULC does not accelerate. The BoJ continues its gradual normalization path, raising rates to around 1.5-2.0% over the next two years. Inflation settles at 2%. This scenario is consistent with current market pricing and would support Japanese government bonds and equities.

**Scenario 2: Sticky Inflation.** Commodity prices remain elevated. The 2027 Shunto produces wage increases of 5% or more. ULC accelerates. Inflation expectations drift to 2.5-3%. The BoJ is forced to accelerate its tightening, raising rates to 2.5-3.0% by 2028. This scenario would cause a sell-off in JGBs and a rotation out of rate-sensitive equities. The yen would appreciate as the rate differential with the US narrows.

**Scenario 3: Wage-Price Spiral.** A combination of persistent commodity shocks, acute labor shortages, and unanchored inflation expectations produces a self-reinforcing cycle of wage and price increases. CPI inflation rises above 4%. The BoJ is forced into emergency tightening, raising rates to 4% or higher. A recession follows. This scenario would cause a sharp sell-off in Japanese assets across the board, with the yen initially weakening before stabilizing.

The report's analysis suggests that Scenario 1 is the least likely, given the initial conditions. The probability weight should be distributed between Scenarios 2 and 3, with the balance depending on the 2027 Shunto outcome and the evolution of commodity prices. The BoJ's own actions will be a key determinant: a preemptive tightening now would tilt the balance toward Scenario 2; continued gradualism would increase the probability of Scenario 3.

## The Unresolved Analytical Questions That Make the Full Report Essential Reading

The report raises several questions that it does not—and cannot—fully answer, precisely because they depend on future data and policy decisions. These are the questions that make the full report and its original charts essential for serious investors.

First, how will the 2027 Shunto negotiations actually play out? The report shows that current wage growth is below the levels that triggered the first oil shock spiral, but the labor market is tighter. The key unknown is whether the institutional memory of deflation is strong enough to restrain wage demands, or whether the experience of the past two years of rising prices has already shifted expectations.

Second, what is the true neutral rate of interest for Japan today? The report uses a 4-year moving average of nominal GDP growth as a proxy, but this is a rough approximation. If the neutral rate has risen due to structural changes in the economy—including labor scarcity and shifting inflation expectations—then the current policy stance is even more accommodative than the report suggests.

Third, how will the exchange rate evolve? The report notes that yen depreciation is a headwind today, unlike the appreciation that buffered the second oil shock. But the yen's trajectory depends on global factors—US monetary policy, risk appetite, and commodity prices—that are outside the BoJ's control. A sharp yen depreciation would exacerbate the inflation problem and force the BoJ's hand.

Fourth, what is the political economy of BoJ independence? The Japanese government has substantial debt, and higher rates would increase debt service costs significantly. The report does not address whether political constraints might delay the BoJ's normalization, but this is a real risk that investors must assess.

These are not gaps in the report's analysis. They are the natural limits of any forward-looking assessment, and they point to the specific data and events that investors should monitor. The report provides the analytical framework; the resolution depends on the incoming data.

## Conclusion: The BoJ Must Act With the Urgency of 1980, Not the Complacency of 1973

Governor Ueda's speech was remarkable for its historical candor. By explicitly comparing the current situation to the oil shocks of the 1970s, he signaled that the BoJ is aware of the risks. But awareness is not the same as action. The report's analysis shows that the current initial conditions are more inflationary than those that preceded the second oil shock, when policy succeeded. The labor market is tighter. The exchange rate is a headwind. And the monetary policy stance, while in the process of normalization, remains the most accommodative since 1980.

The BoJ's own historical lesson from the second oil shock is that "prompt monetary policy response" was the key to containing inflation. The implication for today is clear: the BoJ must be prepared to act more quickly and more decisively than its current gradualist approach suggests. The window for preemptive action is narrowing. If the 2027 Shunto delivers strong wage increases, the BoJ will be forced into reactive tightening that is both larger and more disruptive.

For investors, the report provides a clear analytical framework for monitoring the situation. The key variables are ULC growth, wage negotiations, and the BoJ's policy rate relative to nominal growth. The scenario analysis suggests that the risks are skewed toward the downside for bonds and the upside for the yen, with equities caught in the middle. The report does not provide investment recommendations, but it provides the tools to make them.

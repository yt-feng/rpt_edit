# The Global SPR Release Is About to Hit a Cliff, and the Market Has Not Priced the Gap

The global strategic petroleum reserve release programme, which has been delivering approximately 2.5 million barrels per day of additional supply since April, is on course to collapse by more than 70 percent in July. This is not a forecast of what might happen. It is a mechanical consequence of programmes that are already scheduled to end. The United States Department of Energy has explicitly warned that lift rates will degrade as inventories fall. Japan has declined to authorise a third phase. South Korea's IEA-coordinated tranche is essentially complete. The remaining European commitments, totalling roughly 63 million barrels across Germany, France, Italy, the United Kingdom, and Australia, have produced almost no confirmed physical lifts. The market is currently trading as if the 2.5 million barrel per day flow is a stable baseline. It is not. The most important question for any oil market participant in the next sixty days is whether this cliff is real or whether new programmes will fill the gap before the market is forced to adjust.

The strategic reserve drawdown was always a bridging mechanism, not a permanent supply source. The IEA's initial announcement of 400 million barrels, later expanded to 426 million barrels, was designed to absorb the immediate shock from supply disruptions. The programme has worked. Global strategic stock draws have added roughly 150 million barrels of physical supply through late May, with the United States and Japan accounting for the overwhelming majority. But the structure of these releases matters enormously for what comes next. The US programme is an exchange contract system: borrowers take crude now and must return a minimum of 17 to 28 percent extra by 2027 to 2029. Japan's barrels go directly to four named domestic refiners and are invisible to cargo trackers. European commitments are largely authorisations that have not yet translated into physical lifts. The result is a supply profile that is front-loaded, concentrated, and about to decelerate sharply.

## The US and Japan Dominate the Current Flow, but Both Are Approaching Natural Limits

The United States has contracted 132.9 million barrels of its authorised 172 million barrel pledge, with 50.3 million barrels physically released through late May. The US draw rate hit a record 1.4 million barrels per day in mid-May, exceeding the peak rate achieved during the 2022 release when the SPR held nearly 600 million barrels. The current pace is being run from an inventory level of approximately 365 million barrels. The physics of salt cavern storage means that as inventory falls, the water-displacement pumping rate degrades. The Department of Energy has been explicit on this point. The realistic expectation is that the lift rate moderates to 1.0 to 1.2 million barrels per day in June and July, and then to 0.7 to 0.9 million barrels per day in August as inventory approaches 285 million barrels. This alone would push 5 to 15 million barrels of contracted deliveries past the August deadline into September.

Japan's contribution is often underappreciated because it is invisible. The cumulative committed releases, including the Phase 2 announcement of 10 April, reach approximately 99 million barrels, nearly matching the US contracted volume. Roughly 50 to 55 million barrels have already moved. But Prime Minister Takaichi declined to authorise a third national release in May, citing sufficient crude secured for June. He did not categorically rule out a future Phase 3, but the signal is clear: Japan considers its current commitment sufficient unless conditions deteriorate further. The Japanese programme is structured as direct allocations to refiners, which means it does not appear in cargo tracking data and is therefore systematically undercounted in public commentary. The analytical implication is that the market may have a distorted view of how much supply has actually been added, and by extension, how much is about to be withdrawn.

## The July Cliff Is a Mechanical Step-Down, Not a Scenario

The tracked monthly draw profile is revealing. In April, global strategic draws totalled approximately 75 million barrels. In May, approximately 80 million barrels. In June, approximately 76 million barrels. In July, the figure drops to approximately 22 million barrels. In August, approximately 20 million barrels. This is not a forecast. It is the arithmetic of programmes that are scheduled to end. Japan's Phase 2 ends in early July. South Korea's IEA share is done. Spain delivers its remaining 7.8 million barrel balance by mid-June. Hungary's second release expires on 30 June. After that, only the US Tranche 4 continues, at roughly 18 million barrels per month.

The step-down from approximately 76 million barrels in June to approximately 22 million barrels in July represents a reduction of roughly 54 million barrels in a single month. To put that in context, the entire IEA coordinated release programme is adding roughly 75 million barrels per month during the peak period. The cliff is therefore not a marginal adjustment. It is a withdrawal of the majority of the strategic supply buffer that the market has been relying on for the past three months.

The key analytical question is whether this cliff is hard or soft. Three factors could backfill the gap. First, a US Tranche 5 announcement by mid-June. The probability is moderate and likely conditional on the persistence of the supply disruption. A peace agreement would probably mean this does not go ahead. Second, an IEA second coordinated release. The probability is low. IEA Executive Director Birol stated on 21 May that the organisation is discussing all the time, but member governments need to agree, and the political appetite for a second large-scale release is uncertain. Third, the untracked countries actually delivering. Germany, France, Italy, Australia, and several other IEA contributors have announced plans but produced almost no confirmed physical lifts. It is possible these releases come through in the end. It is equally possible that the authorisations were largely precautionary and will not materialise if the immediate crisis eases.

If none of these factors materialise, July and August are effectively a 20 million barrel per month programme. If two or three materialise, the programme continues at 40 to 60 million barrels per month through the third quarter. The range of outcomes is wide, and the market has not yet begun to debate which scenario is most likely.

## The Untracked Commitments Create a Material Information Gap That the Market Should Not Ignore

The IEA contribution table includes commitments from Germany (19.5 million barrels), the United Kingdom (14 million barrels), France (14.6 million barrels), Italy (10 million barrels), and Australia (4.8 million barrels), plus additional IEA contributors including Canada, Turkiye, Poland, the Netherlands, and Mexico. Combined, these untracked commitments total approximately 135 million barrels. But the tracker of confirmed physical lifts shows that only a small fraction of this volume has been delivered.

The UK provides a useful example. The government has confirmed a 14 million barrel commitment, but the pace of lifting has not been disclosed. The analytical assumption in most tracking models is a pro-rata spread across the delivery window. But there is no confirmation that this assumption is correct. Italy's 10 million barrel authorisation has produced no physical lift confirmations since the window opened on 1 April. Germany's 19.5 million barrel announcement, which is 68 percent products, has no confirmed physical lifts. France's 14.6 million barrel product release is similarly unconfirmed.

This information gap matters for two reasons. First, it means the true global SPR draw rate could be 20 to 40 million barrels higher than the tracker suggests. The IEA Executive Director recently mentioned a figure of 2.5 to 3 million barrels per day, which is higher than the tracker's 2.3 million barrels per day. The difference may be explained by untracked European deliveries. Second, it means that the July cliff could be materially softer if these commitments finally materialise. But the opposite is equally true: if the commitments were largely precautionary and the authorisations expire without physical delivery, the cliff is even harder than the tracker suggests.

The single most informative signal in the next 30 days is whether the US Department of Energy announces a fifth tranche before mid-June. After that, the weekly US lift rate, Spain's June delivery, and any Japanese METI commentary on Phase 3 will provide the next set of clues. The market should be watching these signals with the same intensity it watches production data from OPEC members, because the strategic reserve release programme has been a larger source of incremental supply than any single producer over the past three months.

## The Decision Framework for Market Participants: Treat the Current Flow as Temporary and Build Contingency Plans

For any serious oil market participant, the strategic reserve release programme creates a specific decision framework that should be applied now, not in July when the data becomes obvious.

The first decision is whether the current supply flow is structural or tactical. The evidence supports the tactical interpretation. The US exchange contract programme requires borrowers to return crude with a premium of 17 to 28 percent by 2027 to 2029. This is not a permanent reduction in strategic stocks. It is a time-shift of supply from the future to the present. The same logic applies to Japan's direct allocations to refiners, which are structured as loans. The strategic reserve release programme is therefore adding supply now that will need to be replaced later. The market should be pricing not only the current flow but also the future demand for replacement barrels.

The second decision is how to position for the July cliff. The range of outcomes is wide, but the asymmetry is clear. If the cliff is hard, the market loses approximately 1.8 million barrels per day of supply in a single month. If the cliff is soft, the loss is approximately 0.5 to 0.8 million barrels per day. The probability-weighted outcome suggests a significant tightening of the physical market in the third quarter, regardless of which scenario materialises. The only question is the magnitude.

The third decision is which signals to monitor. The priority list is clear. The US Tranche 5 announcement is the highest-conviction signal because it would indicate that the administration is still chasing its 172 million barrel pledge. The US weekly lift rate is the second most important signal because it will reveal whether the Department of Energy's degradation warning was conservative or accurate. Japan's Phase 3 announcement is the third signal because a reversal of policy would add another 30 to 40 million barrels of supply. Spain's balance delivery by mid-June is the fourth signal because it will confirm whether European product releases are on track. Italy's first physical lift confirmation is the fifth signal because it will test whether the 10 million barrel authorisation is real or paper.

The market currently appears to be pricing a continuation of the current flow. That assumption is unsupported by the programme schedules. The strategic reserve release programme was always a temporary bridge. The bridge is about to end. The question is whether a new bridge is being built before the current one collapses.

# Japan's IT Services Sector Is Entering a Productivity Divergence That Investors Cannot Afford to Ignore

The June 2026 Tankan survey delivered a deceptively simple message: software investment plans are being revised sharply upward, and engineer shortages are easing slightly. But beneath this surface lies a structural shift that will separate winners from laggards in Japan's IT services industry. The critical insight is not that demand is strong—it has been strong for years—but that the mechanism for margin improvement is changing. For the first time, a small group of major system integrators is beginning to deploy AI-driven development tools that could fundamentally alter their cost structures. Meanwhile, the rest of the industry remains stuck in a cycle of tight supply, rising unit prices, and persistent labor constraints. The result is a productivity divergence that will compound over the coming quarters, and the Tankan data provides the earliest signal yet that this gap is widening.

The report's most telling finding is that the employment conditions DI for large enterprises has remained essentially flat near its one-year average, while mid-sized and small firms saw a meaningful improvement of 9 points quarter-over-quarter. This is not random noise. It reflects a structural reality: the largest system integrators are no longer purely dependent on headcount expansion to meet demand. They are beginning to substitute capital for labor through AI-assisted development. The smaller players, lacking both the balance sheet and the technical infrastructure to make this leap, are still fighting the old war for engineers. The divergence in productivity trajectories is already embedded in the data, even if it has not yet fully registered in earnings reports.

## Software Investment Plans Reveal a Demand Regime Shift That Favors Consulting, Network Build-Out, and Cybersecurity

The headline figure from the June Tankan is striking: FY26 software investment plans across all industries were revised up to +12.4% year-over-year, a 5.3 percentage point increase from the March survey. Manufacturers led the charge with a 21.0% growth plan, up 10.7 points. But the composition of this spending matters far more than the aggregate number. The report explicitly notes that while modernization remains the core driver, demand is now beginning to emerge for consulting, network building, and cybersecurity—all of which are being catalyzed by corporate appetite for AI adoption.

This is not a continuation of the old IT spending cycle. Previous waves of software investment in Japan were largely driven by legacy system upgrades, regulatory compliance, and digital transformation initiatives that focused on process efficiency. The current wave is different because it is being pulled by AI adoption, which creates demand for entirely new service categories. Consulting engagements are needed to help clients identify where AI can be applied. Network infrastructure must be upgraded to handle the data flows that AI systems require. Cybersecurity services expand because AI introduces new attack surfaces and regulatory obligations around data governance.

For investors, this means the revenue mix of IT services companies will shift. Firms that can capture consulting and high-value advisory work will enjoy better pricing power and higher margins than those that remain focused on legacy maintenance and system integration. The Tankan data confirms that the demand side is moving in this direction. The question is which firms have positioned themselves to capture it.

## Engineer Supply Is Not Improving for the Market Leaders, and That Is a Feature, Not a Bug

At first glance, the -41 reading for the employment conditions DI across all enterprises appears to suggest that the labor market is loosening. The 9-point improvement quarter-over-quarter is the largest sequential move in recent years. But this aggregate figure masks a critical bifurcation. For large enterprises, the DI remains near its trailing twelve-month average, indicating no material change in their hiring environment. The improvement came entirely from mid-sized and small firms.

This divergence is not an accident. Large system integrators are not hiring aggressively because they are beginning to use AI tools to reduce their dependence on junior engineers. Code generation, automated testing, and AI-assisted design are allowing them to maintain or expand output without proportional headcount growth. The result is that their effective labor supply is increasing even as their reported headcount remains stable. Smaller firms, lacking these tools, must still compete for a shrinking pool of engineers.

The strategic implication is profound. In a declining working-age population, the firms that can decouple output from headcount will enjoy structurally superior margin trajectories. The Tankan data suggests this process has begun at a few major firms, with Fujitsu leading the way, followed by NOM Institute and NEC. For the rest of the industry, the old dynamics of engineer shortages and rising input costs remain unchanged. Investors should expect the margin dispersion between these two groups to widen over the next twelve to eighteen months.

## Output Prices Are Rising Faster Than Input Prices, but Only for Firms That Can Capture the AI Premium

The pricing data in the Tankan survey is often overlooked, but it contains one of the most actionable signals in the entire report. The output price DI for large enterprises in the information services sector stood at +43 in June, with the September outlook suggesting a further rise to +37. Input prices are also elevated, but the gap between output and input prices has been widening for the largest firms. This suggests that they are successfully passing through cost increases to clients and, in some cases, capturing a premium for AI-related services.

For mid-sized and small firms, the pricing dynamics are less favorable. Their output price DI is lower, and the gap with input prices is narrower. This is consistent with a market structure in which smaller players lack the bargaining power to raise prices independently. They are price takers in a market where costs are rising, which compresses margins.

The key analytical question is whether the pricing premium for large firms is sustainable. The report argues that it is, because the underlying demand driver—AI adoption—is still in its early stages. Corporate appetite for AI is only beginning to translate into concrete spending on consulting, network infrastructure, and cybersecurity. As this trend accelerates, the firms that have already invested in AI capabilities will be best positioned to capture the premium. Those that have not will be forced to compete on price for commoditized services.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: How Quickly Will AI-Driven Development Scale Across the Industry?

The report acknowledges that AI-driven development efforts are still slow across the industry as a whole, with only a few major system integrators making meaningful progress. It also notes that in Japan, where the working population is declining, AI is not yet expected to have a significant enough impact to fundamentally resolve the current shortage of engineers. This creates a tension that the report does not fully resolve.

If AI-driven development is still nascent, how much of the current productivity divergence is real versus cyclical? It is possible that the large firms are simply benefiting from a temporary pricing advantage that will erode as smaller competitors catch up. Alternatively, the first-mover advantage in AI adoption could create a self-reinforcing cycle: firms that deploy AI tools become more efficient, which allows them to offer better pricing and win more contracts, which gives them more data to train their AI models, which further widens the gap.

The report does not provide a definitive answer, and investors should be cautious about extrapolating too aggressively from a single quarter of Tankan data. However, the balance of evidence suggests that the divergence is structural rather than cyclical. The reason is that AI adoption in software development is not just about cost reduction; it is about capability expansion. Firms that can leverage AI to take on more complex projects, deliver them faster, and with higher quality will capture market share in a way that cannot be easily replicated by competitors who are simply adding headcount.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Separate the Structural Winners from the Cyclical Beneficiaries

The Tankan data provides a useful starting point, but investors need a framework to translate these macro signals into investment decisions. Based on the patterns in the report, three questions can help distinguish firms that are benefiting from structural tailwinds from those that are merely riding the demand cycle.

First, is the firm investing in AI-driven development tools, or is it still relying on headcount expansion to meet demand? The report explicitly identifies Fujitsu, NRI, and NEC as leaders in this area. Firms that are not on this list should be scrutinized for their technology roadmaps and capital allocation priorities.

Second, is the firm's revenue mix shifting toward consulting, network building, and cybersecurity? The report identifies these as the emerging demand categories driven by AI adoption. Firms that are still heavily weighted toward legacy system integration and maintenance may see margin compression as input costs rise.

Third, does the firm have the balance sheet and scale to invest in AI infrastructure? The report notes that small and mid-sized firms are still slow to adopt AI-driven development. This is likely a function of both capital constraints and technical expertise. Firms that lack the resources to make this investment will fall further behind.

For firms that pass all three tests, the Tankan data supports a constructive outlook. For those that do not, the improving aggregate demand environment may be a trap, masking underlying structural weakness that will become apparent as the cycle matures.

# US Banks Are Winning the AI Race Because They Invested in Talent First, and European Banks Cannot Catch Up by Partnering Alone

The most important strategic insight from the Evident AI Index is not that US banks lead in artificial intelligence. That fact is well known. The real insight is *why* they lead, and what that means for the institutions that are falling behind. The answer is brutally simple: talent. Talent accounts for 45 percent of the AI maturity index, and the gap between US and European banks on this dimension is not narrowing. It is widening.

This is not a technology problem. European banks can buy the same cloud infrastructure, the same large language models, and the same software platforms as their US counterparts. They can form partnerships with the same vendors. What they cannot easily buy is the density of AI engineers, data scientists, and machine learning researchers that US banks have been accumulating for nearly a decade. The US advantage is structural, deeply embedded in the broader technology ecosystem of Silicon Valley, New York, and Boston. European banks are not simply behind on a metric. They are behind on the input that drives every other metric.

The timing of this divergence matters enormously. AI in banking is moving from experimentation to production. Private benchmarks suggest that roughly 85 percent of current use cases are still focused on internal processes and efficiency, not revenue generation. That means the race is still in its early stages. The institutions that build deep AI talent pipelines now will compound their advantage as use cases shift toward customer-facing applications, pricing optimization, and risk management. Those that rely on partnerships and vendor solutions will find themselves permanently dependent, unable to customize, iterate, or innovate at the speed the market will demand.

This article draws on a recent webcast with the CEO and co-founder of the Evident AI Index, along with proprietary analysis from a global investment bank. It argues that the AI maturity gap in banking is fundamentally a talent gap, and that the strategic response for lagging institutions must go far deeper than signing another technology partnership.

## The Talent Advantage Is Self-Reinforcing, and European Banks Are Trapped in a Vicious Cycle

The Evident AI Index weights talent at 45 percent of overall AI maturity, and this weighting is not arbitrary. Talent is the enabling condition for every other dimension of AI capability. Without the right people, a bank cannot execute on strategy, cannot integrate third-party solutions effectively, cannot build proprietary models, and cannot govern AI responsibly.

US banks benefit from what economists call agglomeration effects. The concentration of technology talent in a handful of US metropolitan areas creates a deep labor pool that banks can draw from. When a US bank needs to hire fifty machine learning engineers, it can do so within a quarter. When a European bank needs to hire the same number, it faces a market where technology talent is scarcer, more expensive, and often more attracted to startups than to financial institutions.

The consequence is that US banks are able to build, while European banks are forced to buy. This build-versus-buy divergence is not a neutral strategic choice. It is a reflection of underlying structural constraints. US banks can afford to develop proprietary AI systems because they have the in-house expertise to design, deploy, and maintain them. European banks turn to partnerships because they lack that expertise, but partnerships come with trade-offs. Vendor solutions are less customizable, slower to iterate, and create dependency on external roadmaps.

The vicious cycle for European banks is clear. Because they have less talent, they rely more on vendors. Because they rely on vendors, they develop less internal capability. Because they develop less capability, they attract less talent. Each turn of the cycle widens the gap. The question is whether this cycle can be broken, and if so, how.

## Regulation Is Not the Primary Cause of Europe's AI Gap, but It Is Becoming a Convenient Excuse

A common narrative attributes Europe's slower AI adoption to a more cautious regulatory environment. The Evident AI Index data suggests this explanation is incomplete. European banks are indeed more cautious on what the index calls "responsible AI," but this caution appears to be anticipatory rather than reactive. The regulatory framework in Europe is still forming. Banks are holding back in expectation of rules that have not yet been written.

This anticipatory caution is rational at the level of an individual institution. No bank wants to invest heavily in an AI system only to find that it violates future regulations. But at the aggregate level, this caution has a cost. It slows experimentation, delays learning, and reinforces the talent gap. Talented AI practitioners want to work where they can build things, not where they spend most of their time in compliance meetings.

The danger for European banks is that regulatory caution becomes a self-justifying strategy. It is easier to say "we are waiting for regulatory clarity" than to admit that the organization lacks the talent and execution capability to move faster. Over time, this narrative becomes embedded in the culture. The organization stops trying to catch up and starts rationalizing its position.

This is not to argue that regulation is irrelevant. It matters, and it will matter more as AI applications move from back-office efficiency to customer-facing decisions. But regulation is not the primary driver of the gap. The primary driver is talent, and talent decisions are within the control of bank leadership. Blaming regulation is a way to avoid confronting a harder truth.

## Leadership Commitment Is the Hidden Differentiator That Separates Leaders from Laggards

The Evident AI Index highlights management commitment as a critical determinant of success, and this finding deserves careful attention. It is easy to assume that AI adoption is a technical challenge that can be delegated to a chief technology officer or a central innovation team. The data suggests otherwise. Effective AI adoption requires both strong CEO sponsorship and bottom-up execution, and leading institutions are increasingly embedding AI expertise within business lines rather than relying on centralized teams.

This organizational design choice has profound implications. When AI expertise is centralized, it tends to produce proofs of concept that never scale. The innovation team builds a model, demonstrates its potential, and then struggles to integrate it into the day-to-day operations of the business. When AI expertise is embedded within business lines, the incentives align differently. The business unit owns the outcome, so it invests in making the model work.

The banks that are succeeding are those where the CEO treats AI as a strategic priority, not a technology initiative. These CEOs ask different questions. They ask how AI changes the competitive dynamics of their core businesses, not just how it reduces costs. They ask what new revenue streams AI enables, not just what processes it automates. They ask how to reorganize the company to capture AI's full potential, not just how to add a machine learning capability to the existing structure.

This level of commitment is rare. It requires CEOs to understand AI well enough to challenge their teams, to make resource allocation decisions that favor long-term capability over short-term earnings, and to personally champion initiatives that may take years to pay off. The banks that have this leadership are pulling away. The banks that do not are falling further behind, regardless of how much they spend on technology.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question That It Does Not Fully Answer: Will AI Reduce Banking Jobs or Increase Them?

The conventional wisdom is that AI will destroy banking jobs. The Evident AI Index suggests a more nuanced picture. At leading institutions, AI adoption has been accompanied by continued investment in talent and front-line capacity. Headcount savings from automation are being reinvested into growth initiatives. The banks that are successfully deploying AI are adding resources, not cutting them.

This finding is counterintuitive, but it aligns with historical patterns. Previous waves of automation in banking did not eliminate jobs. They shifted the mix of jobs. Tellers became relationship managers. Back-office processors became data analysts. The same pattern may be repeating with AI. The jobs that disappear are routine and repetitive. The jobs that appear require judgment, creativity, and customer interaction.

The report suggests that the greater risk may lie with institutions that fail to keep pace with adoption. If a bank does not invest in AI, it will be outcompeted on cost, speed, and customer experience. It will lose market share. It will shrink. Job losses in that scenario are not caused by AI. They are caused by competitive failure.

But the report leaves a meaningful question unanswered. What happens when AI moves beyond the 85 percent of use cases focused on internal efficiency? When AI begins to replace not just data entry but credit analysis, portfolio management, and advisory services, will the pattern hold? Will new jobs emerge to replace the ones that disappear? The historical analogy may break down if the scope of automation is broader than in previous waves. This is the uncertainty that investors and bank executives need to watch most closely.

## A Decision Framework for Bank Executives: Three Questions That Determine AI Outcomes

For bank executives trying to navigate this landscape, the Evident AI Index and the supporting analysis suggest a decision framework organized around three questions. The answers to these questions will determine whether an institution becomes an AI leader or an AI laggard.

The first question is about talent strategy. Is the bank building a proprietary AI talent pipeline, or is it relying on vendors and partners? This is the most consequential strategic choice a bank can make. Building a talent pipeline is expensive, slow, and uncertain. It requires investment in university partnerships, internal training programs, and competitive compensation. But it creates a durable advantage. Partnering is faster and cheaper upfront, but it creates dependency. The bank that builds will eventually outpace the bank that buys.

The second question is about organizational design. Is AI expertise centralized in a corporate innovation team, or is it embedded within business lines? The evidence points strongly toward embedding. Centralized teams produce demos. Embedded teams produce results. The organizational structure must align incentives so that business units own AI outcomes, not just AI projects.

The third question is about leadership commitment. Does the CEO treat AI as a strategic priority or as a technology initiative? The banks that are winning have CEOs who personally drive the AI agenda. They allocate capital accordingly. They reshape the organization to capture AI's potential. They communicate a vision that goes beyond cost reduction. Without this level of commitment, no amount of technology spending will close the gap.

These three questions are not independent. Talent strategy shapes organizational design, and both depend on leadership commitment. A bank that answers all three questions correctly will compound its advantage over time. A bank that answers them incorrectly will find itself trapped in the vicious cycle described earlier, falling further behind with each passing quarter.

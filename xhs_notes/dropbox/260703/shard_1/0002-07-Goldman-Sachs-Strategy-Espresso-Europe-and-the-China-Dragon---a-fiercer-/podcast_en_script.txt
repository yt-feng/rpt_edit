EN_A: So there's this report with a pretty dramatic title: "Europe and the China Dragon – A Fiercer Fight." Is China really taking more share in Europe?
EN_B: Yes, more than ever. China's exports to Europe have been growing at double-digit annual rates recently, and it's also gaining share in third markets. China now accounts for 23% of EU imports.
EN_A: Twenty-three percent? That's significant. And what about Europe selling to China?
EN_B: That's the flip side. Europe's export share to China has dropped sharply since 2020. So China is buying less from Europe but selling more into Europe, which is a direct competitive threat to European manufacturers.
EN_A: What's the core question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It's asking why China is so aggressively penetrating European markets, which sectors are most vulnerable, and whether Europe is fighting back.
EN_A: Let's start with the drivers. Why can China do this?
EN_B: The report points to several factors. First, weak domestic demand and overcapacity in many industries are pushing Chinese companies to look abroad. Second, China has massive cost advantages—lower labor, borrowing, energy, and land costs, plus a significantly undervalued currency.
EN_A: So European companies can't compete on price?
EN_B: Exactly. And when you add Europe's own growth hurdles—like regulation, planning laws, and underinvestment—China is rapidly gaining manufacturing share. This isn't temporary; it's structural.
EN_A: Which sectors are most exposed?
EN_B: The report highlights autos, medtech, chemicals, and some industrials. For example, Chinese domestic auto brands gained over 400 basis points of European market share in the year to May, now sitting at 6 to 7 percent.
EN_A: How have the stocks performed?
EN_B: A basket of companies exposed to Chinese competition has consistently underperformed the broader market. The underperformance is driven by earnings downgrades, not valuation compression. These stocks already trade at a 25 to 30 percent discount.
EN_A: So it sounds like it's already priced in. But the report still warns investors?
EN_B: Yes. It argues that because China's export share is still growing and earnings outlooks remain weak, we'll likely see continued earnings-driven underperformance rather than further valuation declines. But there's a key question the report doesn't fully answer.
EN_A: What's that?
EN_B: How the dynamics could shift if Europe imposes more tariffs or if China enters new industries. The report touches on it but doesn't go deep. For instance, the Chinese e-commerce platform JoyBuy recently launched in the UK and quickly captured 10% of the users of a local retailer, Argos.
EN_A: That's a whole new dimension of competition. So is Europe actually defending itself?
EN_B: Currently, less than 10% of Chinese imports into the EU face tariffs, which is very different from the US approach. But EU policy is shifting. In June, the EU Council asked the Commission to propose a response. The report expects a dual-track strategy.
EN_A: Dual-track? What does that mean?
EN_B: One track is diversification to reduce reliance on Chinese components. The other is protection, with targeted tariffs and quotas after sector-specific investigations. But the report says the EU is unlikely to copy the US-style blanket tariffs.
EN_A: Why not?
EN_B: Because China is still a key supplier of refined rare earths, blanket tariffs wouldn't address the lost market share in third countries, and China's cost advantage is so large that tariffs might not close the price gap.
EN_A: That sounds like a cliffhanger. Does this competition matter for the broader European indices?
EN_B: The report says it matters less than investors think. Autos and chemicals combined have a smaller market cap than aerospace and defense or renewables. And tech hardware is now four times the size of autos, compared to being roughly equal three years ago.
EN_A: So not a huge index-level concern. What does this mean for global investors?
EN_B: Here's a key insight: China gaining market share doesn't automatically translate into shareholder returns. Much of China's success comes from aggressive pricing, which often leads to thin profits. So Chinese equities haven't been the primary beneficiaries of the export boom. In fact, European equities have outperformed over most medium to long-term horizons, thanks to better earnings quality and higher returns on capital.
EN_A: That's a bit counterintuitive. What questions does the report leave unanswered?
EN_B: One is the investment and innovation gap. The report notes that European companies spend only 20% of operating cash flow on R&D and capex, compared to 40 to 45% for US and Chinese firms. But it doesn't fully explore which European companies can fight back through innovation, or which sectors will keep bleeding.

# The Fed Is Quietly Rewriting Its Playbook: Why Warsh’s Sintra Comments Signal a Regime Change in Monetary Policy

The most important signal from the Federal Reserve in recent months did not come from a dot plot, a press conference, or a formal statement. It came from a panel discussion in Sintra, Portugal, where Chair Kevin Warsh offered three seemingly modest observations: inflation risks have receded, artificial intelligence is reshaping the supply side of the economy with profound implications for monetary policy, and the names of new task force members would be released shortly. These are not incremental updates. They are the scaffolding of a new monetary policy framework that is being constructed in real time, and investors who treat them as routine commentary risk missing the larger shift underway.

Warsh is not merely updating the market on the data. He is redefining what data matters, how it should be interpreted, and who will decide. The central bank is moving away from backward-looking, government-sourced statistics toward real-time, technology-driven indicators. It is elevating structural productivity growth over cyclical demand management. And it is deliberately dismantling the forward guidance apparatus that has defined Fed communication for over a decade. Taken together, these moves amount to a fundamental rethinking of how monetary policy should operate in an era of AI-driven supply expansion.

The timing is not accidental. The June FOMC meeting revealed a committee struggling to reconcile sticky inflation with resilient growth. Warsh’s Sintra comments offer a way out: if the supply side is expanding faster than anyone measured, then the economy can run hotter without generating inflation, and policy can remain patient without appearing complacent. This is a high-stakes bet on structural transformation, and the evidence to support it is still being assembled. For now, the argument is being made on conviction rather than data, which makes it both intellectually bold and analytically risky.

## The Fed Is Replacing Data Dependence with a New Framework, and the Old Data Is Being Discounted

One of the most striking features of Warsh’s Sintra appearance was his willingness to downplay the very economic data that markets have been trained to obsess over. When asked about his favorite economic indicator, he did not cite nonfarm payrolls, CPI, or GDP. Instead, he expressed hope that the new Data Task Force would produce better, real-time measures within nine to twelve months. He described current government statistics as suffering from “mismeasurement problems” and surveys that are “no longer relevant.” His least favorite indicator? He called many of them “echoes of history.”

This is not a throwaway remark. It is a deliberate signal that the Fed is shifting its epistemic foundation. For years, the central bank’s reaction function was understood as a mechanical response to incoming data: higher inflation means tighter policy, weaker employment means looser policy. Warsh is challenging that entire construct. If the data is backward-looking, mismeasured, and structurally outdated, then policy based on that data will be systematically too slow and too reactive. The implication is profound: the Fed no longer trusts its own measurement tools, and it is building new ones before it fully commits to a policy path.

The practical consequence for investors is that traditional data releases will matter less, and the Fed’s internal narrative will matter more. The market has been conditioned to trade on CPI day and payrolls Friday. Going forward, the most important inputs may come from the task forces themselves, from academic papers commissioned by the Fed, and from Warsh’s own speeches. The old playbook of “data dependency” is being replaced by something closer to “framework dependency,” where the central bank’s interpretation of the economy depends on a new set of lenses that have not yet been fully ground.

## AI-Driven Supply Expansion Is the Central Bet of This Fed, and It Changes the Inflation Calculus

Warsh’s conviction about artificial intelligence is not new, but his Sintra remarks crystallized how central it is to his policy worldview. He described the AI boom as leading to a surge in capital expenditures that is visible on the demand side but that he expects to show up on the supply side as well. He reiterated his belief that AI is a significant disinflationary force, increasing productivity and bolstering competitiveness. And he explicitly linked this to monetary policy: if the supply side expands, it has “huge implications” for how the Fed should set interest rates.

The logic is straightforward but underappreciated. Traditional monetary policy assumes that the economy has a fixed potential growth rate, and that any demand above that potential is inflationary. If AI is structurally raising potential growth, then the same level of nominal GDP growth becomes less inflationary. The economy can run faster without overheating. This gives the Fed permission to be less aggressive in tightening, even if headline growth remains strong. Warsh’s reference to structural productivity running in the “high 2% range” over the last four quarters is an explicit claim that the supply side is already expanding faster than official estimates capture.

The risk is that this bet is premature or wrong. Productivity surges are notoriously difficult to measure in real time, and the history of technological revolutions is littered with overestimates of their immediate economic impact. If AI proves to be more hype than structural shift, the Fed could find itself behind the curve on inflation, having stayed accommodative based on a supply-side miracle that never materialized. Warsh acknowledged this uncertainty, saying “nothing is in the bank at this time of consequence,” but his policy posture is clearly tilted toward betting on the transformation rather than waiting for proof.

## The Task Forces Are Not a Side Project; They Are the Mechanism for a New Monetary Constitution

Warsh announced that names for the various task forces could be released as early as next week, and the first appointment—former Bank of England Governor Mervyn King to co-chair the communications task force—was announced shortly after his Sintra appearance. This is not bureaucratic housekeeping. The task forces are the vehicle through which Warsh intends to rewrite the rules of monetary policy. They cover data, communications, and the balance sheet, three of the most consequential dimensions of central banking.

The communications task force is particularly telling. Warsh has been critical of forward guidance throughout his tenure, and in Sintra he found common cause with his counterparts from the Bank of England, the ECB, and the Bank of Canada in avoiding explicit guidance. But the problem is deeper than a stylistic preference. If the Fed no longer wants to guide markets on the path of rates, it needs a different way to anchor expectations. The communications task force is tasked with designing that new approach. Mervyn King’s appointment signals that the Fed is looking for a framework that is principles-based rather than path-based, one that explains how the Fed will react to new information without committing to a specific sequence of moves.

The balance sheet task force is equally important. Warsh described the current large balance sheet as “bordering on fiscal policy” and indicated that any changes would be “well-deliberated publicly” and “not implemented until financial markets have come to understand what those are.” This suggests a long, transparent process that could take years, but the direction is clear: the Fed wants to shrink its footprint and return interest rate policy to being the primary tool. The appointment of Stanford’s Darrell Duffie, a leading scholar on balance sheet policy and a fellow Hoover Institution scholar, would fit the pattern of seeking academic rigor over political expediency.

## The Report Leaves One Critical Question Unanswered: What Happens If the Supply-Side Bet Fails?

For all the intellectual coherence of Warsh’s vision, there is a gap at the center of the argument. The entire framework depends on the assumption that the supply side is expanding fast enough to absorb demand without generating inflation. But the evidence for this is still thin. Productivity data is noisy and often revised. The AI investment boom is real, but it could take years to translate into broad-based productivity gains. And the inflation risks that Warsh acknowledged have “come down” could easily reemerge if supply chains tighten, labor markets overheat, or geopolitical shocks hit.

The report does not fully address what happens if the supply-side bet is wrong. If inflation reaccelerates while the Fed is still waiting for its new data and task force recommendations, the central bank would be forced to tighten aggressively from a position of having downplayed the traditional indicators. That would be a credibility crisis of the first order. Warsh’s confidence that the committee is “going to deliver price stability” is reassuring, but it leaves open the question of how that commitment would be demonstrated if the current framework proves inadequate.

Investors should watch for two things. First, whether the new data from the task forces shows genuine improvement in real-time measurement, or whether it simply confirms what the old data already showed. Second, whether Warsh’s colleagues on the FOMC share his conviction about AI-driven supply expansion, or whether dissent begins to emerge as the policy implications become clearer. The unity of the committee is the unstated assumption behind the entire strategy, and it is the most fragile.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position for a Regime Change in Monetary Policy

The Sintra comments offer a clear decision framework for investors who want to position for the regime change that is underway. The framework has three pillars: supply-side conviction, data skepticism, and communication opacity.

First, supply-side conviction means that the Fed is willing to tolerate stronger growth and higher asset prices than traditional models would allow. Investors should overweight sectors that benefit from AI-driven productivity gains, including technology infrastructure, automation, and data analytics. They should underweight sectors that depend on inflation protection or that would suffer from a sustained period of above-trend growth without corresponding rate hikes.

Second, data skepticism means that traditional economic releases will be less reliable as signals for policy. Investors should reduce their reliance on monthly CPI and payrolls and instead focus on the Fed’s own internal metrics, including the outputs of the Data Task Force, productivity revisions, and the tone of FOMC communications. The biggest policy moves may come when the Fed believes its new data tells a different story than the old data, creating opportunities for those who understand the new framework.

Third, communication opacity means that the Fed will provide less explicit guidance on the path of rates. Investors should prepare for a world where meetings are more volatile, where the Fed’s reaction function is harder to model, and where the burden of interpretation falls on the market rather than the central bank. This favors strategies that are nimble, that can adapt quickly to new information, and that do not rely on a stable policy path.

The most important implication is that the old rules of monetary policy are being rewritten. Investors who continue to use the old rules will systematically misprice risk. Those who understand the new framework will have a significant informational advantage.

# The Market Has Priced a Hawkish Fed that Will Not Arrive: Why Long 10-Year TIPS Are the Right Bet Now

The most important opportunity in US rates today is not about chasing nominal yield or forecasting the next payroll number. It is about recognizing that the market has built a hawkish policy narrative that the data no longer supports, and that the most efficient way to express this view is through 10-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities. Real yields on 10-year TIPS are trading near the top of their historical range, while the Federal Reserve's implied terminal rate has risen roughly 75 basis points over the past three months to 3.6 percent. This combination creates a structural mispricing. If the Fed's policy trough moves lower as inflation continues to cool, 10-year TIPS will rally. The asymmetry in this trade is unusually favorable.

The timing matters because the narrative is shifting beneath the surface. Eight of the last twelve headline CPI prints have surprised to the downside. Core CPI, excluding volatile components like airfares and hotels, continues to moderate. Oil prices have fallen below USD 80 per barrel following the US-Iran memorandum of understanding, and inflation forward markets have responded by moving lower. The 5-year, 5-year forward CPI swap and the 2-year, 3-year forward are now near levels last seen when the market was pricing rate cuts in February 2026. This is not a market that fears inflation. This is a market that trusts the Fed's commitment to price stability, yet still prices a hawkish terminal rate because it has been conditioned by three consecutive upside payroll surprises.

The disconnect is the opportunity. The market has extrapolated recent labor market strength into a permanent hawkish stance, but the underlying inflation trend tells a different story. Our economists forecast core CPI at 2.4 percent year-over-year by May 2027 and June nonfarm payrolls at 90,000, well below the consensus estimate of 115,000. If the data confirms this softening, the entire rates complex will reprice. And within that repricing, 10-year TIPS offer the most compelling risk-reward.

## Real Yields Are Near the Top of Their Range, and a Lower Fed Trough Will Drive Them Lower

The empirical relationship between 10-year real yields and the Fed's policy terminal rate is well established. Over the past five years, weekly changes in 10-year real yields have shown a 51 percent R-squared when regressed on changes in the Fed policy terminal rate. This is not a loose correlation. It is a structural link. When the market believes the Fed will keep rates higher for longer, real yields rise. When that belief fades, real yields fall.

Today, the market is pricing a terminal rate of approximately 3.6 percent. Our economists expect two rate cuts over the coming quarters, which would push that terminal lower. If the trough rate declines by even 25 to 30 basis points, the historical beta suggests 10-year real yields would fall by roughly 15 to 20 basis points from current levels. That is a meaningful move for a trade that already offers positive carry.

The current 10-year TIPS yield of approximately 2.21 percent is near the high end of its post-pandemic range. It has been higher only during periods of acute inflation fear or aggressive Fed tightening. Neither condition applies today. Inflation is cooling, not accelerating. The Fed is on hold, not hiking. The only reason real yields are this elevated is that the market has priced a hawkish terminal rate based on payroll data that is likely to revert. When it does, real yields will compress, and TIPS will benefit.

## Inflation Is Cooling, and the Burden of Proof Has Shifted to the Data

The inflation narrative has been dominated by sticky core CPI readings and the wide wedge between core CPI and core PCE. But the direction of travel is clear. Eight of the last twelve headline CPI prints have surprised to the downside. The underlying trend in inflation is moderating, even if the monthly prints remain noisy. The burden of proof has shifted. It is no longer on the disinflation camp to prove that inflation is falling. It is on the hawkish camp to prove that inflation is reaccelerating.

This matters because the market has been slow to adjust. Core CPI fixings remain elevated, with the year-over-year curve showing a peak that is susceptible to 60-basis-point moves in a single month, as we have seen during periods of geopolitical tension. But the tension is easing. Oil prices are lower. Supply chains are normalizing. And the most important structural development is the forthcoming change in the Bureau of Economic Analysis methodology for calculating PCE prices. If applied retroactively, our economists estimate this change could lower core PCE year-over-year inflation by up to 20 basis points.

The wedge between core CPI and core PCE has widened to an unusual degree, driven by components like memory, software, healthcare, and financial services that carry larger weights in PCE than in CPI. This wedge has been a source of confusion for markets, because the Fed targets PCE, not CPI. A normalizing wedge would act in favor of rate cuts, because it would bring the Fed's preferred inflation measure closer to target even if CPI remains elevated. The BEA methodology change accelerates this normalization.

## Beta-Weighted Breakevens Near Lows Imply TIPS Are Cheap Versus Nominal Treasuries

Relative value analysis provides a second, independent argument for buying TIPS. The concept of beta-weighted breakevens adjusts the relationship between nominal yields and real yields for their historical covariance. Under normal trading conditions, a 1-basis-point move in 10-year nominal yields corresponds to a roughly 0.75-basis-point move in 10-year real yields, implying a 0.25-basis-point widening in breakevens. This beta has been stable over the past decade, with values between 0.7 and 0.8.

When beta-weighted breakevens are at the low end of their historical range, it means that TIPS have cheapened relative to nominal Treasuries after adjusting for their normal relationship. In other words, the breakeven inflation rate embedded in TIPS prices is lower than what the historical relationship between nominal and real yields would suggest. This is a classic signal that TIPS are undervalued.

The mechanism for mean reversion is well understood. When volatility increases, beta-weighted breakevens tend to revert from extreme levels. The recent decline in breakevens has been driven by falling oil prices and a benign inflation outlook, but the beta adjustment suggests the move has gone too far. If breakevens revert, TIPS will outperform nominals. The carry profile supports this view. The one-month carry for long 10-year TIPS is currently positive at 5.8 basis points, meaning the trade earns money even if yields remain unchanged.

## The Core CPI Fixings Curve Is a Source of Unresolved Risk and Opportunity

The report highlights a tension that deserves more attention. Headline CPI fixings for May 2027 are at 1.8 percent, pulled lower by the negative energy commodities component. But core CPI fixings are at 2.9 percent, up 15 basis points over the past two weeks. The divergence between headline and core fixings is unusually wide, and it reflects a market that is pricing two different narratives simultaneously.

The headline fixings say that energy-driven disinflation will persist. The core fixings say that underlying inflation remains sticky because of strength in the economy and elevated inflation expectations. Both cannot be right indefinitely. If the economy softens as our economists expect, core fixings will need to converge lower. If the economy remains strong, headline fixings will need to move higher. The direction of convergence is the key unknown, and it will determine the path of TIPS.

The report does not fully resolve this tension, and that is a feature, not a bug. The core CPI fixings curve has shown itself to be highly sensitive to shifts in market perception. During the conflict period, the year-over-year core CPI curve peak moved by 60 basis points in a single month. If the upcoming payrolls and CPI prints come in soft, a similar move in the opposite direction is plausible. Investors who enter TIPS now are betting that the convergence will happen through lower core fixings, not higher headline fixings. The data supports this view, but the market has not yet priced it.

## A Decision Framework for the Long TIPS Trade

For institutional investors evaluating this opportunity, the decision framework should rest on three pillars: the direction of the Fed's terminal rate, the trajectory of core inflation, and the relative value signal from breakevens.

First, assess the terminal rate. The market is pricing a 3.6 percent trough. If you believe, as our economists do, that two rate cuts are coming, then the terminal rate will fall and real yields will follow. The historical beta of 0.5 to 0.6 for weekly changes implies a meaningful move. If you believe the terminal rate is correctly priced, the trade loses its primary catalyst.

Second, assess core inflation. The burden of proof has shifted to the upside. Eight of twelve downside CPI surprises, a normalizing CPI-PCE wedge, and forthcoming BEA methodology changes all point to lower core inflation. If the June CPI print confirms this trend, the narrative will break in favor of TIPS. If it surprises to the upside, the trade faces headwinds.

Third, assess the breakeven signal. Beta-weighted breakevens near range lows are a quantitative argument that TIPS are cheap. This signal is independent of the macro outlook and provides a hedge against the possibility that inflation remains elevated. If breakevens mean revert, TIPS outperform regardless of the level of nominal yields.

The trade entry level is 2.21 percent on 10-year TIPS, with a target of 1.8 percent and a stop at 2.35 percent. The risk is an upside surprise in June payrolls or CPI. To hedge this risk, investors can own a 1-month, 10-year payer swaption. The cost is 63 cents, implying that the underlying 10-year rate needs to move up by 7.7 basis points for the swaption to break even at expiry. This is a cheap hedge for a tail risk that the market may be overpricing.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer

The report makes a compelling case for long TIPS, but it leaves several important questions open. First, how persistent is the beta-weighted breakeven signal? The historical mean reversion pattern is well documented, but the current environment of falling oil prices and declining inflation expectations may be structural rather than cyclical. If breakevens stay low because the inflation regime has permanently changed, the relative value argument weakens.

Second, what happens if the labor market remains strong? The report acknowledges that payrolls are a risk, but it does not fully explore the scenario in which the economy reaccelerates. If June payrolls print above 150,000 and the unemployment rate falls, the market will interpret this as confirmation that the Fed's hawkish stance is appropriate. Real yields could rise further, and TIPS would suffer.

Third, how should investors size the trade relative to other positions in a multi-asset portfolio? The report focuses on the TIPS-specific case, but it does not address the correlation with equities, credit, or currencies. In a risk-off scenario, TIPS may underperform nominals as breakevens compress. In a risk-on scenario, they may outperform. The portfolio context matters.

# The Semiconductor Restocking Cycle Has Not Begun — But the Conditions for It Are Now in Place

The semiconductor supply chain is not yet restocking. That is the central finding from the most recent quarterly inventory analysis, and it runs counter to the narrative that has been building across earnings calls and investor presentations over the past several months. Total supply chain days of inventory increased by only 9 days quarter-over-quarter in the first quarter, compared to a typical seasonal increase of 19 days. The supply chain remains 33 days above its historical median. This is not the beginning of a broad-based replenishment cycle. It is something more nuanced: a period of tightening discipline, selective inventory reduction, and diverging trajectories across end markets that will determine where the real opportunities lie.

The data tells a story of restraint rather than recovery. Producers built inventory at below-seasonal levels. Distributors actively leaned out their positions. Customers, as a group, were roughly in line with seasonal norms. The aggregate picture is one of an industry that has learned the hard lessons of the 2021-2022 over-ordering cycle and is now exercising a degree of caution that many investors may be underestimating. The question is not whether restocking will come. The question is when, and which parts of the value chain will benefit first.

The implications for portfolio positioning are significant. In an environment where supply is tightening but demand signals remain mixed, the winners will be those companies with defensible inventory positions and exposure to end markets where supply-demand dynamics are most favorable. The companies that look best positioned today include premium analog names, compute and networking leaders, memory players, and select semiconductor capital equipment firms. But the broader message is that patience is required. The restocking cycle that many are waiting for has not yet arrived, and the data suggests it may be several more quarters before it materializes in a meaningful way.

## The Supply Chain Is Tightening, but the Tightening Is Not Uniform Across Producers, Distributors, and Customers

The most important insight from the first quarter data is that the inventory build was significantly below normal seasonality, and the drivers of that underperformance varied sharply across the three major segments of the supply chain. Producers, distributors, and customers are not moving in lockstep, and understanding the divergence is essential for identifying where risk and opportunity reside.

Producer inventory days increased by only 2 days quarter-over-quarter, compared to a seasonal increase of 5 days. Semiconductor companies now sit 23 days above their historical median, which is elevated but improving. The improvement was driven by meaningful reductions in analog and MCU inventory, which decreased by 2 days, and semiconductor capital equipment, which dropped by 6 days. Memory was flat. The areas that saw inventory builds were smartphones and foundries, which increased by 21 days and 8 days respectively. This is not a broad-based producer drawdown. It is a selective one, concentrated in segments where demand has been weakest and where companies have been most aggressive in managing their balance sheets.

Distributors, meanwhile, are playing a different game. Distributor inventory days decreased by 2 days quarter-over-quarter, against a seasonal increase of 4 days. This is a meaningful outperformance. Distributors are now 7 days above their historical median, down from a much wider gap in prior quarters. The reduction was driven by WPG and Avnet, where cost of goods sold grew faster than inventory in absolute terms. Arrow was the outlier, with a slight increase. The distributor behavior is consistent with a channel that is cautious about holding excess stock in an environment where lead times are normalizing and demand visibility remains limited.

Customers, as a group, were roughly in line with seasonality, increasing by 9 days against a seasonal expectation of 8 days. But the aggregate masks wide dispersion. Auto suppliers actually reduced inventory by 1 day, while communications customers built by 17 days and ODMs by 16 days. The customer segment is not a monolith. Some end markets are still working through excess, while others are beginning to prepare for demand that may or may not materialize.

The strategic takeaway is that the supply chain is tightening, but the tightening is uneven and fragile. Producers are showing discipline. Distributors are leaning out. Customers are mixed. The conditions for a restocking cycle are being created, but the cycle itself has not started.

## The Auto Sector Is a Bellwether for How Inventory Normalization Plays Out Across End Markets

The automotive semiconductor market has been one of the most watched and most misunderstood segments of the supply chain over the past several quarters. The first quarter data provides a clear signal: auto OEMs and auto suppliers are moving in opposite directions, and that divergence has important implications for how the broader normalization process will unfold.

Auto OEM inventory days increased by 7 days quarter-over-quarter, moving above the historical median. This is a notable shift. After several quarters of aggressive inventory reduction, OEMs appear to be rebuilding, albeit cautiously. The increase was driven by a 7% increase in absolute inventory dollars against a decline in cost of goods sold. This is consistent with OEMs preparing for higher production volumes, but the data does not yet confirm that end demand is accelerating. It may simply reflect a normalization from previously depleted levels.

Auto suppliers, in contrast, decreased inventory by 1 day, moving back to in-line with the historical median. This is the most encouraging data point in the entire report for the auto supply chain. After years of elevated inventory driven by the chip shortage and subsequent over-ordering, auto suppliers have finally worked their way back to a neutral position. The trailing four-quarter average for auto suppliers increased slightly, but the sequential decline is the key signal.

The question this raises is whether auto OEMs will continue to build inventory, and whether that will eventually pull through to higher orders for suppliers. If OEMs are serious about rebuilding, auto suppliers should see demand improve in the second half of the year. But if the OEM build is simply a precautionary move rather than a response to real demand, the supplier improvement may be short-lived.

The broader point is that the auto sector is further along in the normalization process than many other end markets. Auto suppliers are at historical median levels. Auto OEMs are slightly above. This is not a sector that is driving the next upcycle, but it is also not a sector that is holding the supply chain back. The risk lies elsewhere.

## Communications and Computing/Mobile Are the Areas Where Inventory Risk Remains Highest

While auto and industrial segments have made meaningful progress in reducing excess inventory, the same cannot be said for communications and computing/mobile. These two end markets stand out as the areas where inventory remains most elevated relative to historical norms, and where the risk of further downside is greatest.

Communications customers increased inventory by 17 days quarter-over-quarter, the largest increase of any end market. They now sit 23 days above their historical median. The increase was driven by an 11% rise in absolute inventory dollars against a decline in cost of goods sold. This is a concerning combination. It suggests that companies are holding more inventory even as their revenue-generating capacity is shrinking. The trailing four-quarter average also increased, confirming that this is not a one-quarter anomaly.

Computing and mobile customers increased by 11 days, now sitting 12 days above the historical median. The absolute inventory dollar increase was even more striking at 27% quarter-over-quarter. This is the highest growth rate of any end market. The computing/mobile segment is being driven by demand for AI-related infrastructure, but the inventory build suggests that the supply chain may be getting ahead of itself. If AI-related demand disappoints or if lead times continue to compress, the computing/mobile segment could be the source of the next inventory correction.

The pattern is clear. The end markets that were most affected by the 2021-2022 over-ordering cycle have largely normalized. The end markets that are benefiting from the AI buildout are now building inventory at a pace that may outstrip demand. The risk is that the next leg of the cycle is not a broad-based restocking but a rotation of inventory problems from one set of end markets to another.

## The Report Leaves an Unanswered Question: What Will Trigger the Restocking Cycle?

The first quarter data provides a detailed picture of where the supply chain stands, but it does not answer the most important question for investors: what will actually trigger a broad-based restocking cycle? The report identifies the conditions that are necessary for restocking to begin, but it does not specify the catalyst that will turn those conditions into action.

The necessary conditions are in place. Producer inventory is declining. Distributor inventory is declining. Customer inventory is roughly in line with seasonality. The supply chain is 33 days above the historical median, which is elevated but improving. If these trends continue for another two to three quarters, the supply chain could reach a neutral position by the end of 2026 or early 2027.

But the sufficient condition is missing. Restocking requires demand visibility. Companies will not rebuild inventory unless they have confidence that end demand will absorb the additional supply. The current demand environment is mixed. AI-related spending remains strong, but the rest of the semiconductor market is experiencing tepid growth at best. Industrial demand is weak. Automotive demand is recovering slowly. Consumer demand is uncertain.

The unanswered question is whether AI-related demand will be strong enough to pull the rest of the supply chain into a restocking cycle, or whether the non-AI segments will remain a drag that prevents a broad-based recovery. The report does not take a firm position on this question, and the data does not yet provide a clear answer. Investors should watch for signs of improving demand in industrial and automotive end markets as the leading indicator that a restocking cycle is imminent.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in the Current Environment

Given the data and the open questions, investors need a clear framework for making decisions. The following approach is designed to translate the inventory analysis into actionable portfolio positioning.

First, prioritize companies with defensible inventory positions. The companies that are best positioned are those where inventory is already at or below historical median levels, or where inventory is declining even as demand holds steady. In the producer segment, analog and MCU companies have reduced inventory by 2 days quarter-over-quarter and are showing discipline. Memory companies are flat, which is neutral but not concerning. Semiconductor capital equipment companies have reduced inventory by 6 days, which is the most aggressive reduction of any producer sub-segment. These are the areas where inventory risk is lowest.

Second, favor end markets where supply-demand dynamics are tightening. The report identifies premium analog, compute and networking, memory, and semiconductor capital equipment as the preferred areas. These are the segments where supply is constrained relative to demand, or where inventory risk is most defensible. The specific companies mentioned in this context include ADI and NXP for analog, NVDA, AVGO, and CBRS for compute and networking, MU and SNDK for memory, and MKSI, KLAC, LRCX, and ONTO for semiconductor capital equipment.

Third, avoid or underweight end markets where inventory remains elevated and demand is uncertain. Communications and computing/mobile are the highest-risk areas. Companies with significant exposure to these end markets may face further inventory corrections if demand does not accelerate. The same caution applies to ODMs, which increased inventory by 16 days quarter-over-quarter and now sit 21 days above the historical median.

Fourth, watch for the inflection point in distributors. Distributors are the most forward-looking segment of the supply chain. When distributors begin to rebuild inventory, it is a strong signal that the restocking cycle has begun. Currently, distributors are still leaning out. The moment that changes will be the most important indicator for the entire semiconductor value chain.

The framework is simple: go where inventory is low and tightening, avoid where inventory is high and rising, and wait for the distributor signal before making a broad-based bet on the restocking cycle.

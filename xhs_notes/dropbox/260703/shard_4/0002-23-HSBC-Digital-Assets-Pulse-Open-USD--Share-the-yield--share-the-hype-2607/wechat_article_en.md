# Open USD Is Not a Disruptor of Stablecoins — It Is a Test of Whether Distribution Beats Issuance

The announcement of Open USD (OUSD) by a consortium including BlackRock, Visa, Mastercard, Stripe, and Coinbase has been widely interpreted as a direct challenge to Tether and Circle. The narrative is seductive: a new stablecoin that shares reserve income with its partners, charges no fees for minting or redemption, and boasts over 140 participating businesses must surely threaten incumbents. Circle's share price dropped sharply on the news, lending credence to the disruption thesis.

But this framing is misleading. It mistakes a business model difference for a structural advantage, and it ignores the most important question: does sharing revenue actually drive adoption? The evidence so far suggests it does not. The Global Dollar Network (USDG), launched in 2024 with the same revenue-sharing model, has accumulated only USD 3 billion in market cap — a fraction of USDT's USD 185 billion and USDC's USD 74 billion. If sharing the yield were the winning formula, USDG would already be a major player. It is not.

What matters now is not whether OUSD is innovative — it is not, in any fundamental sense — but whether its consortium structure can achieve something USDG could not: genuine distribution into real-world payment flows, beyond crypto trading. That is the real strategic question for every participant in the stablecoin ecosystem, from issuers to exchanges to regulators.

The timing of this test is critical. Stablecoin market cap has risen only 25% over the past year, despite regulatory progress including the US Genius Act and frameworks in the UK and EU. The reason is straightforward: stablecoins remain overwhelmingly a tool for crypto trading, and crypto trading has been soft. For stablecoins to reach the multi-trillion dollar forecasts that analysts project, they must break out of this narrow use case. OUSD represents the most serious attempt yet to do so — but it is far from guaranteed to succeed.

This article examines OUSD along three dimensions that the report identifies — innovation, competition, and adoption — but pushes further into the strategic implications that are not fully resolved. It then offers a decision framework for readers who must navigate this increasingly complex landscape.

## The Consortium Model Is Not New, and the Real Innovation Is Not the Revenue Share

The most widely reported feature of OUSD is that it shares reserve income with its partners rather than retaining it like Tether and Circle. This is presented as a radical departure. In reality, as the report notes, USDG has operated on exactly this principle since 2024. The idea that distributing yield to distributors will incentivize them to promote a stablecoin is logical in theory, but USDG's market cap of USD 3 billion — versus USD 185 billion for USDT — suggests that theory has not translated into practice.

Why? Because revenue sharing alone does not solve the core challenge of stablecoin adoption: network effects. USDT and USDC are deeply embedded in crypto exchange infrastructure, trading pairs, and liquidity pools. A merchant or exchange that accepts USDT does so because its counterparties also accept USDT. This is a classic two-sided network effect, and it is extraordinarily difficult to overcome simply by offering better economics to distributors.

The real innovation in OUSD is not the revenue-sharing model itself, but the composition of its consortium. USDG's founding partners were largely crypto-native: exchanges, trading firms, and infrastructure providers. OUSD's consortium includes global payment networks (Visa, Mastercard), major tech platforms (Stripe), the world's largest asset manager (BlackRock), and traditional financial infrastructure players. If these entities commit to integrating OUSD into their products — for cross-border payments, merchant settlement, or institutional treasury management — the distribution footprint would be fundamentally different from anything USDG achieved.

That is a big "if." The report leaves this question open, and it is the central uncertainty. Having a partner listed on a consortium is not the same as that partner actively deploying the stablecoin in its operations. The strategic question for observers is whether the consortium members have sufficient economic incentive to prioritize OUSD over existing stablecoins or traditional payment rails. Revenue sharing helps, but it may not be enough if the operational costs of switching are high or if the liquidity of OUSD is inferior.

## The Competitive Threat to Incumbents Is Real but Overstated in the Short Term

Circle's stock price reaction to the OUSD announcement is understandable. The OUSD model directly challenges Circle's approach of retaining reserve income, and Coinbase's involvement raises the possibility that the exchange may shift its promotional efforts away from USDC. Circle and Coinbase have a partnership to promote USDC on Coinbase's platform; if Coinbase renegotiates terms or promotes OUSD alongside USDC, that would be a meaningful competitive blow.

But the market may be overreacting. Incumbent stablecoins enjoy advantages that are not easily replicated:

First, liquidity begets liquidity. USDT and USDC are the most widely accepted stablecoins across exchanges, DeFi protocols, and OTC desks. A new entrant, even with a strong consortium, faces a chicken-and-egg problem: users will not hold OUSD until it is widely accepted, and platforms will not integrate it until there is user demand. Revenue sharing can help solve this on the distribution side, but it does not automatically create demand on the user side.

Second, the regulatory environment is evolving in ways that may benefit incumbents. The UK's FCA has finalized a comprehensive crypto regime, the EU is advancing the digital euro, and the US Senate is debating the CLARITY Act. Regulatory compliance is costly and complex. Incumbents have already invested heavily in compliance infrastructure. New entrants, even well-funded consortiums, must build this capability from scratch — or rely on partners, which introduces its own risks.

Third, the report notes that stablecoin market cap has grown only 25% in the past year. This suggests that the overall market is not expanding rapidly enough to absorb a major new entrant without cannibalizing existing players. If OUSD gains share, it will likely come at the expense of USDC and perhaps USDT, not from net new adoption. That is a zero-sum game, and incumbents have significant resources to defend their positions.

The competitive dynamics are therefore more nuanced than a simple "new entrant disrupts incumbents" narrative. The more likely scenario is a period of intensified competition that benefits users through lower fees and better services, but does not fundamentally reshape the market unless OUSD achieves the distribution breakthrough that USDG could not.

## Adoption Is the Only Metric That Matters, and It Remains the Open Question

The report's most important insight is that adoption is the parameter that will determine the outcome, not innovation or competition per se. If OUSD succeeds in driving broader adoption of stablecoin infrastructure as a payment rail for businesses and consumers, then all stablecoin players may benefit. The report uses the phrase "all ships may rise," and it is an apt metaphor.

This is not just wishful thinking. The involvement of Visa, Mastercard, and Stripe in the consortium suggests that OUSD is designed for payment use cases, not crypto trading. If these companies integrate OUSD into their merchant networks, the stablecoin could become a vehicle for cross-border remittances, B2B payments, and e-commerce settlement — use cases that have been promised by the crypto industry for years but have not materialized at scale.

The report also raises the possibility that OUSD could accelerate progress on project Qivalis, the initiative among 37 financial institutions to develop a EUR-denominated stablecoin settlement asset. If the consortium model proves effective for USD-denominated stablecoins, it could be replicated for euro-denominated ones, potentially creating a network of regulated, fiat-backed digital currencies that operate across borders.

But there are reasons for skepticism. The report notes that stablecoins are still mostly used for trading crypto currencies. Changing this will require not just a new stablecoin, but a fundamental shift in user behavior, merchant acceptance, and regulatory clarity. The consortium's partners may have distribution power, but they cannot force consumers and businesses to adopt a new payment method if the existing ones work well enough.

Moreover, the regulatory landscape is not uniformly supportive. The Bank of England has softened its stablecoin proposals, removing holding limits but introducing a temporary cap of GBP 40 billion per systemic stablecoin. The US Senate's timeline for crypto legislation is tightening. The EU is advancing the digital euro, which could compete with private stablecoins. Regulatory fragmentation remains a barrier to global adoption.

The open question, then, is whether OUSD can catalyze a shift from stablecoins as a crypto trading tool to stablecoins as a mainstream payment infrastructure. The report does not answer this question, and it cannot — the data does not exist yet. But the strategic implication for readers is clear: the next 12 to 18 months will be decisive. If OUSD achieves meaningful adoption in payment flows, the stablecoin market will look very different. If it does not, the consortium model will be remembered as an interesting experiment that failed to overcome network effects.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Stablecoin Landscape

For readers who must make strategic decisions — whether as investors, treasury managers, product leaders, or regulators — the OUSD announcement creates a need for structured analysis. The following framework is designed to help evaluate the implications for your specific context.

**Step 1: Assess your exposure to stablecoin infrastructure.** Are you a user of stablecoins for trading, a business that accepts stablecoin payments, or a financial institution considering issuing or distributing stablecoins? Your answer determines which parameters matter most.

**Step 2: Evaluate the distribution thesis.** If you are considering integrating OUSD into your products, ask: does the consortium's partner list translate into actual distribution commitments? Are Visa, Mastercard, and Stripe committed to using OUSD in their networks, or are they merely consortium members? The difference between a passive member and an active distributor is the difference between USDG's outcome and OUSD's potential.

**Step 3: Monitor regulatory alignment.** Stablecoin regulation is advancing unevenly across jurisdictions. The UK's FCA regime, the EU's digital euro, and the US CLARITY Act will all shape the competitive landscape. A stablecoin that is compliant in one jurisdiction may not be in another. OUSD's consortium structure may give it advantages in navigating multiple regulatory regimes, but it also introduces coordination costs.

**Step 4: Analyze the network effects.** The incumbents' advantage is not their business model; it is their network. Assess whether OUSD can replicate USDT and USDC's liquidity and acceptance. This is not a question of technology or economics — it is a question of adoption velocity. If OUSD achieves critical mass in payment flows, the network effects will shift. If it remains a niche product for consortium partners, it will not.

**Step 5: Prepare for multiple scenarios.** The range of outcomes is wide. In the optimistic scenario, OUSD drives mainstream adoption, all stablecoin players benefit, and the market grows to multi-trillion dollars. In the pessimistic scenario, OUSD fails to gain traction, incumbents maintain their dominance, and the consortium model is discredited. In the most likely scenario, OUSD achieves moderate success in specific use cases but does not fundamentally reshape the market in the near term. Your strategy should be robust across these scenarios.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer — and Why That Matters

The report is rigorous in its analysis of what is known: the business model differences, the competitive dynamics, and the regulatory context. But it leaves several questions unresolved, and these are precisely the questions that will determine the outcome.

First, the report does not quantify the distribution commitments of OUSD's consortium partners. Having 140+ partners listed is impressive, but how many have made binding commitments to integrate OUSD into their products? How much volume can they realistically route through the stablecoin? Without this data, the distribution thesis remains theoretical.

Second, the report does not address the governance of the consortium. Who controls the reserve assets? Who decides on changes to the business model? How are disputes resolved? These questions matter because the consortium model introduces principal-agent problems that single-issuer models avoid. If partners have conflicting incentives, the consortium may struggle to make strategic decisions.

Third, the report does not analyze the demand side. Revenue sharing may incentivize distributors, but it does not create end-user demand. Why would a consumer or business choose to hold OUSD instead of USDT, USDC, or fiat currency? The answer must be more compelling than "your payment processor prefers it."

Fourth, the report does not model the impact of regulatory divergence. The UK, EU, and US are moving in different directions on stablecoin regulation. A stablecoin that is compliant in one jurisdiction may face restrictions in another. OUSD's global consortium structure may help, but it also creates exposure to regulatory arbitrage and fragmentation.

These open questions are not weaknesses of the report; they are honest acknowledgments of uncertainty. For readers who want to form a complete picture, the report provides the analytical framework, but the data to answer these questions will only emerge over time.

## The Strategic Bottom Line

Open USD is not a disruptor. It is a test — a test of whether distribution beats issuance in the stablecoin market, and a test of whether stablecoins can break out of crypto trading into mainstream payments. The answer is not yet known, but the stakes are high.

For incumbents like Tether and Circle, the threat is real but manageable in the near term. Network effects, liquidity, and regulatory compliance provide significant moats. But complacency would be a mistake. If OUSD succeeds in driving adoption, the competitive landscape will shift, and incumbents that have relied on reserve income will need to adapt.

For consortium participants, the opportunity is significant but not guaranteed. The revenue-sharing model aligns incentives, but it does not guarantee execution. Success will depend on the willingness of partners to invest in integration, marketing, and user education — activities that are costly and uncertain in their returns.

For the broader market, the outcome of this test will shape the trajectory of stablecoin adoption for years to come. If OUSD succeeds, it will validate the consortium model and accelerate institutional adoption. If it fails, it will reinforce the dominance of incumbents and delay the mainstreaming of stablecoin infrastructure.

The next 18 months will provide the answer. Until then, the most prudent stance is to watch closely, analyze carefully, and prepare for multiple outcomes.

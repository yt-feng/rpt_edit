# US Economic Perspectives Warsh at Sintra: 3 takeaways

## We see three takeaways from Chair Warsh's comments at Sintra

One, Chair Warsh said that inflation risks have receded since the June FOMC meeting. Two, he reiterated his belief in the potentially profound effects of Artificial Intelligence and that the related expanding supply side has "huge implications" for monetary policy. In other words, like in his June post-FOMC meeting press conference, he has not distanced himself from his writing and comments last year about the topics. Third, he said names of people on the task forces could be released as early as next week (or this week it turns out).

Chair Warsh was joined on the panel by Andrew Bailey, Governor, Bank of England, Christine Lagarde, President, European Central Bank, and Tiff Macklem, Governor, Bank of Canada. The panel was moderated by CNBC Anchor, Sara Eisen. The wide ranging discussion covered central banking, inflation, and central bank balance sheets. They also noted different starting points for their economies and some different considerations for the policy outlook.

The central bankers seemed to find common cause on avoiding forward guidance, something Chair Warsh emphasized. Though Lagarde suggested what they wanted to provide was "framework" guidance. Bailey noted the problem of getting locked into prior statements. Lagarde said the hope is markets can understand how the central bank would react to new information, understand the decision-making framework, without having to be guided into each policy meeting.

Chair Warsh retained many of his recent themes. He continued to use his "family fight" metaphor. With a reference to Alexis de Tocqueville, the references to pre-1900 thinkers continued too. "We have a task force for that," he said, more than once. He also reiterated the FOMC commitment to price stability, more than once too.

## Warsh: inflation risks have "come down"

His comments started off trying to avoid forward guidance. Asked about whether a rate hike would be considered at the July meeting, Warsh demurred, leaving the sentiment that July is a live meeting, though without forward looking implications, he often sounds like every meeting is a live meeting for a policy decision. However, he pointed out that over the last four weeks, which is since the June FOMC meeting, he said inflation expectations have come down, and inflation risks have come down. "Expectations of inflation over the first four weeks of this period, they've come down. Inflation risks have come down," he said. However bad the outlook looked at the June meeting, presumably the inflation outlook appears better now.

## Expanding supply side could have "huge" implications for monetary policy

## Data dependence? But what data?

With the apparent lack of forward guidance many have been noting that this potentially raises the importance of the data in the Fed's reaction function. However, in comments relating to the data today Chair Warsh did not seem very data dependent and seemed to downplay the data that the Fed currently have at hand. In the post-FOMC press conference he also downplayed the incoming data, and abstracted to note "trends." He had previously been critical of data dependence, arguing that backward looking data can lead to policy being too backward looking too.

Also, in reiterating the importance of price stability Warsh stated that "We're going to deliver price stability in the US. That's what this committee has signed up to do. And our objective is to do that. The tactics, the strategy and the rest that's still to come." implying that the plan to deliver price stability is dependent not on today's data but potentially on the outcome of the task force findings.

Asked about his favorite economic indicator he stated that he hopes his data task force will provide new data, we just need to wait 9-12 months: "with the data project, the Data task force, my hope, my aspiration is that 9 to 12 months from now, we're going to be using new technologies to understand what's happening in the real economy. In a contemporaneous, real time way that positions us as central makers to make better decisions that we're no longer going to have to rely solely on data that we get from government agencies with mismeasurement problems, that have surveys that are no longer relevant, that every business we know that are leading in our country are using new data sources to make better decisions. My favorite data is upon us, and if we do our jobs, we'll be here a year from now and we'll say we've discovered data that helps us make better decisions, and we live up to our promises. We strengthen our credibility, and politics stays at bay."

On his least favorite economic indicator he stated: "Monetary policy works with long and variable lags, as we know. And many of these indicators are echoes of history. We need indicators that tell us what things are when we look out our window today. So when we make judgments, when we next convene, they're as close to real time as possible." "Echoes of history" implies a downplaying of the data flow, to the extent any data point measures the prior months' activity.

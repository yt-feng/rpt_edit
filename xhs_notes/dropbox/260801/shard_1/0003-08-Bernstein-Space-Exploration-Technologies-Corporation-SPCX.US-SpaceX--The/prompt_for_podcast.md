# SpaceX: The debates - Investor views and controversies as we head toward the first earnings report

Since SpaceX's IPO on 12 June 2026, we have been on the road speaking with investors about the stock's long-term opportunity, following our recent initiation, "Space Exploration Technologies Corporation: Doing Time in the Universal Mind - Initiate at Outperform; Target Price \$239". At the same time, we have seen high volatility in the stock, with a sharp decline since its peak after the IPO. Here, we discuss the questions raised by investors regarding the fundamentals for the stock - fundamentals centered on the ability of SpaceX to launch thousands of satellites into orbit to form a data center constellation.

There are four topics that we see as central to the success of the SpaceX valuation. Most important is achieving rapid reuse of Starship. The other three are about having sufficient semiconductor capacity, moving through regulatory processes, and that the need for compute capacity to remain high. Three topics that are legitimate areas of debate, but less relevant to the value proposition of SpaceX are China as a competitive threat, attractiveness of global Starlink broadband, and the feasibility of the company's direct to device mobile plan. On this last topic, we also have skepticism regarding the potential of mobile. Lastly, we highlight four topics where we have seen pushback on the technical feasibility of orbital data centers - none of which we see as material issues. Those are about the ability to cool data center satellites, the source of solar energy, avoiding damage from micro-meteorite strikes, and latency related to space-based data centers.

SpaceX will report its first set of results on August 4 $^{th}$ after the market close. We believe the quarterly results should not matter. What will be important is the level of confidence projected by management regarding the company's growth path. Investors should look beyond short term stock movements as we view the case for a multi-trillion dollar valuation is about "if" not "when", for orbital data center plans.

## DETAILS

Following our initiation investors have challenged us on almost every aspect of the story – from the feasibility of launching from multiple sites across the US, to the practicality of cooling orbital data centers. In this note, we distill 10 key insights from those client conversation.

## THE TOPICS THAT MATTER MOST - MAKE OR BREAK ON THE GROWTH MODEL

## 1. Reusability of Starship rockets - The most critical issue - Will the heat shield hold up?

We see the key issue for the SpaceX value proposition as reuse of the Starship rocket. This topic has been an important issue for many investors, as we believe it should be. Our model has roughly 3,600 Starship launches in 2031 (the company's expectations are much higher), which can only be done with a fully reusable rocket (both stages). So far, the company's expectations has landed Starship V2 boosters on the launchpad, but not yet V3 boosters. Launch 13, which was completed on July $20^{\text{th}}$ , was largely successful, although some engines failed to light on the booster ahead of splashdown. Our assumption is that booster landings with reuse will be achieved as it was with the V2 rockets.

The important success on Launch 13 was the successful recovery of the Starship itself (second stage) with the heat shield much more intact than prior launches. We await the company's assessment of the damage to the Starship heat shield. If there is minimal damage, then the company is moving close to reuse. After Launch 13, Elon Musk said that SpaceX may attempt to do a "chopsticks" catch of the second stage on Launch 14. Demonstrating catches of the booster (Super Heavy) and then the second stage (Starship) is the first step toward the critical achievement of rapid reuse. So far, we see the progress as good. But, there is a long way to go. Reaching our launch goal will still require at least daily launches. The frequency of launches will also be tied to the number of launchpads. Today there are two, with the addition of two more coming. The company is in negotiations with state governments to determine where the next five or six will be located.

## 2. Semiconductor availability - Will this limit growth?

The required semiconductor capacity for SpaceX's orbital data center buildout has been another area of investor pushback. We view this as a valid concern, but still see a credible path for SpaceX to secure sufficient semiconductor supply over time.

Under the assumption that a data center satellite will use a similar architecture, we can estimate how much semiconductor manufacturing capacity will be needed by the company. And the numbers are not small. Our model calls for \~20 GW of incremental orbital capacity to be deployed in 2031, which would require close to 170K satellites to be launched assuming \~120 kW per satellite (the current spec of the AI1), requiring \~2.8 million wafers of annual production capacity. At a fab size of \~50,000 wafer starts per month this would translate to \~5 dedicated semiconductor fabs solely serving SpaceX, probably costing somewhere north of \~\$160B to put in place over the next several years (Exhibit 1). This is a large number, though not necessarily impossible (especially as some will still likely be third-party) and compares reasonably favorably with current company Terafab investment plans (\~\$119B forecast as of this writing) though the timeframe to bring this amount of capacity online is ambitious especially as SpaceX has not done this before. If they cannot deliver on time it will put more pressure on them to secure large amounts of incremental capacity from existing foundry and memory players.

## 3. Will regulatory considerations be a problem?

We have heard a number of investors express skepticism about being able to accelerate the SpaceX vision and remain in compliance with regulatory authorities. The FAA must approve the flight path for each Starship launch. So far, the FAA has not cleared SpaceX for an orbital Starship flight. The hope is that Launch 14 may be able to go orbital. But, the FAA performs environmental assessments that consider the impact on airline fight paths, as well as potential environmental damage and

risks to wildlife, including sea life. The current Environmental Assessment has many strict considerations, with August 3 $^{rd}$ as the deadline for public input. But, this week the FAA announced that it would relax environmental restrictions because of the importance of enabling a higher cadence of space launches. The assessment related to air traffic would remain. While changes made should enable more Starship launches, it is not yet clear how the regulatory path will go when thousands of launches are under consideration and a potentially less supportive administration could be in place.

The FCC must approve satellite communications programs. A fast track process was recently approved. But, this will not apply the very large constellations proposed by SpaceX.. Recent filings include proposals for a \~100,000-satellite Starlink V3 constellation and, separately, a potential large-scale AI data center constellation (up to \~1 million units), neither of which has yet received full regulatory approval. Instead, SpaceX is also looking at incremental steps to build up Starlink's capacity.

There are many other regulatory questions that will need to be resolved. Each country will have its own regulatory environment for telecommunications. The issue of data sovereignty is another important topic. Many countries have rules that prevent data from being stored in other countries. But, the argument for orbital data centers is that data stored in space is not resident in any country and consequently has no issues regarding data sovereignty. But, it is not yet clear exactly how space as a location will ultimately be treated.

## 4. Volume of compute needed - Is AI compute a bubble?

The need for orbital data centers starts with the assumption that there is a massive need for compute capability. Elon Musk has suggested that SpaceX would eventually be launching 1 terawatt of compute into space each year. The plans we are working with now are far more modest. But, making the orbital data center program a success still depends on having a huge volume of compute power. To this day, we have never seen the world have excess compute. Possibly, the rush to add capacity to fuel AI models is too rapid. But, even if that is the case, there should still be value in having a lower cost solution for the compute capacity that is needed, relative to terrestrial capacity.

## LEGITIMATE QUESTIONS - ALTHOUGH THEY DO NOT CHANGE THE ULTIMATE VALUE PROPOSITION

## 5. Rising Chinese Competition

In our discussions with SpaceX management, China was identified as the most serious competitor. The country has tremendous resources that it is willing to use for achieving leadership in space. Nevertheless, China is still far behind SpaceX.

On July $10^{\text{th}}$ , China successfully landed the first-stage booster from a Long March 10B rocket launch. Since then, a lot of investors have questioned us around this rising competition and this remake of the Space race. We see China as the lead competitor for SpaceX. This launch and recovery comes roughly six months earlier than we expected. China is moving aggressively to place a research station on the moon and create large LEO satellite constellations. In December, China filed with the ITU to place more than 200,000 satellites in low earth orbit. Despite China's success in landing its Long March 10B, it is far behind SpaceX. This was the first successful landing of a large launch vehicle booster. It has yet to do the reuse of a booster. SpaceX has been relaunching boosters for nearly ten years with its Falcon 9 rocket. 165 launches were done last year, which also required a robust production line for second stages. To reach a high volume of launch, China will need to demonstrate relaunch capabilities and move to high rate production. Most importantly, Long March 10 is only first stage reuse, whereas Starship is intended to be full reuse (not yet demonstrated).

A key part of the Long March program is landing humans on the moon (official target 2030), leading to an eventual lunar research station. SpaceX is a major part of NASA's space program, which has a goal of a manned moon landing by the end of 2028. There is an emerging space race here, which will likely increase U.S. government support for SpaceX (along with others, such as Blue Origin and Boeing).

As discussed in our SpaceX initiation, we see no real competition in the near-term for SpaceX on either launch capability or satellite operations. But, given China's massive resources and long-standing goals, China must be considered a serious competitor over the long-term. It still appears to us that China lags SpaceX by something on the order of ten years, assuming SpaceX is successful in achieving its goals.

## 6. Starlink broadband penetration - Is there an issue here?

We are expecting rapid growth in Starlink's broadband businesses for consumer, enterprise, and government. The main source of pushback here has been among telecom-oriented investors who see terrestrial fiber as a better option for all but rural users. The most powerful application will be with users in locations that are not served by cable or fiber (where the Starlink share will likely be $100\%$ ), But, we expect there will be much broader adoption by suburban users. Even though there is more latency with Starlink (roughly 23ms round trip), this is not a problem even for most gaming applications. Surveys show that Starlink has the highest customer satisfaction rating of any consumer broadband service. In addition, Starlink has the advantage of having an inherently global network so that it can add subscribers in any country with essentially zero additional cost. This gives Starlink

exceptional flexibility in pricing. With its current 9,600 satellite constellation and planned capacity expansion by more than a factor of ten, no other provider is remotely close. Our forecast for global subscribers and ARPU is shown in Exhibit 3.

Starlink also has the advantage of supporting an enterprise and government customer base, which further spreads cost. The U.S. government invests in the defense version of Starlink (Starshield), while many other countries are adopting Starlink for monitor of remote regions, emergency services, and border control (e.g., UK, Australia, Ukraine, Japan, Netherlands, Poland). On the enterprise side, Starlink offers a global reach that other services lack, in addition to mobile applications where no competitor comes close to the performance (e.g., maritime, aviation). For broadband services, we expect high growth from this business that is already highly profitable.

An area where we have heard strong pushback on SpaceX plans is for direct-to-device mobile service. On this one, we agree.

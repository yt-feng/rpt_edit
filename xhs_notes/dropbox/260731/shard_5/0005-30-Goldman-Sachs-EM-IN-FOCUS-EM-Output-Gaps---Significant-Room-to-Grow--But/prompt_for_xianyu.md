## EM Output Gaps — Significant Room to Grow, But Mixed Picture

Measuring the output gap—the difference between actual and potential output—is difficult but it is impossible to assess the outlook for economic growth, monetary policy, and asset prices without forming some judgment on the degree of overall spare capacity in an economy.

In this EM in Focus, we update our production function output gap estimates for 22 emerging market economies. We have previously outlined our methodology in detail here. Briefly, our approach examines the fundamental drivers of potential output—capital, labour, and technology—to calculate labour and capital usage gaps and combines these estimates to derive an overall output gap.

Given the inherent uncertainty in estimating output gaps, our estimates should not be viewed as the definitive assessment of economic slack for any specific economy (especially as our unified methodology may not fully capture the unique features of individual nations). We therefore present our results alongside a range of other estimates, including those from international organisations such as the OECD and those derived from statistical filters.

## Since Our Last Update, Negative Gaps Have Widened Modestly in CEEMEA and LatAm, and Shifted into Positive Territory in EM Asia ex China

Exhibit 1 shows our regional output gap estimates for CEEMEA, EM Asia and Latin America; detailed estimates for each of the 22 EMs in our sample are provided in the Appendix. The results point to meaningful divergence across regions and economies, with a majority operating below potential but a number slightly above potential. Since our last update, output gaps have turned more negative in CEEMEA and China, while LatAm is operating close to potential in aggregate, and EM Asia ex-China has moved into positive territory.

CEEMEA: Most CEEMEA economies—particularly CEE and South Africa—have grown below potential since the post-pandemic rebound, opening up spare capacity. But the picture is mixed. In Türkiye and Russia, growth has slowed following earlier overheating, but both economies continue to operate slightly above capacity.

EM Asia: Supported by relatively strong growth, output gaps across EM Asia ex-China have generally moved in a more positive direction over the past year. However, in our assessment, much of this strength has been supply-driven, leaving output gaps only moderately positive. This is especially true for economies that have benefited from the semiconductor boom: because the strength is concentrated in a limited number of firms, it has not resulted in a broad-based tightening of labour market conditions. $^{1}$ Our estimated output gaps are only slightly positive in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Malaysia and negative in South Korea. Conversely, growth has slowed notably in the Philippines, unwinding earlier overheating, while Thailand and China

continue to operate below capacity.

Latin America: Latin America is operating close to potential in aggregate, with notable slack in Argentina—and, to a lesser extent, in Mexico and Chile—offset by above-potential output in Brazil. In Colombia, growth has picked up since 2024, and our estimates now point to a modest degree of overheating.

## Implications for Wage Growth and Inflation

We assess the implications of our output gap estimates for inflation and wage growth. In an overheating economy, stronger labour demand typically puts upward pressure on wages, while excess capacity, by weakening workers' bargaining power, tends to work in the opposite direction. Although such wage pressures had historically fed through to inflation, this relationship has become less stable over recent decades as supply chains have become more globalised and external factors have started to play a larger role in EM inflation dynamics. Across the EMs in our sample, our estimates point to a strong correlation between economic slack and the wage gap—defined as deviation of wage growth from target-consistent levels—with above-potential output associated with larger wage overshoots, and vice versa (Exhibit 3, LHS). However, consistent with cross-country evidence of flatter Phillips curves, we find a weaker, though still positive, relationship between output gaps and inflation gaps (Exhibit 3, RHS).

In Hungary and Czechia, where significant spare capacity exists, relatively strong wage growth largely reflects real wages catching up to their pre-pandemic trend and is less concerning for inflation given the openness of these economies and the dominance of external factors in driving their inflation. On the other hand, positive output gaps and tight labour markets in Colombia and Brazil have complicated disinflation, leading to a renewed hiking cycle in Colombia and more cautious monetary easing in Brazil. Wage growth also remains elevated in Türkiye and Russia despite the recent reduction of these countries' output gaps.

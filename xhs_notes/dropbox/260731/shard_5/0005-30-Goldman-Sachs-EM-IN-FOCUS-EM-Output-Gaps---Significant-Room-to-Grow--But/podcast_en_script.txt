EN_A: Hey, welcome back. Today we are digging into a report from a global investment bank, all about emerging market output gaps. So first things first, what exactly is an output gap and why should we care?
EN_B: Great question. An output gap is basically the difference between what an economy is actually producing and what it could produce at full capacity. Think of it like a speed limit. The gap tells you how much room an economy has before it starts overheating.
EN_A: So this is not just academic. It actually matters for real investment decisions.
EN_B: Absolutely. If you do not know how much spare capacity an economy has, you cannot really judge whether its growth will trigger inflation or whether its central bank will tighten or ease. So this report tries to measure that for 22 emerging market economies.
EN_A: Okay. So what is the big question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It is really about headroom. Which emerging markets still have room to grow without stoking inflation, and which ones are already running above their potential and need to be watched.
EN_A: And what is the headline conclusion?
EN_B: Most emerging markets are still operating at or below their potential. But there is a lot of divergence. Since the last update, output gaps have become more negative in Central and Eastern Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and China. Latin America is roughly at potential, and emerging Asia outside China has actually moved into positive territory.
EN_A: Positive territory. Does that mean those economies are overheating?
EN_B: Not necessarily. And this is one of the most important insights in the report. Take Taiwan, South Korea, Hong Kong and Malaysia. They have seen a huge semiconductor-led growth surge. But the report argues this is not broad-based overheating. The strength is concentrated in a small number of firms, so it has not really tightened the labor market across the board.
EN_A: So what does that mean in practice?
EN_B: It means if you just look at GDP growth, these economies look hot. But if you look at the output gap, they still have room. That matters because overheating usually means the central bank will tighten. Here that may not happen.
EN_A: So which economies are really below potential?
EN_B: The report names South Africa, Central Europe excluding Poland, South Korea, China, Chile and Argentina. These have significant spare capacity. They look best placed to deliver above-trend growth in the coming years without generating inflation.
EN_A: And on the flip side, which ones are above potential?
EN_B: Brazil is a key one. The report says Brazil is running above potential, which is complicating its inflation picture. Turkey and Russia are also still slightly above capacity, even though their growth has slowed. So inflationary pressures remain.
EN_A: Here is a question. Is the link between output gaps and inflation as straightforward as the textbooks say?
EN_B: That is actually one of the most interesting parts of the report. It finds a strong correlation between output gaps and wage growth. But the link to inflation is much weaker.
EN_A: Why is that?
EN_B: Because of globalization and external factors. Supply chains are more integrated now, so external prices play a bigger role in emerging market inflation. The report gives the example of Hungary and the Czech Republic. They have spare capacity, but wage growth is still decent. That is mostly about real wages catching up to pre-pandemic trends. Inflation there is driven more by external factors, so it is less of a concern.
EN_A: And what about the other side? Brazil and Colombia?
EN_B: They have positive output gaps and tight labor markets. That makes disinflation harder. Colombia has actually started hiking rates again, and Brazil is being more cautious with easing. That is the real-world impact of an output gap.
EN_A: So what does this report not fully answer?
EN_B: A few things. For one, the methodology is uniform across all 22 economies, so it might miss country-specific nuances. The report itself says its estimates should be viewed alongside other measures, like those from the OECD, not as the final word. And output gap estimation is inherently uncertain, especially in real time.
EN_A: I also noticed the report highlights a big discrepancy for Taiwan. Why is that?
EN_B: Right. Traditional methods use statistical filters, and they see the sharp GDP acceleration driven by semiconductors as evidence of broad overheating. But this report argues that is misleading, because the growth is coming from a few companies with limited spillover to the wider economy. So its estimate of Taiwan's output gap is much smaller.

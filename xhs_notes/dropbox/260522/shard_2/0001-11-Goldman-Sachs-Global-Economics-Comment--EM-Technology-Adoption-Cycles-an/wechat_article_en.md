# The Emerging Market AI Productivity Upside Is Larger Than Most Models Assume

The consensus forecast among global economists has been clear: artificial intelligence will boost emerging market productivity by roughly 4% annually over the next decade. That is a meaningful uplift, but it is less than half the 10% or more expected for developed economies. The rationale appears straightforward on the surface. Emerging markets have historically adopted new technologies more slowly. Their workforces are concentrated in production jobs that are less exposed to automation. The gap seems structural, inevitable, and baked into baseline projections.

That consensus is now worth challenging. A detailed re-examination of technology adoption patterns, current AI diffusion data, and the unique characteristics of digital technologies suggests the conventional wisdom may be significantly underestimating the EM opportunity. The question is not whether EMs will benefit less than DMs, but by how much less. And the answer may be far narrower than currently priced into long-term growth forecasts.

The argument for a larger EM upside rests on three interconnected shifts that are not fully captured in standard macroeconomic models. First, the adoption lag between developed and emerging economies is compressing far faster than historical precedent would suggest. Second, AI is not simply another automation technology; its digital and cloud-based architecture enables a phenomenon that previous industrial technologies could not: technological leapfrogging. Third, the benefits of AI in EMs may extend well beyond workplace automation into areas like healthcare that have outsized effects on labor supply and human capital.

If these dynamics play out as the early evidence suggests, the 4% productivity boost currently assumed for EMs could prove conservative by a wide margin. The implications for global growth, capital allocation, and investment strategy are substantial.

## Emerging Market AI Adoption Is Already Running Ahead of Historical Technology Cycles

The most direct argument for revising EM productivity forecasts upward comes from the adoption data itself. According to survey data from a major technology platform, EM AI adoption rates currently average 22%, compared to 37% in developed markets. That gap of roughly 15 percentage points is far smaller than what historical technology adoption curves would predict at this stage of the cycle. More importantly, global AI adoption is rising at approximately 5 percentage points per year. At that pace, the current data implies an EM adoption lag of roughly three years, not the five years embedded in most baseline forecasts.

This compression is not an anomaly. It reflects a structural shift in how technology diffuses across borders in the digital era. For technologies that require significant physical infrastructure, such as electricity grids or road networks, adoption lags have historically been measured in decades. For digital technologies, the lag has been shrinking with each successive cycle. Cloud services adoption provides the clearest parallel. OECD data shows that 41% of EM businesses now purchase cloud services, only modestly below the 48% rate in developed economies. Among large firms, the gap nearly disappears entirely.

The pattern is consistent across multiple measures. Not only are EM firms adopting cloud services at rates close to their DM counterparts, they are also deepening their usage at a comparable pace. The number of cloud service use cases per adopting firm has been rising steadily, suggesting that integration into business processes is proceeding in parallel, not sequentially.

What this means for AI is straightforward. The current generation of AI tools is fundamentally a cloud-based digital technology. It does not require laying cable, building factories, or retooling physical plants. It requires internet access, digital literacy, and organizational willingness to experiment. These are precisely the conditions where EM adoption gaps have been narrowing most rapidly. The three-year lag implied by current data is not an outlier; it is the continuation of a trend that has been visible for at least a decade.

## Digital Technologies Enable a Different Kind of Productivity Leap That Benefits EMs Disproportionately

The second reason the consensus forecast may be too conservative is more subtle but potentially more consequential. Standard productivity models assume that technology adoption follows a predictable path: firms gradually incorporate new tools into existing processes, realizing incremental efficiency gains over time. This framework works well for automation technologies that substitute for specific tasks. It works less well for technologies that enable entirely new ways of organizing production.

AI belongs to the latter category, and the evidence suggests that EMs may benefit disproportionately from this characteristic. A detailed firm-level dataset from the World Bank reveals a striking pattern. When a business function is both digital in nature and novel, EM firms are far more likely to adopt the most advanced available technology rather than an intermediate version. In digital business functions, 29% of EM firms use frontier technology, compared to 16.5% for non-digital functions. The pattern reverses for non-digital functions, where only 11% of EM firms use frontier technology.

This is technological leapfrogging in action. EM firms are not condemned to follow the same incremental adoption path that DM firms took. When a technology is digital and does not require replacing an existing installed base, EM firms can jump directly to the frontier. The absence of legacy systems, which is typically viewed as a disadvantage, becomes an advantage in the context of digital transformation.

AI amplifies this dynamic because its most valuable applications are often in areas where EMs have the most ground to cover. Consider healthcare, which is the most developed example to date. Early academic studies of AI deployment in EM healthcare settings have documented remarkable outcomes. One study reported a 75% reduction in stillbirth and early neonatal deaths following AI adoption. Another found a 64% improvement in cervical precancer screening accuracy. Malaria microscopy detection rates improved by 36%. Maternal mortality declined by 27%.

These are not marginal improvements. They represent discrete jumps in effectiveness that would take decades to achieve through conventional investment in training, infrastructure, and personnel. The mechanism is clear: AI tools can compress years of learning and experience into software that can be deployed at near-zero marginal cost. An AI system trained on millions of medical images can outperform a human radiologist with years of experience, and it can be deployed in a rural clinic with the same effectiveness as a urban teaching hospital.

The macroeconomic implications are substantial. Poor health is a major drag on EM economic output, reducing labor supply through sickness, premature death, and informal caregiving. If the early healthcare results scale, a proportional reduction in the current EM-DM healthcare gap would imply a 5% cumulative uplift to EM labor supply. That is a benefit that falls entirely outside the standard task-automation framework used in most productivity models.

## The Nature of EM Labor Markets Creates Both Constraints and Underappreciated Opportunities

The most common objection to a more optimistic EM AI outlook is structural. EM workforces are more concentrated in production and manual labor roles that have lower direct exposure to AI-driven automation. This is factually correct. The share of white-collar workers in most EMs is significantly lower than in DMs, and the occupations most directly affected by generative AI are concentrated in white-collar functions.

But this objection conflates two distinct questions. The first question is how much AI will automate existing tasks. The second question is how much AI will enable new capabilities. The standard framework focuses almost exclusively on the first. The leapfrogging argument addresses the second.

For EM firms, the most transformative AI applications may not be in automating what workers currently do. They may be in enabling workers to do things they currently cannot. A farmer in rural India does not need AI to automate crop monitoring; they need AI to forecast weather patterns, optimize irrigation schedules, and access market prices. A small manufacturer in Vietnam does not need AI to replace assembly line workers; they need AI to optimize supply chains, improve quality control, and access global customers.

These are capability-enhancing applications, not task-automation applications. They are also applications where EM firms have significant catching up to do, which means the potential for productivity gains is correspondingly larger. The standard framework underestimates this channel because it is designed to measure substitution, not augmentation.

There is also a specific sectoral angle that deserves attention. While most EM workforces are concentrated in production, several large EMs have significant exposure to knowledge-intensive services. India's IT sector employs roughly 1% of the workforce. The Philippines has approximately 4% of jobs in call centers and business process outsourcing. These sectors are directly exposed to AI, and the direction of impact is not predetermined. AI could displace some of these jobs, but it could also make Indian IT firms and Philippine BPO operators more competitive by reducing costs and expanding the range of services they can offer.

## What the Report Does Not Yet Answer: The Crucial Unknowns That Will Determine the Outcome

For all the compelling evidence of upside, the analysis leaves several critical questions unresolved. These are not weaknesses in the framework; they are genuine uncertainties that will determine whether the optimistic scenario materializes.

The first unknown is whether the early healthcare results will scale. The documented improvements are impressive, but they come from controlled academic studies with specific implementations. Scaling these results to national healthcare systems requires solving problems that AI alone cannot address: regulatory approval, integration with existing workflows, training of healthcare workers, and maintenance of digital infrastructure. The 5% labor supply uplift is illustrative, not predictive.

The second unknown is the political economy of AI adoption in EMs. Faster adoption could trigger labor market disruptions that generate political backlash. If AI displaces workers in formal sectors faster than they can be absorbed into new roles, the resulting social costs could slow adoption or redirect its trajectory. EMs have fewer social safety nets than DMs, which means the adjustment costs could be more concentrated and politically salient.

The third unknown is the distribution of benefits across EMs. The analysis treats EMs as a group, but the variation within the category is enormous. China and India are not comparable to smaller EMs in terms of digital infrastructure, human capital, or institutional capacity. The compression of adoption lags may hold for the largest EMs while smaller or less connected economies continue to lag significantly.

The fourth unknown is the second-order effects on global value chains. If AI enables EM firms to leapfrog in digital capabilities, it could accelerate the ongoing shift in global production networks. But it could also create new forms of dependency if EM firms become reliant on AI platforms developed and controlled by DM-based technology companies. The balance between empowerment and dependency will shape the long-term outcome.

These unknowns do not invalidate the upside case, but they should temper expectations about timing and magnitude. The optimistic scenario is plausible. It is not guaranteed.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position for the EM AI Opportunity

For investors and strategists, the analysis suggests a framework for evaluating EM AI exposure that goes beyond simple GDP forecasts. Four dimensions matter.

First, assess digital readiness. The leapfrogging argument depends on the existence of digital infrastructure, digital literacy, and regulatory environments that support technology adoption. EMs that score high on these dimensions are better positioned to realize the upside. This includes countries with high mobile penetration, supportive regulatory frameworks for digital services, and a workforce with basic digital skills.

Second, identify sectors with leapfrogging potential. The most compelling opportunities are in sectors where EMs have large gaps relative to the frontier and where AI solutions can be deployed without requiring complementary physical infrastructure. Healthcare is the clearest example. Education, financial services, and agricultural extension services are others. Sectors where AI deployment requires significant complementary investment in physical capital or regulatory reform will see slower adoption.

Third, distinguish between automation exposure and capability enhancement. The standard framework overweights automation risk and underweights capability enhancement. For EM equities, companies that use AI to expand their addressable markets or improve their competitive positioning are more interesting than companies that simply automate existing tasks. The former creates growth; the latter creates cost savings that may be competed away.

Fourth, monitor the adoption data. The key variable to track is not the absolute level of AI adoption but the rate of change in the adoption gap between EMs and DMs. If the gap continues to narrow at the pace implied by current data, the upside scenario becomes increasingly likely. If the gap stabilizes or widens, the consensus forecast will prove more accurate.

## The Full Picture Requires Granular Data That Cannot Be Summarized in a Single Article

The argument for a larger EM AI productivity boost is analytically sound and supported by early evidence. Adoption lags are compressing. Leapfrogging is real. The benefits extend beyond automation. But the difference between a directional argument and an investable thesis lies in the details. Which EMs are leading the adoption curve? Which sectors show the strongest leapfrogging patterns? How do the healthcare results translate into macroeconomic estimates? What do the cloud services data tell us about the trajectory of AI adoption specifically?

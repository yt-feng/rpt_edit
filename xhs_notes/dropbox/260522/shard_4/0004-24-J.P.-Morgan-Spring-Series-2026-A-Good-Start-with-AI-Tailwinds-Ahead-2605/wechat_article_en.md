# The Security Market Has Reached an Inflection Point: Platform Vendors, Not Point Solutions, Will Capture the AI-Security Budget

The most transformative technology shift of our lifetimes comes with a larger attack surface. This is not a prediction about the future; it is a description of the present. Enterprise security has entered a phase where the fundamental economics of offense have collapsed, and the defensive response must be structural, not incremental. The report from a leading global investment bank makes a clear argument: the window for legacy security architectures has closed, and platform vendors with deep telemetry, proprietary data, and decades of domain expertise are the only credible answer to threats that now move faster than human teams can track.

The thesis is straightforward but its implications are not. The report identifies that mean time to exploit has turned negative. Adversaries are now weaponizing vulnerabilities before patches exist. This is not a temporary acceleration; it is a permanent shift in the operational environment. The traditional model of detect, analyze, and respond no longer works when the response window has collapsed to minutes, and in some cases, seconds. The report argues that platform vendors, those that have already invested in AI, proprietary data, and cross-domain telemetry, are best positioned to address these emerging threats. But the strategic question for investors and enterprise buyers alike is more nuanced: which platform vendors will actually capture the budget, and which will be left defending legacy architectures that cannot adapt?

The report provides a framework for answering this question, but it also leaves several critical unknowns open. The most important of these is whether the current generation of security platforms can scale their defensive AI fast enough to match the offensive capabilities that are now proliferating through open-source models. The report notes that Anthropic's Glasswing coalition and CrowdStrike's Quiltworks illustrate the critical role security platforms will play, but it does not fully answer whether the defensive AI arms race will consolidate power among a few winners or fragment into a landscape of specialized tools. The answer to that question will determine which companies in the coverage list become long-term compounders and which become acquisition targets or legacy players.

## The Collapse of Offensive Economics Has Made Autonomous Defense a Strategic Necessity, Not a Luxury

The report presents a stark cost comparison that every enterprise buyer and investor needs to internalize. Scanning an entire operating system for security flaws now costs less than $50 when performed by AI. Developing a complete attack that gains full control of a computer costs under $1,000 over half a day, compared to $500,000 to $2 million on the exploit black market. Running 1,000 automated security scans costs under $20,000, a task that previously required dedicated research teams. These are not theoretical projections; they are documented costs from real-world operations.

The implication is that the barrier to sophisticated offensive operations is no longer technical skill. It is access to a capable model and compute. The report cites a real-world attack from September 2025 where a Chinese state-sponsored group used Claude models to autonomously conduct cyber espionage against approximately 30 entities, with AI performing 80-90% of tactical operations independently. The models used in that attack were less capable than currently publicly available open-source models. This means the threat environment is not just accelerating; it is fundamentally different in kind from what existed before.

For enterprise security teams, this creates an impossible math problem. Human-operated security teams cannot match the speed of AI-enabled discovery and exploitation. The report notes that industry leaders including CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, and SentinelOne have already begun deploying autonomous defensive agents in production. This is not an experimental initiative; it is a survival response. The long-term outcome, if discovery is paired with accelerated remediation, could be more secure infrastructure. But the transition period will be brutal for organizations that rely on legacy tools and manual processes.

The strategic insight here is that the cost collapse in offensive capabilities creates a corresponding urgency for defensive automation. Enterprises that delay investing in autonomous defensive platforms are not just falling behind; they are exposing themselves to risks that are no longer theoretical. The report estimates that security budgets can account for over 10% of total IT spend, and applying that proportion to expected AI infrastructure spending suggests billions of dollars in new security spending directed at AI systems. The vendors that capture this budget will be those that can demonstrate autonomous defensive capabilities, not just point solutions for specific vulnerabilities.

## Platform Vendors with Proprietary Telemetry and Domain Expertise Have an Evolutionary Competitive Advantage Over Point Solution Providers

The report makes a critical distinction between vendors that have access to deep, cross-domain telemetry and those that do not. Platform vendors like CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks have decades of data across endpoint, network, identity, and data estates. This data is not just a historical record; it is the training ground for defensive AI models that can detect and respond to novel threats in real time. The report notes that these vendors have been leveraging AI with decades of domain expertise, proprietary data, and deep telemetry to help protect customers.

This is not a feature advantage; it is a structural moat. A point solution that scans for specific vulnerability types cannot compete with a platform that has visibility across the entire enterprise attack surface. The report highlights that partnerships with foundation model vendors offer a lens into leadership. Anthropic's Glasswing, OpenAI's Trusted Access for Cyber, and CrowdStrike's Quiltworks illustrate the critical role security platforms will have to address the incremental threats foundation models introduce. Those leading the partnerships with insight from foundation model vendors have an evolutionary competitive advantage.

The strategic implication is that the security market is consolidating around a small number of platform vendors that can offer end-to-end protection across the AI attack surface. The report identifies CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks as obvious beneficiaries, but it also notes that others within coverage, including Check Point, Zscaler, Netskope, and others, have been actively involved with foundation model companies. Some may be limited by agreements they have, but the direction is clear: platform vendors that can integrate AI-native security across the full stack will capture the majority of new security spending.

The question that the report leaves open is whether this consolidation will accelerate or whether new entrants can carve out defensible niches. The report's overweight list includes several mid-cap and small-cap names, suggesting that the market has not fully priced in the opportunity for specialized platform vendors that focus on specific attack surfaces like identity, cloud security, or vulnerability management. But the trend toward platform consolidation is powerful, and investors need to assess which companies have the data, the partnerships, and the execution capability to become essential infrastructure rather than optional tools.

## The AI Attack Surface Introduces Entirely New Vulnerability Classes That Legacy Security Tools Cannot Address

The report catalogs a set of AI-specific attack vectors that did not exist in the pre-AI enterprise. Prompt injection, data and model poisoning, AI supply chain compromise, excessive agent authority, shadow AI, AI-generated code vulnerabilities, sensitive data exposure, deepfakes and synthetic identity, and non-human identity sprawl are not incremental additions to the threat landscape. They represent entirely new categories of risk that require fundamentally different defensive approaches.

The critical insight from the report is that these surfaces are not yet being attacked by serious adversaries at scale. This creates a security risk overhang where the attack surface grows faster than defenders can empirically prioritize it. Organizations need dialable controls that are lightweight today but can be tightened as real threats materialize. This is a strategic challenge for enterprise buyers because it requires investing in capabilities that may not have immediate ROI but will become essential as the threat environment matures.

The report notes that AI-generated code vulnerabilities are particularly concerning because AI coding assistants produce insecure or exploitable code at production scale, and developers often trust it without adequate review. This is not a theoretical risk; it is a structural feature of how AI-assisted development works. The report also highlights that non-human identity sprawl creates a surge of machine identities that operate continuously without human oversight, creating persistent and ungoverned access paths. These are not problems that can be solved with traditional identity and access management tools.

The strategic implication for investors is that the security budget is not just growing; it is shifting toward entirely new categories of spending. Vendors that can address the AI-specific attack surface, whether through agentic security controls, identity governance for machine identities, or code security for AI-generated software, will capture budget that did not exist three years ago. The report's overweight list reflects this thesis, with names like CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Zscaler, and others that are well-positioned to address the new attack surface.

## The Proliferation of Open-Source Offensive Capabilities Means the Current Access Constraints Are Temporary, Creating a Window for Defensive Investment

The report provides a detailed analysis of Anthropic's Mythos Preview, which represents a step change in autonomous cybersecurity. Mythos outperforms all publicly available models across every security and coding benchmark tested. It discovered critical vulnerabilities that survived decades of automated testing. It autonomously develops complete working attacks, collapsing what previously required two different, expensive skillsets into a single automated process.

But the report also notes that current access constraints are temporary. Mythos is significantly larger than publicly available models with enterprise-class pricing, limiting broad deployment today. Both cost and compute are expected to decline as future models gain efficiency. More importantly, open-source models are approaching the same performance threshold. The report cites Kimi K2.6, an open-source Chinese model, which now matches the coding performance level at which real-world exploit capability was demonstrated. Multiple other models are approaching the same threshold. Anthropic's own offensive cyber lead estimates broad availability within months, not years.

The strategic implication is that the window for defensive investment is finite. Enterprises that invest in autonomous defensive platforms now can gain a temporary advantage, but the underlying offensive capability will become widely available. The report's analysis of Mythos suggests that the model's capabilities are so powerful that Anthropic deliberately reduced cyber capability in its public release. Open-source models do not have these constraints. This means the defensive arms race will escalate rapidly, and the winners will be those that can integrate defensive AI into their platforms at scale.

The report leaves open the question of how long the defensive advantage will last. If offensive capabilities continue to improve faster than defensive capabilities, the security industry will face a structural challenge. But if platform vendors can leverage their proprietary data and domain expertise to build defensive AI that stays ahead of open-source offensive models, the current leaders will extend their moats. The answer to this question will determine whether the security market becomes a winner-take-most market or remains fragmented.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: Whether Defensive AI Can Scale Faster Than Offensive Proliferation

The report provides a compelling framework for understanding the current security landscape, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important of these is whether the defensive AI capabilities of platform vendors can scale fast enough to match the offensive capabilities that are proliferating through open-source models. The report documents that Mythos discovered vulnerabilities that survived decades of automated testing, but it does not provide a clear timeline for when defensive AI will reach comparable capability.

The report also does not fully address the question of whether the current generation of security platforms can adapt to the AI-specific attack surface. The report identifies eight new vulnerability classes introduced by AI, but it does not provide a detailed assessment of which vendors are best positioned to address each category. The report's overweight list includes several names that are strong in traditional security domains, but the transition to AI-native security may require different capabilities.

Another open question is whether the partnership model, as exemplified by Glasswing and Quiltworks, will scale. The report notes that Anthropic has restricted Mythos access to 52 vetted organizations for defensive use only, backed by $100 million in resources. But if offensive capabilities become widely available through open-source models, the defensive partnership model may not be sufficient to protect the broader enterprise ecosystem. The report does not address whether the partnership model can scale to protect thousands of enterprises or whether it will remain a boutique solution for the largest organizations.

Finally, the report does not fully address the regulatory and geopolitical dimensions of the AI security landscape. The White House Office of Management and Budget is establishing guardrails for federal agency access to Mythos, and nation-state AI operations are scaling rapidly. The report notes that a Chinese state-sponsored group used Claude models in real-world attacks, but it does not provide a framework for assessing how geopolitical competition will shape the security market. These are questions that investors and enterprise buyers will need to monitor closely as the landscape evolves.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Enterprise Buyers in the AI Security Era

The report provides a clear thesis, but translating that thesis into actionable decisions requires a structured framework. For investors, the key question is which vendors have the proprietary data, the domain expertise, and the partnership relationships to become essential infrastructure in the AI security era. The report identifies platform vendors with deep telemetry across endpoint, network, identity, and data estates as the primary beneficiaries. But the framework should also consider which vendors are investing in autonomous defensive capabilities, which have the strongest relationships with foundation model companies, and which have the balance sheet to sustain the R&D investment required.

For enterprise buyers, the framework should prioritize vendors that can address the full AI attack surface, not just traditional security domains. The report identifies eight new vulnerability classes introduced by AI, and enterprise buyers should assess whether their current security stack can address each category. The framework should also consider the dialable controls that the report recommends: lightweight today but can be tightened as real threats materialize. Enterprise buyers should look for platforms that offer modular capabilities that can scale with the threat environment.

The framework should also consider the timeline. The report suggests that current access constraints on offensive capabilities are temporary, with open-source models approaching the performance threshold within months. Enterprise buyers should accelerate their investment in autonomous defensive platforms, and investors should position for a market that will consolidate around platform vendors that can scale defensive AI. The window for action is finite, and the cost of inaction is rising.

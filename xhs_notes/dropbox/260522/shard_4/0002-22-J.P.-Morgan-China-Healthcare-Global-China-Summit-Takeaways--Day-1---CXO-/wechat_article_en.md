# China’s CXO Sector Is No Longer Just About Outsourcing—It Is Becoming a Platform for AI-Driven Drug Discovery and Product-Led Growth

The narrative around China’s contract research and manufacturing organizations has long centered on cost arbitrage and capacity expansion. That story is now incomplete. A detailed analysis of recent management commentary from leading Chinese healthcare companies reveals a structural shift: the sector is evolving from a service-only model into a hybrid platform that combines high-margin productization, AI-enabled discovery, and commercial-scale biologics manufacturing. This is not a cyclical recovery story. It is a fundamental transformation of how value is created and captured in Chinese life sciences.

Why now matters. The global biotech funding environment remains uncertain, and geopolitical tensions continue to pressure cross-border deal flow. Yet several Chinese CXO leaders are guiding to revenue growth of 19-22% for 2026, with order backlogs exceeding US$1.3 billion. They are expanding into new modality platforms—peptides, oligonucleotides, antibody-drug conjugates—that command structurally superior margins. They are integrating artificial intelligence not as a buzzword but as a measurable driver of new business, with one firm estimating it captures roughly 50% of new protein synthesis demand from AI-driven drug discovery. And they are beginning to sell products, not just services, unlocking a revenue model with fundamentally different scalability and margin profiles.

The implications for investors are significant. The sector is no longer a single-factor bet on global R&D spending. It is becoming a multi-layered opportunity that demands a more nuanced analytical framework.

## The Old CXO Playbook of Capacity Expansion and Cost Advantage Is Giving Way to a Modality-Led Margin Transformation

For years, the competitive advantage of Chinese CXOs rested on two pillars: lower labor costs and the willingness to build massive manufacturing capacity ahead of demand. That model worked well during the era of small-molecule outsourcing. But the data now suggests a deliberate pivot toward high-barrier, high-margin new modality platforms is reshaping the economics of the industry.

Consider the trajectory of one leading contract development and manufacturing organization. Management has guided to 19-22% revenue growth for 2026, supported by a US$1.385 billion order backlog that provides exceptional revenue visibility. But the more important signal lies in the business mix shift. Peptides, oligonucleotides, conjugation payloads, and biologics collectively contributed nearly 30% of 2025 revenue and are growing at 60-100% or more. These platforms carry structurally superior margins compared to the company’s already best-in-industry small-molecule margins of 46-47%. Management expects that as these newer modalities scale, they will drive meaningful blended margin expansion over the next two to three years.

This is not incremental improvement. It is a step-change in the profit profile of the business. The implication is that investors who continue to value Chinese CXOs purely on revenue growth multiples may be underestimating the earnings power that comes from this modality mix shift. The question becomes: how should one value a company whose core margin structure is improving by several hundred basis points per year, driven by platforms that are still early in their adoption curve?

## Commercial Manufacturing Scale-Up and Regulatory Milestones Are Creating a New Layer of Revenue Visibility and Quality

One of the persistent criticisms of the CXO business model has been the lumpy, project-driven nature of its revenue. Early-stage discovery work is inherently unpredictable, and even late-stage clinical manufacturing can face delays. But the management commentary from several firms suggests that commercial manufacturing is becoming a material and recurring revenue stream.

One leading antibody-drug conjugate CDMO has expressed high confidence in its 2026 growth trajectory, anchored by rapid commercial manufacturing scale-up. The company has mapped out GMP readiness through 2027 and 2028 for its advanced facilities. Critically, it now has eight process performance qualification projects, which provide strong commercial project visibility. The company also expects its first Biologics License Application commercial project to potentially begin within this year. This is a milestone that transforms the revenue base from project-dependent to annuity-like.

The quality of revenue is changing as well. Commercial manufacturing contracts are typically multi-year, involve higher quality standards, and carry better margins than early-stage work. They also create switching costs for clients, as moving a commercial product to an alternative manufacturer requires regulatory reapproval. For investors, the presence of multiple PPQ projects and approaching BLA milestones is a leading indicator that the revenue base is becoming more durable and more valuable.

Yet an open question remains: how many of these commercial projects will ultimately achieve blockbuster status? Not all biologics succeed at launch. The sector needs to demonstrate that a meaningful percentage of these late-stage pipeline projects convert into long-term commercial revenue streams. Until that data accumulates, some uncertainty around the magnitude of the commercial opportunity will persist.

## AI Is No Longer a Theoretical Opportunity in Drug Discovery—It Is Driving Measurable Demand for Protein Synthesis and Creating New Competitive Dynamics

The integration of artificial intelligence into drug discovery has been one of the most discussed themes in life sciences over the past two years. But the conversation has often been heavy on promise and light on proof. The management commentary from Chinese life science companies now provides concrete evidence that AI is translating into real business activity.

One leading life science tools company estimates that its business is capturing approximately 50% of the new demand for protein synthesis generated by AI-driven drug discovery. This is a striking statistic. It suggests that the AIDD revolution is not just about computational models and in silico screening—it is generating physical demand for actual biological materials that need to be synthesized, validated, and scaled. The companies that can provide these services at high quality and low cost are direct beneficiaries.

Moreover, the same company is aggressively integrating AI into its own operations, specifically within its enzyme discovery division, to accelerate discovery and reduce costs. This creates a dual effect: AI drives external demand for the company’s services, and AI improves the company’s internal cost structure. The competitive implications are significant. Companies that can harness AI to lower their own cost of goods sold while simultaneously capturing new AI-driven demand will build a widening moat against competitors that lack these capabilities.

The strategic question for investors is whether the current market pricing adequately reflects this AI tailwind. Most valuation models for CXOs still rely on traditional metrics like headcount growth, capacity utilization, and historical win rates. If AI is structurally increasing the addressable market for outsourced protein synthesis and enzyme discovery, the total opportunity set may be materially larger than current forecasts assume.

## The Shift from Services to Products Represents a Fundamental Business Model Evolution with Major Margin Implications

Perhaps the most strategically significant development in the sector is the move by some companies to sell products directly to customers, rather than only providing services. This is not a minor commercial adjustment. It is a fundamental change in how value is created, captured, and scaled.

One company has articulated a clear strategic update: it will sell products directly to its customers to enable them to conduct experiments themselves. This model commands better gross profit margins and scales more efficiently than the traditional service model. The central pillar of this transition is a newly launched in-house protein expression kit, which was just introduced outside of China. The product is designed to be off-the-shelf, high-margin, and highly scalable—a deliberate departure from the bespoke, labor-intensive service model that has historically defined the sector.

The implications are profound. Service businesses are inherently capacity-constrained: revenue growth requires hiring more scientists, building more lab space, and managing more projects. Product businesses, by contrast, can scale with manufacturing efficiency and distribution reach. Once a product is developed, the marginal cost of each additional unit sold is low, and the revenue can grow without proportional increases in headcount. This creates the potential for operating leverage that service models cannot match.

The open question is whether the market will fully appreciate this transition. Product businesses trade at different valuation multiples than service businesses—typically higher, given their superior margin profiles and scalability. If the transition is successful, these companies could see multiple expansion as the market reclassifies them from service providers to life science tools companies. But the transition is still early, and execution risk is real. Building a product business requires different capabilities than running a service business: manufacturing consistency, distribution logistics, customer support, and brand building. Investors will need to monitor whether these companies can successfully develop these new muscles.

## The Full Report Leaves Several Important Questions Unanswered That Deserve Investor Attention

Despite the depth of management commentary, several critical uncertainties remain that the report does not fully resolve. First, the degree to which geopolitical risks could disrupt the commercial manufacturing buildout remains unclear. Several companies are expanding facilities in both China and Singapore, but the regulatory environment for cross-border biologics manufacturing is evolving rapidly. Investors need to understand the contingency plans if supply chain decoupling accelerates.

Second, the sustainability of the AI-driven demand surge is unproven. While one company estimates it captures 50% of new AIDD-related protein synthesis demand, the total addressable market for AI-generated drug targets is still being defined. If AI fails to deliver on its promise of increasing drug discovery productivity, the demand tailwind could prove transient.

Third, the competitive response to the productization strategy is unknown. If the move toward off-the-shelf kits proves successful, it will inevitably attract competition from both established life science tools companies and other CXOs seeking to replicate the model. The barriers to entry in product development are different from those in services, and the competitive dynamics could shift rapidly.

Finally, the valuation implications of the modality mix shift are not straightforward. While higher-margin peptides and oligonucleotides should improve overall profitability, they also require different manufacturing capabilities and regulatory expertise. The transition period could involve elevated capital expenditure and execution risk that may temporarily suppress returns on invested capital.

These unanswered questions are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are the natural consequence of an industry in transition. They are precisely the kind of analytical puzzles that reward deep, ongoing research engagement.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the New CXO Landscape

For investors seeking to position in this evolving sector, a structured decision framework is more useful than a simple buy-or-sell recommendation. The following questions can help differentiate between companies that are genuinely transforming and those that are merely benefiting from cyclical tailwinds.

First, assess the revenue composition trend. Is the company explicitly disclosing the share of revenue coming from new modalities such as peptides, oligonucleotides, and ADCs? Are these segments growing faster than the core business? Companies that can demonstrate a clear, accelerating mix shift toward higher-margin platforms deserve a premium.

Second, evaluate the commercial manufacturing pipeline. How many PPQ projects does the company have? What is the expected timeline for first BLA approval and commercial launch? Companies with multiple late-stage projects approaching commercialization have a higher-quality revenue base than those still dependent on early-stage discovery work.

Third, measure the AI integration depth. Is the company merely benefiting from external AI-driven demand, or is it actively using AI to improve its own operations? Companies that can demonstrate cost reduction or efficiency gains from internal AI deployment have a structural advantage.

Fourth, analyze the productization strategy. Is the company selling standardized products in addition to services? What is the gross margin differential between product and service revenue? Companies that can build a scalable product business alongside their service offerings have the potential for significant multiple expansion.

Fifth, monitor the balance between capacity expansion and capital discipline. Companies that are investing in new facilities while maintaining or improving return on invested capital are executing well. Those that are building capacity ahead of demonstrated demand may be taking on excessive risk.

Applying this framework systematically can help investors separate the companies that are genuinely transforming their business models from those that are simply riding the current wave.

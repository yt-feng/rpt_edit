# Gold's Divergence: Why China's Unrelenting Demand Cannot Shield the Market from a Near-Term Correction

The global gold market is currently trapped between two forces that have rarely been this misaligned. On one side, China's gold imports are running at an extraordinary pace, with spending approaching all-time highs of roughly $300 billion per annum, a level that once represented total global gold spending in pre-COVID years. On the other side, the continued closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the resulting energy-driven inflation are pushing real rates higher, strengthening the US dollar, and compressing investor demand outside China. The central question for any serious gold investor is not whether gold has long-term value, but whether the current price can hold while the world's largest marginal buyer is increasingly isolated in its buying behavior.

This is not a story about gold being overvalued or undervalued. It is a story about a market that is becoming structurally dependent on a single demand engine, and about the risks that emerge when that engine faces its own vulnerabilities. The near-term outlook for gold is cautious, with a 0-3 month price target of $4,300 per ounce, and the possibility of a much sharper decline if a risk-off event materializes. The long-term case for gold remains intact, but the path to that destination is likely to be more volatile than many investors currently anticipate.

The report from a global investment bank makes this tension clear. China's apparent gold demand reached a record high in value terms in the first quarter of 2026, driven by retail bar and coin demand that hit record levels in both volume and value. Jewelry demand remained resilient. Central bank buying rebounded. Yet the report explicitly states that these factors are "not changing the main delta which is lower investor demand, notably in India and from ex-China retail." This is the critical disconnect that the market has not yet fully priced.

## The Historical Correlation Between Gold and Real Rates Has Returned, and That Changes the Near-Term Calculus

For the better part of three years, from 2022 through 2024, gold behaved as if it had broken free from its traditional relationship with US real interest rates. Central bank buying, particularly from emerging market central banks diversifying away from dollar reserves, created a demand floor that seemed to decouple gold from its usual macro drivers. The average daily correlation between gold and the 5-year real rate was only -0.2 in 2024, a shadow of its historical strength.

That era has ended. Year-to-date in 2026, the correlation has plunged to -0.75, meaning gold is once again moving almost in lockstep with real rates, just in the opposite direction. This is not a temporary statistical artifact; it reflects a fundamental shift in the composition of gold demand. As non-central-bank investor participation has increased, the market has become more responsive to the traditional macro variables that drive portfolio allocation decisions.

The implication is straightforward but uncomfortable. With the Strait of Hormuz closure persisting and energy prices remaining elevated, market expectations for Federal Reserve rate hikes are increasing. Higher real rates and a stronger US dollar are precisely the conditions that historically have weighed most heavily on gold prices. The report's base case assumes that the Strait situation will de-escalate by July, at which point these macro headwinds should ease. But base cases are not certainties, and the longer the closure continues, the more pressure builds on gold from the real rate channel.

Investors who have become accustomed to gold's resilience during the central bank buying era need to recalibrate their expectations. The correlation has returned, and with it, the old rules apply.

## China's Demand Is Record-Setting but Structurally Different from What the Market Needs

China's gold import data for April 2026 show continued strength, with non-monetary gold imports down only slightly from the March highs. In volume terms, imports were up 25% year-over-year. In value terms, the increase was approximately 83% year-over-year, reflecting both higher volumes and the elevated price level. China's apparent demand in the first quarter of 2026 reached a record high in value terms, driven primarily by retail bar and coin demand that hit all-time highs in both volume and value.

This is remarkable by any historical standard. The report notes that total world spending on gold during pre-COVID years was normally around $300 billion per annum in today's dollars, and China alone is now spending roughly that amount. The "unaccounted for demand" in the apparent demand modeling, which had turned negative in the fourth quarter of 2025, rebounded in the first quarter of 2026, suggesting that even the official categories may be understating the true scale of Chinese buying.

Yet this demand has not been sufficient to drive gold prices decisively higher. The reason is structural. Chinese gold demand is overwhelmingly driven by retail bar and coin investment, which is a different species from the institutional investor demand that typically provides the marginal price support in Western markets. Chinese retail investors are buying gold as a store of value, often in response to domestic economic uncertainty and a strengthening renminbi. They are less sensitive to US real rates and dollar movements than institutional investors in the United States and Europe.

The strength of the renminbi is a particularly important factor. A stronger renminbi makes gold cheaper for Chinese buyers in local currency terms, supporting demand. But it also means that Chinese demand is partially endogenous to the same macro forces that are pressuring gold in the rest of the world. If the Strait of Hormuz situation persists long enough to affect China's own energy costs and growth outlook, the renminbi could weaken, and Chinese gold demand could soften. The report does not model this scenario in detail, but it is a logical extension of the current dynamics.

## The Divergence Between China and India Creates a Demand Hole That Central Bank Buying Cannot Fill

The two largest consumer markets for gold, China and India, are now moving in opposite directions. While Chinese demand is at record levels, Indian demand is weakening, and the report explicitly cites lower investor demand in India as a key concern. This divergence is not coincidental. It reflects fundamentally different local policy environments and currency trajectories.

India's gold demand has historically been sensitive to domestic gold prices, import duties, and government policies aimed at curbing gold imports to manage the current account deficit. With gold prices at elevated levels in rupee terms, and with the Indian rupee under pressure from global dollar strength, the affordability of gold for Indian consumers has deteriorated. The report does not provide granular data on Indian demand trends, but the implication is clear: the combination of high prices and a weak currency is suppressing demand in what has traditionally been the world's second-largest gold market.

Central bank buying, which rebounded in the first quarter of 2026, provides some offset, but it is not a perfect substitute for consumer demand. Central banks are strategic buyers with long time horizons; they are less likely to chase prices higher during periods of macro uncertainty and are more likely to buy on dips. The report's data shows that central bank purchases, while robust, are not accelerating at the same pace as Chinese retail demand. The composition of global gold demand is shifting in a way that makes the market more dependent on a single, concentrated source of buying.

This concentration risk is underappreciated. If Chinese demand were to moderate for any reason, whether due to a change in domestic policy, a weakening renminbi, or a shift in retail investor sentiment, the global gold market would face a significant demand vacuum. Central bank buying and institutional investor demand would need to fill that gap, but at current price levels, that is far from guaranteed.

## The Stagflation Scenario Is the Most Undervalued Risk in Current Gold Pricing

The report identifies three potential scenarios for the gold market, but the one that receives the least attention from investors is arguably the most consequential. The base case is a de-escalation of the Strait of Hormuz situation by July, which would ease energy-driven inflation, allow real rates to stabilize, and provide a floor for gold prices. The downside case is a sharp risk-off event, such as a major equity selloff or a re-escalation of US-Iran tensions, which could push gold prices well below $4,300 per ounce, potentially revisiting levels near $3,400 that were seen only twelve months ago.

The stagflation scenario is the third path. If the Strait of Hormuz closure persists much longer than expected, and energy prices remain much higher for much longer, the market concern will shift from inflation without recession into stagflation. This is the worst possible environment for most asset classes, but historically it has been extraordinarily positive for precious metals. The report references the second oil crisis as a precedent, a period during which gold and silver delivered exceptional returns while equities and bonds suffered.

The current market pricing does not reflect significant stagflation risk. US high-frequency economic data remain overall robust, and the dominant narrative is still one of inflation that is sufficiently persistent to delay rate cuts but not severe enough to trigger a recession. This is a Goldilocks scenario that is actually quite negative for gold, because it keeps real rates elevated without creating the kind of economic distress that would drive safe-haven buying.

The stagflation scenario would change that calculus entirely. In that world, central banks would face an impossible choice between fighting inflation and supporting growth, and gold would likely emerge as the only asset class with positive real returns. The report's data on historical performance during stagflationary periods is compelling, but it does not attempt to quantify the probability of this scenario or to model gold's potential price trajectory under those conditions. This is one of the most important open questions in the report, and it is a question that every serious gold investor should be asking.

## A Decision Framework for Gold Investors in a Fractured Market

The report provides the analytical ingredients for a structured decision framework, but it does not synthesize them into a clear set of actionable questions. The following framework is designed to help investors navigate the current environment, based on the report's data and logic.

The first question is about time horizon. For investors with a three-month or shorter horizon, the report's cautious stance is appropriate. The return of the gold-real rate correlation, the persistence of the Strait of Hormuz closure, and the concentration of demand in a single market all argue for near-term caution. The 0-3 month price target of $4,300 per ounce represents a meaningful downside from current levels, and the risk of a sharp selloff on a risk-off event is real. Short-term investors should be positioned defensively, with an awareness that gold's historical pattern during equity market corrections is to fall first and rebound later.

The second question is about scenario weighting. The base case of a July de-escalation is the report's central assumption, but it is not a certainty. Investors should assign explicit probabilities to the three scenarios: base case de-escalation, prolonged closure with stagflation, and a sudden risk-off event. Each scenario has very different implications for gold. The base case suggests a near-term bottom followed by a recovery. The stagflation scenario suggests a potential breakout to new highs. The risk-off scenario suggests a sharp decline followed by a rebound. The appropriate portfolio positioning depends on which scenario an investor believes is most likely.

The third question is about the sustainability of Chinese demand. The report provides extensive data on Chinese gold imports but does not fully answer the question of what would cause this demand to moderate. The key variables to monitor are the renminbi exchange rate, Chinese retail savings rates, and any policy changes related to gold imports or capital outflows. A sustained weakening of the renminbi would be the most obvious trigger for a pullback in Chinese demand, and it is a risk that is not fully captured in the current market consensus.

The fourth question is about the role of central bank buying. The report shows that central bank demand rebounded in the first quarter of 2026, but it does not provide a detailed breakdown by country or a forward-looking assessment of central bank buying intentions. The strategic motivations for central bank gold purchases, particularly de-dollarization and reserve diversification, remain intact, but the pace of buying is unlikely to accelerate at current price levels. Central banks are price-sensitive buyers, and the current elevated price environment may actually slow the rate of accumulation.

## The Questions the Report Leaves Unanswered

For all its analytical depth, the report leaves several important questions unresolved, and these are the questions that should drive further research and community discussion.

The most significant is the question of what happens to Chinese demand if the renminbi weakens. The report notes that a strengthening renminbi has supported Chinese gold buying, but it does not model the reverse scenario. Given the size of Chinese demand relative to the global market, even a modest reduction in Chinese buying would have outsized price effects. The report's apparent demand modeling includes a category for "unaccounted for demand" that rebounded in the first quarter, but the composition of this demand is opaque. Is it speculative buying by Chinese institutional investors? Is it related to capital outflows disguised as gold imports? The answers matter for assessing the sustainability of current demand levels.

The second unresolved question is about the behavior of non-China retail investors. The report notes that ex-China retail demand is weak, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the regions or channels where this weakness is most pronounced. Is the weakness concentrated in India, or is it broader across Southeast Asia and the Middle East? Are Western retail investors also pulling back, or are they being offset by institutional flows? Without this granularity, it is difficult to assess whether the current demand imbalance is temporary or structural.

The third question is about the interaction between gold and other asset classes in a stagflation scenario. The report provides historical data on gold performance during stagflation, but it does not model the portfolio implications for multi-asset investors. In a stagflation scenario, gold would likely be one of the few assets with positive returns, but the magnitude and timing of those returns would depend on the severity of the economic downturn and the policy response. A deeper analysis of these portfolio dynamics would be valuable for institutional investors.

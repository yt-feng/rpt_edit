# The strength of the industrial cycle is outweighing the energy shock

At the centre of two countervailing forces: Our recent research has been focused on two issues – 1) the strength of the industrial cycle and 2) the potential disruptions arising from the geopolitical tensions in the Middle East. It is simultaneously the key beneficiary of an upswing in the industrial cycle – given its high share of industrial value-added globally and a high weight of the industrial sector in its GDP. So far, Asia has managed the energy shock well, and the strength of the industrial cycle is supporting Asia's growth.

High-frequency data are affirming that the industrial cycle is pulling ahead: Asia entered this period with clear cyclical momentum: manufacturing PMIs had strengthened, industrial production was improving, exports – especially for non-tech – were holding up, and capital goods imports pointed to a firmer investment cycle. The most powerful industrial cycle since mid-2000s: Meanwhile, Asia is entering its most powerful industrial super-cycle since the mid-2000s. A sustained, multi-year rise in capex is underway, underpinned by structural forces (see Asia Economics: Headed Towards an Industrial Super-Cycle, April 27, 2026).

Four key drivers: We identify four interlocking structural drivers that distinguish this cycle.

1. AI and AI-related infrastructure: AI and related spending are still a top priority for CIOs and Asia's corporate sector has to accelerate spending to catch up with the US. At the same time, Asia remains a key supplier to the AI supply chain and these companies will collectively lift their capex too to meet the rising demand.   
2. Energy and energy transition: Across the region, economies are having to spend more on energy for powering AI, making the energy transition to renewables and for energy security. This would have moved up the policy agenda after the recent energy shock.   
3. Defense spending: Policymakers across the region had already been budgeting increases in their defense spending in the coming years. The geopolitical tensions have only further cemented this planned increase in expenditures.   
4. Broader industrial capex spillovers: The rise in spending in the three segments above will catalyse a broader capex and industrial cycle as the full effects work through the entire supply chain. There will be a positive reinforcement loop.

Asia's capex to grow 3x faster in the next five years: We forecast Asia's gross fixed investment to reach US\$16trn by 2030, up from US\$11trn currently — a 7% CAGR over the next five years, approximately three times the pace of growth seen over 2023–25. The high-growth structural segments — AI infrastructure, energy transition, and defense — are expected to expand at a 16% CAGR over 2026–30, with combined spending in these four segments (including broader industrial spillovers) rising to a US\$9trn annual run rate in 2030, up from US\$5trn in 2025.

These are not standalone factors — they are intertwined and will have an effect on the industrial cycle greater than the sum of their parts. Moreover, Asia stands to enjoy a double benefit given its role as the production powerhouse. This capex surge will not remain confined to investment alone: as capex rises, it will catalyse job creation and wage growth, which in turn lifts consumption, creating a self-reinforcing virtuous cycle.

Geopolitical tensions – a near-term drag but a medium-term catalyst: The same tensions that are raising oil and gas costs are simultaneously forcing governments and corporations to accelerate spending on energy security, AI infrastructure, and defense — all of which are structural tailwinds for Asia's industrial cycle.

Capex leads to a trade boom: While capex was initially driven by AI and tech-related spending, it led to a strong rise in Asia's tech exports, and over the past six months, we have seen a sustained rise in non-tech exports. From an outlook perspective, we see this positive dynamic being sustained. Our global economics team highlights that the outlook for global trade is improving, underpinned by a stronger global capex cycle. They project that global capex growth (ex-China property investment) will improve from $3.9\%$ Y in 2025 to $4.3\%$ in 2026. This also means that global capex growth will be much stronger than overall GDP growth. This backdrop will continue to support Asia's trade.

# Asia Economics Mid-Year Outlook

# The industrial super-cycle is outweighing the energy shock

We remain more constructive than consensus, as we expect the strength of the industrial cycle to offset the effects of higher energy prices. At the same time, we don't expect as many rate hikes as the market is pricing in.

# Key Takeaways

Asia's macro outlook hinges on two factors – the strength of the industrial cycle vs. the disruptions posed by the energy shock.   
2Q growth momentum is holding up better than we had expected. The strength of the industrial cycle is outweighing the drag from geopolitical tensions.   
The ongoing tech and non-tech exports recovery and a robust capex cycle sustain job creation and growth in wages and consumption – broadening the recovery.   
Korea, Taiwan, China, and Japan are the key beneficiaries of this upswing in the industrial and capex cycle.   
The risk is if a severe re-escalation leads to a disruptive sharp rise in oil prices through US\$150/bbl for a sustained period.

In this report, we highlight how the strength of the industrial cycle benefits Asia and how Asia's growth cycle is broadening.

# The strength of the industrial cycle is outweighing the energy shock

At the centre of two countervailing forces: Our recent research has been focused on two issues – 1) the strength of the industrial cycle and 2) the potential disruptions arising from the geopolitical tensions in the Middle East. Asia, interestingly, sits right in the middle of these forces. It is simultaneously the key beneficiary of an upswing in the industrial cycle – given its high share of industrial value-added globally and a high weight of the industrial sector in its GDP. But it is also the most exposed region to energy shocks – given its high dependence on imported oil and gas. So far, Asia has managed the energy shock well, and the strength of the industrial cycle is supporting Asia's growth.

High-frequency data are affirming that the industrial cycle is pulling ahead: Asia entered this period with clear cyclical momentum: manufacturing PMIs had strengthened, industrial production was improving, exports – especially for non-tech – were holding up, and capital goods imports pointed to a firmer investment cycle. Though growth data points did soften in March as the initial rise in energy prices filtered through, growth has since recovered momentum in April, as evident in the pickup in PMIs and exports.

Asia has managed the energy shock well: Asia has managed its fuel supplies in several ways:

- Sharp cuts in China's energy imports: China has cut its oil and LNG imports $30\%$ and $56\%$ from the December 2025 peak. This has increased supplies for the rest of Asia.   
- Drawdown of inventories at sea and strategic reserves   
- Shift to alternative energy sources, including coal, nuclear, and renewable energy   
- Increasing domestic refined product production – for example, India has increased LPG production by 40%

Separately, an increase in US exports of oil and gas has also helped Asia manage this energy shock better (see The Oil Manual: Why Is the Price of Oil Not Higher? May 11, 2026). For example, the US has increased its seaborne crude and petroleum exports 71%Y – from 5.2 mb/d a year ago to 8.9 mb/d currently. This has meant that Asia has been able to mitigate the supply disruptions and keep production going, albeit with the backdrop of higher prices.

The most powerful industrial cycle since mid-2000s: Meanwhile, Asia is entering its most powerful industrial super-cycle since the mid-2000s. A sustained, multi-year rise in capex is underway, underpinned by structural forces (see Asia Economics: Headed Towards an Industrial Super-Cycle, April 27, 2026).

Four key drivers: We identify four interlocking structural drivers that distinguish this cycle.

1. AI and AI-related infrastructure: AI and related spending are still a top priority for CIOs and Asia's corporate sector has to accelerate spending to catch up with the US. At the same time, Asia remains a key supplier to the AI supply chain and these companies will collectively lift their capex too to meet the rising demand.   
2. Energy and energy transition: Across the region, economies are having to spend more on energy for powering AI, making the energy transition to renewables and for energy security. This would have moved up the policy agenda after the recent energy shock.   
3. Defense spending: Policymakers across the region had already been budgeting increases in their defense spending in the coming years. The geopolitical tensions have only further cemented this planned increase in expenditures.   
4. Broader industrial capex spillovers: The rise in spending in the three segments above will catalyse a broader capex and industrial cycle as the full effects work through the entire supply chain. There will be a positive reinforcement loop.

Asia's capex to grow 3x faster in the next five years: We forecast Asia's gross fixed investment to reach US\$16trn by 2030, up from US\$11trn currently — a 7% CAGR over the next five years, approximately three times the pace of growth seen over 2023–25. The high-growth structural segments — AI infrastructure, energy transition, and defense — are expected to expand at a 16% CAGR over 2026–30, with combined spending in these four segments (including broader industrial spillovers) rising to a US\$9trn annual run rate in 2030, up from US\$5trn in 2025.

These are not standalone factors — they are intertwined and will have an effect on the industrial cycle greater than the sum of their parts. Moreover, Asia stands to enjoy a double benefit given its role as the production powerhouse. This capex surge will not remain confined to investment alone: as capex rises, it will catalyse job creation and wage growth, which in turn lifts consumption, creating a self-reinforcing virtuous cycle.

Geopolitical tensions – a near-term drag but a medium-term catalyst: The same tensions that are raising oil and gas costs are simultaneously forcing governments and corporations to accelerate spending on energy security, AI infrastructure, and defense — all of which are structural tailwinds for Asia's industrial cycle.

Capex leads to a trade boom: While capex was initially driven by AI and tech-related spending, it led to a strong rise in Asia's tech exports, and over the past six months, we have seen a sustained rise in non-tech exports. From an outlook perspective, we see this positive dynamic being sustained. Our global economics team highlights that the outlook for global trade is improving, underpinned by a stronger global capex cycle. They project that global capex growth (ex-China property investment) will improve from $3.9\%$ Y in 2025 to $4.3\%$ in 2026. This also means that global capex growth will be much stronger than overall GDP growth. This backdrop will continue to support Asia's trade.

Which economies benefit most from this industrial and capex super-cycle: While the super-cycle will lift all Asian economies, we highlight Taiwan, Korea, China, and Japan as the primary beneficiaries, given the high exposure to AI, energy, and defense high-growth sectors. They will benefit from both domestic capex pickup and expanded export opportunities. From a market perspective, the industrial sector carries a larger weight in their MSCI indices too. India will also benefit from the industrial cycle pickup, alongside a domestic capex boost driven by easy fiscal and monetary policies. Australia and Indonesia stand to gain as commodity exporters, with the raw materials price index already at a four-year high.

The growth cycle is broadening: Six months ago in our Year Ahead Outlook, we had drawn attention to the notion that Asia's recovery was broadening. Six months on, we still see this unfolding. We think Asia is in a virtuous cycle, where the rise in exports – both tech and non-tech – and the robust capex cycle will help to support job creation and wage growth – underpinning consumption growth. This backdrop will generate positive spillovers to Asia's growth momentum.

For China, considering the starting point of excess capacity and deflationary pressures, we think that the strength in exports will first show up in improving capacity utilization, which will then reduce deflationary pressures and improves nominal GDP growth. As that unfolds, corporate revenue growth picks up, followed by wage growth. Meanwhile, job creation should improve with a lag, as companies will first utilize the slack in their capacity before looking to start hiring.

Growth moderates from 2Q but stabilizes thereafter: We expect growth to moderate in 2Q as higher energy prices will lead to a drag on growth. But the combination of the mitigation strategies as well as the strength of the industrial cycle has meant that 2Q26 was already tracking better at 4.6%Y than we had expected in the April forecast update (4.2%Y). From 2Q onwards, we are expecting growth to stabilize around these levels. In our April forecast update, we had brought our growth estimate for Asia down 40bps – but in this forecast round, we have lifted it back up. Indeed, our 2026 GDP growth forecast for Asia now stands at 4.8% – unchanged from our Year Ahead Outlook. Our 2027 GDP growth forecast is now moderately higher than it was then.

Risks to the outlook – industrial cycle vs. oil price trajectory: The key upside risk is if the capex and industrial cycle is even stronger than we had built in, or if oil prices rapidly move lower. The key downside risk is if we get a severe re-escalation that results in oil prices moving through US\$150/bbl, which then triggers a global recession, with spillovers to Asia.

Our rule of thumb is that each sustained US\$10/bbl increase in oil prices reduces Asia's GDP growth \~20-30bp on a gross basis, or \~10bp after mitigating measures and fiscal offsets. But we are concerned that non-linear effects could kick in as a result of stronger oil prices.

# Outlook for the top six economies by PPP weight

China – ey beneficiary of industrial super-cycle: China’s relatively limited exposure to the energy shock and continued support from robust exports lead our Chief Economist for China, Robin Xing, to expect 2026 growth to reach 4.8%Y. We have been highlighting China as one of the key beneficiaries of Asia’s capex super-cycle, given that it is more exposed to the high-growth sectors of AI, energy, and defense, and will benefit from a pickup in export opportunities and domestic capex. Reflecting this, real exports growth within GDP is likely to accelerate to 8%Y (vs. 5.4% in 2025), while we estimate real GFCF growth (excluding property investment) should pick up to 5.8%Y this year (vs. 4.6% in 2025). With growth on track to reach the 4.5-5% target, additional policy easing is likely to be limited. We see an improvement in nominal GDP growth to a 4-year high of 5.1%Y (vs. 4% in 2025).

India – domestic demand momentum intact: Incoming high-frequency indicators suggest that the domestic demand recovery since 2H25 remains largely intact. While the drag from negative terms of trade and supply shortage disruptions to select sectors is likely to weigh on 2Q growth temporarily, we expect growth to gradually recover from 3Q onwards as these challenges ease. Overall, our Chief Economist for India, Upasana Chachra, expects growth to average a healthy 6.8%Y for 2026. Within domestic demand, urban consumption and government capex are likely to be key sources of support. Measures to stabilize fuel prices should help keep real private consumption growth buoyant at 7%Y for 2026, even as rural demand faces headwinds from weather and fertilizer-related disruptions. Government capex is likely to be focused on infrastructure and defense spending.

Japan – stronger exports and fiscal support to cushion the energy shock: Our Chief Economist for Japan, Takeshi Yamaguchi, expects real GDP growth to slow given the headwinds from weaker terms of trade, higher inflation weighing on real wage growth (and hence consumption), and shortages in refined products such as naphtha affecting production in certain sectors. But stronger exports and pro-growth policies to be unveiled during the summer will help cushion growth. Importantly, we also see Japan as one of the economies that benefit more from the upswing in the industrial/capex cycle. Structural drivers of Japan’s reflationary environment that we have previously highlighted (such as favorable r-g gap) are still intact.

Indonesia – waiting for the capex turnaround: Growth momentum had picked up in 1Q26, supported by a 21.8% surge in government spending. We have been highlighting that a pickup in the capex cycle remains key to sustain the next phase of the expansion. But, so far, this has not yet transpired. The investment/GDP ratio has been flat-lining since 1Q25 and remains well below its pre-covid levels, creating challenges for job creation and sustained consumption growth.

We see three key factors that could help improve the macro outlook:

1. A pickup in the pace of policy reforms that supports corporate sentiment,   
2. A sustained rise in commodity prices helping to lift terms of trade,   
3. A rise in public capex which catalyses an overall capex cycle.

Korea – recovery is broadening to consumption: While Korea is among the economies relatively more exposed to the energy shock in Asia, offsets from strength in exports and fiscal support lead our Chief Economist for Korea, Kathleen Oh, to expect real GDP growth to pick up to 2.8%Y in 2026 (vs. 1.1% in 2025). Real export growth is likely to accelerate to 10%Y this year (vs. 4.2% in 2025), led by semiconductor exports but also supported by Korea’s high exposure to other high-growth sectors of energy/energy transition, defense, and broader capital goods exports. Kathleen also expects the recovery to broaden out to consumption, supported by 1) lower income household support from supplementary budgets, (2) improving labor income, and 3) positive wealth effects.

Taiwan – riding the AI super-cycle: Taiwan’s central role in advanced semis production is continuing to boost exports, and we expect growth to remain strong at 8.9%Y this year (vs. a high base of 8.6% in 2025). The much-stronger-than-expected 1Q26 GDP print underscored the continued strength in external demand, and Kathleen sees sustained strength in semis exports through next year, fueled by strong US hyperscaler capex guidance. The positive spillover from exports to domestic capex is likely to lift gross capital formation (GCF) growth to 10%Y this year (vs. 4.9% last year), while the government’s universal cash handout programme and wealth effects from equity market are tailwinds for private consumption recovery.

# Inflation – Headline to rise, driven by energy, but still remain largely within central banks' comfort zone

Policymakers continue to limit fuel price pass-through for now: Policymakers in the region have largely continued to limit the pass-through of international oil price increases into domestic fuel prices. On a nominal dollar GDP-weighted basis, Asia's oil prices in local currency terms have risen 59% over the past two months, but domestic fuel prices have risen only 15% – implying that policymakers have absorbed 75% of the impact. Reflecting this, headline CPI was relatively muted at 1.9% in March (vs. 2% in February), and we expect it to average a still benign 2.1% in 2Q. On a PPP-adjusted basis, over 90% of the region still had inflation within the central banks' comfort zone in March.

China – from deflation to lowflation: Higher input prices have led to one round of reflation in China across PPI and CPI. Our economics team for China expects PPI and CPI to average 1.5% and 0.8% in 2026, respectively, ending three years of deflation. China’s lesser exposure to the energy shock also limits the inflation upside from the energy shock. More importantly, we believe a return to a moderate sustainable reflationary environment of around 2% inflation would require more proactive fiscal policy to fill the domestic demand shortfall. As such, still-soft domestic demand and weak labor market conditions are likely to cap the spillover to core inflation – and we expect core CPI to average 1%Y in 2026 (vs. 0.8% in 2025). Our economics team for China also notes that the relationship between headline inflation and the GDP deflator may weaken during periods of large shocks, and as such only expects the deflator to average 0.5%Y in 2026 (see China's 3D Journey: False Dawn: Decoding China's Inflation Dynamics, March 24, 2026).

Japan – US-style core CPI rising only gradually: While policymakers have continued with fuel sUBSidies for now, Takeshi Yamaguchi sees a possibility that the fuel sUBSidies could be reduced in the summer months. He and his team expects headline CPI to breach 2% by July 2026, peaking at 4%Y – driven by higher energy and food prices. That said, while not incorporated in our forecasts at the moment, policymakers are discussing plans to reinstate sUBSidies for electricity and gas for July-September. If taken up, that would imply some downside to our inflation forecasts. With higher headline inflation likely to weigh on real wages and private consumption once again, US-style core CPI is expected to rise only gradually to 1.7%Y in 4Q26 (vs. 1.4%Y currently).

# Policy – A gradual and modest rate hike cycle

We still expect less rate hikes than priced in by markets: Reflecting our inflation forecasts, we are also expecting central banks in Asia to be hiking less than what markets are pricing in.

Highlighting four central banks with tightening bias – Japan, Australia, Korea, and the Philippines: We see two main factors determining Asian central banks' reaction function;

1. Starting point and persistence of inflation relative to central banks' comfort zone   
2. Sensitivity to energy shock.

On this basis, we highlight Japan, Australia, Korea, and the Philippines where we have seen more persistent inflation in the run-up to geopolitical tensions. In the past 12 months, headline inflation has been above the central bank's inflation target or midpoint of target range for between two to nine months for these four economies. For the Philippines, Australia and Korea, the latest inflation print is also above central banks' comfort zone across both headline and core inflation, reflecting the higher starting point of inflation and the larger pass-through to domestic fuel prices. Taken together, we see BOJ, BOK, and BSP as most likely to retain/signal a tightening bias. RBA, after having tightened 75bps, is moving into a more data-dependent mode.

# Risks

In our view, the key upside risk for Asia could come from the capex super-cycle being even stronger than we expect for the US and Asia: For the US, our global economics team believes upside to US growth could emanate from:

1. Stronger private sector spending amid strong household wealth effect and elevated business confidence, and   
2. Faster-than-expected AI adoption leading to a productivity boost to US growth.

This would have positive spillover effects for Asia's trade and capex cycle too, given that Asia will benefit strongly from both increased demand for exports. This would then catalyse a further rise in Asia's broader industrial capex and production.

# The key downside risk would be re-escalation of geopolitical tensions in the Middle

East: The Strait of Hormuz could be shut for many more months, leading oil prices to exceed US\$150/bbl and remain elevated for several months – which would trigger a global recession. Pricing could also remain elevated for other materials critical to the supply chain, including petrochemicals and fertilizers, plastics, and inputs for semis manufacturing, metal processing, battery manufacturing, etc. This triggers broad-based demand destruction. Asia would be the most exposed region given its imported energy dependence, while economies with larger industrial activity and exports exposure would face greater downside.

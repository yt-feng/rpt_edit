# The US Economy's New Center of Gravity: Capex, Not Consumption, Will Define the Next Two Years

The most consequential shift in the US economic landscape over the next 24 months is not the oil shock, the Fed's patience, or the midterm elections. It is the structural rebalancing of growth away from consumer spending and toward business investment, led overwhelmingly by artificial intelligence. This is not a cyclical rotation. It is a regime change in how the economy generates momentum. A global investment bank's mid-year outlook makes this case with striking clarity: personal consumption expenditures are projected to decelerate to just 1.8% in 2026, while nonresidential fixed investment accelerates to 7% growth in the same year and 8% in 2027. The gap between these two forces—consumption dragging and capex pulling—is the single most important dynamic for investors, corporate strategists, and policymakers to understand.

Why now matters is straightforward. The US economy has just absorbed its fourth supply shock in six years: a Middle East conflict that has pushed Brent crude from $70 per barrel to a range of $90-120. Each prior shock reshaped the economic architecture in lasting ways—pandemic-era fiscal transfers, the Russia-Ukraine energy crisis, and the 2025 tariff regime. This time, the shock arrives at a moment when the consumer is already fatigued, fiscal stimulus is exhausted, and the Federal Reserve is trapped between sticky inflation and political transition. The conventional playbook of waiting for the consumer to rescue growth no longer applies. The engine of expansion has moved from the household to the data center.

The argument that follows is not a summary of forecasts. It is an interpretation of a structural thesis: that the US economy is exiting a multi-decade regime optimized for efficiency and entering one optimized for resilience, and that this transition expresses itself most clearly in the supremacy of capex over consumption. The implications for interest rates, labor markets, inflation, and portfolio construction are profound. They are also far from settled.

## The Consumer Drag Is Real but Not Fatal; the Real Story Is Where Growth Is Migrating

The headline numbers for consumer spending look troubling at first glance. Real consumption growth is projected to fall to 1.8% in 2026 from 2.1% in 2025, and the bank's report explicitly identifies higher gasoline prices as the mechanism that neutralizes what little fiscal support remains from prior stimulus. This is a classic energy-tax effect: when households spend more at the pump, they spend less on discretionary goods. The pain is not evenly distributed. Lower- and middle-income cohorts, who spend a larger share of their income on energy, will feel the squeeze most acutely. Upper-income households, buoyed by elevated wealth levels from equity and real estate holdings, will largely absorb the shock without cutting spending.

But the real insight is not that consumption is weak. It is that the economy no longer needs consumption to be strong. In prior cycles, a consumer slowdown of this magnitude would have dragged GDP growth below trend. This time, it does not. The bank projects real GDP growth of 2.3% in 2026 and 2.6% in 2027—essentially trend-like performance—because business fixed investment fills the gap. If you strip out imports and look at final private domestic demand, consumption and business spending contribute nearly equal shares to growth across the forecast horizon. That is a structural shift, not a temporary offset.

The so-what here is critical for investors. If you are positioned for a consumer-led recovery, you are positioned for the wrong cycle. The marginal dollar of economic activity is now being generated in factories, data centers, and infrastructure projects tied to AI buildout, not in retail stores or service sectors. This has direct implications for sector allocation, credit risk assessment, and even real estate strategy. The companies that will outperform are those that supply the capex cycle, not those that depend on the consumer wallet.

## AI Capex Is Not Cyclical; It Is the New Structural Backbone of Business Investment

The bank's forecast for nonresidential fixed investment—7% in 2026, 8% in 2027—is striking not because of the growth rate itself, but because of what is driving it. Equipment investment is projected to grow at over 10% in both years, and intellectual property products investment at roughly 8-9%. These are not typical mid-cycle numbers. They reflect a capital expenditure cycle that is more structural than cyclical, more strategic than reactive.

The report identifies hyperscaler spending exceeding $1 trillion by 2027. That is not a forecast of incremental IT budgets. It is a statement about the scale of infrastructure required to build and operate the next generation of AI systems. Data centers, power generation, networking equipment, and cooling systems are not discretionary purchases that can be deferred when uncertainty rises. They are the physical foundation of a technological paradigm shift. The bank's analysis suggests that AI-related spending provides resiliency even amid geopolitical uncertainty—precisely because it is driven by long-term competitive necessity rather than short-term confidence.

This has a second-order implication that the report does not fully develop but invites readers to consider. If AI capex is structurally resilient, then the traditional relationship between geopolitical risk and business investment may be breaking down. In previous cycles, an oil shock and Middle East conflict would have triggered a broad capex freeze. This time, business investment is accelerating. That suggests the economy's sensitivity to geopolitical shocks is changing. The sectors that matter most for growth—technology, energy infrastructure, domestic manufacturing—are being driven by forces that are largely insulated from the headlines.

The question this raises is whether the capex cycle can sustain itself without a consumer recovery. The bank's baseline assumes it can, but the margin of safety is thin. If AI spending disappoints or if the oil shock deepens into a full recession scenario, the entire growth architecture collapses. That is why the capex-over-consumption thesis is powerful but not invulnerable.

## Inflation Is Sticky for Structural Reasons, Not Just Energy; the Fed's Patience Is Rational but Risky

The inflation narrative in this report is more nuanced than a simple energy-shock story. Core PCE inflation is projected at 2.8% in 2026 and 2.3% in 2027—above target for the entire forecast horizon. The bank explicitly notes that risks are in the direction of inflation persistence, not disinflation. But the mechanism is not just oil. The report argues that the AI buildout itself may have near-term demand effects on inflation, particularly through power demand, infrastructure costs, and commodity prices. In other words, the same force that is driving growth is also contributing to price pressures.

This creates a tension that the Federal Reserve cannot easily resolve. The bank's baseline assumes the Fed holds rates steady through 2026 and begins cutting only in early 2027, with 25 basis point cuts in March and June to a terminal range of 3.0-3.25%. But the report also acknowledges a deeper question: what if the neutral rate of interest has moved higher? If r-star is rising, then the current policy stance may not be as restrictive as it appears, and the normalization cycle may already be over. The bank flags this explicitly: "If inflation is persistent, or r* is moving higher, then the normalization cycle is likely over."

This is the most consequential unresolved question in the report. The Fed is about to undergo a leadership transition, with incoming Chair Kevin Warsh potentially pushing for changes in communication policy and balance sheet strategy. The report suggests a Warsh-led Fed may talk less, creating more short-term policy uncertainty. But the deeper issue is whether the Fed's framework itself needs to adapt to a world where supply shocks are frequent, fiscal deficits are persistent, and the natural rate of interest is higher. The bank's analysis implies that the old playbook of cutting rates at the first sign of economic softness is no longer viable. The bar for easing has genuinely risen.

For readers, the actionable insight is that inflation risk is now two-sided in a way it has not been for decades. Energy and tariffs push it up; AI-driven productivity pushes it down. The outcome depends on which force dominates and on how the Fed interprets the data. The bank is optimistic on inflation moderating in 2027, but that optimism is conditional on a benign de-escalation of the Middle East conflict and on AI productivity gains materializing faster than AI demand effects.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Labor Market Paradox and the Productivity Puzzle

The bank's labor market outlook is unusually cautious. It projects employment growth of only 50-60 thousand jobs per month in 2026 and 2027, with the unemployment rate falling to just 4.1% in 2027. That is a very slow improvement from the current 4.5% level. The report attributes this to a "curious balance" that persists in the labor market—a phrase that signals uncertainty rather than conviction.

The special topics section on AI and labor markets is revealing. The bank finds that AI displacement is visible in narrow, early-stage data but not yet a macro labor story. Productivity is rising faster in high-AI-exposure industries, but the mechanism is faster output growth and capital deepening rather than outright labor sUBStitution. This is the productivity puzzle: if AI is boosting output without displacing workers, then the traditional relationship between growth and employment may be shifting. But the bank also notes that AI adoption puts "some minimal upward pressure" on the unemployment rate. The net effect is ambiguous.

What the report does not fully answer is whether this ambiguity is temporary or permanent. If AI productivity gains accelerate, the economy could grow faster with less labor—a scenario that would be disinflationary but socially disruptive. If AI demand effects dominate, the economy could overheat. The bank's alternative scenarios capture both possibilities: one where AI boosts productivity but pushes unemployment to 4.8%, and another where aggregate demand surges and the Fed has to hike. But the baseline does not resolve which path is more likely. This is the open question that should drive further research and discussion.

For decision-makers, the implication is that labor market data will be unusually noisy and difficult to interpret over the next two years. Traditional indicators like the unemployment rate and jobless claims may not tell a clear story. The signal will be in the composition of hiring—which sectors are adding jobs, which are shedding them, and whether the AI-related displacement is concentrated or broad.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Capex-Over-Consumption Regime

Translating this analysis into actionable decisions requires a framework that goes beyond forecasting. Here is a structured approach for readers to apply the report's logic to their own portfolios, corporate strategies, or policy positions.

First, assess your exposure to the consumer versus the capex cycle. If your revenue depends on discretionary consumer spending, the next 12-18 months will be challenging. The energy tax on households is real, and fiscal support is absent. If your business supplies equipment, software, infrastructure, or power to the AI buildout, the tailwind is structural. The key question is whether your specific sub-sector is part of the hyperscaler supply chain or the broader industrial ecosystem.

Second, evaluate your inflation sensitivity. The bank's analysis suggests that inflation will remain above target through 2027, but the composition matters. Energy-sensitive businesses face immediate cost pressures. AI-sensitive businesses face demand-driven price increases. The Fed's response will be data-dependent, but the bar for cuts is high. If you are a borrower, assume rates stay at current levels through 2026. If you are a lender, the risk is that inflation persists and rates rise further.

Third, build scenario awareness. The report outlines four alternative scenarios, each with dramatically different outcomes for growth, inflation, and rates. The baseline is a benign de-escalation, but the bank explicitly states that "multiple paths for the US economy remain viable and baseline forecasts are less relevant than normal." Decision-makers should stress-test their positions against a permanent oil premium scenario, an AI productivity boom with labor displacement, and a global recession scenario where oil hits $140-160 per barrel. The worst mistake would be to anchor on the baseline and ignore tail risks.

Fourth, monitor the Fed's regime shift. The transition to a new Chair and potential changes in communication policy will create short-term uncertainty. But the structural question is whether the Fed's reaction function has changed. If the bank is right that r-star is moving higher, then the terminal rate may be higher than markets expect. That has implications for duration positioning, currency strategy, and equity valuation.

## The Unresolved Questions That Make This Outlook Worth Studying

The bank's mid-year outlook is analytically rigorous, but it leaves several meaningful questions open. Why is AI capex so resilient to geopolitical shocks? The report says it is structural, but it does not fully explain the mechanism. Is it because hyperscalers have long lead times and cannot easily pause? Is it because AI is viewed as a national security priority? Or is it simply that the returns to AI investment are so high that they overwhelm geopolitical risk? The answer matters for assessing whether the capex cycle can survive a deeper crisis.

How should investors distinguish between temporary and permanent inflation? The bank expects energy-driven inflation to fade out of year-on-year rates by 2027, but AI-driven demand effects may persist. The distinction between cyclical and structural inflation is the central challenge for asset allocation. If the bank is wrong and inflation proves more persistent, the Fed may be forced to hike, not cut.

What happens to the labor market if AI productivity gains accelerate faster than expected? The bank's alternative scenario of unemployment rising to 4.8% is not a tail event. It is a plausible outcome if automation displaces workers in sectors like customer service, logistics, and data processing. The social and political consequences of that scenario are not addressed in the report, but they would ripple through fiscal policy, consumer spending, and ultimately the capex cycle itself.

These are not gaps in the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of any forecast. The bank is honest about what it knows and what it does not. For serious readers, that honesty is more valuable than false certainty.

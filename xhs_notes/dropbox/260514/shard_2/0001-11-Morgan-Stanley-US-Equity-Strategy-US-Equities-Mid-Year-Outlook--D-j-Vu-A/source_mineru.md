# Déjà Vu All Over Again...with Some Twists

If the first half of this year feels familiar, it's because it is to some extent. In several respects, the path of equities over the past few months has followed a script we've seen many times—markets weakening under the surface well ahead of the headlines, many focusing on the “new” risk after prices have already adjusted, and sentiment deteriorating just as the forward setup is quietly improving. The main difference relative to last spring is where we are in the economic and earnings cycles.

A year ago, we were coming out of the tail end of a rolling recession that was punctuated by a recession in the government sector (aka DOGE). Today, we are still in the early stages of a rolling recovery. That distinction is important when it comes to how one should interpret the correction we've just experienced and, more critically, how to position for what comes next. From our perspective, the key message at mid-year is straightforward and consistent with our messaging over the past several months: the correction we experienced was one within a well-established bull market.

First, it's worth revisiting what has actually taken place beneath the surface of the market over the last few months. Much has been made of the fact that the S&P 500 decline was less than $10\%$ on a price basis at the March lows. However, that view overlooks the more important adjustment that took place—namely, a significant reset on valuations and breadth. Roughly half of the stocks in the broader market (Russell 3000) experienced drawdowns of $20\%$ or more, and the forward P/E multiple for the S&P compressed by $18\%$ from its peak as forward earnings continued to surge even throughout the conflict (another hallmark of a bull market correction; Exhibit 5). That's not complacency, in our view, but a market that did a sUBStantial amount of work to price in the numerous risks that appeared over the past 6 months—Iran war/oil spike, AI disruption and private credit concerns being the most significant. In the areas of the market more directly affected by these risks, we witnessed $40\%+$ corrections in many instances.

We think it's worth focusing on time, valuation and price when evaluating the extent to which uncertainty/risks have been discounted. In terms of the recent drawdown, all 3 conditions were met, in our view. The correction began last fall as liquidity tightened and funding markets showed signs of stress. It progressed through the winter as concerns around AI disruption, private credit, and policy uncertainty weighed on sentiment. It then culminated at the end of March as geopolitical tensions and the spike in oil prices provided the final catalyst for capitulation in the primary indices. The natural question at the time was whether the spike in oil prices represented the type of late-cycle shock that has historically ended business cycles and led to recessions? Our answer was, and remains, "no."

The main reason comes back to earnings and our rolling recession-to-recovery thesis. In prior cycles where oil shocks derailed growth, earnings were already decelerating or contracting before the shock occurred. Today, the opposite is true. Earnings growth is accelerating from already well above average levels, supported by a combination of operating leverage, improving demand, capital spending and structural tailwinds like AI-driven efficiency gains.

The magnitude of the oil move itself is also less significant than in those historical episodes (which were 100%+ year-over-year), both in nominal and real terms. Adjusting for the CPI, US gasoline prices are materially lower than where they were after the Russia/Ukraine conflict started, and oil spiked to similar levels (Exhibit 10). Relative to the price of gold (another proxy for broad inflation), gasoline is even cheaper today and close to a 20-year low.

We would also argue that the global economy is more resilient than many assume, with a proven ability to navigate supply disruptions more effectively than expected. In our view, this situation is unlikely to be different, as market participants adjust logistics and identify alternative solutions and even sUBStitution. Recall that nearly 4 years ago, following the destruction of the Nord Stream pipeline, Germany was able to construct LNG facilities within months—an outcome widely considered unlikely at the time—reinforcing the idea that necessity drives innovation. We would not underestimate that same flexibility today. Finally, there is likely some demand destruction as well which will help to balance the market.

Cost impacts from the Iran conflict are likely to be felt idiosyncratically vs. at the index/sector level since: the higher-end consumer remains strong, the Energy sector is now a notable positive contributor to EPS growth/revisions this year (Exhibit 8) amid higher oil prices, and commodity hedging/inventory practices differ at the company level. Today's index composition (50% Tech; Exhibit 9) is also more insulated, contributing to this dynamic.

As discussed for the past 12 months and dating back to our last mid-year outlook, the main driver of our constructive view on US equities is a rebound in earnings growth. We see this continuing throughout our forecast horizon as detailed in this report. Recent earnings results are supportive of our stance, and we're encouraged by the resiliency displayed this reporting season despite the geopolitical backdrop. Exhibit 11 shows the fairly unprecedented acceleration of current year EPS expectations relative to the typical seasonal/historical trend. Further, revenue surprise is robust at \~2% (versus a historical average of 1.3%). This, coupled with higher ISM Manufacturing Prices, indicates that pricing power continues to improve for corporates.

While hyperscaler and semiconductor revenue/earnings revisions have been major contributors to this durability, and recent results bolster that dynamic (hyperscalers seeing acceleration in cloud + solid backlogs), the strength is not limited to these cohorts. Specifically, 1Q EPS surprise for the median S&P 500 stock is 6%, the strongest it has been in 4 years (Exhibit 12), and S&P 1500 median stock forward EPS growth has also accelerated—now 12%; up from 8% at the start of the year. In addition, S&P 600 Small Cap Index forward EPS growth is 22%, up from 8% at the start of the year. Furthermore, S&P 500 earnings revisions breadth has accelerated higher since reporting season started (back up to 22% from 5% at the beginning of earnings season) driven by cyclical cohorts (Exhibit 13 and Exhibit 14).

Operating leverage has and continues to play a major role in the earnings rebound we've been focused on for the last year. Much of this has been driven by compressed cost structures amid a deceleration in the second derivative on compensation expense since the end of 2021. This is a classic early cycle dynamic, and not surprising to us given our rolling recovery view. More recently, AI is helping to facilitate this operating leverage and efficiency dynamic, and we expect this to continue in a more material way over a structural time horizon. As our economists recently showed, output per employee is picking up in "high-AI exposure" industries (Exhibit 17). Further, our systematic transcript analysis shows that companies are increasingly quantifying the benefits they are seeing from AI Adoption. Among the S&P 500, 25% mentioned at least one quantitative impact in 1Q26, up from 13% in 1Q25 (Exhibit 18). While gains have been broad, Technology and Financials lead the pack. Another effect of AI is that companies know operational change/ efficiencies are coming and want to get leaner on labor even in advance of full implementation of AI applications. This is leading to less hiring rather than firing but is having a positive impact on the bottom line as revenue growth firms.

It's also important to recognize that this risk around the Fed's reaction function is inherently self-correcting. Financial conditions can only tighten so much before they begin to impact the real economy and, ultimately, force a response from policy makers. Bond volatility, funding stress, and liquidity conditions are the variables to watch in this regard. We have already seen early signs of this dynamic at work, with the Fed and other central banks showing a strong willingness to adjust their balance sheet policies, when necessary. More specifically, when funding markets showed stress last fall, the Fed reacted by ending quantitative tightening earlier than expected and then restarting asset purchases in December. These asset purchases continue today even with funding market stress now absent. Second, when bond volatility increased in March with the spike in oil prices, the Treasury was quick to act with its bond repurchase program. More specifically, the Treasury has bought approximately \$100-125 in the last 2 months focusing its actions on the 10-30 year tenor relative to issuance. This has helped reduce bond volatility and had a positive impact on liquidity and equity multiples, in our view. There is nothing to suggest these activities (Fed's RMPs and Treasury buybacks) won't continue into the future, under the labeling of financial market stability.

It's also worth addressing the broader policy framework we laid out in our year-ahead outlook, which remains intact. In short, the Trump administration's policy objectives center on a simultaneous three-pronged rebalancing of the economy:

# The Triple Rebalancing:

- Current / Trade Accounts—The "International" Imbalance
  - Policy: Tariffs / Weaker USD

\- Over-Consumption and Under-Investment—The "Domestic" Imbalance
  - Policy: Tariffs / Weaker USD and OBBBA Tax Incentives for Capex/Reshoring

\- The K-Economy—The "Inequality" Imbalance
  - Policy: Immigration Policy and De-Regulation

In our view, the end goal is the same as the prior administration's—to grow out of the debt problem—but the approach is focused on companies making the capital allocation decisions, rather than the government, while increasing income through the wage channel rather than open borders and sUBSidies. The result is higher nominal GDP growth with a greater contribution from real GDP via higher productivity and earnings (both corporate and personal).

We believe there is now evidence that this rebalancing is happening. First, the trade deficit is down materially year-over-year, albeit against very easy comparisons from last year's stocking up ahead of Liberation Day. Nevertheless, as a percentage of GDP the trade deficit has improved over the past 18 months. Second, fixed investment is up sharply since the Big Beautiful Bill was passed last year, and March saw another upside surprise to factory orders in spite of all of the concerns cited above. US businesses are moving forward with capital spending plans as if war-related concerns are temporary or immaterial to their capital allocation decisions that are likely being driven more by the new policies in place and a need to adapt to a new world order of Multi-polarity / "Don"roe Doctrine and De-globalization.

Finally, we would like to re-introduce a 4th pillar to our rebalancing thesis: more economic growth via the private economy, rather than the government. On this front, we would note that DOGE did have a major impact on the number of federal government employees, which saw a sharp 10% fall y/y, taking Federal government payrolls to its lowest level in several decades. Second, the non-farm payroll data for March and April showed a 190k and 123k increase in private payrolls, respectively. Meanwhile, non-private jobs continued to decrease in both months. This may be an early indicator that this rebalancing back toward the private economy is beginning in earnest. Immigration changes may also raise real income growth for types of work not likely to be replaced by AI anytime soon—i.e., service workers in hotels, restaurants and workers in agriculture, landscaping and construction. While some might lament this dynamic as inflationary, we would argue this is the kind of inflation we need to rebalance many of the inequities over the past several decades. It could also help to alleviate some of the heightened concern we hear about how labor is going to be completely marginalized by AI.

Our rebalancing thesis is starting to play out and underlies our more bullish view than most on where the economy will be in 6 months. We think the market understands this dynamic, which is another reason why the rally has been so powerful over the past 6 weeks.

# Forecast Update

Strong earnings facilitate a continuation of the rolling recovery. This mid-year outlook builds on three themes we have emphasized since last year: running the economy/earnings cycle hot, public to private rebalancing, and a broadening of earnings and performance. Our view dating back to last spring has been that Liberation Day marked the trough of the rolling recession and the start of the rolling recovery. Over the next 12 months, we see the rolling recovery continuing to progress, driven by a strong earnings environment as positive operating leverage persists and is further enhanced by AI adoption. Resiliency in earnings data despite geopolitical risk, private credit concerns and AI disruption is supportive of our view. Specifically, 1Q EPS surprise for the median S&P 500 stock is 6%, the strongest it has been in 4 years, and S&P 500 earnings revisions breadth has accelerated higher since reporting season started (back up to 22% from 5% at the beginning of earnings season).

Our mid-year 2027 S&P 500 price target is 8,300 (20.5x forward EPS of \$404). We see 2026 EPS of \$339 (23% growth), 2027 EPS of \$380 (12% growth), and 2028 EPS of \$429 (13% growth). Positive operating leverage, AI adoption/"run it lean", improving pricing power and an AI capex cycle that continues to show momentum are key drivers of our constructive view on earnings. We continue to diverge from consensus in our call for a broadening in earnings growth and market leadership this year. While strength in S&P 500 EPS growth has been more widely discussed recently, S&P 1500 median stock forward EPS growth has also accelerated—now 12%; up from 8% at the start of the year. With the prospect of near-term Fed cuts fading based on market pricing/our economists' forecast, and the rolling recovery entering a more mature phase over the next 12 months, we see modest multiple compression (to 20.5x) from current levels (21.2x). In short, our bullish index view is an earnings story, not a multiple expansion one. The primary risks to our constructive stance are that our "run it hot" thesis leads to an inflationary impulse the Fed can't ignore and/or they underestimate how much capital the economy is using for investment and the rolling recovery, and we see tighter liquidity. Our year-end 2026 S&P 500 price target also moves higher to 8,000 from 7,800 previously—a reflection of earnings durability.

Sector/style recommendations: We see cyclicals outperforming defensives, which is consistent with our rolling recovery/earnings acceleration thesis. Small cap earnings are inflecting higher (forward growth is 22%), but less accommodative than expected monetary policy in 2026 means small caps remain equal weight relative to large caps for the time being. In terms of sector preferences, we favor Industrials, Financials, and Consumer Discretionary Goods. # Bull Case Scenario

In our bull case scenario (9,400 price target), earnings expansion exceeds what we assume in the base case. We model EPS growth of 28% in 2026, 18% in 2027, and 16% in 2028. The upside is driven by a more pronounced operating leverage story. This is supported by AI-driven productivity gains achieved by companies running it hot, too. In some cases, the productivity is driven by AI directly and indirectly as companies hire less in advance of the technology being fully implemented. This drives margin expansion alongside stronger revenue growth, which is underpinned by resilient demand across AI infrastructure plays and more pro-cyclical areas (i.e., transports, chemicals, consumer, etc.). A resolution to the Iran conflict is reached in the near-term, allowing oil prices to fall back to pre-war levels, and cost risks related to fertilizer, petrochemicals, and other key commodities (i.e., sulphur) are mitigated quickly. The associated relief in gasoline prices plus improvement in real wage growth for lower income consumers leads to a shift in the bifurcated nature of the k-shaped economy as lower income consumers catch up and drive overall consumption. Corporates have no issue passing through higher prices to end markets/consumer, yet this inflation does not show up in core CPI/PCE in a way that causes the Fed to hike rates. Importantly, our bull case is also an earnings story, not a multiple expansion story (multiple remains flat at 21x in our bull case); the differentiation between the 2 scenarios comes down to degree of earnings growth upside driven by the aforementioned factors.

From a policy perspective, the Fed refrains from hiking rates at least through mid-year next year even as growth runs hotter as lagging services and housing inflation keep a lid on core CPI/PCE. New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh does not wind up being hawkish on the balance sheet through mid-2027, and the Fed/Treasury even provide additional liquidity through RMPs and buybacks. Back-end rates and bond volatility stay contained via a lack of back-end issuance. On the fiscal policy front, bonus depreciation and immediate R&D expensing further catalyze a broad-based capex rebound, benefiting industrials, technology, and commodities. Loan growth and capital markets activity exceeds expectations and private credit risks remain contained/limited. International demand for U.S. equities is strong amid superior earnings growth and structural advantages, while retail participation remains robust as capital rotates out of money market funds and into equities. The U.S. dollar weakens more than in our base outlook, as narrower front-end rate differentials outweigh demand for dollar assets, providing an additional tailwind for multinational earnings.

# Bear Case Scenario

Our bear case scenario (5,900) assumes that the Fed has to lean in a more hawkish direction on rates as a result of inflationary pressures that return. Inflation in this scenario would be driven by a combination of strong, preceding demand, the fiscal impulse, lagging tariff impacts, and energy price pass through—running it hot has negative repercussions. In addition to rate hikes, the Fed under new leadership from Kevin Warsh also gets more hawkish on asset holdings, laying out plans to shrink the size of the balance sheet. Valuation de-rates by \~2-turns as bond volatility and funding market stress rises. Earnings growth also slows materially as back-end yields rise toward/through 5% without Treasury buybacks and RMP action, and act as a headwind for demand/top line and capex into 2027. In other words, this setup shares similarities with 2022: while the economy avoids a formal recession, earnings come under pressure due to higher rates—aka, Fire and Ice as the hotter but shorter cycle ends. In this backdrop, higher-beta and longer-duration equities are likely to lag. This scenario is very unlikely to play out before year end but ironically its probability increases if our bull case plays out in the 2H of this year and an inflation impulse lags a historic demand recovery.

# A Broadening Capex Cycle Remains Underappreciated

We entered 2026 with a view that the capex cycle was gaining momentum as a result of 3 tailwinds—(1) a strong EPS backdrop from the rolling recovery (earnings/capex cycles are positively correlated), (2) tax incentives from the OBBBA and (3) the structural support from the AI data center build-out and manufacturing renaissance (reshoring / "Don"roe Doctrine). Early indications on this front are supportive with median stock capex growth running 5-10%, and our factor work continuing to show that the market is rewarding high capex/sales on an equal weighted basis. These tailwinds are likely to stay with us in 2026. We believe Multi-Industry remains the best pure-play across these themes, while Regional Banks are an underappreciated winner amid a C&I loan growth inflection. See our Trade Ideas section for more.

In tandem with this theme, there has been a lot of focus on "hard/heavy asset, low obsolescence" (HALO) areas of the market recently as these stocks have been seen as relatively insulated from AI disruption. While these cohorts indeed outperformed while the market was focused on AI disruption earlier this year, their relative strength is not new. In fact a number of these groups like Multi-Industry and Materials/Metals showed outperformance dating back to 2025. In our view, this outperformance has more to do with the broadening of the capex cycle in an early cycle backdrop than anything else, though rotation away from areas perceived to be AI-disrupted has played a role more recently.

# The AI Capex, Disruption and Labor Debate

AI-related uncertainty like we saw earlier this year is not uncommon in major investment cycles where volatility bands can widen and dispersion is higher at times. That said, fundamental tailwinds remain in place for the AI enabler complex, and the AI adopter trade remains underappreciated, in our view. The current status of (1) the affiliated credit cycle (relatively early innings), (2) the cost of capital (benign so far/the Fed's not hiking), (3) the cost of compute (still elevated) and (4) valuation (still undemanding for many of the AI leaders) does not indicate a market environment that's ready to structurally impose capex discipline on the major AI infrastructure providers. While this has been an out of consensus view at times this year amid anecdotal examples of capex discipline, our

systematic factor work continues to show that the market is actually rewarding higher capital spending on an equal weighted basis. Until the four variables noted above shift to a more adverse posture, we continue to think mega cap tech stocks/Mag 7/hyperscalers are likely to trade more off revenue revisions versus capex/FCF expectations.

On AI disruption, the narrative has moved faster than actual implementation. The enterprise application layer is still in early days, and in the near term, AI looks more likely to support, rather than compress, margins, particularly for early adopters. It also gives companies an excuse and an incentive to keep hiring low, as this new economic cycle continues to lead to operating leverage surprises on the upside. This positive operating leverage is surfacing in earnings growth and breadth as the median company is growing EPS in the double digits—the fastest pace since 2021. Furthermore, to get realization of efficiencies, don’t we first need the broader buildout of the agentic application layer and the sUBSequent workflow integration to occur at the enterprise level?

In our view, the investment case for owning AI adopters continues to strengthen, helping to counter disruption fears. This is particularly relevant because many of the areas perceived to be disrupted are also well-positioned as adopters based on our thematic research team's AI mapping work and our industry-level work (i.e., Transports, Software & Services, Professional Services). In fact, forward net margin expectations continue to accelerate for adopters with significant/core to thesis materiality and neutral/high pricing power (Exhibit 37). At the index level, we continue to expect 100bps of net margin expansion cumulatively through 2027 as a result of AI adoption.

Given that Midterm elections coincide with our forecast horizon, we highlight recent work on US equity markets around midterm elections. For more cross-asset views, see the full note here: US Public Policy: Mapping the Midterms: A First Look.

# US Equity Strategy: Stay the Course

- Election outcomes may drive short term rotation, but we think the business cycle path ultimately outweighs the policy agenda.   
- We agree with our policy strategists that the policy vectors likely most important for markets through the end of President Trump's term will remain under his control.   
- Historical seasonality is a modest headwind from May through September. Seasonality improves at the start of October and US equities historically see +22% returns in the 12 months following the midterms (early November).

While history shows lower annualized returns for equities in the second year of Presidential cycles relative to the other three years, we'd point out that returns are still solid in absolute terms (+9%). Furthermore, earnings growth today far surpasses the historical precedent in second year periods of Presidential cycles (Exhibit 41). This speaks to a point we've long been partial to, which is that the path of the business cycle ultimately outweighs the policy agenda. With forward 12-month earnings growth north of 20%, the business cycle inflecting and our expectation for the Fed to cut rates later this year, we think returns in 2026 are likely to be stronger than the historical precedent suggests. The other variable to consider is that we've just experienced a 10% correction (18% in P/E terms) due to a host of risk variables. History suggests it's rare to see more than one 10% correction over the course of a year (just 5 instances in the last 55 years). So in this sense, we've likely pulled forward some of the volatility often seen into midterm elections.

We agree with our policy strategists' view discussed above that the policy vectors likely most important for markets through the end of President Trump's term will remain under his control—i.e., foreign policy (US-Iran, Venezuela, US-China, etc.), tariffs (USMCA, US-China, EU, etc.), de-regulation, and immigration controls. While there could be some rotational dynamics in a divided Congress outcome as discussed in the industry sections (i.e., within Healthcare and Industrials), we don't see a divided outcome as a material, market-moving event if it does play out via a Democrat win of the House and a Republican win of the Senate. As noted above, if Democrats secure both the House and Senate but remain internally fragmented or unable to converge around a particular policy agenda, it could have a similar gridlock-type result as a Democratic House and a Republican Senate—likely not a major, market-moving result. In that case, the current policy trajectory would remain the same for the next 2-3 years, a trajectory we believe is equity bullish.

The remaining scenario involves a Democratic win of both chambers where the party is able to build a clear consensus around a political agenda. While the President retains veto power in this outcome, the Democrats could extract concessions (see above). Perhaps more importantly for markets, investors may then look ahead to 2028 in this outcome with the assumption that the odds of a Democrat sweep (including the Presidency) appear likely to rise. This could lead the market to question the durability of the current policy path – specifically, de-regulation (which aids the Banks and small/mid caps), the full scope of the One Big Beautiful Bill (which has helped Industrials and Technology), and the rebalancing from government to the private sector that we have been focused on (see here and here). In our view, this could lead to an initial risk-off reaction function either before mid-terms if polling/prediction markets convincingly point in this direction or post midterms. Why not a more sustained risk-off move? The President has veto power and the 2028 Presidential election would still be 2 years away.

In terms of the historical performance precedent in midterm election years, defensives have typically outperformed in the months leading up to midterms with broad markets gaining 10% in 1Q but giving back half of these returns during Q2 and Q3 before bottoming at the start of Q4. Relative leadership during this period comes from Health Care, Staples, Utilities, and Telecom.

Seasonally, index returns bottom one month before the midterms (start of October) as markets digest political uncertainty in advance and then favor a more risk-on posture following the event. US equities see a +22% return in the 12 months following midterm elections and +19% in year three of the presidential cycle. Relative outperformance is driven by growth, cyclicals ex Energy, and small-cap factors after the midterm election while defensives are the relative laggards. On a sector level, nearly all sectors see strong absolute returns and relative winners are Technology and Consumer Discretionary while laggards are Energy, REITs and Utilities.

Our tracking of markets today versus historical seasonality shows a mixed picture depending if you look at the average stock (Russell 1000 equal weighted) or the S&P 500 (Exhibit 42). The average stock is up 14% year-to-date while the S&P 500 is up 5% after a strong recovery in April. While we remain respectful of Presidential cycle seasonality, we continue to believe that 2026 will be an above average market return year with strong EPS returns being driven by both strong top-line revenue growth and by margin expansion (Exhibit 43). More importantly, we continue to believe the recent rolling correction is occurring within a new bull market and economic cycle that began in April of last year, suggesting the expansion still has significant room to run.

# How Are Companies Discussing Benefits from AI Adoption?

One question we frequently receive from investors is: "to what extent are companies seeing quantifiable efficiency gains from AI adoption?" Last fall, in collaboration with the QuantWise team, we leveraged the GPT model to analyze approximately >15,000 conference transcripts and track how management teams are communicating the quantifiable benefits of AI adoption to investors. # AI Disruption Debate

Recent dislocation tied to AI disruption risk presents opportunities in well-positioned incumbents, strong growers and high quality names. Below, our analysts provide stock picks and broader views across impacted groups.

- Banks: We view banks as net AI beneficiaries. AI is already driving some operating leverage and should materially lift productivity and earnings over time. Core businesses remain resilient with strong investment banking pipelines, trading tailwinds from volatility, low disruption risk across deposits and custody, and healthy credit trends with normalization in losses already reflected in estimates.   
- Business Services: We have seen broad selling across the space, which we believe has impacted names that have a strong defensible position to AI threats given their proprietary data, strong brands, and regulatory barriers.   
- Consumer Finance: We view Consumer Finance stocks as net beneficiaries of AI. These stocks are highly sensitive to consumer health – any acceleration in AI-driven jobs losses would be a net negative. However, in our view, any short-term disruption can be offset by long-term efficiency gains in the underlying business of lending/payments. The core activities of card payments, fraud detection, underwriting, marketing, and loan servicing are data-rich and rules-based, and thus well-suited for AI. Within credit card, we are skeptical that agentic AI can disintermediate credit card interchange, given trust in the system/fraud protections, credit extension, and rewards.   
- Insurance: We believe AI should gradually improve insurance brokering, but complex contracts, tower construction, market access, regulatory review, compliance, and other factors still need expertise, and are unlikely to face near-term disruption.   
- Internet: We believe agentic commerce – in effect, the ability to have a personal digital interactive shopper – is set to be the best next sUBStantial GenAI-enabled unlock for internet. We see the consumer "e-commerce funnel" evolving to be more conversational, personalized, interactive.   
- Payments & Fintech: We see the payments ecosystem, and especially MA and V, as net beneficiaries of AI and agentic commerce. In particular, we expect that MA's and V's respective value-added services segments (44% of rev growing 22% in 4Q25 and 27% of revenue growing 28% in 4Q25, respectively) will see incremental demand. The emergence of AI-driven and AI-based transactions are very similar in nature to existing bot-based fraud attacks, which will increase the opportunity for both companies to provide incremental high-value fraud detection remediation. Further, MA and V feature transaction facets that are nearly unsurpassable, including cost, reliability, accuracy, speed, trust, and legality. We are watching if these payment network moats can be narrowed by fast growth by agentic networks (including the rapidly developing OpenClaw) which may opt for other schemes even if those alternative schemes (e.g., Ether) don't initially offer all the advantages of those of MA and V.   
- REITs and CRE Services: We think AI disintermediation risk in the CRE services space is low in the near and medium term. In our view, both the ability of an AI-

enabled tool to provide highly complex advice related to CRE transactions, and the willingness of enterprises to seek out such a tool for some of the most consequential CRE decisions they make, are high hurdles to jump in the near and medium term.

\- Software: The question is not whether Software will ultimately monetize in this innovation cycle, but rather who will participate in building out these additional capabilities.

• AI IS Software—GenAI fundamentally expands the capabilities of enterprise software – presenting opportunities along with risks, with the pullback giving attractive entry points in well positioned incumbents. The average software multiple is back into the range last seen during the peak uncertainty around Public Cloud (2014-2016). The bear case arguments around GenAI appear to give too little credence to the ability of incumbent software vendors to participate in this innovation cycle. Exhibiting an ability to participate in GenAI, with ramping product cycles, likely proves a necessary catalyst in addition to the attractive valuations.

\- Transportation: Net-net, we believe most Freight Transportation sUBSectors are more likely to be beneficiaries of AI adoption vs. outright victims of disruption, but the balance differs meaningfully by business model.

# The Mobile Era disruption analog also suggests a stock-specific approach. Areas

thought to be disrupted following the launch of the iPhone in 2007 included gaming, PC, printer/camera, GPS, and desktop software/engagement. From a data perspective, we think there are a few takeaways worth noting:

\- Median relative performance of related stocks was quite choppy in the years following the iPhone launch. The max drawdown of the series shown in Exhibit 54 was \~30%, though this period also included a \~40% rally.

- Relative valuation for the group compressed directionally over this period, while forward earnings showed more resilience.   
- Dispersion among stocks perceived to be disrupted in this time period was extremely high across performance, revenue, earnings and valuation. For example, the strongest performance came from Google/Alphabet (+28%), while the weakest came from Nokia (-73%).   
- We tested a number of fundamental variables and found the following explained forward performance post the iPhone announcement/launch with strong significance: change in forward earnings, change in forward sales, consistently strong operating margins (i.e., strong in 2007 and 5 years later), change in revenue share.   
- The strongest variable in terms of explaining forward returns post the iPhone announcement/launch was forward earnings (0.9 Spearman rank order correlation).

In our view, what's going on now is typical of a major investment cycle. Volatility bands tend to widen at times and there are intermittent periods along the way where markets question both the pace of capital spending and which areas of the market could be disrupted. Dispersion is also elevated, which is typical as the market contemplates relative leaders/laggards. What's somewhat unique about this capex boom, particularly relative to the late 1990s (for instance), is that today we're in an early cycle backdrop—specifically in terms of earnings as we've come off the rolling recession of 2022-2025. Thus, as capital is rotating out of certain areas, it's not just finding its way into structural leaders, it's also finding its way into cyclical leaders.

# Sectors and Screens

# Long Industrials

We're encouraged by the resiliency of earnings revisions and macro data points for both Capital Goods and Transportation despite risks from the Iran conflict. Specifically, earnings revisions breadth for both cohorts has inflected higher over the past several weeks. On the macro front, manufacturers' new orders data has continued its ascent as inventory/sales ratios remain benign/have declined. The ISM Manufacturing Index has held up quite well (remaining around 52-53) despite risks from the war, and manufacturing backlogs have increased materially over the past several months based on data from the survey. On the transports side, the Cass Freight Expenditure Index has risen significantly in growth rate terms since the rolling recession that transpired post-2022. While shipment growth has not seen the same inflection, it does look to be stabilizing around current levels. At the same time, we're seeing C&I loan growth improve, which is a leading indicator for industrial activity, and short-cycle organic revenue growth is also stabilizing. Lastly, the capex cycle is very much in gear and is a function of 3 distinct variables in our view: (1) a rebound in the earnings/business cycle as capex is correlated to EPS, (2) fiscal tailwinds from the OBBBA, and (3) the data center buildout. These developments are contributing to the strongest capex growth we have seen in years (see exhibits below); this is evident both for the AI-related complex and for the S&P 493.

1Q earnings season reinforced a more resilient industrial backdrop than many feared just a few months ago. Early reporters have generally exceeded expectations, with solid order growth, suggesting underlying demand is holding up despite geopolitical noise and cost pressures. At the same time, companies had set a relatively low bar for the quarter, which helped drive beats, but forward commentary has been more measured—particularly as margin expansion through the balance of the year is questioned and cost inflation begins to build into the outlook. This has left investors debating how much of the strong first-half performance reflects true end-market strength versus timing effects. We're more in the former camp versus the latter camp given our constructive views on the broader earnings/capex cycles and our Multi-Industry analyst Chris Snyder's long-standing reshoring thesis, but we acknowledge risks from pull-forward potential, the 232 tariff update and downstream cost impacts from the Iran conflict.

Chris Snyder's reshoring thesis resonates with us. In effect, reshoring is shifting the drivers of growth away from pure end-demand and toward supply-chain realignment, helping sustain industrial momentum even amid macro uncertainty. The best positioned pockets in the sector to benefit from this themes are those with relatively inelastic volumes, pricing power, and domestic exposure. The team is overweight: AYI, ROK, TT, ETN, VRT, RAL, JCI.

# Long Financials

Our Banks team led by Manan Gosalia has argued that the backdrop for his coverage is attractive given a significant release of excess capital alongside improving underlying business conditions. Currently, banks in the MS Large Cap Banks coverage hold roughly \$137B of excess capital which could rise to \$179B, pending upcoming regulatory changes such as Basel III Endgame and GSIB surcharge revisions. These changes are expected to expand balance sheet capacity meaningfully, allowing banks to support stronger lending, higher capital markets activity, and increased shareholder returns. Importantly, their work suggests that consensus estimates underestimate both the scale and pace at which banks will deploy this capital.

Our work shows that Banks forward earnings growth continues to rise (now 14%), while valuation is still attractive at 12x forward earnings for the overall group. For Financial Services more broadly, earnings revisions breadth has inflected higher once again post-reporting season, an incremental positive development. At the same time, loan growth is picking up and the Senior Loan Officer Survey points to continued upside momentum this year, benefitting the mid-size/regional banks (Exhibit 70). Capital markets/M&A beneficiaries within the sector should benefit from a pick-up in deal activity (Exhibit 71). Lastly, positioning is light in the sector based on our prime brokerage data (0 percentile) a sign that the excess capital release/positive earnings revisions story for the space remains out of consensus.

# Consumer Discretionary Goods Over Consumer Staples

The consumer backdrop in the US remains resilient despite higher gasoline prices given the large share of consumption driven by higher income households (top 20% of earners make up 40% of consumption) as well as the \~\$65B real personal income tailwind hitting this year from the OBBBA. As the exhibits below show, household liabilities as a share of household assets continue to fall, and households' corporate equity and real estate holdings continue to grow. Further, despite falling job openings, the labor market remains on solid footing and there are signs that wage growth at the low end is stabilizing. Clearly higher gas prices are having an adverse impact on lower income consumption trends; however, given the higher income wallet share, aggregate consumer earnings expectations are actually up 3% since the Iran war started (Exhibit 80).

Interestingly, we find that Consumer Staples is more sensitive to changes in energy prices than Consumer Discretionary (Exhibit 81); this is a result of lower income households having a larger share of staples spend vs. discretionary categories. Our relative preference for Discretionary over Staples also fits with our bullish view on the earnings cycle/business cycle, and the fact that Discretionary earnings revisions breadth is re-accelerating (Exhibit 83).

Why a preference for Discretionary Goods, in particular? For one, we're seeing signs of wallet share reversion from services to goods after multiple years of dominance the other way. In addition, consumer goods pricing is improving off of low levels, while pricing for services is decelerating. For consumer goods, this likely started as a response to tariffs, but we would argue many pockets within Discretionary needed a push to raise pricing after seeing persistent discounting for much of the past several years. Lastly, from a technical standpoint, Discretionary Goods relative performance looks to be stabilizing around 20-year+ support levels (Exhibit 85).

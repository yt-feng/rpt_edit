# US Travel Demand Is Reshuffling, Not Weakening: The Summer 2026 Survey Points to Structural Shifts Beneath the Surface

The conventional narrative heading into summer 2026 is one of cautious optimism tempered by geopolitical noise and persistent inflation. A fresh survey of over 1,000 US consumers conducted in April 2026, well after the escalation of Middle East tensions, tells a more nuanced story. Travel intentions are not merely holding up; they are strengthening in ways that challenge the prevailing bearish consensus. Forty-seven percent of respondents now expect to travel more than usual over the next twelve months, up from 43 percent a year ago. More striking, thirty percent plan to travel "significantly more" this summer compared to last, a fifty percent increase in the share of the most enthusiastic travelers. This is not a market that is buckling under pressure. It is a market that is reconfiguring its preferences, its booking channels, and its relationship with technology. The real question for investors is not whether travel will be strong, but which platforms and business models will capture the value of this reshuffling. The survey data provides a roadmap, but it also leaves critical questions unanswered, particularly around the durability of loyalty program economics and the true competitive threat from generative AI.

## The Resilience of Travel Intentions Masks a Growing Price Sensitivity That Will Favor Platforms with Loyalty Depth and Inventory Breadth

The headline numbers on travel intentions are unequivocally positive. Eighty-one percent of respondents plan to travel the same or more this summer versus last year. This is a clear signal that the US consumer, at least in the aggregate, is not retrenching on leisure travel despite higher airfares and hotel rates. However, the survey also reveals a critical fault line. When asked directly about the impact of higher prices, sixty-four percent of respondents said that costlier flights and hotel rates would affect their travel choices. This is a sUBStantial majority, and it represents a significant shift in sentiment. The share of respondents who said they would "delay or limit" their vacation plans if prices increased jumped from twenty percent in the 2025 survey to thirty percent in 2026. Meanwhile, the share who said they would "travel the same regardless" actually rose from fourteen to twenty percent, suggesting a bifurcation between price-insensitive and price-sensitive travelers.

This dynamic has direct and strategic implications for online travel agencies. The survey shows that price remains the most important factor when making online travel bookings, but its relative importance has declined from fifty-one percent to forty-two percent year-over-year. What has filled the gap is "convenience and ease of use," which surged from eighteen percent to thirty percent. This is a powerful finding. It suggests that travelers are not simply looking for the cheapest option; they are increasingly willing to pay a modest premium for a frictionless experience. Platforms that can combine competitive pricing with superior user interfaces, integrated loyalty programs, and seamless cross-channel booking will be best positioned to capture the growing cohort of travelers who prioritize convenience. This is a structural advantage for large OTAs with established technology stacks and loyalty ecosystems, particularly Booking Holdings and Expedia, over smaller players or direct hotel bookings that may lack the same level of integration.

## AI Adoption in Travel Planning Has Reached an Inflection Point, but the Battle for the Autonomous Booking Agent Is Still in Its Early Innings

The most dramatic year-over-year change in the survey is the acceleration of AI adoption for travel planning. Forty-four percent of respondents have now used ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude for travel planning, up from just eighteen percent in the 2025 survey. This is not a niche behavior. It represents a mainstreaming of generative AI as a travel research tool in a single year. Furthermore, forty percent have used Booking.com or Expedia's own AI chatbots, up from twenty-six percent. The implication is clear: consumers are rapidly becoming comfortable with AI-assisted travel planning, and they are using both standalone generative AI tools and embedded OTA chatbots.

However, the survey also reveals a critical gap between planning and execution. When asked about using an AI agent to autonomously book travel, only thirty-eight percent said they would do so today. Thirty percent are interested but not yet comfortable, and thirty-two percent said they would not use an AI agent for this purpose. This is the key strategic question for the industry. The planning phase is being disrupted quickly, but the booking phase remains a human-mediated activity for the majority of travelers. For OTAs, this creates both an opportunity and a threat. The opportunity is to capture users who start their journey with a generative AI tool but then move to an OTA platform for the actual transaction. The threat is that if generative AI tools, particularly those developed by large technology companies, become effective at booking directly or at steering users to specific platforms based on undisclosed algorithms, the OTA's role as the intermediary could be compressed.

The survey data suggests that OTA-embedded chatbots are still lagging behind standalone generative AI tools in terms of user adoption. This is a strategic risk. If consumers develop a habit of starting their travel planning with a general-purpose AI tool, the OTA may lose the critical first touchpoint in the customer journey. The platforms that can most effectively integrate generative AI into their own search and booking flows, and that can offer a differentiated experience that standalone tools cannot replicate, will be the winners. The battle for the autonomous booking agent is not yet decided, but the survey makes clear that the window for OTAs to establish their own AI credentials is narrowing.

## Hotel Loyalty Programs Are Gaining Ground, but the Genius Program's Surge Raises Questions About the Sustainability of OTA Loyalty Economics

Loyalty program membership is undergoing a significant shift. Hotel direct loyalty programs remain the most popular, with forty-four percent of respondents reporting membership, up from forty percent. This is a positive signal for hotel chains like Hilton and Marriott, which have invested heavily in their direct booking channels and loyalty ecosystems. However, the standout data point is the surge in Booking Holdings' Genius program. Membership jumped from thirteen percent in the 2025 survey to twenty-eight percent in 2026, more than doubling in a single year. Expedia's One Key program saw a more modest increase from twenty-one to twenty-two percent.

This divergence is strategically important. The Genius program's rapid growth suggests that Booking Holdings has successfully communicated the value proposition of its loyalty tier to a broader user base. Genius offers benefits such as discounts, free breakfast, and room upgrades, and its tiered structure incentivizes repeat bookings. The survey data indicates that this strategy is working. For Expedia, the relative stagnation of One Key membership is a concern, particularly given that the survey was conducted after the integration of Expedia's various loyalty programs into a single One Key currency. It may be that the One Key value proposition is less compelling to consumers, or that the marketing message has not yet fully penetrated.

The broader implication is about the economics of OTA loyalty programs. OTAs have historically been aggregators of inventory, not owners of customer relationships in the same way that hotel chains are. The rise of OTA loyalty programs represents an attempt to change this dynamic, to create a stickier relationship with the traveler that reduces the likelihood of direct booking. The Genius program's success suggests that this is possible, but it also raises a question that the survey does not fully answer: at what cost? Loyalty program benefits typically require the OTA to accept lower margins on bookings made by loyalty members, or to negotiate better terms from hotel partners. If Genius membership continues to grow rapidly, Booking Holdings may need to absorb more of the cost of these benefits, or risk alienating hotel partners who see their margins squeezed. The sustainability of OTA loyalty economics in a high-growth scenario is an open question that investors will need to monitor closely.

## The Search for Accommodation Is Shifting Away from Google and Toward OTAs, but the Starting Point for Travel Is Becoming More Fragmented

One of the most consequential findings in the survey relates to how consumers search for accommodations. For the first time in this survey series, Expedia has overtaken Google as the most popular site for searching for hotels or accommodations, with nineteen percent of respondents naming it as their top choice versus eighteen percent for Google. Booking.com also gained ground, rising from twelve to fifteen percent. This is a significant shift. Google has long been the dominant starting point for travel search, and its ability to monetize this traffic through its own hotel booking platform and advertising has been a competitive threat to OTAs.

The survey data suggests that OTAs are winning back share in the search phase. This is likely driven by a combination of factors: improved user interfaces, the integration of user reviews and ratings, and the growing appeal of loyalty program benefits that are only accessible through direct OTA booking. However, the picture is not entirely clear. In a separate question, respondents indicated increasing use of Google as a "travel starting point" versus OTAs. This apparent contradiction may reflect a distinction between general travel inspiration and specific accommodation search. Google may remain the preferred starting point for broad travel research, but when it comes to the specific task of finding and comparing accommodations, OTAs are gaining ground.

For investors, this is a critical distinction. The battle for the travel funnel is not a single battle; it is a series of battles at each stage of the customer journey. OTAs appear to be winning the accommodation search battle, but Google may still dominate the inspiration and initial research phase. The strategic implication is that OTAs need to continue investing in their search and discovery capabilities, including AI-driven personalization, to maintain this momentum. They also need to be aware that Google is unlikely to cede this ground without a fight, and that the search giant's deep pockets and AI capabilities make it a formidable competitor. The fragmentation of the travel starting point is a positive development for OTAs in the near term, but it also means that the competitive landscape is becoming more complex and more expensive to navigate.

## The Survey's Income Skew and the "No Preference" Shift in Accommodation Create Blind Spots That the Report Does Not Fully Address

Any survey-based analysis must account for its methodological limitations, and this survey has two notable blind spots. First, the report acknowledges that the 2026 survey sample skewed higher in household income compared to the 2025 survey, with fewer respondents earning under $50,000 and more earning over $100,000. This income skew may have inflated the positive travel intention data. Higher-income households are more likely to travel more, less likely to be deterred by price increases, and more likely to engage with loyalty programs and AI tools. The strength of the data, particularly the surge in "significantly more" travel intentions, may partly reflect the composition of the sample rather than a genuine acceleration of demand across all income cohorts.

Second, the survey introduced a new response option for accommodation preference: "no preference" between hotels and alternative accommodations. Fifteen percent of respondents selected this option, which is a meaningful share. This is a new category that did not exist in prior surveys, and its introduction complicates the year-over-year comparison. The share of respondents preferring hotels dropped from sixty-four to sixty percent, and the share preferring alternative accommodations dropped from thirty-six to twenty-five percent. It is possible that some of the respondents who previously chose alternative accommodations have simply moved into the "no preference" category, rather than actually shifting their behavior. The survey does not provide a clear answer on this point.

These blind spots are not fatal to the survey's conclusions, but they do mean that investors should be cautious about extrapolating the headline numbers. The true strength of travel demand, and the true trajectory of alternative accommodations versus hotels, may be less dramatic than the raw data suggests. The report's conclusion that the data is "most constructive for Expedia and then Airbnb" is a reasonable interpretation, but it is based on a sample that may overrepresent the very consumers who are most likely to use those platforms. A decision framework that accounts for this income skew would be more conservative on the upside and more attentive to downside risks if the economy softens.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask Before Acting on This Survey

For investors trying to translate this survey into actionable insights, three questions should guide the analysis.

First, which platforms are best positioned to capture the growing cohort of convenience-seeking travelers? The survey shows that price sensitivity is declining in relative importance while convenience is rising. This favors platforms with strong user interfaces, integrated loyalty programs, and seamless cross-device booking. Booking Holdings' Genius program surge and Expedia's overtaking of Google in accommodation search both suggest that these platforms are making progress. However, the survey also shows that hotel direct loyalty programs remain the most popular, which means that hotel chains are still a formidable competitive force.

Second, how will the AI adoption trend affect OTA economics? The rapid adoption of generative AI for travel planning is a positive for the industry in terms of overall travel demand, but it is a potential threat to OTA margins if it leads to greater price transparency or if it allows consumers to bypass OTA platforms entirely. The survey indicates that most consumers are not yet comfortable with autonomous AI booking, but this comfort level will likely increase over time. OTAs that invest in their own AI capabilities and that can offer a differentiated experience will be better positioned. Those that rely solely on their existing aggregation model may find themselves disintermediated.

Third, is the loyalty program growth sustainable? The Genius program's membership doubling is impressive, but it raises questions about the cost of acquiring and retaining these members. If the program's benefits are funded primarily by hotel partners, there is a risk of partner pushback. If they are funded by the OTA itself, there is a risk to margins. Investors should look for evidence of whether loyalty program membership is translating into higher booking frequency and higher average order value, or whether it is simply a cost center that is inflating top-line metrics without improving profitability.

The income skew in the sample and the introduction of the "no preference" accommodation category add layers of uncertainty that the headline numbers do not fully capture.

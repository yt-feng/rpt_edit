On July 20, Samsung Biologics announced its intention to acquire a 100% stake in PolyPeptide Group AG, a European peptide contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO) that has posted losses for three consecutive years. But for observers focused on China-based CDMO players such as Wuxi Apptec and Asymchem, the immediate question is whether this move reshapes the competitive landscape. Yet the deal also reveals something more subtle about the nature of competitive advantage in this industry. The barriers to entry in peptide CDMO are not merely technological; they are structural. Building a peptide manufacturing business from scratch requires years of investment in capacity, process development, and regulatory qualification. This is precisely why Chinese CDMOs have been investing heavily in capital expenditure over the past several years, and why Samsung Bio chose to acquire rather than build. The implication is clear: the existing leaders have a moat that cannot be crossed quickly.

What matters for observers is not whether Samsung Bio will eventually become a competitor, but whether it can do so in a timeframe that alters the earnings trajectory of the current leaders. The evidence suggests it cannot. The short-term strategic logic of the deal is about capability acquisition and integration, not capacity expansion. And history shows that running overseas CDMO facilities has been a persistent drag on margins for Chinese players, raising the question of whether Samsung Bio can manage PolyPeptide's turnaround more efficiently than its peers have managed similar integrations.

Samsung Bio will need to rationalize PolyPeptide's production footprint, align its quality systems with its own standards, and retain key talent. All of this takes time and carries execution risk.

Samsung Bio may be a world-class operator in biologics, but peptide manufacturing has its own distinct process chemistry and supply chain requirements.

Whether Samsung Bio can turn around PolyPeptide efficiently is therefore an open question. If it succeeds, it will have a credible peptide CDMO platform in perhaps two to three years. ## The Entry Barrier to Peptide CDMO Is Structural, Not Just Technological, and It Favors Incumbents

The report emphasizes that the entry barrier to peptide CDMO is "relatively high" because starting from scratch requires substantial effort and, more importantly, manufacturing capacity matters a lot. This is a critical framing because it distinguishes between two types of barriers: technological know-how and capacity scale.

Technological know-how can be acquired—that is exactly what Samsung Bio is doing by buying PolyPeptide. But capacity scale cannot be acquired overnight. It requires years of capital expenditure, regulatory qualification by clients, and the accumulation of manufacturing data that allows for process optimization. Wuxi Apptec and Asymchem have been investing in this for years. Their capex programs are not discretionary; they are structural responses to the fact that clients want suppliers who can handle both small-scale development and large-scale commercial production.

This explains why Samsung Bio chose to acquire rather than build. Building a greenfield peptide CDMO facility would take three to five years and would lack the track record needed to win contracts from risk-averse pharmaceutical companies. Acquiring PolyPeptide gives Samsung Bio an existing customer base, regulatory filings, and operational processes. But it does not give it the scale to compete with Wuxi Apptec's CNY 11 billion TIDES business.

The structural barrier also has a second-order implication: even if Samsung Bio successfully integrates PolyPeptide and begins to expand capacity, it will face a chicken-and-egg problem. To justify building more capacity, it needs more customer commitments. To win more customer commitments, it needs to demonstrate reliability at scale. This is a slow cycle. Incumbents like Wuxi Apptec have already completed that cycle and are now compounding their advantage.

If all three conditions materialize simultaneously, the thesis for Chinese CDMOs would need to be revisited. But as of now, none of these conditions are present. The framework suggests that observers should maintain their current view unless the data changes.

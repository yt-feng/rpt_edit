# The Strait of Hormuz Closure Has Turned Qatar From a Winter Supply Backstop Into a Structural Risk for Global LNG Markets

Since 12 July, not a single laden LNG vessel has crossed the Strait of Hormuz. Qatar, the world's third-largest LNG exporter, is now effectively cut off from its customers. The market has responded by pricing a risk premium into winter TTF contracts at around €60/MWh, but that level still assumes a resolution within weeks. The data suggests this assumption is dangerously optimistic. Qatar has roughly 20-25 days of operational buffer at current reduced loading rates of approximately 22% of capacity, with floating storage providing some additional cushion. Once onshore storage reaches tank-full—likely within 2-3 weeks—Qatar may be forced to shut down production entirely. From the point of any shutdown, a full restart would take another 2-3 months, meaning the Northern Hemisphere winter would already be underway. Europe, which had been relying on Qatar's full return by winter as a cornerstone of its supply planning, now faces a structural gap that spot LNG from the US cannot fill.

The prevailing market view before this escalation assumed that Qatar would be fully back on line by winter and that Europe could source additional spot cargoes as needed. That assumption kept TTF prices contained. It is now being tested in real time. The following analysis examines the mechanics of the Qatar supply squeeze, the limitations of US LNG as a replacement, the implications for European storage trajectories, and a decision framework for navigating the coming months.

## Qatar's Production Clock Is Ticking: Tank-Full Likely Within 2-3 Weeks, Followed by a 2-3 Month Restart Timeline

The arithmetic of Qatar's current situation is stark. At the onset of the conflict, Qatar had roughly five days of onshore storage capacity when operating at full liquefaction capacity. Today, with loadings running at about 22% of capacity on a 7-day moving average, that storage cushion mathematically extends to 20-25 days. Floating storage—23 LNG vessels currently sitting in the Gulf—adds some buffer, but the operational reality is more constrained than the math suggests.

The critical constraint is not just total storage volume but the operational complexity of processing and storing rich versus lean LNG. Onshore facilities are not designed to handle both types indefinitely. Once the wrong gas type fills available storage, production must adjust or stop. Furthermore, not all 23 vessels in the Gulf are available to Qatar Energy for loading. Some belong to other owners, and the breakdown between already-loaded cargoes and available empty vessels is unclear. The report identifies 23 vessels in the Persian Gulf as of 20 July, but only 14 are owned by Qatar Energy. The rest are owned by ADNOC, Seapeak, Chinese banks, and other entities, each with their own operational priorities and insurance constraints.

The margin of error on the tank-full timeline is significant. But the direction of travel is not. If no transit through Hormuz resumes within the next 2-3 weeks, Qatar will likely be forced to shut down production again or drop output materially. From the point of any shutdown, a full restart would take another 2-3 months. By that time, the Northern Hemisphere winter would already be underway. The market is beginning to price this risk, but the current TTF premium of around €60/MWh still reflects an assumption of resolution that the physical data does not support.

The competitive dynamics of US spot cargoes offer little comfort for Europe. Netback analysis shows that JKM/TTF spreads remain largely flat for the next month or two. A sizeable TTF netback premium only emerges from November, implying higher reliance on LNG imports in winter. But this is a risky strategy for Europe given three compounding factors: historically low storage levels, rising uncertainties around Qatar supply, and normal winter weather risks in Europe, the US, and Asia.

The weekly export data confirms the pattern. During the week of July 13-19, LNG deliveries to Europe decreased by 0.6 Bcm week-over-week and 1.2 Bcm year-over-year. Deliveries to China increased by 1.5 Bcm week-over-week and 1.0 Bcm year-over-year. Other Asian importers also increased. The US cargoes that are available are flowing to Asia, not Europe. Europe's share of US Gulf Coast LNG exports remains below levels needed to correct the storage path.

The implication is clear: Europe cannot count on spot US LNG to fill the gap left by Qatar, at least not at current price levels. For US cargoes to divert from Asia to Europe in meaningful volumes, TTF would need to trade at a significant premium to JKM. That premium only appears in November under current forward curves. But by November, the winter heating season is already beginning, and storage levels will already be critically low if Qatar remains offline.

The weekly import data reveals a deteriorating picture for European storage. Total LNG deliveries to Europe decreased by 0.6 Bcm week-over-week and remain well below seasonal norms. ## Decision Framework: Three Scenarios for the Next 90 Days and Their Implications for Buyers, Traders, and Policymakers

The situation can be understood through three scenarios, each with distinct implications for market participants.

Scenario One: Hormuz reopens within 7-14 days. In this case, Qatar resumes exports at pre-conflict levels relatively quickly. The tank-full risk recedes. The winter supply outlook normalizes. TTF risk premium dissipates. This is the scenario currently priced into the forward curve. It is also the least likely, given that the conflict shows no signs of de-escalation and the last transit was on 12 July.

Scenario Two: Hormuz remains closed for 3-6 weeks. Qatar reaches tank-full and must shut down production. Restart takes 2-3 months. Winter supply gap emerges. TTF spikes sharply. Europe must compete aggressively for spot US cargoes, driving up global LNG prices. Asian buyers face higher costs. This scenario has not yet been priced in fully. The market is still discounting the probability of a prolonged closure.

Scenario Three: Hormuz remains closed for more than 6 weeks. Qatar shuts down. Restart is delayed into early 2027. Winter supply crisis in Europe. Storage depletion becomes acute. Governments may need to intervene with demand-side measures, including industrial gas curtailments. This is the tail risk that the market is not pricing at all.

For buyers and traders, the key decision is whether to hedge against Scenario Two or Three now, before prices move sharply higher. The cost of hedging is the current risk premium. The cost of not hedging is exposure to a winter supply crisis. For policymakers, the implication is that relying on spot markets to fill structural gaps is a high-risk strategy. The physical constraints on Qatar production, US export orientation, and European storage levels are not theoretical. They are measurable, and they are moving in the wrong direction.

The weekly data on global LNG supply shows a marginal decrease in overall loadings of 0.8 Bcm week-over-week, led by the US and rest of North America. This is not a dramatic drop, but it is consistent with a market that is beginning to feel the strain. The real test will come in the next 2-3 weeks, when Qatar's storage buffer runs out. The market will then have to confront the possibility that the winter supply assumption that has kept prices contained was never more than a hope.

EN_B: The core takeaway from this report is that while these measures could increase demand for JGBs, the impact is more about signaling and creating positive feedback loops, not about huge direct buying. The initial market reaction may have been a bit overdone.
EN_A: Signaling? That sounds a bit vague. Let’s break it down. What’s the main question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: The big question is whether tweaking pension fund rules and tax-free savings accounts can actually redirect Japan’s massive household savings into domestic assets, especially JGBs. And crucially, whether that can sustainably lower yields and reduce volatility.
EN_A: Okay, let’s start with the biggest lever: the Government Pension Investment Fund, or GPIF.
EN_B: Right. The GPIF manages about 2.8 trillion dollars. It targets a 25% allocation to domestic bonds. Under its current strategy, it has room to buy about 75 billion dollars more in JGBs.
EN_A: 75 billion sounds like a lot. But the report says it’s “not particularly large.” Why?
EN_B: Because the GPIF already has to buy JGBs just to keep its allocation steady. Strong equity returns and a weaker yen have pushed up the value of its foreign assets. So, it needs to rebalance by buying domestic bonds. The 75 billion is extra capacity on top of that ongoing buying.
EN_A: So active buying is different from mechanical rebalancing?
EN_B: Exactly. Active buying sends a stronger signal. The report estimates that shifting the domestic bond share from 27% to 31% would affect 30-year swap spreads by about 3 to 6 basis points. The market has already moved 8 basis points just this week on the news alone. So the market might be pricing in more than what’s actually there.
EN_A: What about the second idea? Letting people hold JGBs in their NISA tax-free accounts. That sounds like it could bring in a lot of retail money.
EN_B: The report is skeptical. Right now, Japanese households hold very few JGBs directly, only about 4% of their portfolio. But the report argues this is because yields have been so low for so long, not because of the tax treatment.
EN_A: So even with a tax break, retail investors might not bite?
EN_B: The report uses the example of Italy. In Italy, when retail ownership of government bonds goes up, it’s usually because yields are already rising, not the other way around. People buy after yields have gone up. So this measure might not be a driver of lower yields.
EN_A: So if both direct measures are limited, what does the report see as the real solution?
EN_B: It’s very clear. To get a sustainable decline in JGB volatility, you need changes in fiscal and monetary policy, not just supply-side tweaks. Most of the cheapening in the JGB curve has been driven by macro factors.
EN_A: So these are band-aids, not a cure?
EN_B: That’s a fair way to put it. The report sees some room for the 20-year and 30-year part of the curve to do better because of supply dynamics. But for the 10-year and the belly of the curve, the macro headwinds are too strong.
EN_A: It sounds like the report leaves a few things hanging.
EN_B: It does. For example, how fast would the GPIF actually adjust? The report notes the domestic bond share only changes by 1 to 2 percentage points per year. But what if the policy signal is strong enough to trigger a broader shift across the entire pension industry? That’s not fully answered.
EN_A: What else?
EN_B: Another one is the spillover effect. If Japan really starts pulling money home, what happens to other bond markets, like US Treasuries? The GPIF holds a lot of those. The report says the impact would be “limited,” but it doesn’t go deep into that scenario.

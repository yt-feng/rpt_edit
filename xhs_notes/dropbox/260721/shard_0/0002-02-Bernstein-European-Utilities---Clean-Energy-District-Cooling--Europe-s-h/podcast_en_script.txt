EN_B: The big picture is a 31 billion dollar global market that is being forced to grow faster by extreme weather. The core argument is that district cooling is not niche—it is becoming essential infrastructure for cities dealing with heat.
EN_A: So what exactly is this report trying to answer?
EN_B: It is asking: when heatwaves become the norm, can district cooling networks cope? And what does that mean for investors? The report starts with a real-world stress test from Paris. In June, the La Defense business district used up two-thirds of its ice storage in one day. Water temperature rose from 4.5 to 7.5 degrees Celsius. The system was not designed for that kind of heat.
EN_A: So this is not theory—it is a real pressure test. What are the key takeaways?
EN_B: First, efficiency. District cooling has a coefficient of performance of 6.5, compared to 3.0 for traditional air conditioning. That means more than double the cooling for the same electricity. In new developments, it can cut annual power use by 45 to 86 percent.
EN_A: And the second takeaway?
EN_B: It helps fight the urban heat island effect. Regular AC units dump heat outside, raising nighttime temperatures by over one degree in some cities. District cooling uses rivers or seawater for heat exchange, so it does not add to the problem. The report also points out that this reduces peak power demand and the need for new grid investments.
EN_A: But Europe only has 7 percent of the global district cooling market, right?
EN_B: Correct. The Middle East and Africa have 42 percent, North America 26 percent, Asia-Pacific 25 percent. Europe is behind, but France has set a target to double its cooling output by 2030 to over 2 terawatt-hours. That is policy plus heatwaves driving growth.

The report does not fully answer what happens if Europe accelerates investment. That is the open question.

EN_A: So we just went through a pretty detailed report on China’s economy. The headline is that growth slowed sharply in Q2. What’s the one thing investors should take away from this?
EN_B: The big question is whether policymakers will respond with another major easing package. The report draws a direct parallel to mid-2024, right before a big stimulus was announced. So the setup feels familiar: slowing growth, weak consumption, underperforming equity markets, and local governments under financial strain.
EN_A: How bad is the slowdown exactly?
EN_B: Real GDP growth dropped from 5.3 percent annualized in Q1 to 3.6 percent in Q2. Retail sales and fixed asset investment both weakened. The report breaks down the causes: about 40 percent of the slowdown came from a negative fiscal impulse, meaning the government actually tightened spending after a strong Q1. Another 30 percent is attributed to the Iran war disrupting chemical and oil production. The rest is weather and payback from Lunar New Year spending.
EN_A: So the government itself contributed to the slowdown. That’s ironic. But what about the so-called K-shape? We hear that term a lot.
EN_B: It’s very real in this data. Exports and high-tech manufacturing are booming. For example, 3D printer production was up 48 percent year on year, lithium-ion batteries up 39 percent, industrial robots up 28 percent. But the rest of the economy is struggling. Property sales down 14 percent in June, auto sales down 19 percent. Infrastructure investment fell 9.4 percent. So you have this two-speed economy.
EN_A: What does the report think policymakers should do?
EN_B: It argues for four shifts: more market-based, more long-term, more expansionary, and more demand-side policies. For instance, mortgage rates are still too high relative to rental yields. The report says lowering effective mortgage rates would be more effective than having local governments buy back empty apartments. Also, short-term subsidies like the trade-in program only create temporary spikes. Home appliance sales went from plus 53 percent last May to minus 15 percent this May.
EN_A: So the right policies are clear. But will the government actually do them?
EN_B: That’s the tension. From a pure macro perspective, they need easing. But geopolitical tensions and high debt make them cautious. They prioritize security and tech. Still, the report expects stronger easing rhetoric at the July Politburo meeting. The likely path is first to accelerate existing tools: there’s 800 billion yuan in policy-based financing that hasn’t been used yet. That alone could boost GDP by 0.5 percentage points. Then they might tap into unused bond quotas from previous years. Only as a last resort would they ask for new fiscal approval from the National People’s Congress.
EN_A: So there’s a clear ladder of steps. What could go wrong?
EN_B: Two risks. One is complacency at the central level. Base effects improve in the second half, so they might think they only need 4.3 percent growth in H2 to hit the full-year target. That could make them fiscally conservative. The other risk is local implementation. With anti-corruption campaigns and political turnover ahead of the next Party Congress, local officials might be reluctant to spend, even if funds are available. The report doesn’t fully answer how the central government would enforce spending if locals drag their feet.
EN_A: That sounds like a real wild card. So even if Beijing says the right things, the actual impact might be muted?
EN_B: Exactly. And that’s one of the questions the full report explores in more depth. Another is what happens if the July meeting disappoints. Will markets react as sharply as they did in mid-2024? Those are the details worth digging into.

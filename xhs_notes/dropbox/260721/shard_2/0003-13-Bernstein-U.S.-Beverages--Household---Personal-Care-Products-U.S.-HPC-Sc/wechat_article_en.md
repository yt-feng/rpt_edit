The contrast is not subtle.

This divergence matters because it challenges the assumption that large-cap HPC portfolios offer balanced, defensive growth. ## Beauty's Volume-Driven Growth Proves Consumer Willingness to Pay a Premium, Unlike the Price-Dependent Household Categories

P&G's Beauty segment generated 6.4% sales growth on the back of 4.7% volume growth and 1.7% price growth. This is a healthy mix. Consumers are buying more units, not merely paying higher prices for the same quantities. The category itself grew 3.4% year over year, confirming that the tailwind is market-wide rather than company-specific.

Contrast this with Fabric & Home Care, where P&G's 2.2% sales growth came from 3.2% price growth offset by a 1.0% volume decline. The category itself shrank 0.5%. P&G gained 0.7 percentage points of market share, but that share gain was achieved through pricing power in a declining category. This is not a sustainable growth model. When volume falls while prices rise, the consumer is being squeezed, and that pressure eventually creates an opening for private-label alternatives.

The same pattern appears in Baby, Feminine & Family Care. P&G's 0.5% sales growth came from 1.1% volume growth and a 0.6% price decline. The category grew 1.1%, but P&G lost 0.2 percentage points of market share. This is the segment most exposed to private-label competition in commoditized categories like bathroom tissue and disposable diapers. The report explicitly identifies these pressures as unlikely to dissipate.

Beauty, by contrast, operates in a different competitive dynamic. The category's 3.4% growth rate and P&G's 0.3 percentage point share gain indicate that branded innovation and consumer engagement still command premium positioning. The lesson is clear: in a low-growth macro environment, the ability to generate volume growth through product differentiation is the single most valuable competitive advantage.

## A Decision Framework for Allocating Capital Across HPC in a Two-Speed Market

The data from this scanner update supports a structured decision framework for those evaluating exposure to U.S. HPC. The framework rests on three axes: category growth trajectory, volume versus price mix, and private-label vulnerability.

The first axis separates categories into three tiers. Tier One includes Beauty and Pet Nutrition, where category growth exceeds 3%, branded share is stable or gaining, and volume growth is positive. These categories justify premium valuation multiples and warrant investment in innovation and marketing. Tier Two includes Oral Care and Home Care, where category growth is low but positive, and volume trends are mixed. These categories require careful cost management and selective innovation. Tier Three includes Personal Care, Baby Care, and Family Care, where category growth is near zero or negative, volume is declining, and private-label pressure is intensifying. These categories demand restructuring, not investment.

The second axis evaluates the quality of revenue growth. Volume-driven growth with stable or declining prices, as seen in Naturium and Colgate's Home Care, signals consumer adoption and potential for future pricing power. Price-driven growth with declining volume, as seen in P&G's Fabric & Home Care and Colgate's Personal Care, signals consumer resistance and eventual share loss. The ideal combination is balanced volume and price growth, as demonstrated by P&G's Beauty segment.

The third axis assesses private-label exposure. Categories with low brand differentiation, high price sensitivity, and minimal innovation cycles, such as bathroom tissue, paper towels, and basic household cleaners, are most vulnerable. Categories with high emotional involvement, frequent innovation, and strong brand communities, such as color cosmetics and premium skin care, are least vulnerable.

Applying this framework to the three covered companies yields clear observations. P&G's Beauty segment justifies its position as the portfolio's brightest spot, but the company's heavy exposure to Tier Three categories in Baby, Feminine & Family Care creates a drag that will persist. Colgate's Pet Nutrition business is the most attractive asset, while the Oral Care and Personal Care segments require strategic attention. e.l.f. Beauty's core cosmetics business is in Tier Three territory, while its Skin Care business, particularly Naturium, is in Tier One. The Hair Care entry provides optionality but not yet conviction.

## The Structural Shift Toward Beauty and Pet Is Not Cyclical, and Portfolio Rebalancing Is the Only Rational Response

The most important conclusion from this data is that the divergence between beauty and household categories is not a temporary phenomenon driven by consumer mood or seasonal factors. It reflects deeper shifts in spending priorities, demographic patterns, and competitive dynamics.

Beauty benefits from the convergence of social media influence, product innovation cycles measured in weeks rather than years, and a consumer base that increasingly views personal appearance as an extension of identity. These forces are secular, not cyclical. The category's 3.4% growth rate in a period when overall HPC categories are growing at 2.3% or less is evidence of structural outperformance.

Pet Nutrition benefits from the humanization of pets, the premiumization of pet food, and the demographic trend of smaller households where pets serve as companion substitutes for children. Colgate's Pet business growing at 6.5% in a 3.3% category is a testament to these forces.

Household and family care categories face the opposite dynamic. Categories like laundry detergent, bathroom tissue, and disposable diapers are mature, penetrated, and increasingly commoditized. Private-label penetration is rising because the value proposition is simple: the store brand performs adequately at a lower price. Innovation in these categories is incremental, not transformative. The report's identification of private-label challenges in Bathroom Tissue and smaller competitor pressure in Disposable Diapers as persistent issues confirms this structural reality.

For P&G, Colgate, and e.l.f., the strategic implication is that portfolio composition matters more than ever. Companies with disproportionate exposure to beauty and pet categories will generate superior organic growth and face less pricing pressure. Companies with heavy exposure to household and family care categories will face margin compression and volume stagnation regardless of execution quality.

The market's valuation of these companies reflects this reality only partially. P&G trades at 21.7 times forward earnings, Colgate at 23.8 times, and e.l.f. at 24.9 times. These multiples embed assumptions about growth that the scanner data does not fully support. e.l.f.'s multiple, in particular, appears to discount a cosmetics recovery that the data does not yet show. Colgate's multiple seems to price the Pet business's premium characteristics but may not fully reflect the Personal Care and Oral Care headwinds. P&G's multiple appears reasonable for a diversified staple company but offers little upside unless the Beauty segment's outperformance accelerates or the household segments stabilize.

The next twelve months will test whether these companies can rebalance their portfolios toward the categories that are winning. The scanner data suggests that those who do will be rewarded, and those who do not will face an increasingly difficult operating environment.

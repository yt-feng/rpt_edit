## The MAX-10 Will Deliver a 14% CASK Reduction Versus the Current Fleet, and That Advantage Begins Arriving in 2027

The most tangible near-term catalyst for Ryanair's cost trajectory is the Boeing 737 MAX-10. The report estimates that the MAX-10 will achieve a 14% lower CASK than the 737-NG and a 7% lower CASK than the 737-8200, the variant Ryanair currently operates. These are not marginal improvements. In an industry where a 1% CASK advantage can translate into hundreds of millions of euros in annual profit, a 14% gap is transformative.

The source of this advantage is straightforward mathematics. The MAX-10 seats 228 passengers, compared with 189 on the NG and 197 on the 8200. That 21% increase in seat count relative to the NG spreads fixed costs across more units. But the benefit goes beyond gauge. The report breaks down the cost per ASK by category: fuel costs are 27% lower than the NG, personnel costs 11% lower, navigation charges 12% lower, and maintenance costs 17% lower. The only category where the MAX-10 is slightly worse is depreciation, which is 3% higher per ASK due to rising aircraft acquisition costs. That trade-off is acceptable when the total CASK reduction is 14%.

The strategic significance of this fleet upgrade is often misunderstood. Many analysts treat it as a routine replacement cycle. It is not. Ryanair has been constrained by the 737-8200's limited upgauging relative to the NG, which delivered only a 4% increase in seat count. The MAX-10 finally gives Ryanair the gauge step-change that Airbus operators have enjoyed for years with the A321neo. The report notes that an A321neo can carry up to 244 passengers versus 180 on the A320ceo, a 36% increase. Ryanair's 737-8200 offered only 197 seats versus 189. The MAX-10 closes that gap.

First deliveries begin in spring 2027. By the mid-2030s, the MAX-10 will be the largest share of Ryanair's fleet. That means the cost advantage is not a one-time event but a compounding structural shift that will lower the airline's average CASK for a full decade as older aircraft are retired.

## The CFM Engine Deal Creates a 23% IRR and Insulates Ryanair from Industry-Wide Maintenance Cost Inflation

Airlines are facing a maintenance environment. Next-generation engines require more frequent shop visits, parts inflation is running well above general inflation, and third-party MRO capacity is tight. For most carriers, this means rising maintenance costs that are difficult to control. Ryanair has responded by signing a 15-year agreement with CFM that combines fleet support with progressive insourcing of engine maintenance.

The economics of this deal are striking. The report estimates that Ryanair will invest approximately EUR 1.5 billion in two new engine facilities in Europe, with the first scheduled for end-2028 and the second during 2029. Against this upfront cost, the airline will benefit from lower parts inflation, higher productivity, reduced spare-engine requirements, and the elimination of third-party MRO profit margins. The report models two scenarios: an insourced model under the CFM deal and an outsourced model where Ryanair continues to rely on third-party providers. Through 2029-2033, both scenarios generate roughly EUR 1 billion in costs. After that, the gap widens dramatically. By the late 2030s, outsourcing costs exceed EUR 2.5 billion annually, while insourcing costs remain below EUR 2 billion.

The internal rate of return on this investment is estimated at 23%. That is an exceptional return for an infrastructure investment, and it reflects the structural nature of the advantage. Once the facilities are built and the CFM parts supply chain is locked in, Ryanair's maintenance cost trajectory diverges permanently from the industry. The airline will be less exposed to MRO capacity constraints, less vulnerable to parts inflation, and less dependent on third-party pricing discipline.

This is particularly important because maintenance costs are one of the fastest-rising components of airline expense. The report assumes 4% annual parts inflation under the CFM deal versus 7% in the outsourced scenario. That 3-percentage-point gap compounds over 15 years. By the end of the deal period, the cumulative savings are estimated at EUR 0.5-0.8 billion. For a single cost line item, that is a material contribution to overall profitability.

## Labor Productivity Remains a Structural Advantage That Competitors Cannot Easily Match

Labor is one of the largest controllable costs for any airline, and Ryanair's advantage here is often underestimated. The report notes that Ryanair operates with 43 employees per aircraft, compared with 54 at easyJet. While Wizz Air and Vueling have even fewer employees per plane, Ryanair insources more of its activity, meaning its productivity is higher when measured on a like-for-like basis.

The source of this productivity is not simply aggressive staffing ratios. It is a combination of factors that are difficult for competitors to replicate. Ryanair operates a single-type fleet, which simplifies crew training, scheduling, and maintenance. Its pay model is largely linked to role, activity, and base location rather than automatic annual increases. Its diversified network across more than 80 bases and 200 airports means that a labor dispute in one country cannot cripple the entire operation. The report highlights that Ryanair has already completed its largest country labor agreement renewal in Italy, reinforcing industrial relations stability.

The strategic implication is that Ryanair's labor cost advantage is structural, not cyclical. Other airlines can attempt to reduce headcount, but they face legacy union agreements, fragmented fleet types, and network structures that limit their ability to redeploy labor efficiently. Ryanair's advantage will persist even as European labor markets tighten.

## Airport Cost Leverage Creates a Virtuous Circle That Competitors Cannot Replicate

Airport and handling charges are the third major component of operating costs, and Ryanair's advantage here is perhaps the most difficult for competitors to challenge. The report states that Ryanair enjoys the lowest airport costs per ASK in Europe, benefiting from attractive incentives, a focus on less congested airports, and strong bargaining power derived from its scale.

The mechanism is straightforward. Ryanair's network flexibility allows it to remove aircraft from markets where airport costs are high and reallocate them to markets where incentives are more attractive. The report cites a recent example: Ryanair reduced its presence in Spanish regions and increased allocation to Sweden and Albania. This is not a one-time adjustment but a continuous process of capital reallocation. Airports compete for Ryanair's business because the airline can stimulate passenger traffic that would not otherwise exist. This creates a virtuous circle: more seats generate more traffic, which attracts greater incentives, which reinforces Ryanair's cost advantage.

Competitors cannot replicate this because they lack the network scale. An airline with 10 or 20 bases cannot credibly threaten to move capacity to another country. Ryanair, with more than 80 bases, can. The result is that Ryanair's airport costs are likely to remain structurally lower than peers for the foreseeable future.

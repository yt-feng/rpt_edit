Naver has abruptly pivoted into neocloud (Link) - and the logic holds. Naver isn't starting from zero: it brings deep search heritage and uniquely defensible Korean-language AI assets. A stock that had slipped off investors' radar is suddenly relevant again. The debate has flipped fast to can they execute - and more importantly, who is willing to back this pivot.

Naver's AI factory may look like CoreWeave today, but its endgame is closer to Nebius (not covered). At first glance, Naver's AI factory screens similarly to CoreWeave. Strategically, however, we believe Naver is pursuing something broader: a Nebius-like path toward becoming a regional AI platform anchored in software, digital sovereignty, and full-stack integration. While neoclouds primarily monetize AI compute, Naver is using a neocloud framework to build national AI infrastructure and cultivate a regional AI ecosystem. As a result, early operating metrics may resemble those of neocloud players, but we believe the appropriate long-term valuation framework is fundamentally different.

Why Naver resembles a Neocloud today. At the infrastructure level, Naver is executing a classic neocloud strategy. It is deploying SPVs, ring-fenced financing, and forward-demand visibility to accelerate AI capacity buildout rather than waiting for organic cloud demand curves. The industrial logic is equally aligned. Naver is leaning into GPU-first infrastructure, high-density AI factory design, and tight NVIDIA partnership, starting with a 55 MW footprint and scaling ambitions beyond.

Building a stack, not just selling compute. The divergence lies in intent. Neocloud model centers on renting GPU capacity in a supply-constrained market. Nebius, by contrast, represents a move toward integrating infrastructure with a broader AI platform. Naver clearly leans toward the latter. Its edge is not just in compute, but in combining search heritage, domestic distribution, enterprise ties, and Korean-language AI assets. The ambition is not to maximize GPU hours sold, but to own the regional AI stack.

What's \$50bn between friends? This won't be a solo build. The signals are already there, with recent Naver meetings with chip and LLM players pointing to a partnership-driven neocloud from day one. If Meta showed the blueprint, Naver is writing its own playbook: SPVs to offload capex, partners to anchor demand, and long-term contracts to lock in returns. We assume this is how a \$50bn ambition gets de-risked. Capital comes from infra investors, compute from GPU suppliers, intelligence from model owners. Naver sits in the middle - orchestrating the stack, not just funding it. Now that Naver is back on the radar, the question isn't strategy. It's execution - and who's willing to step in and back it.

Naver's AI factory is no longer just a corporate capex story - it is now part of Korea's national AI agenda. The Korean government's 8.4 GW AI infrastructure initiative effectively positions Naver's planned 1 GW buildout as sovereign AI infrastructure, improving funding visibility, reducing key execution risks around power and capacity, and broadening the addressable market beyond domestic cloud demand. In our view, policy support increasingly strengthens the case for viewing Naver as a strategic AI platform rather than simply a capital-intensive GPU infrastructure builder.

## NAVER: WHAT'S \$50BN BETWEEN FRIENDS? THOUGHTS ON NEOCLOUD FUNDING, STRATEGY

Structurally, Naver's AI factory shares many characteristics with leading neocloud providers such as CoreWeave today. The building blocks are familiar: SPVs, contract-backed capex, GPU-native infrastructure, and an NVIDIA-centric ecosystem designed for speed, density, and rapid deployment. At first glance, the model resembles the emerging neocloud playbook seen across the industry.

Where Naver may ultimately differentiate itself is not necessarily in how it builds AI infrastructure today, but in what it intends to build on top of that infrastructure over time. While CoreWeave and other neocloud players are seeking to monetize growing demand for AI compute, Naver's longer-term ambition appears broader - leveraging AI infrastructure as a foundation for search, services, and a regional AI ecosystem.

## Why Naver resembles a Neocloud today

At the infrastructure level, Naver's strategy shares many of the attributes that have defined the neocloud model. The buildout relies on dedicated project financing structures, visibility on long-term demand, and accelerated deployment of AI-native capacity rather than the traditional cloud investment cycle.

Like its global peers, Naver is emphasizing high-density GPU infrastructure and close alignment with NVIDIA. The initial 55 MW deployment and longer-term expansion ambitions suggest an approach that is more consistent with purpose-built AI infrastructure providers than with traditional telecom operators or colocation businesses.

In this respect, Naver's economics during the early phases of development may look similar to those of CoreWeave and other neocloud operators. Success will depend on many of the same factors: utilization, power availability, financing execution, and the ability to secure long-term customer demand.

## Where Naver's long-term vision may differ

The key distinction, in our view, lies less in today's infrastructure model and more in the potential evolution of the platform. While neocloud providers increasingly offer software and platform services alongside infrastructure, Naver enters the AI era with several assets that extend beyond compute: a search franchise, consumer and enterprise distribution, Korean-language AI capabilities, local content ecosystems, and a strategic position within Korea's broader digital infrastructure landscape.

As a result, we believe Naver is attempting to build more than a pure AI infrastructure business. Over time, management's ambitions appear increasingly focused on connecting infrastructure, models, applications, and services into a broader regional AI ecosystem. This is not to suggest that Naver is fundamentally different from other neoclouds today. Rather, we believe the strategic opportunity may extend beyond infrastructure monetization if the company successfully leverages its existing platform assets.

## Why the Distinction Matters

For investors, the most balanced way to view Naver may be as a hybrid story. On one hand, the near-term investment case shares many features with the broader neocloud universe: substantial capital requirements, infrastructure execution risk, and sensitivity to utilization and demand trends.

On the other hand, Naver possesses platform assets that many infrastructure-focused peers do not. If successfully integrated, these assets could create additional avenues for monetization through enterprise AI services, search, content, software, and sovereign AI initiatives. Viewed through this lens, Naver is not simply importing a neocloud model into Korea, nor is it already a fully realized AI platform. Rather, it is using a neocloud-style infrastructure framework as the foundation for a potentially broader AI ecosystem.

## What investors should watch

The key question is whether Naver can extend beyond Phase 1 infrastructure economics and create meaningful value from the platform opportunities that sit above the infrastructure layer. In the near term, we think investors should focus on familiar infrastructure metrics: utilization, customer commitments, financing discipline, power availability, and returns on invested capital.

Over the longer term, the differentiation case will increasingly depend on whether Naver can leverage its search heritage, enterprise relationships, AI services, and sovereign AI positioning to create value beyond pure compute. If successful, we believe Naver could evolve into one of Asia's more differentiated AI infrastructure platforms. If not, investors may continue to evaluate the business primarily through the same framework applied to other capital-intensive AI infrastructure providers.

## FINANCING THE AI FACTORY: SCALING INFRASTRUCTURE WITHOUT SCALING THE BALANCE SHEET

We believe that Naver is firmly back on the radar - and that, in itself, matters. The debate has moved decisively from if to how: not whether the strategy works, but whether management can execute. That shift is constructive. Scrutiny beats irrelevance, and the market is now engaging on credibility, timelines, and delivery rather than questioning strategic direction. In our view, this is exactly where Naver belongs. Against that backdrop, the most logical next step for Naver Neocloud is to follow a Meta-like playbook - carving out data center and compute assets into SPVs, ring-fencing capital intensity while retaining long-term usage and control.

Meta's deal with Blue owl capital (Link), the SPV is structured to own the data center, while Meta (covered by Mark Shmulik) keeps a minority stake and a long-term lease. Blue Owl's (not covered) SPV raises roughly 27 B USD of investment-grade debt to fund construction, and that debt largely sits outside Meta's consolidated balance sheet. Meta effectively runs a triple-net lease on infrastructure it does not fully consolidate, while guaranteeing a minimum IRR via long-dated contracts and residual value protections. The economic gist: the SPV owns and finances data centers, big tech secures capacity through long-term leases and minimum revenue guarantees, and the leverage shows up more in the SPV than at the parent.

We assume that Naver's GPU SPV would mirror this logic by putting the physical GPUs, racks, power, and cooling inside a vehicle and raising GPU-collateralized debt against them. Naver might hold a meaningful share of the SPV equity as operator with infrastructure investors owning the remaining shares and targeting a $7 - 9\%$ IRR. Neocloud then could sign 5-plus-year leases or capacity-based service contracts with minimum usage and pre-agreed GPU pricing, effectively backstopping investor returns. With careful structuring under accounting rules, much of the GPU exposure could be kept off Naver's parent balance sheet, flowing through P&L as service or lease expense rather than as large capitalized assets and associated debt.

For greenfield data centers, Naver could potentially split the structure into a property company that owns land, buildings, and core infrastructure, and an operating company that runs the AI cloud. The property company SPV could possibly issue long-dated, investment-grade style project bonds or green infrastructure debt to fund the heavy CapEx, while Naver might inject equity into the operating company and could sign long-term service and lease agreements back to the property company. Operating company could be majority (potentially 51%) owned by Naver, preserving operating control and margin capture, while property company could be majority held by infra investors, keeping most of the debt ring-fenced. In effect, Naver might leverage its capital and operating capabilities while pushing land, building, and GPU CapEx into project finance structures held by external investors.

In our view, the first design pillar is ring-fencing: SPV debt should not visibly stretch Naver's reported leverage, so facilities are structured as non-recourse or limited-recourse, with returns secured by long-term contracts, minimum volumes, and price floors. The second is accounting: contract tenor, renewal options, and residual guarantees are tuned to keep as much exposure as possible in service contract territory rather than full-blown finance leases under IFRS. Third, Naver aims for minority equity but majority control where it matters, holding c.51% in operating entities, while infra investors carry most of the CapEx and leverage. Finally, Naver leans into its local strengths – Korean language-specific AI stacks, and a track record with public and enterprise customers – to convince capital providers that demand and operating risk are structurally lower than in a generic neocloud.

## POLICY POWERING NAVER'S AI AMBITIONS

The Korean government's mega project formally positions Naver's AI data centre buildout as part of a state-backed 8.4 GW AI infrastructure program, allocating around 1 GW to Naver alongside SK (5GW) and GS (2.4GW). This shifts Naver's AI factory from a company-level initiative to a core component of Korea's national AI backbone, embedding its roadmap within a 550 trillion won investment framework and raising the strategic stakes on execution.

## Sovereign backing strengthens funding and growth

Because the mega project is explicitly framed as an effort to industrialize and export AI data centre solutions, Naver's SPV-based financing model now sits under a policy umbrella that favors access to favourable government and policy-bank capital. That support can lower Naver's effective cost of debt and strengthen non-recourse SPV structures, making its plan to finance c.40-60 billion USD of capex with limited parent balance sheet strain more credible.

The announcement (Link) ties AI data centres directly to national AI sovereignty, large-scale token production, and exportable “AI data centre solution” industries, including domestic AI chips and power/cooling technologies. For Naver, this broadens the demand story from domestic cloud and enterprise customers to a mix of sovereign AI workloads and overseas AI DC projects where Naver’s stack, operations, and Korean hardware ecosystem can be packaged as an export offering.

## Policy tailwinds turn AI capex into strategic value

The mega project is backed by a comprehensive infrastructure plan that prioritizes timely power and water supply, discloses high-voltage grid capacity, and introduces AI data centre-specific electricity tariffs. This directly addresses two of the biggest operational risks in Naver's AI factory model - securing enough energy and controlling long-term power costs - and makes its utilization and 20% margin assumptions more defensible if its sites qualify for these preferential regimes.

By naming Naver as a core partner in the national AI data centre buildout, the government effectively recasts Naver as a candidate for “national champion” status in AI infrastructure and sovereign AI platforms. For equity investors, that means Naver should increasingly be analysed not just as a capital-intensive GPU builder, but as an operator of policy-supported strategic assets with platform and export upside, altering both risk perception and potential valuation multiples.

## Bernstein brand

The Bernstein brand rates stocks based on forecasts of relative performance for the next 12 months versus the S&P 500 for stocks listed on the U.S. and Canadian exchanges, versus the Bloomberg Europe Developed Markets Large and Mid Cap Price Return Index EUR (EDME) for stocks listed on the European exchanges and emerging markets exchanges outside of the Asia Pacific region, versus the Bloomberg Japan Large and Mid Cap Price Return Index USD (JPL) for stocks listed on the Japanese exchanges, and versus the Bloomberg Asia ex-Japan Large and Mid Cap Price Return Index (ASIAX) for stocks listed on the Asian (ex-Japan) exchanges -unless otherwise specified.

The Bernstein brand has three categories of ratings:

\- Outperform: Stock will outpace the market index by more than 15 pp

• Market-Perform: Stock will perform in line with the market index to within +/-15 pp

• Underperform: Stock will trail the performance of the market index by more than 15 pp

Coverage Suspended: Coverage of a company under the Bernstein brand has been suspended. Ratings and price targets are suspended temporarily, are no longer current, and should therefore not be relied upon.

Not Rated: A rating assigned when the stock cannot be accurately valued, or the performance of the company accurately predicted, at the present time. The covering analyst may continue to publish research reports on the company to update investors on events and developments.

Not Covered (NC) denotes companies that are not under coverage.

Bernstein brand stock ratings are based on a 12-month time horizon.

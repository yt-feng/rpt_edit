## Near-Term BBU Demand Is Driven by Rising Rack Power Density Under Existing Architecture, Not by HVDC Commercialization

The most common source of investor anxiety about the BBU thesis is the possibility that HVDC rack commercialization gets delayed. This concern is understandable because HVDC architecture is often discussed as a transformative enabler for higher-power BBU modules. But framing HVDC as a necessary condition for BBU adoption misidentifies the current demand driver.

Near-term BBU demand is already being propelled by rising AI rack power density under the existing rack-scale architecture. The products currently in volume production and near-term ramp are the 3kW and 5kW BBU modules, which serve racks operating within today's power envelope. These are followed by 8kW and 12kW modules expected to begin meaningful ramp in the fourth quarter of 2026. None of these products require HVDC architecture to function. They are designed for the present infrastructure.

HVDC should be viewed as an incremental long-term content value driver, not a gating factor. If HVDC commercialization takes longer than expected, the upside from higher-power BBU products—such as the 25kW modules that Dynapack is developing—would be postponed. But the investment thesis does not collapse because the 3kW, 5kW, 8kW, and 12kW product cycle provides a multi-year volume trajectory that is independent of HVDC timing. The risk is a delay in upside, not a structural impairment.

This distinction matters for portfolio construction. An investor who requires HVDC to work for the thesis to work is taking an unnecessary timing risk. An investor who understands that HVDC is a second-order accelerator, not a first-order necessity, can build exposure on the back of the existing product cycle and treat HVDC as a free option on incremental content per rack.

## PSU Suppliers Are Unlikely to Vertically Integrate into BBU Because the Required Technical Capabilities Are Fundamentally Different

A second recurring investor concern is that PSU suppliers, who already serve the data center power chain, could decide to build BBU capabilities in-house and cut out specialized battery pack makers like Dynapack. This fear is intuitive but technically unfounded.

BBU manufacturing requires a set of capabilities that PSU suppliers do not possess and would find costly and risky to develop. These include battery pack design, battery management system (BMS) integration, thermal management at the pack level, safety certification for lithium-ion energy storage, and highly customized manufacturing processes that differ from standard power supply production. The safety-critical nature of BBU products amplifies the stakes. A failure in a BBU module within an AI rack can have catastrophic consequences for uptime and hardware integrity. Cloud service providers (CSPs) and their tier-one suppliers are therefore conservative about qualification processes and reluctant to entrust BBU production to unproven entrants.

The more likely outcome is continued partnership between PSU suppliers and qualified battery pack makers, especially as BBU products become more complex under higher power and HVDC architectures. PSU suppliers gain access to BBU content through their existing customer relationships and platform integration capabilities. Battery pack makers gain access to PSU platforms and CSP qualification processes. The specialization creates a mutually reinforcing dependency rather than a competitive threat.

This partnership model is precisely what gives Dynapack its distribution advantage. By working through PSU partners, Dynapack can access CSP platforms without needing to build a direct sales relationship with every hyperscaler. The model scales with the PSU partner's success, which is itself driven by the broader AI infrastructure buildout. The risk of disintermediation is low because the PSU partner would have to replicate a decade of battery safety and certification expertise to go it alone.

## CSPs' Preference for Supplier Diversification Creates a Structural Opening for Taiwanese Suppliers to Gain Share from Panasonic

Panasonic has long dominated the BBU market, supported by strong cell technology, manufacturing scale, and direct relationships with CSPs. But its dominant market share is increasingly vulnerable as the market scales. The reason is not technological inferiority but buyer behavior.

CSPs typically prefer supplier diversification for three reasons: supply security, pricing leverage, and qualification redundancy. A single-source dependency on one battery pack supplier creates concentration risk in a mission-critical component. As BBU becomes a standard feature in AI racks, CSPs will want at least two or three qualified suppliers to ensure continuity of supply and competitive pricing. Panasonic's current share, while a testament to its capabilities, is also a structural invitation for share loss.

Taiwanese suppliers such as AES and Dynapack currently hold less than 10 percent market share each. This means they have substantial room to expand even if the total addressable market grows only modestly. The combination of CSP diversification demand and low starting share creates a multi-year volume growth trajectory that is independent of any single product cycle.

Dynapack's specific model amplifies this opportunity. Its PSU partnership structure gives it a differentiated route to market. Instead of competing head-on with Panasonic for direct CSP qualifications, Dynapack can gain share by becoming the preferred BBU partner for PSU suppliers that are themselves winning platform positions. As PSU suppliers expand their share of the AI power architecture, Dynapack's share expands in tandem. This indirect share gain mechanism is harder to replicate and less visible to competitors, which gives it a longer competitive runway.

## The Stock's Underperformance in Early 2026 Reflects a Catalyst Gap, Not a Thesis Failure

The most practical question for an investor considering Dynapack is why the stock has underperformed in the first half of 2026 despite being a direct beneficiary of AI power architecture evolution. The answer lies in the timing of earnings visibility, not in any deterioration of the fundamental thesis.

Three factors explain the underperformance. First, in the first half of 2026, BBU still accounts for less than 50 percent of Dynapack's group sales. The overall sales momentum is diluted by the legacy IT battery business, which is estimated to decline year over year. The company is effectively carrying a drag from its traditional product lines while the BBU ramp is still scaling. Until BBU becomes the majority of revenue and profit, the market cannot see the pure-play growth story it wants to price.

Second, the stock experienced a significant re-rating in 2025 as the market first priced in the BBU opportunity. That re-rating created a high base and a lack of near-term catalysts to sustain momentum. Without a quarterly earnings beat or a major product announcement, the stock drifted in the absence of fresh positive signals.

Third, sector rotation toward other high-momentum tech segments has contributed to relative underperformance. When the broader tech market shifts attention to other themes, a stock in a catalyst lull gets sold down, not because the thesis is broken, but because capital rotates to where the next catalyst is visible.

The inflection point is expected in the second half of 2026. Continued strong demand for 3kW and 5kW BBU products, combined with the initial ramp of 8kW and 12kW modules, should drive meaningful sales growth. As BBU becomes a larger share of group sales and an even larger share of group profit, the market should refocus on Dynapack's earnings inflection. The stock's underperformance in the first half of 2026 is therefore a timing issue, not a structural one. It creates an entry point for investors who can look through a quarter or two of mixed signals.

# The US-Iran Interim Deal Reshapes Currency Markets: Why Selective Dollar Shorts and Relative Value Trades Now Dominate Strategy

The impending signing of a US-Iran interim agreement marks a strategic inflection point for global currency markets. For investors who have been navigating the volatile landscape of Middle Eastern geopolitics, this development validates a critical pivot: the era of broad-based dollar strength driven by energy supply fears is giving way to a more nuanced environment where selective dollar shorts and relative value trades driven by local idiosyncrasies will determine outperformance.

This is not merely a tactical adjustment. It represents a fundamental reassessment of how currency markets price geopolitical risk, energy dependencies, and monetary policy divergence. The report from a global investment bank, which we analyze here, makes a clear argument: the de-escalation thesis adopted since late May is now being confirmed by events, and the portfolio implications are substantial. The question is no longer whether to position for a weaker dollar, but how to do so with precision.

The strategic logic is straightforward but powerful. When the Strait of Hormuz reopens, the terms-of-trade shock that had been supporting a stronger broad dollar begins to reverse. Lower energy prices directly undermine one of the key pillars of dollar strength. Simultaneously, this creates a more favorable environment for currencies that had been punished by the energy premium, while exposing those that had benefited from safe-haven flows. The result is a market where broad-based directional bets give way to carefully constructed relative value trades.

What makes this moment particularly significant is the confluence of factors. The potential outcomes from upcoming central bank meetings, combined with medium-term drivers for a weaker dollar, create a window of opportunity that may not remain open indefinitely. Investors who delay repositioning risk missing the initial wave of adjustment, which often carries the highest risk-reward profile.

## The De-escalation Thesis Is Confirmed, but Oscillation Risk Requires a Disciplined Approach to Conviction Levels

The report makes a crucial distinction that many investors will overlook: confirmation of the agreement does not mean the end of uncertainty. The discrepancies reported between US and Iranian officials regarding the terms of the Memorandum of Understanding—including whether ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz would be administered by Iran or Oman, whether the Israel-Lebanon ceasefire can hold, and whether further progress on thorny issues is possible—mean that the risk of oscillation remains real.

This is where the report's analytical rigor becomes most valuable. Rather than treating the agreement as a binary event, the strategy team has adjusted conviction levels across their portfolio in a calibrated manner. They lowered conviction on short South and Southeast Asian FX positions on May 22, before the news broke, and then made further adjustments as details emerged. This forward-looking approach, rather than reactive repositioning, is the hallmark of sophisticated geopolitical risk management.

The practical implication for investors is clear: do not assume that a single agreement resolves all uncertainty. The path from interim deal to lasting stability is rarely linear. The report's approach of maintaining positions but adjusting conviction levels—rather than exiting entirely—reflects an understanding that the direction of travel is more important than the precise timing of each step.

## Selective Dollar Shorts Require a Framework That Distinguishes Between Energy Sensitivity and Structural Support

The report's core argument for being selectively short the dollar rests on three pillars: lower energy prices reversing the terms-of-trade impact, the potential outcomes from FOMC and BOJ meetings, and medium-term structural drivers. But the key word is "selectively." Not all dollar shorts are created equal, and the report provides a clear framework for differentiation.

The most compelling case is made for short USD/CNH, held at high conviction. The logic here is multi-layered and mutually reinforcing. First, USD/CNH has shown greater sensitivity to downward moves in DXY than upward moves since the start of the Iran conflict—a pattern the report expects to continue. Second, the daily CNY fixing continues to drift lower, reaching levels not seen since early 2023. Third, FX settlement demand from onshore corporates remains robust, supported by strong export growth and a trade surplus that continues to generate substantial inflows. Fourth, the RMB remains significantly undervalued by multiple valuation models, with productivity-adjusted REER showing nearly 20% undervaluation.

For short USD/TWD, the case is similarly strong but with different drivers. Taiwan's tech sector strength, which has allowed TWD to outperform within Asia despite high exposure to the Strait of Hormuz energy shock, now positions it to benefit from an easing of energy prices. The structural support from global AI demand and capital expenditure provides a fundamental tailwind that is independent of the geopolitical cycle.

The critical insight here is that these trades are not simply bets on a weaker dollar. They are bets on specific currencies that have structural reasons to appreciate, independent of the dollar's direction. The de-escalation removes a headwind, but the tailwinds come from local factors.

## Relative Value Trades in Asia FX Reveal a Sophisticated Understanding of Balance of Payments Dynamics

The most analytically interesting part of the report is its treatment of relative value trades within Asia FX. The long SGD/IDR position, for example, has seen its conviction level reduced multiple times as the market reassesses the implications of the war on Indonesia's balance of payments dynamics and recent policy measures.

The report identifies five specific risks that justify the reduced conviction: the possibility that Bank Indonesia may not hike at its upcoming meeting, mixed signaling from BI regarding its bond purchases, nascent social concerns over government economic policy, the upcoming MSCI free float assessment, and the risk of a credit rating downgrade. Each of these factors represents a distinct channel through which IDR could underperform, and the report's willingness to acknowledge them—rather than simply maintaining a bullish stance—is analytically honest.

The reinitiation of long EUR/INR at higher conviction is equally instructive. The report argues that EUR may be a strong beneficiary of lower energy prices reversing negative terms-of-trade pressures, while INR faces headwinds from portfolio outflows, potential fiscal slippage, and the RBI's likely continued FX intervention. The key insight is that the FCNR(B) deposit scheme, which might superficially appear supportive for INR, ultimately does not change the underlying dynamics because banks will swap those deposits directly with the RBI.

This analysis demonstrates why relative value trades require a deeper understanding of balance of payments mechanics than simple directional bets. The report's framework for evaluating these trades—considering energy sensitivity, monetary policy divergence, portfolio flows, and central bank behavior—provides a template that investors can apply across other currency pairs.

## G10 FX Strategy Reveals That Monetary Policy Divergence, Not Just Geopolitics, Will Drive the Next Phase

The G10 FX recommendations in the report shift the focus from geopolitics to monetary policy divergence, which is likely to become the dominant theme once the initial adjustment to the US-Iran agreement is complete.

The high-conviction short AUD/NZD position is built on the view that the RBA has reached the end of its hiking cycle while the RBNZ is still moving toward tightening. This is reinforced by positioning data showing that long AUD/NZD remains one of the most stretched positions in G10 FX space. The report also notes that lower oil prices should favor NZD over AUD, given New Zealand's status as a large oil importer versus Australia's net energy exporter status.

The long USD/CAD position is more nuanced. The report acknowledges that this trade faces near-term headwinds from the US-Iran agreement, which should encourage bearishness on both the dollar and oil prices. However, the key analytical insight is that USD/CAD has recently shown less sensitivity to oil price movements than it did prior to the escalation. If this pattern persists, it would strengthen the conviction that Canadian dollar weakness may continue even as geopolitical volatility fades, driven by macro divergence between the US and Canadian economies and interest rate differentials.

The long CHF/JPY and long EUR/GBP positions are explicitly designed as low-beta trades to both the USD and oil. This is a sophisticated portfolio construction choice: rather than trying to predict the exact path of the dollar or energy prices, these trades capture specific local dynamics—CHF's tendency to outperform in June, SNB intervention being measured, BOJ rate hikes being priced in, and UK political and fiscal risks—while minimizing exposure to the two variables that remain most uncertain.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Magnitude of the Dollar Weakening

For all its analytical sophistication, the report leaves several important questions unanswered. First, while it makes a compelling case for a weaker dollar, it does not specify the magnitude or timeline of the expected decline. The targets provided for individual currency pairs imply gains of 3-4% over several months, but the aggregate effect on the dollar index is not quantified.

Second, the report acknowledges the risk of oscillation in the US-Iran situation but does not provide a framework for how to distinguish between temporary noise and a genuine reversal of the de-escalation trend. What specific triggers would cause the strategy team to abandon their selective dollar short stance?

Third, the report's treatment of the Federal Reserve and Bank of Japan meetings is notably brief. Given that these meetings could produce outcomes that either reinforce or undermine the dollar weakness thesis, the lack of detailed scenario analysis is a gap. What if the Fed surprises with a hawkish stance? What if the BOJ fails to deliver the rate hike that markets expect?

Fourth, the report does not fully address the potential for coordinated intervention by central banks to stabilize currency markets. The mention of MOF intervention risk in the context of USD/JPY is a reminder that authorities may not passively accept large currency moves.

These unanswered questions are not weaknesses in the report. Rather, they define the frontier of uncertainty that investors must navigate. The report provides a framework, but the application requires judgment.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position for the De-escalation Trade

Based on the report's analysis, investors can construct a decision framework with three layers:

**Layer 1: Directional Exposure.** The core thesis is that the dollar weakens as energy prices decline and geopolitical risk premiums unwind. The most direct expression is short USD/CNH, which benefits from both dollar weakness and structural RMB appreciation drivers. Short USD/TWD provides similar exposure with a different risk profile, given Taiwan's tech sector strength.

**Layer 2: Relative Value Within Asia.** The key distinction is between currencies that benefit from lower energy prices (SGD, TWD, KRW) and those that face headwinds from domestic factors (IDR, INR). Long SGD/IDR captures this differential, while long EUR/INR adds the dimension of EUR strength from terms-of-trade improvement.

**Layer 3: G10 Crosses with Low Beta to USD and Oil.** For investors who want exposure to the de-escalation theme without the binary risk of direct dollar or oil bets, long CHF/JPY and long EUR/GBP offer attractive risk-reward profiles driven by monetary policy divergence and local political dynamics.

The critical insight from the report is that conviction levels matter as much as direction. A position held at 1/5 conviction is fundamentally different from one held at 4/5. The former is a watchlist item, not a commitment. The latter is a core portfolio holding. Investors should apply the same discipline, adjusting position sizes as conviction evolves rather than treating all trades equally.

# The Strait of Hormuz Agreement Does Not Solve the Oil Market's Core Problem — It Reveals It

The announcement of a US-Iran Memorandum of Understanding over the weekend, 106 days after the Strait of Hormuz conflict began, has shifted the oil market's center of gravity. Yet the most important insight from this development is not that supply will return — it is that the physical oil market was already under significant pressure before any deal was struck. The agreement, while politically significant, does not resolve the structural imbalance that has been building for months. Instead, it exposes a market that has been softening even as nearly 11 million barrels per day of Middle Eastern production remained shut-in. The implication is uncomfortable: the supply disruption was never the full story, and the re-opening of the Strait will not, by itself, restore price strength.

The report from a global investment bank makes this point with unusual clarity. Despite a cumulative supply loss of approximately 1.4 billion barrels since March 1, the Brent market has weakened visibly in recent weeks. Dated Brent, the DFL and CFD curves, physical differentials, the cash Dubai premium, and refined product cracks have all moved lower. The naphtha crack spread in Asia relative to Dubai crude has fallen $13 per barrel in four weeks, approaching a one-year low — a deeply counter-intuitive outcome for a market where one-third of seaborne supply has been cut off. This is not a market that was starved for oil. It was a market that was struggling to place barrels even as a major supply artery was blocked.

The agreement brings forward the expected timeline for production recovery by roughly one to two weeks. The report now assumes 50 percent of lost production will return by September, and 80 percent by December. But the more consequential adjustment is in the price forecast: Brent is revised down to $90 per barrel for the third quarter and $80 per barrel from the fourth quarter onward. That is a $10 to $15 per barrel reduction from previous estimates. The message is clear — the summer tightness that was expected to support prices will now be both shorter and shallower than previously assumed.

## The "Twin Solvers" of High US Exports and Low Chinese Imports Have Shielded the Market from 10 Million Barrels per Day of Tightness

The physical weakness in the oil market can be traced to two structural flows that have operated in parallel with the Hormuz disruption. US seaborne net exports of crude and refined products have increased from approximately 5 million barrels per day last year to nearly 9 million barrels per day recently. At the same time, China's net imports have declined from approximately 13 million barrels per day to roughly 7 million barrels per day. Together, these two shifts have removed 10 million barrels per day of tightness from the global market — an amount that more than offset the supply loss from the Middle East.

This is not a temporary coincidence. The report provides detailed tracking of known tanker movements to project near-term flows. US net exports for June are tracking the same trajectory as April, which ended in the 8.5 to 9 million barrels per day range. July data, while still early, is tracking below May and June but remains above any month in the prior twelve. For China, the picture is even more striking. Known tanker movements imply that June net imports will be lower than May, and July is also tracking below the May path. The "firming fans" — charts that show how tanker movement data becomes more reliable as time progresses — suggest that neither side of this equation is about to change meaningfully in the coming weeks.

The report also tracks spot crude deals entered into by Chinese refiners, trading houses, and other companies. Spot deals typically cover approximately 6 million barrels per day of China's 11 million barrels per day of crude imports. In April, spot deals supplied an unusually low 4.5 million barrels per day. In May, that fell further to 4.1 million barrels per day. Known deals for June are tracking even lower, and data for July and August shows little to no improvement. The buying interest from the world's largest crude importer has not merely paused — it has contracted structurally, and there is no visible catalyst for a near-term reversal.

## The Apparent Demand Collapse Is Largely a Delayed Purchasing Phenomenon, Not a Consumption Crisis

One of the most analytically subtle points in the report concerns the nature of the demand decline. Global refinery crude runs were down approximately 5.8 million barrels per year in May, and global crude demand fell by the same amount. On the surface, this looks like a dramatic demand destruction event. But the report argues that this sharply overstates the underlying consumption decline.

The mechanism is straightforward but often overlooked in market commentary. When buyers expect supply to return — or when they anticipate lower prices — they delay purchases. Refiners reduce crude runs not because end-user demand has collapsed, but because they are drawing down inventories and waiting for a more favorable pricing environment. The report provides a concrete example: German households, expecting the Strait of Hormuz to reopen, stopped filling their heating oil tanks. Consumption continued, but buying was delayed. The same dynamic is playing out at a global scale across the supply chain.

This distinction matters enormously for forecasting. If demand had genuinely collapsed, the market would face a prolonged period of surplus even after supply returns. But if the weakness is primarily a timing issue — a deferral of purchases rather than a destruction of consumption — then the market could tighten relatively quickly once buyers re-enter. The report does not resolve this question definitively, but the evidence it presents leans toward the delayed purchasing interpretation. The unsold cargo data, the weak physical differentials, and the persistent softness in Chinese buying all point to a market that is waiting, not one that is structurally oversupplied.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: What Will Break the Current Equilibrium?

For all its analytical rigor, the report does not fully answer what might cause the current pattern to change. The "twin solvers" of high US exports and low Chinese imports have created a self-reinforcing equilibrium. US exports remain high because global demand for US crude has been robust, particularly from Europe and Asia, where buyers have diversified away from Middle Eastern supply. Chinese imports remain low because the country's refining system is oversupplied, its economic growth is moderating, and its strategic purchasing behavior has shifted.

The report identifies the key question: when will US exports fall or Chinese imports rise? But it does not provide a clear catalyst. The tanker movement data suggests no imminent change. The spot deal data suggests no imminent change. The demand data, while ambiguous, does not suggest an imminent rebound. The re-opening of the Strait of Hormuz, if anything, reduces the urgency for buyers to act. They can now wait longer, knowing that supply will eventually return.

This creates a strategic dilemma for market participants. If the current equilibrium persists through the summer, the anticipated tightness may fail to materialize entirely. The report's revised forecast — $90 per barrel for the third quarter — already reflects this risk. But if the equilibrium breaks — if US exports decline due to logistical constraints or domestic demand, or if Chinese imports rebound due to policy stimulus or inventory rebuilding — then the market could tighten rapidly, and the current price weakness would look like a buying opportunity.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Next Three Months

For readers who need to position for the coming quarter, the report provides the basis for a structured decision framework. The framework has three variables: the pace of Middle Eastern production recovery, the trajectory of US exports, and the trajectory of Chinese imports.

First, the pace of production recovery. The report assumes 50 percent of lost production back by September and 80 percent by December. This is slightly faster than previous assumptions, but it still implies that approximately 5 to 6 million barrels per day of supply will remain offline through the end of the third quarter. The risk is to the upside — if recovery is faster, the market could loosen further. The risk is also to the downside — if clearing sea mines, rebuilding commercial confidence, and repositioning tankers takes longer than expected, the recovery could be delayed into the fourth quarter.

Second, US exports. The key question is whether the current level of approximately 9 million barrels per day is sustainable. The report notes that US exports are tracking similar to April and May levels for June. But July data, while still above historical averages, is tracking lower. If this trend continues, it would remove some of the pressure on the global market. The risk is that US exports remain high, prolonging the current softness.

Third, Chinese imports. This is the most important variable. The report's data shows that Chinese buying has not yet recovered and shows no sign of doing so in the near term. The risk is that Chinese imports remain low through the third quarter, which would keep the global market well-supplied even as Middle Eastern production returns. The opportunity is that Chinese imports rebound sharply — either due to policy stimulus, inventory rebuilding, or a change in purchasing behavior — which would tighten the market significantly.

The framework suggests that the most likely scenario is continued softness through the third quarter, with a gradual tightening in the fourth quarter as Middle Eastern production returns and Chinese buying eventually normalizes. But the confidence in this scenario is low, precisely because the report's data raises more questions than it answers.

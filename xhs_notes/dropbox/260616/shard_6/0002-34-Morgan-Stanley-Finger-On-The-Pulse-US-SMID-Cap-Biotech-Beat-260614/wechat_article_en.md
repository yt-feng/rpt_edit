# The US Biotech M&A Cycle Is Now Structural, Not Episodic: Why SMID-Cap Investors Cannot Afford to Wait for Lower Rates

The most important development in US SMID-cap biotechnology this year is not a single data readout or FDA decision. It is a structural shift in the character of mergers and acquisitions. After years of episodic deal-making driven by patent cliffs or opportunistic balance-sheet repairs, M&A in this sector has become a sustained strategic imperative for large-cap acquirers. The first half of 2026 has already delivered a pace of deal activity that, if maintained, would make this a record year for biotech M&A. This is not a cyclical uptick. It is a reconfiguration of how value is created and captured in the sector.

The implications for investors are profound. When M&A is episodic, the correct strategy is to wait for a catalyst, buy the target, and sell into the premium. When M&A becomes structural, the correct strategy is to own the asset class before the deal is announced, because the premium reflects not just a one-time price bump but a fundamental repricing of the platform. The report from a leading investment bank makes this case explicitly: M&A is structural, not episodic, and the data from 2025 and 2026 support that conclusion.

Why now matters. The macro environment is shifting. Interest rates, while still elevated relative to the zero-rate era, have stabilized. Commercial execution for approved drugs is improving. The IPO window, while still narrow, has opened enough to allow high-quality Phase 2 and Phase 3 companies to access public markets. And the FDA, despite ongoing policy debates, continues to approve drugs at a steady clip. Together, these forces have created a rare alignment: large-cap acquirers have both the strategic need and the financial capacity to buy, while SMID-cap companies have the clinical assets and the market access to be worth buying.

This article unpacks the core argument of the report, extends its logic into second-order implications for portfolio construction, and identifies the key questions that remain unanswered. It is written for serious investors who want to understand not just what is happening, but what it means for their decision-making.

## The M&A Wave Is Not a Reaction to Low Valuations; It Is a Response to a Structural Pipeline Deficit

The conventional narrative about biotech M&A is that it accelerates when valuations are cheap. That narrative is incomplete. The current wave, as documented in the report, is being driven by something more durable: a pipeline deficit among large-cap pharmaceutical companies that cannot be filled through internal R&D alone. The report notes that deal activity in 2025 and early 2026 has been broad-based across therapeutic areas, with significant transactions in oncology, immunology, rare disease, and neurology. This is not a fire sale. It is a procurement cycle.

The distinction matters for investors. If M&A were purely valuation-driven, the correct response to rising rates would be to wait for further compression and then buy. But if M&A is pipeline-driven, then the premium that acquirers are willing to pay is a function of the scarcity of high-quality assets, not the cheapness of the stock price. In that environment, waiting for lower prices is a losing strategy because the best assets get taken out before they become cheap.

The report provides concrete evidence for this view. The average IPO proceed in 2026 year-to-date is significantly higher than in recent years, indicating that the companies coming to market are more mature and more de-risked. At the same time, follow-on offerings have been robust, with the last two weeks of December 2025 alone seeing nearly $6 billion in public equity follow-ons for US biotech. These are not signs of a distressed sector. They are signs of a sector that is attracting capital because its assets are in demand.

The strategic implication is clear: investors should not treat M&A as a beta trade on interest rates. They should treat it as an alpha source that requires bottom-up identification of assets that fill a specific gap in a large-cap pipeline. The report's weekly stock movers section, which highlights Incyte's acquisition of Vega Therapeutics and the market's positive reaction, is a case study in this dynamic. Incyte paid $1.25 billion upfront for a Phase 3 asset in von Willebrand disease, a market with a total addressable opportunity of $3.5 billion to $5 billion. The deal was not about buying cheap. It was about buying a specific, high-value asset that addresses a looming revenue gap as Jakafi faces loss of exclusivity.

## Rising Rates Are a Feature, Not a Bug, for Commercial-Stage SMID-Cap Biotech

The conventional wisdom holds that rising interest rates are uniformly negative for biotechnology because they increase the cost of capital and reduce the present value of future cash flows. The report challenges this assumption in a nuanced way. While rates do compress valuations for pre-revenue companies, they have a different effect on commercial-stage SMID-cap companies that are generating revenue and managing their own balance sheets.

The report's section on commercial outlook improvement, referenced in the "In Case You Missed It" list, suggests that the Q1 earnings season revealed a pattern: companies with approved products are executing better than expected. This is not a coincidence. When rates rise, the cost of capital for large-cap acquirers also rises, which makes them more selective about which assets they buy. They prioritize assets that are closer to commercialization, have clearer regulatory paths, and offer nearer-term revenue contributions. That favors SMID-cap companies with approved products or late-stage pipelines.

The report also notes that the IPO market in 2026 has been dominated by Phase 2 and Phase 3 companies, with no preclinical or Phase 1 IPOs so far. This is a striking departure from the 2020-2021 era, when preclinical companies routinely went public. The market is demanding maturity. And that demand for maturity is exactly what makes the current crop of SMID-cap biotech companies attractive acquisition targets. They have data. They have regulatory visibility. And they have commercial infrastructure that a large-cap acquirer can scale.

For investors, this means that the traditional playbook of buying early-stage biotech and waiting for a binary catalyst needs to be updated. The higher-return strategy in this environment is to identify SMID-cap companies that have already cleared the proof-of-concept hurdle and are now building commercial momentum. These are the companies that will attract acquisition interest, not because they are cheap, but because they are derisked.

## The IPO Market Is Sending a Signal About Quality That Investors Should Not Ignore

The report includes a detailed breakdown of IPO activity by stage of development from 2017 through 2026 year-to-date. The data reveal a clear pattern: the quality of companies coming to market is improving. In 2021, at the peak of the zero-rate era, 30 of 96 IPOs were preclinical. In 2026 year-to-date, zero of 10 IPOs have been preclinical. All 10 have been clinical-stage, with seven in Phase 2 and three in Phase 3.

This is not a sign of a weak market. It is a sign of a disciplined market. The IPO window is open, but only for companies that have real data and real assets. The average IPO proceed in 2026 is $330 million, nearly double the average in 2024 and significantly above the average in 2021. Investors are paying up for quality.

The second-order implication is that the aftermarket performance of these IPOs is likely to be stronger than in previous cycles. When companies go public at earlier stages, they are more dependent on future capital markets for survival, and any macro shock can wipe out their equity. When companies go public at later stages, they have more runway, more data, and more negotiating power in M&A. The report's data on cash runways, which is referenced in the table of contents, is likely to confirm that the current cohort of SMID-cap biotech companies has longer runways than the 2021 cohort.

For investors, this means that the IPO market is no longer a speculative casino. It is a pipeline of high-quality, late-stage assets that are likely to be acquisition targets within 12 to 24 months. The correct strategy is not to buy the IPO and flip it. It is to buy the IPO, hold it, and wait for the M&A premium.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Durability of Commercial Execution in a Higher-Rate Environment

The report makes a compelling case that commercial execution is improving, but it leaves an important question unanswered: how durable is that improvement? The Q1 earnings season showed better-than-expected commercial results, but one quarter does not make a trend. The real test will come when these companies face a full year of higher rates, tighter formularies, and potentially slower adoption of new drugs by payers who are themselves managing higher costs of capital.

The report's section on macroeconomic factors, referenced in the table of contents, likely addresses some of these concerns, but the public parsing does not provide the full analysis. Investors need to ask themselves: is the improvement in commercial execution a structural shift driven by better products and better commercial strategies, or is it a cyclical improvement that will reverse as rates stay higher for longer?

The answer to this question determines whether the current M&A cycle is sustainable. If commercial execution is structurally improving, then large-cap acquirers will continue to pay premiums for SMID-cap companies with approved products. If commercial execution is cyclically fragile, then the M&A wave could slow as acquirers become more cautious about the revenue projections of their targets.

This is the key analytical question that the report raises but does not definitively answer. Investors who want to position for the next phase of the cycle need to develop their own view on commercial durability.

## A Decision Framework for SMID-Cap Biotech Investing in a Structural M&A Cycle

Based on the report's analysis, investors can construct a three-part decision framework for allocating capital in this environment.

First, prioritize companies with approved products or Phase 3 assets in large, well-understood markets. The report's M&A table shows that acquirers are paying significant premiums for assets in oncology, immunology, and rare disease. The Incyte-Vega deal is a textbook example: a Phase 3 asset in a bleeding disorder with a clear regulatory path and a large addressable market. Investors should screen for companies that have similar profiles: late-stage assets, clear regulatory pathways, and markets that are large enough to move the needle for a large-cap acquirer.

Second, avoid companies that are dependent on a single binary catalyst in a small market. The report's "Stock Down" section on Cullinan Therapeutics (CGEM) is instructive. The company presented promising early data from its immunology program, but the market focused on the uncertainty around durability and the therapeutic window. The stock fell 16% despite positive data. In a structural M&A cycle, binary risk is not rewarded. Investors should favor companies with multiple shots on goal or with assets that have already demonstrated proof of concept.

Third, monitor the follow-on offering market as a leading indicator of M&A activity. The report notes that the last two weeks of December 2025 saw nearly $6 billion in follow-on offerings. This is a signal that companies are raising capital to fund their pipelines, which makes them more attractive acquisition targets. When follow-on activity is strong, it suggests that the capital markets are functioning well, which in turn supports higher valuations and more M&A. When follow-on activity dries up, it is a warning sign that the M&A cycle may be peaking.

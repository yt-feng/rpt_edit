# Liquid Cooling's Unsung Hero: Why Coolant Distribution Units, Not Cold Plates, Will Define the Data Center Infrastructure Winners

The data center industry is reaching a thermal inflection point. Air cooling, long the default modality for server heat rejection, is no longer sufficient for the rack densities that hyperscalers are now deploying. As chip thermal design power (TDP) continues its relentless climb, liquid cooling has transitioned from a niche solution for high-performance computing into a mainstream requirement. Within this shift, most investor attention has focused on cold plates and the broader narrative around direct-to-chip cooling. But that focus misses the more critical structural opportunity. The coolant distribution unit (CDU) is the component that will separate the winners from the also-rans in the liquid cooling ecosystem. It is more complex, more mission-critical, and more defensible as a business than the cold plates that sit atop the chips. Understanding why requires a deeper look at the engineering, the economics, and the competitive dynamics that are only now coming into focus.

The argument is straightforward: CDUs are a great business to be in because they are not easily commoditized, they carry a material service attach, and their failure carries catastrophic consequences. When a cold plate fails, you lose a single rack. When a CDU fails, you lose multiple racks. In an era where each rack can hold hundreds of thousands of dollars in GPU hardware, the cost of downtime far outweighs any savings from choosing a cheaper supplier. This creates a technical moat that protects pricing power and margins, even as the market scales. The question for investors is not whether liquid cooling will grow, but which companies have the right to win in the CDU segment, and what the technology roadmap will demand of them over the next five years.

## The Market Is Still Forming, But the Direction Is Clear: CDUs Will Be a Multi-Billion Dollar Opportunity by 2030

Market sizing for CDUs is a subject of genuine debate, and for good reason. The figures are highly sensitive to three variables that are themselves uncertain: the total gigawatts of new data center capacity added, the cost per kilowatt of cooling, and the useful life of the CDU equipment. Depending on how those inputs are modeled, the addressable market in 2026 could range from the low single-digit billions to something meaningfully higher. What is less debatable is the growth trajectory. A reasonable base case suggests a market size in the low single-digit billions for 2026, growing at mid-teens or higher compound annual growth over the following five years to reach a mid-to-high single-digit billion figure by 2030. That is a substantial opportunity, and it is one that is currently supply-constrained. Anyone with a credible CDU offering today is likely to see large backlogs through 2028.

The near-term dynamic is therefore one of scarcity. The number of companies that have both the engineering capability and the manufacturing scale to produce reliable, high-capacity CDUs is limited. This scarcity gives vendors pricing power and allows them to prioritize customers and applications. But the market will not remain supply-constrained forever. As capacity expands and more players enter, the risk of commoditization will rise. The key question is how much pricing power can be preserved. The answer depends on the complexity of the product, the importance of service, and the pace of technological change. On all three dimensions, CDUs look far more defensible than cold plates.

## CDUs Are More Defensible Than Cold Plates Because Complexity and Service Create a Technical Moat

Cold plates are simpler devices. They are essentially slabs of metal with internal channels through which coolant flows. Their performance depends on material science and surface geometry, but the underlying engineering is relatively straightforward. Multiple suppliers can produce cold plates that meet the specifications set by hyperscalers. The risk of commoditization is real and already visible. CDUs, by contrast, are integrated systems that combine pumps, heat exchangers, valves, sensors, and control logic. They must manage flow rates, pressure, temperature differentials, and redundancy across multiple racks simultaneously. The engineering challenge is orders of magnitude greater.

This complexity creates a natural barrier to entry. A CDU is not a component that can be reverse-engineered and replicated quickly. It requires deep expertise in fluid dynamics, thermal management, and control systems. It also requires a track record of reliability. Data center operators are risk-averse when it comes to cooling infrastructure. They will not switch to an unproven supplier to save a few percentage points on capital expenditure when the cost of a single failure could wipe out years of savings. This dynamic is reinforced by the service attach. Cold plates do not require ongoing maintenance. CDUs do. Pumps wear out. Filters need replacing. Control software needs updating. The service contracts that accompany CDU sales are a recurring revenue stream that further cements the customer relationship and makes it harder for competitors to displace an incumbent.

The combination of engineering complexity and service dependency means that the CDU market is likely to remain concentrated among a small number of players who can demonstrate both technical leadership and operational reliability. The winners will be those who can innovate faster than their customers can specify, who can build trust through uptime, and who can use their installed base to generate service revenue that smooths out the cyclicality of equipment sales.

## The Technology Roadmap Is Set by NVIDIA and the Open Compute Project, Creating a Right to Win for Those Who Participate

The direction of liquid cooling technology is not determined by the CDU manufacturers themselves. It is set by the chip designers, particularly NVIDIA, and by the hyperscaler-driven standards bodies such as the Open Compute Project (OCP). NVIDIA's GPU roadmaps dictate the thermal loads that cooling systems must handle. OCP specifications define the interfaces and performance requirements that equipment must meet. Companies that are closely aligned with both of these forces have a structural advantage. They see the roadmap before it is published. They can design their next-generation products in parallel with the chip designs they will need to cool. They can influence the standards to favor their own engineering strengths.

This is not a theoretical advantage. The companies that are part of the NVIDIA liquid cooling ecosystem, including Vertiv, nVent, Boyd, and Motivair, have all demonstrated the ability to produce CDUs that meet the most demanding performance targets. Trane Technologies, while less commonly associated with data center cooling, punches above its weight with a product lineup that includes high-capacity modular CDUs. The companies that are not in this ecosystem, or that are only peripherally involved, face a significant competitive disadvantage. They are reacting to standards rather than helping to shape them. They are learning about next-generation requirements at the same time as the broader market, rather than months or years in advance.

The implication is clear: the right to win in CDUs is not available to everyone. It is earned through deep engagement with the chipmakers and the hyperscalers who define the technology trajectory. Investors should be skeptical of claims that any company can succeed in this market simply by hiring a few engineers and building a factory. The moat is not just technical; it is relational.

## The Shift to Two-Phase Direct-to-Chip Cooling Could Reshape the Competitive Landscape

The biggest unresolved question in the CDU market is the transition from single-phase to two-phase direct-to-chip cooling. In single-phase systems, the coolant remains in liquid form throughout the cycle. In two-phase systems, the coolant boils and evaporates when it contacts the hot chip surface, absorbing far more heat through latent heat of vaporization. This allows two-phase systems to handle higher thermal loads without requiring proportionally higher flow rates. But it also demands fundamentally different equipment design. Gas behaves differently from liquid in a closed loop. The pumps, heat exchangers, and control systems must all be re-engineered.

Today, only two companies among the major CDU players have publicly announced two-phase products or published detailed technical perspectives: Vertiv and Accelsius (in which Johnson Controls has invested). Most other serious players are likely working on two-phase offerings, but they are not yet ready to discuss them publicly. The commercial scale is still at least a year away, if not more. But when it arrives, the transition has the potential to disrupt the market. Companies that are late to develop two-phase CDUs could find themselves locked out of the highest-growth segment of the market, where the most demanding hyperscaler applications will reside.

This creates a strategic fork. Companies that invest early in two-phase R&D and establish a track record of reliability in this new modality will be well-positioned to extend their leadership. Those that wait for the market to mature before developing their products will face an uphill battle. The window for making this bet is narrow. The engineering lead time is long, and the hyperscalers will not wait for laggards.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sensitivity of Market Size to CDU Lifespan and the Role of Facility-Level vs. Rack-Level Deployment

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several important questions open. The most significant is the useful life of CDUs. The market size estimates are highly sensitive to this assumption. If CDUs last ten years, the replacement market will be smaller than if they last five years. But nobody knows with certainty how long these early-generation CDUs will last in production environments. The data does not exist yet. The first wave of large-scale liquid cooling deployments is only now coming online. The failure modes, wear patterns, and maintenance requirements of CDUs operating at scale are still being discovered. This uncertainty matters because it directly affects the total addressable market and the revenue models of the CDU manufacturers.

A second open question is whether CDUs will migrate from rack-level to facility-level deployment. Today, most CDUs are deployed at the rack or row level. But as data centers scale, there is a logical argument for moving the cooling distribution to the facility level, where economies of scale and centralized maintenance can reduce costs. This would change the product mix and the competitive dynamics. Facility-level CDUs would be larger, more expensive, and more engineering-intensive. They would also create a different service model. The companies that succeed in rack-level CDUs may not have the capabilities to compete in facility-level systems, and vice versa. The direction of this migration is not yet clear, and it will depend on the preferences of the hyperscalers who are the dominant buyers.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Evaluate CDU Players on Roadmap Visibility, Service Capability, and Two-Phase Readiness

For investors trying to navigate this market, a structured decision framework is essential. The evaluation of CDU companies should focus on three dimensions. First, roadmap visibility. Does the company have a close relationship with NVIDIA and participation in OCP? If not, it is likely reacting to the market rather than shaping it, and that is a structural disadvantage. Second, service capability. Does the company have a global service network that can provide maintenance and support for CDUs deployed in hyperscale data centers? The service attach is a key differentiator, and it is not easy to build. Third, two-phase readiness. Does the company have a credible two-phase product in development or on the market? If not, it faces a risk of being disrupted when the technology transition accelerates.

These three dimensions are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. A company that scores well on all three is likely to be a long-term winner. A company that scores poorly on any one of them faces a significant strategic risk. The market will not reward companies that are strong on two dimensions but weak on the third. The technology transition is moving too fast, and the hyperscalers are too demanding, for partial excellence to suffice.

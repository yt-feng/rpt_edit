# The Hard Disk Drive Cycle Is Not Peaking — It Is Structurally Expanding, and the Market Is Still Underpricing the Earnings Power

The most important investment insight from the latest Asia-based supply chain checks is not that hard disk drive demand is strong. It is that the HDD cycle is elongating into a multi-year structural expansion, driven by a convergence of demand growth, supply discipline, and pricing power that the equity market has not yet fully discounted. The consensus view has treated the current HDD upcycle as a cyclical peak, but the evidence now points to a fundamentally different reality: shortages are expected to persist through at least calendar 2028, pricing is moving higher in a controlled and durable manner, and the two dominant vendors are positioned to deliver earnings that could exceed current Street estimates by 70% or more within three years.

This is not a marginal upgrade to a cyclical thesis. It is a re-framing of the HDD industry's earnings power over the next several years. The report from a global investment bank, based on three weeks of on-the-ground checks in Taiwan, COMPUTEX, and a Western Digital Asia roadshow, provides the most detailed evidence to date that the HDD market is entering a new phase. The critical question for investors is no longer whether the cycle will last. It is whether the market is willing to re-rate these stocks to reflect an earnings trajectory that looks more like a structural growth compounder than a cyclical spike.

The data points are unambiguous. Nearline HDD supply is projected to fall short of demand by 300 exabytes in calendar 2026, 400 exabytes in 2027, and a further 400 exabytes in 2028. Demand growth is running at 40-50% annually, while supply growth is constrained to 30-35%. Hyperscalers are deploying HDDs at close to 100% of available supply, compared to a historical rate of roughly 70%, indicating that inventory accumulation at the largest customers is minimal. Original design manufacturers are holding just one to two weeks of HDD inventory. These are not the conditions of a topping cycle. They are the conditions of a structurally undersupplied market with multi-year visibility.

Pricing is the second pillar of the argument and the source of the most dramatic upside. The blended nearline price per terabyte for Seagate and Western Digital is currently in the range of $14.30 to $14.90. The latest Taiwan checks indicate that HDD vendors are targeting a blended nearline price of $25 to $30 per terabyte over the next two to three years. Spot market prices are already 30% higher than contract prices, with some distributors selling HDDs for $30 to $35 per terabyte today. The mechanism for capturing this pricing is not speculative. It is embedded in prescriptive quarter-over-quarter pricing escalators within long-term contracts. The vendors are also limiting the duration of new long-term agreements to avoid locking in today's lower prices, even as hyperscalers push for commitments extending to 2032.

The financial impact of even a partial realization of these pricing targets is staggering. The report estimates that if blended nearline pricing reaches $25 per terabyte in calendar 2027 and $30 in calendar 2028, Seagate could generate over $70 of earnings per share in 2027 and over $100 in 2028. Western Digital could produce over $40 of EPS in 2027 and over $70 in 2028. For context, both companies generated roughly $10 and $7 of EPS respectively in calendar 2025. A tenfold increase in earnings over three years is not a forecast that can be dismissed as extrapolative optimism. It is a direct mathematical implication of the pricing data collected from the supply chain.

The market is not pricing this outcome. The report's base case forecasts, which are themselves significantly above consensus, assume a blended nearline price of only $19 to $22 per terabyte in 2027 and 2028. Even at these conservative assumptions, the report's fiscal 2028 EPS estimates are 74% above consensus for Seagate and 66% above for Western Digital. The gap between what the supply chain data implies and what the equity market has discounted is the central analytical opportunity.

## The structural drivers of HDD demand are broadening beyond traditional cloud storage, creating a more durable demand base than in prior cycles.

The conventional narrative has been that HDDs are a legacy technology, gradually displaced by solid-state drives and subject to secular decline. The current cycle challenges that narrative on multiple fronts. The demand growth is not coming from a single source. It is being driven by three concurrent forces: core cloud services growth that is outperforming expectations, the expansion of AI inferencing and agent-based workloads, and the resulting increase in general-purpose server shipments that require HDD-based cold storage.

The report's supply chain checks indicate that hyperscalers are deploying HDDs at nearly 100% of available supply, a rate that implies they are not building inventory buffers but are instead absorbing every unit they can receive. This is consistent with a demand environment where the applications for HDD storage are expanding, not contracting. The total cost of ownership advantage of HDDs relative to NAND-based storage for cold and warm data tiers is improving as HDD areal density increases and as NAND prices experience parabolic increases. The report notes that HDD vendors are gaining greater conviction to raise prices precisely because the TCO comparison with NAND is shifting in their favor.

The breadth of demand is also reflected in the elongation of visibility. The report's checks indicate that shortages are expected to persist through at least calendar 2028, which is a far longer horizon than typical for a hardware cycle. This is not a demand spike driven by a single customer or a one-time inventory build. It is a structural imbalance between demand growth of 40-50% annually and supply growth of 30-35%. The supply constraint is self-imposed and deliberate. The HDD vendors have been disciplined about capacity additions, with no greenfield capacity buildouts. The industry is operating as a rational oligopoly, and that rationality is now a key driver of pricing power.

## HDD pricing power is structural, not cyclical, because the industry structure and technology barriers prevent new supply from emerging.

The most common objection to a sustained HDD pricing upcycle is that high prices will attract new entrants or encourage existing players to break discipline and add capacity. The report addresses this risk directly, and the evidence suggests it is limited. The barriers to entry in the HDD industry are not primarily about capital expenditure. They are about technology, time, and supply chain consolidation.

The report cites Western Digital's management in explaining that becoming competitive as a new entrant requires navigating three distinct challenges. First, the time required to qualify a next-generation recording technology is measured in years, not quarters. It has taken over a decade to qualify heat-assisted magnetic recording, the latest areal density technology. Second, the vertical integration required spans disparate fields of knowledge, including magnetics, material science, photonics, and nanofabrication. Third, the HDD supply chain is highly consolidated, with limited underutilized capacity available for a new entrant to access. The report notes that Chinese players, including Huawei with its Magneto-Electric Disk, have attempted to create cold-storage alternatives but have found little success.

The implication is that the current HDD duopoly has a multi-year window of pricing power that is protected by structural barriers, not by market timing. The vendors are not merely benefiting from favorable demand-supply dynamics. They are operating in an industry where the cost of entry is prohibitive and the time to scale is measured in technology generations. This is the kind of industry structure that can sustain pricing power even as demand growth normalizes.

The report also highlights an important behavioral shift. HDD vendors are limiting their use of 12-month or longer long-term agreements, preferring to keep commercial terms shorter to capture future pricing increases. Hyperscalers are asking for agreements extending to 2032, but the vendors are resisting. This is a clear signal that the vendors themselves believe pricing will be higher in the future and are structuring their commercial relationships to capture that upside. It is also a signal that the balance of power in negotiations has shifted.

## The earnings sensitivity to pricing is extreme, making the bull case a plausible outcome rather than a tail risk.

The report provides a detailed sensitivity analysis that every investor should study. For Seagate, a $1 increase in blended nearline price per terabyte in calendar 2027 translates into roughly $10 of incremental EPS. For Western Digital, the same $1 increase adds roughly $5 of EPS. The leverage is driven by the fact that incremental pricing flows through almost entirely to profit, after tax effects. The cost structure is largely fixed in the near term, and the vendors are not making material changes to operating expenses or capital expenditure plans.

This means that the difference between the base case and the bull case is not a question of speculative demand. It is a question of pricing. The base case assumes a blended nearline price of $19 to $22 per terabyte in 2027 and 2028. The bull case assumes $22 to $29. The Taiwan checks indicate that vendors are targeting $25 to $30. If the vendors achieve even the midpoint of their target range, the bull case becomes the base case, and the upside to current consensus estimates is multiples of what the market is pricing.

The report's price targets reflect this asymmetry. For Seagate, the new base case price target is $1,035, based on 20 times calendar 2027 EPS of $51.67. The bull case valuation is $1,446, based on 22 times bull case EPS of $65.74. For Western Digital, the base case target is $650, and the bull case is $920. These targets are not extrapolations of current trends. They are direct mathematical consequences of the pricing data collected from the supply chain. The market is being asked to decide whether to trust the channel checks or to assume that pricing will revert to historical norms.

## What the report does not fully answer: the path of cost declines, the risk of demand elasticity at higher prices, and the timing of capacity additions.

The report is thorough in its analysis of demand, supply, and pricing, but it leaves several important questions open. The first is the trajectory of cost declines. The report assumes that cost per exabyte continues to decline at roughly 13% annually, in line with historical patterns. But if the vendors are able to sustain higher pricing while also benefiting from areal density improvements, the margin expansion could be even more dramatic than the report models. Conversely, if cost declines accelerate due to technology transitions, some of the pricing upside could be offset. The report does not provide a detailed sensitivity on the cost side.

The second open question is demand elasticity. The report assumes that hyperscalers will absorb higher HDD prices without reducing their deployment rates. But at $25 to $30 per terabyte, the TCO advantage over NAND narrows. It is possible that some workloads shift to SSD or that hyperscalers optimize their storage architectures to reduce HDD dependence. The report acknowledges that the TCO comparison is improving for HDDs, but it does not model a scenario where demand growth decelerates in response to higher prices.

The third question is the timing of capacity additions. The report is clear that no greenfield capacity is being built, but it does not address the possibility that existing vendors could accelerate technology transitions to increase areal density and effectively add supply without building new factories. The HAMR transition is a multi-year process, and the rate at which it increases effective supply is uncertain. If the vendors are able to increase areal density faster than expected, the supply-demand balance could shift, limiting pricing upside.

These open questions do not invalidate the thesis. They define the range of outcomes. Investors who want to understand the full set of assumptions and sensitivities should read the complete report and review the original charts.

## A decision framework for investors: the HDD thesis rests on three conditions that can be monitored in real time.

For institutional investors evaluating this opportunity, the thesis can be reduced to three conditions that are observable and testable. The first is the trajectory of nearline HDD pricing. If blended pricing moves toward $20 per terabyte within the next 12 to 18 months, the base case will prove conservative. If pricing remains below $18, the bull case will be delayed. The second condition is the pace of supply growth. If the vendors announce new capacity additions or if technology transitions accelerate, the supply constraint will ease, and pricing power will diminish. The third condition is the behavior of hyperscaler procurement. If hyperscalers begin to push back on pricing escalators or if their deployment rates decline from the current near-100% level, the demand picture will weaken.

These three conditions can be tracked through quarterly earnings calls, industry conferences, and supply chain checks. The report provides a clear baseline against which new data can be measured. The risk for investors is not that the thesis is wrong. It is that the market will take time to re-rate the stocks even as the data improves. The report's price targets imply significant upside, but the path to that upside requires patience and conviction.

The HDD cycle is not peaking. It is expanding. The evidence from the supply chain is consistent, directional, and increasingly difficult to dismiss. The market will eventually adjust its expectations. The question is whether investors will act before the adjustment occurs.

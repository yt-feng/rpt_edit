EN_B: It’s a big deal for China’s transportation sector. The report models a benign scenario where Gulf oil exports normalize by the end of July 2026. Under that assumption, the impact on tankers, airlines, and shipbuilders could be massive.

EN_B: Yes, tankers benefit the most, especially COSCO Shipping Energy. The report thinks that if Hormuz reopens, VLCC daily charter rates could jump from around 90 to 100 thousand dollars today to 150 thousand in the base case. In the benign scenario, they could hit 250 thousand dollars a day.
EN_A: 250 thousand a day? That’s a huge jump. What’s driving that?
EN_B: It’s all about supply and demand flipping. The closure created a 9 to 11 percentage point oversupply of tanker capacity. When it reopens, that reverses. First, there’s a massive restocking need. Global crude inventories are 705 million barrels below pre-war levels, which means about 4.8 percent more demand for seaborne transport.
EN_A: So restocking is the first driver. What’s the second?
EN_B: Pricing power. The report points out that the VLCC market has become more concentrated after consolidation by Chinese and Korean operators. The top ten compliant players now control 68 percent of the market. That gives them stronger pricing power. Every one percent change in net demand now moves daily rates by 20 thousand dollars, compared to just 6 thousand before.
EN_A: That’s a big shift in elasticity. What about the blue sky scenario where Iranian oil sanctions are lifted?
EN_B: That’s even more aggressive. If sanctions go away, about 5 percent more shipping demand could shift from the shadow fleet to compliant tankers. That could push VLCC rates to 350 thousand dollars a day. COSCO Energy’s earnings could rise 57 percent in the benign scenario and 114 percent in the blue sky scenario.
EN_A: What does that mean for the stock price?
EN_B: The report’s scenario analysis shows 113 percent upside for COSCO Energy’s H-shares in the benign case, and 189 percent in the blue sky case. Even after applying a 25 percent discount to the valuation multiple, the potential is still significant.
EN_A: Airlines are also mentioned as beneficiaries. How does that work?
EN_B: Airlines benefit from lower oil prices. If Hormuz reopens, the report’s commodities team estimates oil could drop by 10 dollars a barrel. For the big three Chinese airlines, that could boost earnings by 16 to 26 percent, thanks to lower fuel costs and better demand. Spring Airlines sees a smaller 4 percent gain because it’s already more fuel-efficient.
EN_A: And what about the stock upside for airlines?
EN_B: The report estimates the H-shares of the big three could have 60 to 70 percent upside. The logic is that lower oil not only cuts costs but could also reduce fuel surcharges, which stimulates travel demand.
EN_A: Shipbuilders are also in the mix. How do they fit in?
EN_B: Shipbuilders have a more indirect story. A Hormuz reopening won’t directly boost their near-term earnings because their order books are already full. But the report argues that higher freight rates and better trade visibility could encourage shippers to place new orders. That could re-accelerate order momentum after a slowdown in May, which would be a positive catalyst for shipbuilder stocks.
EN_A: So tankers, airlines, and shipbuilders are all winners. Is anyone hurt by this scenario?
EN_B: Yes, container shipping could face downside. The report says that if Hormuz reopens and the Red Sea also normalizes, effective capacity could increase by about 10 percent. Ships can take shorter routes, and lower fuel prices encourage faster sailing. That would put pressure on container freight rates.

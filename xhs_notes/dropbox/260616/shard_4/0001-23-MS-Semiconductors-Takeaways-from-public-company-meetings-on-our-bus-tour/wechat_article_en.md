# The AI Infrastructure Battle Has Shifted from Compute to Interconnect, and the Winners Will Be Those Who Own the Full Optical-to-Silicon Stack

The semiconductor industry's center of gravity is moving. For the past two years, the dominant narrative in AI infrastructure has been about who builds the fastest training chips. That narrative is now incomplete. Based on recent meetings with CEOs from three of the most important companies in the AI connectivity ecosystem, a clear strategic inflection point has emerged: the next phase of value creation in AI semiconductors will be driven not by raw compute performance, but by how efficiently data moves between an exponentially growing number of accelerators.

This is not a subtle shift. It is a structural reordering of the industry's competitive dynamics. The companies that recognize this early and position themselves accordingly will capture disproportionate value. Those that continue to view the market through a compute-first lens risk missing the most important growth vector of the next decade.

The evidence from these meetings is compelling. Astera Labs sees its content per XPU climbing from roughly $1,000 today as AI infrastructure becomes more interconnect-intensive. Marvell frames its entire AI opportunity as increasingly interconnect-driven, with a connectivity business growing approximately 70% with no near-term slowdown. Even Intel, a company whose identity has been defined by the microprocessor, is now betting its future on a foundry strategy that depends on advanced packaging and interconnect capabilities.

The message is clear: the era of treating interconnect as an afterthought in AI infrastructure design is over. The question for investors and strategists is not whether this transition will happen, but which companies are best positioned to profit from it.

## The Scale-Up Opportunity Is Larger and More Durable Than the Scale-Out Opportunity Most Investors Understand

The market has largely absorbed the scale-out narrative. The idea that AI workloads require massive clusters of GPUs connected across data centers is well understood. What is less appreciated is the scale-up opportunity within the rack and between adjacent racks, where the technical challenges are more complex and the economic rewards potentially greater.

Astera Labs' management articulated this distinction with unusual clarity. They described a PCIe TAM of roughly $10 billion and an Ethernet TAM of another $10 billion, with NVL and ICI solutions representing the largest components of a broader market that exceeds $100 billion. The critical insight is that these are not static numbers. As AI infrastructure becomes more interconnect-intensive, the content per XPU continues to grow. A $1,000 content opportunity today becomes significantly larger as customers demand higher bandwidth, lower latency, and more sophisticated switching architectures.

Marvell's management reinforced this point by emphasizing that today most growth comes from scale-out, but a significant scale-up and scale-across opportunity lies ahead. Their model contemplates custom silicon growing from roughly $4 billion to $10 billion, while connectivity grows approximately 70% with no near-term slowdown. Importantly, none of the potential agentic-AI CPU uplift is included in current numbers. More agentic workloads would drive additional NICs, CXL, and switches, creating further upside that the market has not yet priced in.

The strategic implication is straightforward. Companies that can capture the scale-up opportunity will benefit from a growth trajectory that is both larger and more durable than the scale-out opportunity. Scale-out is ultimately a volume game, driven by the number of accelerators deployed. Scale-up is a value game, driven by the complexity and sophistication of the interconnect solution required for each accelerator. The latter is inherently more defensible and more profitable.

## The Optical Transition Creates a Structural Shift That Rewards Companies with Full-Stack Capabilities

The migration from copper to optical connectivity is not a question of if, but when. The technical constraints are clear: at 400G rack-to-rack, copper is no longer viable. The economic implications are equally clear: optical links are materially more valuable than the copper links they replace, even when accounting for the loss of retimer content.

What is less obvious is which companies will capture this value. Astera Labs' management argued that the most important factor is owning a switch product that optics can attach to. This is a critical insight. The company that controls the switch controls the architecture, and the company that controls the architecture captures the majority of the economic value.

Marvell's positioning is even more aggressive. The company sees itself as one of the few vendors spanning DCI, die-to-die IP, scale-out switching, scale-up switching, optics, SerDes, CXL, NICs, and custom silicon. Management argues that this full-stack position matters more as customers confront the complexity of integrating switches, optics, third-party IP, and XPUs. In a world where supply chains are constrained and integration challenges are growing, the company that can offer a complete solution has a structural advantage.

This creates a fascinating competitive dynamic. The optical transition is not a simple technology upgrade. It is a structural shift that rewards companies with breadth and penalizes those with narrow specialization. The winners will be those that can offer customers a seamless path from the compute die to the optical transceiver, with proprietary technology at every layer.

## The Foundry and Packaging Bottleneck Creates an Opening for Intel, but the Execution Hurdle Is Daunting

Intel's new CEO has made the strategic decision to double down on foundry, believing that the internal product engine and external foundry business should reinforce each other. The logic is sound. In a world where leading-edge capacity is scarce, having both internal and external options creates healthy competitive tension and improves customer confidence.

The critical milestone is 14A, which management describes as the make-or-break process node. All Intel-designed products will move to 14A, and early process indicators are improving. The company is seeing roughly 0.5 defect density and approximately 40% yield, with a goal of reaching 0.1 to 0.2 defect density by the first quarter of next year. Risk production is framed around 2028, with volume around 2029, broadly comparable to TSMC timing.

The board is now asking Intel to invest more in capex, and management says the message has been received. But this creates a tension that is difficult to resolve. Intel needs to prove that incremental spend can translate into customer wins, volume, and margin recovery. Initially, management suggested volume is more important than margin, particularly in foundry, where scale and customer confidence are prerequisites. This is a reasonable strategic posture, but it raises difficult questions about capital allocation and return on investment.

The advanced packaging opportunity is equally compelling and equally challenging. Intel describes EMIB as a "secret weapon" with strong technology and large customer potential, but also meaningful complexity and reliability questions. Individual customer opportunities could be worth billions, but execution depends on building the right team, improving automation, and proving reliability at scale.

The strategic question is whether Intel can execute on both fronts simultaneously. The foundry business requires massive capital investment and years of patient execution. The advanced packaging business requires technical excellence and customer trust. Both are necessary for Intel's transformation, but both are difficult to achieve. The company does not have the balance sheet of a hyperscaler to secure critical inputs like DRAM, and recent Asia checks suggest that some of the current wins being reported have yet to be completely secured.

## The Report Raises More Questions Than It Answers About Valuation and Competitive Dynamics

For all the strategic clarity these meetings provide, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most pressing is valuation. Marvell trades at approximately 2.5 times NVIDIA's multiple for a company that has not outgrown NVIDIA in a long while. Astera Labs has seen a strong run-up in its stock price that creates a high bar for future performance. Intel's valuation at roughly 42 times 2027 earnings reflects high leverage potential on numbers that are still depressed, but also significant execution risk.

The competitive dynamics are equally unclear. Marvell argues that customer preference for non-Broadcom alternatives should support its position, particularly where software, silicon, and optical integration matter. But Broadcom is a formidable competitor with deep customer relationships and a proven track record. Astera Labs believes the switch opportunity will not be winner takes all, but this is an assertion, not a proven outcome. The market for scale-up switching is still up for grabs, and the eventual structure is uncertain.

The timeline questions are equally important. First deployments for UAL are expected next year, with a bigger ramp in 2028. CXL revenue is expected in 2027. CPO for scale-up is more likely a 2028 opportunity at minimum. These are not near-term catalysts. The market is pricing in significant future growth, but the path from here to there is uncertain and likely nonlinear.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Interconnect Transition

For investors and strategists trying to navigate this transition, the following framework may be useful. First, assess the company's position in the optical-to-silicon stack. Companies that own proprietary technology at multiple layers of the stack have a structural advantage over those that are dependent on third-party components. Second, evaluate the company's exposure to scale-up versus scale-out. Scale-up is more defensible and more profitable, but also more technically demanding. Third, consider the company's ability to execute on its roadmap. The interconnect transition is technically complex, and delays are likely. Companies with proven execution track records should be preferred.

Fourth, be realistic about timelines. The optical transition is real, but it will take longer than the market expects. Near-term catalysts are scarce, and the risk of disappointment is significant. Fifth, watch the competitive dynamics carefully. The market for scale-up switching is still up for grabs, and the eventual structure will determine which companies capture the most value.

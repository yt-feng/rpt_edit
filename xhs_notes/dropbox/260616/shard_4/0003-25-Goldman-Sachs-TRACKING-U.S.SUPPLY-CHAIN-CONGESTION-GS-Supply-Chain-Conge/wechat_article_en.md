# The U.S. Supply Chain Is No Longer the Problem. The Question Is Whether It Was Ever the Real Problem.

For the first time in nearly five years, the U.S. supply chain has become a non-event for corporate strategists. The latest weekly congestion index, monitored by a leading global investment bank, fell another 4% week-over-week, with the bottleneck scale holding steady at a reading of 2 on a 10-point scale. That number is not just low—it is historically unremarkable. It sits at levels last seen before the pandemic, before the port backlogs, before the container ship armadas anchored off Los Angeles and Long Beach. The index is now tracking at a level that, in any other era, would be considered normal fluidity.

This is not a story of recovery. Recovery implies a return from crisis. The U.S. supply chain, by this measure, has already returned. The real story is what comes next—and whether the normalization of physical logistics is masking deeper fragilities in demand, labor, and trade policy that could make the next disruption far more strategic and far less visible.

The data is clear: West Coast container ship backlogs remain at one vessel. East Coast backlogs, while slightly higher at five, have been stable for weeks. Rail intermodal traffic on the West Coast accelerated to 16% year-over-year growth, up from 10% the prior week. Chassis dwell times have declined. Ocean shipping rates from East Asia to the U.S. West Coast fell 12% year-over-year, a sharp deceleration. These are the metrics of a system that has absorbed past shocks and returned to a steady state.

But steady state is not the same as resilience. And the difference matters enormously for anyone making capital allocation decisions, negotiating freight contracts, or planning inventory strategies for 2026 and beyond.

## The Bottleneck Index Has Normalized, But the Metrics That Matter Most Are Decoupling From Each Other

The headline index tells a simple story: congestion is gone. But a closer look at the underlying components reveals a more complex picture. Some metrics are improving faster than others, and a few are actually deteriorating in ways that do not show up in the aggregate score.

Consider the divergence between the West Coast and East Coast. West Coast backlogs are at one vessel—effectively zero congestion. East Coast backlogs remain at five, unchanged for weeks. This is not a uniform recovery. It is a regional one, shaped by shifting trade routes, labor dynamics, and port investment decisions made years ago. The East Coast is not yet back to pre-pandemic fluidity, and the gap is not closing quickly.

Then look at rail. West Coast Class 1 rail intermodal traffic growth accelerated sharply to 16% year-over-year. That sounds like a positive signal. But rail velocity—how fast trains move—is declining. BNSF intermodal velocity fell 10% year-over-year, and Union Pacific velocity dropped nearly 2%. More traffic at slower speeds means the system is handling volume but not efficiently. Dwell times, while mixed, remain above pre-pandemic norms at some points in the network.

The chassis data tells a similar story. Street dwell times for 40/45-foot containers actually increased to 6.2 days from 5.8 days the prior week. Terminal dwell times for 20-foot containers dropped from 12.4 days to 10.8 days—an improvement, but still far above the 5- to 7-day range that operators consider healthy. The system is not broken, but it is not optimized either.

What this means for decision-makers is that aggregate indices are becoming less useful. The days when one number could capture supply chain health are over. The normalization is real, but it is uneven. Companies that rely on national or coast-wide averages risk missing local bottlenecks that could disrupt specific routes, ports, or modes of transport.

## The Real Risk Is Not Congestion—It Is Demand Fragility Masquerading as Stability

The most dangerous assumption in supply chain strategy today is that low congestion equals strong demand. It does not. In fact, some of the metrics in the report suggest the opposite.

Ocean shipping rates from East Asia to the U.S. West Coast fell 12% year-over-year. That is a dramatic decline. Rate drops of this magnitude typically signal one of two things: excess capacity or weakening demand. The global container shipping fleet has grown, but not enough to explain a 12% rate decline on its own. The more likely driver is that import demand is softening.

The monthly data supports this interpretation. Inbound loaded container volumes at the three largest West Coast ports have been negative year-over-year for seven of the last twelve months. The declines are not catastrophic—typically in the single digits—but they are persistent. The LMI Transportation Capacity index, which measures how easily companies can find trucking and rail capacity, has been above 50 (indicating expansion) for most of the past year, and in April it surged to 55.2. That is not a sign of a strained system. It is a sign of a system with room to spare.

Warehousing utilization tells a similar story. After peaking at 68.3 in January 2025, it has drifted down to 64.4 by April 2026. Warehousing capacity, meanwhile, has been expanding. The LMI Warehousing Capacity index has been above 50 for most of the period and hit 61.2 in December 2025. Companies are not struggling to find storage space. They are not rushing to restock.

This is not the profile of a supply chain under pressure. It is the profile of a supply chain that has normalized because demand has normalized—or, more precisely, because demand has not grown as fast as capacity. The risk is not that congestion returns. The risk is that companies mistake operational calm for strategic strength and fail to prepare for a demand downturn that could leave them with excess inventory, underutilized logistics assets, and margin pressure from falling freight rates.

## An Unresolved Analytical Question: What Happens When Tariffs and Geopolitics Intersect With a Normalized Supply Chain?

The report acknowledges a critical question that remains unanswered: what impact will tariffs and geopolitical conflicts have on demand and the timing of freight flows? This is not a peripheral concern. It is the central strategic uncertainty for the next 12 to 24 months.

The current supply chain normalization occurred in a specific policy environment. Trade flows adjusted to existing tariff structures. Companies built inventory strategies around known costs and routes. But that environment is not static. Tariff regimes can change with elections, trade negotiations, or geopolitical flashpoints. The report notes that if supply chain pressures continue to mitigate, the index could fall to a consistent '1' by 2026. But that projection assumes no major policy shocks.

What if tariffs on Chinese goods increase? What if new restrictions target specific sectors like semiconductors, electric vehicles, or pharmaceuticals? What if conflict in the South China Sea or the Taiwan Strait disrupts shipping lanes? Each of these scenarios would hit a supply chain that is currently calm but not resilient. The system has normalized, but it has not hardened. It has not built the redundancy, the inventory buffers, or the route diversification that would absorb a major shock.

The report does not model these scenarios in detail. It does not need to—that is not its purpose. But for the reader, the implication is clear: the current congestion index is a rearview mirror. It tells you where the system has been. It does not tell you where it is going. The next disruption will not look like the last one. It will not start with a pandemic. It will start with a policy decision, a military escalation, or a trade negotiation that reshapes the cost and flow of global goods overnight.

## A Decision Framework for Supply Chain Strategy in a Post-Normalization World

If the old framework was about managing congestion, the new framework must be about managing volatility. The two are not the same. Congestion is a measure of physical capacity utilization. Volatility is a measure of how quickly conditions can change. The current environment is low on congestion but potentially high on volatility. That demands a different set of strategic priorities.

First, shift from aggregate monitoring to route-specific and mode-specific tracking. The national index is no longer sufficient. Companies should track dwell times, velocity, and rate trends at the specific ports and rail corridors they use. The divergence between West Coast and East Coast metrics is a warning sign that aggregate data can mask local risk.

Second, treat falling ocean rates as a signal, not a benefit. Lower rates may improve short-term margins, but they often precede a demand slowdown. Companies should stress-test their inventory and logistics plans against a scenario where import volumes decline 5% to 10% over the next six months. If the logistics network is already operating with spare capacity, a demand contraction could lead to aggressive rate competition and margin compression for carriers and logistics providers.

Third, build tariff scenario planning into supply chain modeling. The current normalization assumes current policy. That assumption is fragile. Companies should model at least three scenarios: a moderate tariff increase on targeted goods, a broad-based tariff escalation, and a geopolitical disruption that closes or reroutes major shipping lanes. Each scenario will have different implications for inventory placement, sourcing strategy, and logistics partner selection.

Fourth, reconsider the definition of resilience. In the congestion era, resilience meant having enough inventory to survive a port closure. In the volatility era, resilience means having the flexibility to change routes, suppliers, and inventory levels quickly. That requires investments in data, analytics, and relationships—not just in warehouses and containers. The companies that will outperform are those that can see the next shift coming, not those that can survive the current state.

EN_A: So there is this debate going on about whether we are entering a capex supercycle in upstream oil and gas. What is your take?
EN_B: We looked into it and we are not convinced. A recent report from a global investment bank used a five W framework to assess the situation, and the conclusion is that capex will only see modest adjustments, not a massive expansion.
EN_A: Five Ws? That sounds like a journalism technique. How does it apply to oil and gas?
EN_B: Exactly. It is why invest, what to invest in, who will invest, where to invest, and when to invest. The report uses these five dimensions to systematically analyze the outlook for upstream capital spending.
EN_A: Let us start with the first W. Why invest? Oil prices are pretty high right now, right?
EN_B: Brent is around 90 dollars a barrel, and cash flows have improved significantly. But the problem is that companies are not increasing their reinvestment appetite. Investors are still rewarding capital discipline, buybacks, and free cash flow over production growth.
EN_A: So even with higher oil prices, companies do not want to spend more?
EN_B: Exactly. There is a fascinating finding in the report. Companies that returned more cash flow to shareholders actually saw better stock performance. That means the market is not encouraging a big increase in capital spending.
EN_A: What about the second W? What barrels are being developed?
EN_B: Roughly one third of the discovered resources over the past century remain undeveloped. But most of these are deepwater projects, which are increasingly difficult to convert into supply. Shale production is plateauing, and oil sands need more policy clarity.
EN_A: So it is not a lack of resources, but the cost and complexity are too high?
EN_B: Right. And the third W tells us who is actually going to invest. National oil companies, not US shale independents, will lead the next cycle. That is a major shift.
EN_A: What does that mean? How are NOCs different from independent companies?
EN_B: NOCs are more driven by national strategy than pure commercial returns. They might be more willing to invest, but they could be less efficient. And many of the best resources are in jurisdictions with high political risk.
EN_A: That brings us to the fourth W. Where can capital go?
EN_B: This is the key insight. Many of the largest undeveloped barrels sit in countries with high fiscal take, political instability, sanctions, or weak rule of law. The best geology is often in the worst jurisdictions.
EN_A: That sounds like a classic dilemma. And the last W, when to invest?
EN_B: Normally high oil prices trigger a sharp pickup in rig activity, but that pattern has broken down this time. Oilfield service companies have not raised their guidance. And upstream inflation means companies are spending more just to keep activity flat.
EN_A: So even with high oil prices, there is no signal from service companies to ramp up?
EN_B: No. The report highlights this anomaly. Putting all five Ws together, we estimate upstream capex will be around 560 billion dollars in 2026, up about 50 billion from previous forecasts, but nowhere near a supercycle.
EN_A: What if oil prices stay high for longer? Would that change corporate behavior?
EN_B: There is a very interesting discussion in the report about planning prices. Companies are still using 70 dollars a barrel as their planning assumption, not the current 90 dollars. That is a big gap.
EN_A: That is fascinating. So what is the investment implication from this report?
EN_B: The report favors companies with strong capital discipline and free cash flow, like some of the names with advantages in the Permian Basin. But it leaves a big question mark about what happens after the Strait of Hormuz crisis ends.

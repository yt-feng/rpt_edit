EN_A: So there's this really interesting framework in a recent research report that breaks down data center power into three distinct models. Why does this distinction matter so much?
EN_B: It matters because it answers a fundamental question: in a world where grid interconnection is getting slower and slower, are hyperscalers paying for speed or for scale? Bloom Energy, Fervo Energy, and Ormat are all in the data center power business, but their business models, risk profiles, and potential returns are completely different.
EN_A: Let's start with speed. Bloom Energy seems to be the pure speed play.
EN_B: Exactly. Bloom is a classic behind-the-meter model. The generation sits on the customer's site and bypasses the grid interconnection process entirely. The report says Bloom can go from order to energized power in 55 to 90 days. Compare that to the three to eight years it takes for a standard grid interconnection today. That speed gap is its entire value proposition.
EN_A: So Bloom helps customers skip the biggest bottleneck. But what's the price for that speed?
EN_B: The price is a higher cost. The report estimates Bloom's levelized cost of energy is between 85 and 115 dollars per megawatt-hour, which is a real premium over wholesale power in most markets. That premium comes from two things: first, it runs on natural gas, so it's exposed to fuel price volatility. Second, the fuel cell stacks need to be replaced every five to seven years, which is a significant recurring capital cost.
EN_A: And Fervo? It's a front-of-meter model, which sounds more traditional.
EN_B: Yes, Fervo is front-of-meter. It connects its geothermal plants to the grid and sells power through utility PPAs. So it still has to wait in the interconnection queue. But Fervo's big bet is on its cost curve. The report says Fervo's current project cost is about 7,000 dollars per kilowatt. But its stated target is to get that down to 3,000 dollars per kilowatt. If it hits that target, front-of-meter geothermal becomes very competitive.
EN_A: What does that mean? If Fervo succeeds, does it become a direct threat to Bloom?
EN_B: In the medium term, yes. If Fervo hits that 3,000 dollar per kilowatt target, its LCOE would drop to between 48 and 60 dollars per megawatt-hour. That's cheaper than Bloom's post-tax-credit cost, and it has no fuel price risk. But that's a big if. Fervo has to prove it can achieve that cost curve at scale, and it's still in the early stages of its first commercial project.
EN_A: And what about Ormat? It seems less flashy.
EN_B: Ormat is the most conservative, lowest-risk player. Its two data center deals are basically brownfield expansions of existing geothermal plants. Think of it as a power plant uprate. The economics are the cheapest of the three, but the model doesn't scale. The report argues that Ormat is limited by the availability of good, uprate-able geothermal assets near data center clusters. It's a scarce source of supply, not a scalable one.
EN_A: So these three companies are really competing in different races.
EN_B: That's right. Bloom is selling speed. Fervo is betting on its cost curve. Ormat is collecting low-risk, incremental returns. The report makes a key point: if interconnection reform really accelerates, Bloom's speed premium will shrink. Then the market will focus on who has the better long-term cost structure. And that points to Fervo if it executes.
EN_A: What does the report say about interconnection reform itself?
EN_B: It notes that PJM, ERCOT, and FERC are all pushing reforms like fast-track cycles and cluster studies. But these are still early in implementation. Faster-than-expected reform compresses Bloom's premium. Slower-than-expected reform extends Bloom's runway and the case for more utility-backed deals like the AEP one. This uncertainty is itself a key variable for investors.

# The AI Packaging Race Has Shifted from Die Count to Power Delivery, and Japan's Substrate Suppliers Are Already Winning

The most consequential data point in Japan's electronic parts sector this quarter is not a company's earnings guidance or a product launch. It is a single number from the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry's Current Survey of Production: packaging substrate shipments reached ¥27.8 billion in May, a 36% year-on-year increase that shattered the previous record set just two months earlier. For observers tracking the AI hardware supply chain, the signal is clear: the companies that solve power integrity at the package level will capture the highest value in the coming cycle, and Japan's substrate manufacturers are already benefiting from this shift.

The record shipment value is particularly telling because it is not driven by volume alone. Shipments in terms of square meters rose only 10% year-on-year, while the average price per square meter surged 23% to ¥1.356 million, surpassing the previous record of ¥1.226 million set in April. This price escalation reflects a fundamental change in what is being shipped. These are not commodity printed circuit boards. These are high-density, multilayer packaging substrates designed to handle the extreme power and signal integrity demands of multi-die AI accelerators. The price-per-area metric is a direct proxy for technical complexity, and its acceleration indicates that customers are ordering more sophisticated substrates, not just more of the same. The report infers that shipments of Rubin packages will pick up in earnest this summer, around August at the latest, suggesting that the current data already captures early production ramps.

This is not a demand story about AI adoption in the abstract. It is a supply-chain story about a specific technical bottleneck that is reshaping competitive dynamics between the two dominant players in advanced packaging: TSMC and Intel. The conventional narrative has focused on die counts and reticle sizes, with TSMC's CoWoS-L and Intel's EMIB competing to integrate ever-larger numbers of compute and memory dies. The report's analysis of Intel's newly published technical paper on EMIB-T at the Electronic Components and Technology Conference reveals that the real competitive differentiator is not how many dies can be placed, but how much power can be delivered to them without unacceptable voltage drop. The blunt implication is that standard EMIB was not viable for accelerator packages because the power supply decline was too sharp. Intel has solved this by adding through-silicon vias to a 3x18mm silicon bridge, along with a power mesh layer and metal-insulator-metal capacitors. This is not an incremental improvement. It is a recognition that power delivery, not die placement, is the binding constraint for next-generation AI hardware.

The strategic implications for the packaging substrate market are profound. TSMC has long held a competitive edge in power supply and heat management within package substrates, leveraging its 3DFabric Alliance to co-develop solutions with ecosystem partners. Intel's EMIB-T is a direct response, and the report expects Intel to build a similar ecosystem and catch up rapidly. The core technologies that will determine the strength of these ecosystems are power supply, heat management, co-packaged optics, and 3D stacking via hybrid bonding. For Japan's substrate suppliers, the key takeaway is that both TSMC and Intel are pursuing architectures that demand larger, more expensive, and more technically demanding substrates. TSMC's roadmap appears to be shifting toward 3.5D packaging, which combines 3D stacking with 2.5D packaging, as a bridge solution until visibility improves on the shift to larger interposers. Intel's EMIB-T, by contrast, is a 2.5D packaging approach that does not use interposers at all, and the company expects to complete mass production preparations for a product with 8x the reticle size featuring four dies in 2026, with a package substrate measuring 120x120mm capable of integrating up to 12 HBM4 memory stacks.

## The Shift from Four-Die to Two-Die Architectures Signals a Fundamental Rethink of Packaging Economics

The most significant architectural development in the report is not a technical specification, but a design change that has sweeping implications for substrate demand. There is a growing industry view that the Rubin Ultra accelerator will be changed from a four-die structure, as announced at GTC in March, to a two-die design. Some observers are also beginning to take the view that the Feynman architecture will adopt a two-module structure with two dies in a 3D stacking configuration. This is not a step backward. It is a recognition that combining larger interposers, those exceeding 5.5x the reticle size, with HBM4 memory that has more wiring, has become technically more difficult than anticipated. The report suggests that companies developing cutting-edge packages using interposers, such as TSMC, may consider their future roadmap to be based on 3.5D packaging until the path to larger interposers becomes clearer.

For the substrate supply chain, this architectural shift has a counterintuitive implication. A move from four dies to two dies does not necessarily mean fewer substrates or simpler substrates. In fact, the opposite may be true. A two-die architecture with 3D stacking places even greater demands on the substrate's power delivery and thermal management capabilities because the power density is concentrated rather than distributed. The package substrate for Intel's EMIB-T implementation is 120x120mm, which is larger than many current-generation substrates. The ability to integrate up to 12 HBM4 memory stacks alongside the compute dies requires a substrate with extremely fine line widths, multiple layers, and precise impedance control. The substrate is becoming a more critical performance differentiator, not less.

This dynamic explains why Japan's packaging substrate shipments are hitting record prices even as the industry debates architecture changes. The substrate manufacturers are not passive suppliers of a commodity input. They are active participants in solving the power delivery and signal integrity challenges that define AI accelerator performance. The 23% year-on-year increase in average price per square meter reflects the value that substrate manufacturers are capturing as they deliver increasingly sophisticated products to meet the demands of both TSMC's and Intel's roadmaps.

## Google's Adoption of Intel's EMIB-T Validates the Multi-Sourcing Strategy That Will Drive Substrate Demand

The report identifies a concrete customer move that crystallizes the competitive dynamics: there have been moves toward uptake of Intel's EMIB-T at Google, which is developing accelerators with a quad-die structure. This is strategically significant because Google has historically been a major customer of TSMC's advanced packaging. A shift toward Intel's EMIB-T for at least some of Google's accelerator designs would represent a validation of Intel's packaging technology and a diversification of Google's supply chain. For the substrate market, the implication is that demand will not be concentrated on a single packaging architecture or a single foundry. Both TSMC's CoWoS-L and 3.5D packaging, and Intel's EMIB-T, will require high-end substrates. The substrate manufacturers are effectively hedged across the competing packaging ecosystems.

The report's analysis of Intel's technical paper provides granular detail on why EMIB-T is now competitive. The key innovation is the addition of TSV to the silicon bridge, which was not present in previous EMIB implementations. This allows power to be delivered vertically through the bridge rather than only laterally, significantly reducing the impedance of the power delivery network. The addition of a power mesh layer and MIM capacitors further stabilizes voltage. The result is that Intel has closed the gap with TSMC in power delivery, which was previously considered TSMC's proprietary advantage. The substrate requirements for EMIB-T are demanding, but they are also well-defined. The 120x120mm substrate size and the integration of up to 12 HBM4 stacks create a substantial addressable market for Japan's substrate manufacturers, who have the technical capability and production capacity to serve this demand.

For readers who need to translate this analysis into actionable strategic decisions, the following framework organizes the key variables that will determine which substrate manufacturers and which parts of the supply chain capture the most value over the next 18-24 months.

Substrate manufacturers that can deliver these complex products at scale will command premium pricing and higher margins.

The second variable is ecosystem alignment. TSMC's 3DFabric Alliance and Intel's nascent EMIB-T ecosystem are both attempting to create standardized interfaces and design tools that reduce the time and cost of developing advanced packages. Substrate manufacturers that participate actively in both ecosystems will have access to a larger addressable market and will be better positioned to capture demand from multiple end customers. The report notes that core technologies determining ecosystem strength include power supply, heat management, CPO, and hybrid bonding. Substrate manufacturers that invest in these adjacent capabilities, either organically or through partnerships, will become more integral to their customers' product roadmaps.

The report indicates that Rubin package shipments will pick up in earnest around August, and Intel expects to complete mass production preparations for its 8x reticle size EMIB-T product in 2026. Substrate manufacturers must align their capacity expansion with these timelines. Over-investment ahead of demand would destroy returns, but under-investment would cede market share to competitors who can deliver when customers need them. Substrate manufacturers that can support both interposer-based and interposer-less architectures will be more resilient to shifts in the packaging roadmap.

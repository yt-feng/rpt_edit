You have China moving up the value chain, competing directly in cars, for example. You have energy price shocks in Europe and Chinese subsidies. Then they use more sophisticated models, like relative PPP based on producer prices, and external balance models that look at trade surpluses. The 15% is the average of their preferred models.
EN_A: So different models give different answers. What’s interesting is that they didn’t just look at the euro. They also looked at a hypothetical Deutschmark.
EN_B: That’s a crucial part of the analysis. When you use German price data, the yuan looks even more undervalued. For example, the detrended Big Mac Index shows a 22% undervaluation against the Deutschmark, compared to 20% against the euro. This really illustrates how Germany’s competitiveness challenge is more acute than the rest of the Eurozone.

EN_B: One big caveat the report itself flags is around subsidies. If Chinese manufacturers pay market prices for inputs but then get subsidies later, the producer price index might not fully capture the cost advantage. That means the real undervaluation could be larger than what the PPI-based model suggests. Also, the external balance models depend heavily on what you consider a ‘normal’ trade surplus, which is a debatable assumption.

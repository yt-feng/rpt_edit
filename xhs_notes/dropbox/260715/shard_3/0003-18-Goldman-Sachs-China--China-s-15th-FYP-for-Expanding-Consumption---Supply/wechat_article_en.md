# China's New Consumption Plan Offers Supply-Side Architecture but Leaves Demand-Side Constraints Unresolved

On July 13, China's State Council approved the 15th Five-Year Plan for Expanding Consumption, consolidating existing measures into a comprehensive medium-term framework. The timing is revealing: retail sales grew only 1.4% year-on-year during January-May, a sharp deceleration from the 5.0% pace over the same period in the prior year. The plan targets total retail sales of consumer goods reaching approximately RMB 60 trillion by 2030, up from RMB 50.1 trillion in 2025. That implies a relatively modest 3.7% annualized nominal growth rate during the 15th FYP period, below the 5.0% pace achieved during the 14th FYP. The ambition is not aggressive by historical standards.

The plan's structure reveals a deliberate choice. It emphasizes services upgrading, goods replacement demand, new consumption formats, household income and social security support, and the removal of purchase restrictions for autos. But the policy architecture tilts heavily toward supply-side enhancements—improving what is available for purchase, upgrading quality standards, and reducing institutional frictions—rather than directly boosting household purchasing power. This asymmetry matters because the constraints on Chinese consumption today are predominantly demand-side: weak labor market conditions, subdued income expectations, and still-negative property-related wealth effects.

The central tension in the plan is therefore unavoidable. It is a medium-term framework designed to reshape the consumption landscape over five years, but it arrives at a moment when near-term spending momentum is faltering for reasons that supply-side measures alone cannot address. Understanding where this plan can move the needle and where it cannot is essential for assessing its real economic impact.

## The Plan Targets Services Consumption as the Primary Growth Engine, but Supply-Side Upgrades Cannot Create Demand That Does Not Exist

The plan devotes substantial attention to expanding higher-quality supply across daily services and demographic categories: catering, lodging, domestic services, elderly care, childcare, culture and tourism, healthcare, sports, and education and training. It emphasizes standards, workforce training, brand development, digitalization, and local infrastructure. This is a coherent supply-side agenda. It seeks to unlock services consumption by making more and better options available.

The logic is not without merit. China's household consumption rate and the services share of household consumption remain well below major developed-market economies. Structural factors—limited urbanization of services, regulatory barriers to entry, underdeveloped private provision in elderly care and childcare—have suppressed consumption that households would otherwise undertake. Removing those frictions should release latent demand over time.

But the implicit assumption is that latent demand exists. In the current environment, that assumption is fragile. When households are tightening spending due to income uncertainty and negative wealth effects from housing, improving the quality of restaurant options or expanding elderly care facilities does little to increase the propensity to spend. The plan's services agenda is necessary for the long-term rebalancing of China's consumption structure, but it is unlikely to generate a near-term boost. It improves the supply curve without shifting the demand curve.

## Goods Consumption Policy Focuses on Replacement and Upgrading Rather Than Subsidies, Reflecting Fiscal Constraints and a Shift in Strategy

The plan supports big-ticket durable goods including housing upgrades, auto consumption and aftermarket sectors, and smart home appliances. It highlights product innovation, flexible manufacturing, daily consumer goods upgrades, and stronger domestic brand building. Notably absent are the large-scale, one-off consumer subsidies that characterized earlier stimulus efforts.

This is a deliberate choice. Policymakers appear to have concluded that blanket subsidies are fiscally expensive and generate pull-forward effects that leave a demand void in subsequent periods. The plan instead emphasizes replacement demand and higher-quality supply as the mechanism for sustaining goods consumption. For autos, this means supporting the aftermarket and encouraging trade-ins rather than subsidizing new purchases. For housing, it means urban village redevelopment and upgrade housing supply rather than direct purchase subsidies.

The approach has a strategic logic. It aligns consumption policy with industrial upgrading themes—domestic brands, smart manufacturing, green supply chains—rather than treating consumption support as a standalone transfer program. But it also places the burden of adjustment on household willingness to spend, which is precisely where the current weakness lies. Replacement demand requires confidence in future income to justify the upfront cost. That confidence is currently lacking.

## New Consumption Formats Represent the Most Innovative Element of the Plan, Linking Consumption to Industrial Policy and Structural Transformation

The plan prioritizes digital, green, "first-launch" economy, experience-based consumption, and inbound tourism. It explicitly supports AI-enabled products and services, low-carbon goods and recycling systems, "first-store" and "first-show" platforms, immersive retail and cultural experiences, and tourism-related inbound spending. This is the section of the plan that most clearly connects consumption policy with China's broader industrial strategy.

The logic is that incremental demand can be generated through new consumption scenarios and better supply, rather than simply relying on existing categories. The "first-launch" economy, for example, creates a mechanism for brands to generate excitement and capture premium pricing through limited releases and exclusive events. Experience-based consumption shifts spending from goods to services and activities, which have higher labor intensity and lower import content. AI-enabled products create upgrade cycles that can sustain consumer electronics demand even when aggregate income growth is weak.

These are genuine innovations in consumption policy. They reflect an understanding that consumption growth in a mature economy increasingly comes from new categories and experiences rather than from simply buying more of the same things. But they also share the same limitation as the broader plan: they work best when household confidence is stable and spending is already occurring. Creating new formats is unlikely to offset a fundamental retrenchment in household consumption.

## The Plan Acknowledges Household Consumption Capacity as a Necessary Condition but Provides No Specifics on Funding, Timing, or Implementation

Beyond supply expansion, the plan highlights employment support, income growth, stronger social security, and higher public consumption as key conditions for more durable household spending. Targeted areas include job creation and skills training, wage and redistribution reforms, higher pension and medical insurance support, broader coverage for flexible and migrant workers, long-term care insurance, and increased public spending on education, healthcare, elderly care, and childcare.

This section represents the plan's most direct engagement with demand-side constraints. It is consistent with the State Council's Special Action Plan to Boost Consumption announced in March 2025 and the 15th FYP full report released in March 2026. But it suffers from a familiar problem in Chinese policy documents: the absence of specifics on funding sources, magnitudes, timelines, and implementation details.

The gap is critical. Increasing pension and medical insurance support or expanding coverage for migrant workers requires substantial fiscal resources. Improving public spending on education and healthcare requires both funding and institutional capacity. The plan acknowledges these as necessary conditions for durable consumption growth, but it does not resolve how they will be achieved. This creates a risk that the demand-side commitments remain aspirational while the supply-side measures proceed on schedule, leaving the fundamental constraint on consumption unaddressed.

## A Decision Framework for Assessing the Plan's Real Impact

For business leaders and investors evaluating the plan's implications, three questions determine its practical significance

First, what is the time horizon? The plan's supply-side measures—services upgrading, infrastructure investment, removal of purchase restrictions—will take years to implement and generate results. The demand-side measures lack specifics and timelines. For near-term consumption forecasts, the plan provides little reason to revise expectations upward. The 3.7% annualized nominal growth target embedded in the plan is itself a modest number, suggesting policymakers are realistic about the constraints.

Second, which sectors benefit from structural changes regardless of the cycle? The new consumption formats agenda—AI-enabled products, first-launch economy, green consumption, inbound tourism—creates regulatory tailwinds and policy support that will persist even if aggregate consumption growth remains subdued. Companies positioned in these categories face a more favorable policy environment than those in traditional goods categories.

Third, what would change the demand-side picture? The plan's own logic implies that a more durable recovery requires firmer job and wage growth, stabilization in household balance sheets, and more direct demand-side support to households. The plan does not provide these. Until they materialize through other policy channels or exogenous improvement in economic conditions, the consumption outlook will remain constrained by the same factors that have driven the current weakness.

The 15th FYP for Expanding Consumption is a serious attempt to build the institutional and structural foundations for China's consumption rebalancing. It addresses real frictions in services supply, goods upgrading, and market access. But it arrives at a moment when the binding constraint on consumption is not supply quality or regulatory friction, but household willingness and ability to spend. That constraint requires demand-side solutions that the plan does not deliver.

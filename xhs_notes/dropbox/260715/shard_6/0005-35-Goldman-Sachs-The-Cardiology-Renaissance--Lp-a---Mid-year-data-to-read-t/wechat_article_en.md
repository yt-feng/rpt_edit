# Lp(a) Data in Mid-2026 Will Either Validate or Challenge an Entire New Class of Cardiovascular Drugs

The cardiology pipeline is approaching a defining moment. Within the next quarter, the first Phase 3 outcomes data from a dedicated lipoprotein(a)-lowering therapy will read out, testing a hypothesis that has been supported by genetics but never proven by a large, placebo-controlled trial. Novartis and Ionis' HORIZON study of pelacarsen, an antisense oligonucleotide targeting Lp(a), will report cardiovascular outcomes in over 8,300 patients. A positive result would validate Lp(a) as a causal risk factor amenable to drug intervention, opening a market that affects roughly 1.4 billion people globally. A negative or ambiguous result would not only set back the lead asset but cast doubt on the entire mechanism for competitors including Amgen and Eli Lilly, whose own Phase 3 programs are years behind.

The stakes are unusually high because Lp(a) is not like LDL cholesterol. It is a largely genetically determined lipoprotein particle that carries oxidized phospholipids and exerts proatherogenic, prothrombotic, and proinflammatory effects. Approximately 20% of the global population has Lp(a) levels at or above 70 mg/dL, a threshold associated with significantly elevated cardiovascular risk. Yet no approved therapy exists to lower it. Statins have no meaningful effect. PCSK9 inhibitors reduce Lp(a) by only 25-30%, and this is a secondary rather than primary mechanism. The field has been waiting for a targeted agent that can reduce Lp(a) by 70-100%, and that is what pelacarsen and its competitors can deliver.

Observers' expectations are measured. The trial has faced delays. Event rates have been slower than anticipated, partly because the study design mandates aggressive treatment of other cardiovascular risk factors to isolate the Lp(a) effect. This is scientifically rigorous but operationally demanding. The overall population requires a hazard ratio of approximately 0.88 to reach significance, and the high-risk subgroup with Lp(a) above 90 mg/dL requires a hazard ratio of approximately 0.86. Statistical analysis suggests these thresholds are achievable, but the margin for error is slim. A trend that falls short of significance would still provide read-through for the field, but it would delay commercial conviction and complicate regulatory and payer discussions.

The central question is not whether Lp(a) can be lowered. It can. Pelacarsen reduces Lp(a) by approximately 80% at the Phase 3 dose, and nearly all patients fall below the 50 mg/dL high-risk threshold. Amgen's olpasiran, a siRNA, achieves even greater reductions approaching 100%. The question is whether lowering Lp(a) translates into a reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events, and whether the magnitude of that reduction is clinically meaningful and commercially defensible.

## The amount of Lp(a) lowering required for cardiovascular benefit remains uncertain, and this uncertainty favors assets with deeper reductions

Multiple studies have attempted to estimate the Lp(a) reduction needed to achieve a 20% relative risk reduction in cardiovascular events, benchmarked against the effect of lowering LDL-C by approximately 39 mg/dL. The estimates vary dramatically by study design and prevention setting. Mendelian randomization studies in primary prevention populations suggest a required reduction of 66 to 102 mg/dL. Clinical trial data from secondary prevention populations, which is the relevant setting for the current Phase 3 trials, suggest a lower range of 32 to 50 mg/dL.

This range matters because it determines whether all Lp(a)-lowering agents are equivalent or whether deeper reductions confer additional benefit. Pelacarsen reduces Lp(a) by approximately 80%, which from a median baseline of 100 mg/dL brings patients to roughly 20 mg/dL. This is within the normal range. Olpasiran reduces Lp(a) by approximately 105%, which from a similar baseline brings patients even lower. If the benefit of Lp(a) lowering flattens once patients reach normal levels, then the incremental reduction from pelacarsen to olpasiran may not translate into incremental clinical benefit. If, however, the relationship between Lp(a) and risk is continuous and dose-dependent, as it is with LDL-C, then olpasiran's deeper reduction could prove superior.

The literature does not resolve this debate. There is epidemiological evidence that very low Lp(a) levels are associated with an increased risk of diabetes, though causality is unclear. There is also evidence that patients with the highest baseline Lp(a) derive the greatest absolute benefit from reduction, which would favor agents that can achieve near-complete suppression. The HORIZON data will provide the first direct evidence, but because it tests only one agent at one dose, it will not fully answer the dose-response question. That answer will come from Amgen's olpasiran program, but not until 2027 or 2028.

A related debate concerns the lack of impact on high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. Neither pelacarsen nor olpasiran has shown a meaningful reduction in hs-CRP in Phase 2 studies. This has raised concerns by analogy to the cholesteryl ester transfer protein inhibitor class, where multiple drugs failed in Phase 3 despite favorable effects on HDL cholesterol. The CETP failures were partly attributed to the absence of hs-CRP reduction, which was interpreted as a sign that the drugs were not addressing inflammation.

This analogy is misleading. The CETP inhibitors failed not because they lacked an hs-CRP effect but because they did not lower LDL-C. LDL-C reduction is a well-validated pathway for cardiovascular benefit, and the CETP class did not engage it. The PCSK9 antibodies, by contrast, do not lower hs-CRP but do lower LDL-C, and they produce clear cardiovascular benefit. A post-hoc analysis of the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trial showed that patients with high baseline Lp(a) derived greater and earlier benefit from alirocumab than those with low Lp(a), even though both groups achieved similar LDL-C reduction. The high-Lp(a) group saw a 20% relative risk reduction that became statistically significant after 1.25 years, compared to 2.45 years in the low-Lp(a) group. This suggests that Lp(a) lowering and LDL-C lowering are synergistic, and that the proatherogenic and prothrombotic pathways of Lp(a) may be more important than its proinflammatory pathway.

## Trial design choices in HORIZON are scientifically justified but create commercial and statistical risk

The HORIZON trial design reflects a deliberate strategy to isolate the effect of Lp(a) lowering from other cardiovascular risk factors. Patients are required to have optimal treatment of LDL-C, blood pressure, and diabetes at baseline. Median LDL-C at enrollment is 65.7 mg/dL, well within the normal range. Only approximately 11% of patients are on PCSK9 inhibitors at baseline. This design is logical from a regulatory and payer perspective. It answers the question: in a patient whose other risk factors are well managed, does additional Lp(a) lowering reduce events?

The cost of this rigor is slower event accrual. The trial has already experienced delays, and the overall event rate is lower than initially projected. This increases the statistical burden. The co-primary endpoints test for a reduction in MACE in the overall population with Lp(a) at or above 70 mg/dL and in a high-risk subgroup with Lp(a) at or above 90 mg/dL. Approximately 80% of enrolled patients fall into the high-risk subgroup, which has a median baseline Lp(a) of approximately 100 mg/dL. The study is powered for different hazard ratios in each population, and success requires meeting either endpoint.

The risk is that the overall population event rate is too low to detect a signal, while the high-risk subgroup, though enriched, may still require a larger treatment effect than the drug can deliver. This is not a flaw in the drug. It is a consequence of a design that optimizes for regulatory clarity at the expense of statistical power. A positive result in the high-risk subgroup alone would be sufficient for approval, but it would narrow the label and complicate payer discussions. A trend in both populations that falls short of significance would be interpreted as a failure, even if the underlying biology is sound.

The read-through from HORIZON to the rest of the field is asymmetric. A positive result de-risks the entire mechanism and accelerates development for Amgen, Lilly, and others. A negative result does not disprove the Lp(a) hypothesis, because it could reflect trial design, patient selection, or the specific properties of pelacarsen. But it would reset expectations and delay investment. Amgen's olpasiran trial uses a different design with less aggressive background therapy, which could produce higher event rates and a larger signal. But it will not read out for at least two years after HORIZON.

## A decision framework for interpreting the HORIZON data and positioning for the Lp(a) landscape

Observers and strategic planners should evaluate the HORIZON results against three thresholds rather than a binary pass-fail metric. The first threshold is statistical significance in either co-primary endpoint. Even a modest hazard ratio of 0.88 in the overall population or 0.86 in the high-risk subgroup would validate the mechanism. The second threshold is the magnitude of the treatment effect. A hazard ratio of 0.80 or better would support the dose-response argument and strengthen the case for deeper Lp(a) reduction. The third threshold is the consistency of the effect across subgroups, particularly by baseline Lp(a) level, LDL-C level, and hs-CRP status.

If HORIZON meets the first threshold but not the second, the field advances but the commercial debate shifts to testing rates, payer coverage, and the differentiation of later entrants. Lp(a) testing is not routine. It is not part of standard lipid panels. Widespread adoption will require guideline changes, physician education, and reimbursement support. A modest benefit from pelacarsen may not be enough to drive testing, especially if the drug requires monthly subcutaneous injections and has no effect on hs-CRP.

If HORIZON meets the second threshold, the competitive dynamics shift. Amgen's olpasiran, with deeper Lp(a) reduction and less frequent dosing, becomes more attractive. Lilly's lepodisiran, also an siRNA, and muvalaplin, an oral small molecule, become more viable. The oral option is particularly important for the long-term market, because it removes the injection barrier and could enable earlier intervention in primary prevention.

If HORIZON fails to meet even the first threshold, the field is set back but not eliminated. The Lp(a) hypothesis is supported by Mendelian randomization, epidemiological data, and post-hoc analyses of PCSK9 trials. A single negative Phase 3 does not overturn that evidence. But it would create a funding gap for earlier-stage programs and force a reassessment of trial design for olpasiran. The most likely outcome of a negative HORIZON is not abandonment of the mechanism but a delay of two to three years and a shift toward higher-risk, less aggressively treated patient populations.

The HORIZON data will not answer every question. It will not resolve the dose-response debate. It will not determine whether oral agents can match injectables. It will not settle the hs-CRP controversy. But it will answer the one question that matters most: whether lowering Lp(a) by a large amount in a well-treated secondary prevention population reduces cardiovascular events. That answer will determine whether the Lp(a) class becomes a multibillion-dollar market or a cautionary tale about the gap between genetic epidemiology and clinical trial reality.

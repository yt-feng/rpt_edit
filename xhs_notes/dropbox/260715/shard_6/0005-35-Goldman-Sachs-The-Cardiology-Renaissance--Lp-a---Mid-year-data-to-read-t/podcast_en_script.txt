EN_A: So we are talking about one of the most anticipated events in the healthcare sector right now. Two major phase three cardiovascular trials are expected to read out later this year. Can you break down what is at stake?
EN_B: Absolutely. The first one is Novartis and Ionis’s pelacarsen, targeting a lipoprotein called Lp(a). The second is Novo Nordisk’s ziltivekimab, which is an anti-IL-6 drug. But today, let us focus on the Lp(a) story. It is a genetic risk factor that affects about one point four billion people globally. And here is the thing: there is no approved drug to lower it yet.
EN_A: So the big question is whether lowering Lp(a) actually reduces cardiovascular events. Is that the core hypothesis this report is testing?
EN_B: Exactly. We have had epidemiological data for years, but never a large-scale, placebo-controlled phase three trial. The HORIZON study is the first to test this directly. If it is positive, it would validate the entire drug class and open the door for competitors like Amgen and Eli Lilly.
EN_A: What is the market expecting? Are people optimistic?
EN_B: Surprisingly, expectations are quite low. There are a few reasons. First, Lp(a) is an unproven target. Second, the trial design is very strict. They optimized all other risk factors for patients, which could lower the overall event rate and make it harder to show a statistically significant benefit. And third, Novartis itself has been fairly cautious in their commentary. But the report does its own statistical analysis and suggests that a twelve to fourteen percent risk reduction could be enough to hit significance.
EN_A: So if it works, what does that mean for the rest of the field?
EN_B: It would be a massive read-through. Amgen’s olpasiran is the next in line, with phase three data expected around 2027 or 2028. Lilly has both an injectable siRNA and an oral small molecule. But the report also flags a major uncertainty: commercial adoption. Even if the data is positive, will doctors test for Lp(a) routinely? Will insurers pay for these drugs? Those questions are not fully answered yet.
EN_A: That sounds like a real barrier. But what about the science itself? Is it as simple as “lower Lp(a) equals lower risk”?
EN_B: Not exactly. There is a debate about how much you need to lower it. For LDL cholesterol, the mantra is “lower is better.” But for Lp(a), it is unclear if the benefit plateaus below a certain threshold. For example, bringing someone from seventy milligrams per deciliter down to thirty might be very beneficial. But going from thirty to ten? That might not add much. And interestingly, these drugs do not seem to lower a key inflammatory marker called hs-CRP.
EN_A: That is a bit concerning, because inflammation is a big driver of heart disease. Does that mean these drugs might not work?
EN_B: The report argues not necessarily. PCSK9 inhibitors also do not lower hs-CRP, but they still reduce cardiovascular events by lowering LDL. So Lp(a) might work mainly through other pathways, like promoting plaque formation and blood clotting. But the report does not fully resolve this question. It dedicates a lot of space to comparing Lp(a) drugs with a failed class called CETP inhibitors, and that analysis is worth reading in the full report.

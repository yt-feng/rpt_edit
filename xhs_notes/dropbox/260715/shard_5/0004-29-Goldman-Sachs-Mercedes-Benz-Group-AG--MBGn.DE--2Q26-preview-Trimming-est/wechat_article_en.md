# Mercedes-Benz Faces a Two-Speed Reality: Strong Product Momentum in the West Cannot Mask a Changing Profit Equation in China

Mercedes-Benz is entering its second-quarter 2026 earnings season with a paradox that observers must confront directly. On July 14, the company pre-announced that its Cars division would deliver an adjusted EBIT margin within the full-year guidance range of 3 to 5 percent, with a consensus expectation settling around 3.5 percent. This headline figure, while unremarkable, conceals a fundamental divergence: new product launches in Europe and the United States are generating genuine momentum, yet China—the profit engine that once defined Mercedes’ premium economics—is now exerting a structural influence that is significantly more severe than previously modeled.

The tension is not merely cyclical. It reflects a strategic rebalancing in which Mercedes is successfully rejuvenating its model cycle in mature markets while simultaneously confronting a Chinese demand environment that has shifted far beyond what most forecasters anticipated. Group unit sales in the second quarter reached 418,000 vehicles, down 8 percent year over year, with declines concentrated entirely in China. Europe delivered positive momentum, supported by the new CLA, GLB, and GLC models. The United States was buoyed by the GLE and GLE Coupe. But these gains were insufficient to offset a 30 percent volume decline in China versus the previous estimate of a 14 percent drop.

This is not a temporary blip. The underlying profitability of Mercedes’ Chinese joint venture, BBAC, has been adjusted from a 6 percent margin assumption to 4 percent for the full fiscal year 2026. The implication is clear: the structural premium that China once commanded is evolving, and the earnings trajectory for the entire company must be recalibrated accordingly.

## New Product Cycle in Europe and the US Is Delivering Tangible Volume Gains, but the Magnitude Is Insufficient to Offset the China Headwind

The second-quarter data offers a clear reading on the strength of Mercedes’ product renewal. Europe posted a 4 percent volume increase relative to previous estimates, while the US exceeded expectations by 10 percent. These are not marginal improvements. They reflect genuine consumer response to the electric CLA, the GLB, and the GLC, all of which are seeing orders that extend into next year. The electric C-Class opened for orders in May and is contributing to a broader electrification push: global battery electric vehicle sales rose 51 percent year over year, and in Europe, BEVs now represent 26 percent of Mercedes’ mix.

Yet the arithmetic is unforgiving. A 10 percent beat in the US and a 4 percent beat in Europe, applied to their respective base volumes, cannot compensate for a 30 percent miss in China. The Chinese market accounted for roughly 36 percent of Mercedes’ global sales in recent years, and the margin structure there has historically been superior to that of any other region. When volume declines are accompanied by pricing pressure and the need for dealer support payments, the profit impact is disproportionate to the unit shortfall.

The top-end vehicle segment, which includes the S-Class, GLS, and Maybach models, came in at 58,000 units in the second quarter, representing a top-end vehicle share of approximately 14 percent. This was affected by planned product changeovers and timing of availability. While this is a transient factor, it introduces additional uncertainty into the near-term earnings mix, as top-end vehicles carry significantly higher margins than the volume models that are currently driving the growth story in Europe.

## China Is No Longer a Growth Story for Mercedes: It Is a Profitability Consideration That Requires Active Capital Reallocation

The most consequential shift in Mercedes’ earnings profile is the structural change in its Chinese operations. Management flagged intensifying pressure on both volume and pricing. The first-quarter MSRP adjustment helped dealer profitability temporarily, but second-quarter profit changed significantly. Management has stated that further dealer payments in the second half cannot be ruled out if the trend persists.

This is not a demand shock that will self-correct when the cycle turns. It reflects a deeper structural change in the Chinese luxury automotive market. Domestic competitors, particularly in the battery electric vehicle segment, are offering vehicles with comparable or superior technology at price points that undercut Mercedes’ traditional premium. The electric GLC is launching in China with an entry model priced at RMB 299,000, which management describes as attractive and which has generated strong pre-order numbers with high conversion rates. But this pricing represents a significant discount to previous Mercedes entry points in China, and it compresses the margin structure of the entire local operation.

The BBAC joint venture margin has been adjusted from 6 percent to 4 percent for fiscal 2026. This is a 33 percent reduction in the profitability of what was once the most profitable part of Mercedes’ global manufacturing footprint. If the Chinese market continues to shift toward lower-priced electric models, and if domestic competition intensifies further, the margin could compress further. Observers should consider a scenario in which Chinese joint venture profitability stabilizes at 3 to 4 percent rather than recovering to the 6 to 8 percent range that prevailed in prior years.

## A Decision Framework for Observers: How to Evaluate Mercedes in a Two-Region World

The analytical challenge for observers is no longer whether Mercedes is a good company. It is. The product cycle is strong, the balance sheet is sound, and management is executing a credible electrification strategy. The question is whether the stock price adequately reflects the China consideration and the associated earnings revision.

The following framework can guide the assessment. First, isolate the China exposure. Mercedes’ Chinese joint venture contributed approximately 1.5 to 2.0 billion euros in equity income in recent peak years. If that contribution falls to 1.0 billion euros or less, the implied earnings power of the group declines by roughly 0.50 to 0.75 euros per share. Second, evaluate the pace of recovery in top-end vehicle sales. The second-quarter top-end vehicle share of 14 percent is below the historical average. If product changeovers resolve quickly and the S-Class and GLS regain momentum, the margin mix improves. If not, the earnings recovery is delayed. Third, monitor the buyback trajectory. Management has concluded the previous 2 billion euro buyback program ahead of schedule and has 3 billion euros of remaining authorization under the current 5 billion euro program. The company has stated that the next buyback tranche is not contingent on monetizing the Daimler Truck stake, and the balance sheet can support further repurchases. For fiscal 2026, the forecast calls for 3.1 billion euros in share buybacks, above the consensus estimate of 2.3 billion euros. If this materializes, it provides a floor for earnings per share even if operating earnings decline.

Fourth, assess the non-cash risk. The carrying value review of participations is a variable factor. If an impairment is taken, reported net income will decline, but adjusted earnings per share will remain unaffected. The market reaction will depend on whether the impairment is seen as a clean-up or as a signal that management expects further change.

Finally, compare the valuation to peers. Mercedes trades at approximately 8.1 times forward earnings for fiscal 2026, with a dividend yield of 7.8 percent. The free cash flow yield is projected at 12.4 percent. These multiples are low relative to historical averages and relative to the broader European auto sector. The discount reflects the China uncertainty. If the China situation stabilizes, the stock offers significant upside. If it changes further, the discount widens further.

The buyback program is proceeding at an accelerated pace, and management has signaled a willingness to return capital to shareholders.

But the factors outside management’s control are moving in a different direction. The Chinese luxury market is undergoing a structural shift that is affecting both volume and margins. Domestic competitors are gaining share, and the pricing environment is changing. The non-cash impairment risk adds a layer of uncertainty to reported earnings. And the earnings revision cycle has further to run if Chinese conditions continue to evolve.

For observers, the decision hinges on whether the current valuation adequately discounts these considerations. At 8.1 times forward earnings, with a 7.8 percent dividend yield and a 12.4 percent free cash flow yield, the stock is priced for a cautious outcome. If Chinese conditions stabilize or improve, the upside is substantial. If they change further, the downside is limited by the balance sheet strength and the buyback support. The asymmetry of risk and reward is favorable, but it requires patience and a willingness to look through the near-term noise.

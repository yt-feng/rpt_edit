## HORIZON’s Primary Endpoint Is Achievable at a 12% Risk Reduction, but Commercial Uptake Remains the Deeper Debate

The HORIZON trial is the first large-scale Phase 3 study designed to test whether lowering Lp(a) directly reduces cardiovascular risk. With 8,323 patients randomized to monthly subcutaneous pelacarsen or placebo, the trial is powered to detect a hazard ratio of approximately 0.80 in the overall population (Lp(a) >=70 mg/dL) and 0.75 in the high-risk subgroup (Lp(a) >=90 mg/dL). Statistical analysis of the trial design suggests that significance in the overall population could be achieved with a 12% relative risk reduction, and in the high-risk subgroup with a 14% reduction. Novartis management has indicated that a 13-15% benefit, while modest by LDL-lowering standards, would be clinically meaningful for Lp(a).

The critical nuance is that the trial tests two primary populations sequentially. If the overall population misses but the high-risk subgroup hits, the result would be considered mixed—not a clean failure, but not a clear win. This scenario would leave the field in a gray zone where regulatory approval might still be possible for a narrower indication, but commercial uptake would face significant headwinds from payors questioning the value proposition.

Even if HORIZON succeeds, the commercial debate will center on three unresolved issues. First, Lp(a) testing rates remain low globally, with no universal screening guidelines in place. Second, emerging longer-acting and oral options—including Amgen’s olpasiran (siRNA, Phase 3 data expected 2027/2028) and Lilly’s lepodisiran (siRNA) and muvalaplin (oral)—could erode pelacarsen’s first-mover advantage if they demonstrate comparable or superior efficacy with less frequent dosing. Third, the lack of benefit on high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, an inflammatory marker, in prior pelacarsen trials raises questions about whether Lp(a) lowering alone is sufficient in patients with residual inflammatory risk.

For observers, the implication is that HORIZON’s outcome will be judged not just on statistical significance but on the magnitude and consistency of the benefit across subgroups. A narrow win in the high-risk subgroup only would de-risk the field for later entrants like Amgen and Arrowhead while limiting Novartis’ commercial upside. A clean win across both populations would validate Lp(a) as a target and likely trigger a wave of pipeline investment, but the revenue potential for pelacarsen specifically would still be constrained by the competitive dynamics and testing infrastructure that will take years to build.

## ZEUS Will Validate Whether Inflammation Reduction Translates to Cardiovascular Risk Reduction, with a 12% MACE Benefit Likely Sufficient for Statistical Significance

The ZEUS trial of ziltivekimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL-6, is testing a fundamentally different hypothesis from HORIZON. While Lp(a) lowering aims to reduce a specific lipid-mediated risk, ziltivekimab targets the inflammatory cascade upstream of cardiovascular events. The trial randomizes 6,200 patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) to ziltivekimab or placebo over 48 months, with the primary endpoint being time to first occurrence of 3-point Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE).

Because no prior cardiovascular outcomes trial has been conducted with an IL-6 inhibitor, there is no historical benchmark for what constitutes a meaningful efficacy signal. Industry convention suggests that a 15-20% relative risk reduction is the standard for a cardiovascular medication to be considered useful. However, statistical modeling of the ZEUS trial design indicates that a 12% MACE benefit would be sufficient to achieve statistical significance. This means that the efficacy bar for success is lower than the industry standard for clinical meaningfulness—a tension that will create interpretive challenges if the trial lands in the 12-15% range.

A 12% result would be statistically significant but clinically underwhelming, particularly given the safety and tolerability profile of chronic IL-6 inhibition. Ziltivekimab’s mechanism carries known risks including increased infection rates, potential gastrointestinal perforations, and effects on lipid metabolism. Payors and prescribers will weigh these risks against a modest cardiovascular benefit, especially in a patient population already managed with statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors.

The strategic importance of ZEUS extends beyond Novo Nordisk’s own pipeline. A positive result, even in the 15-20% range, would provide strong validation for the broader hypothesis that inflammation reduction—measured through lowering of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein—translates directly to cardiovascular risk reduction. This would read favorably to BioAge Labs’ oral NLRP3 inhibitor, which is expected to report proof-of-concept data in obese patients with cardiovascular inflammation by year-end 2026. A failure, by contrast, would cast doubt on the entire inflammation-CV risk reduction thesis and likely delay or terminate multiple early-stage programs.

## A Decision Framework for the Third Quarter: Prioritize Companies with Multiple Catalysts and Low Embedded Expectations

The CARDIO-TTRansform outcome taught observers a painful lesson: even well-designed Phase 3 trials with strong mechanistic rationale can fail when the control arm performs better than expected. In that case, tafamidis-treated patients in the combination group showed better-than-expected outcomes, diluting the treatment effect of Wainua. The same dynamic could reappear in HORIZON or ZEUS if background therapy improvements—including wider use of statins, PCSK9 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors—compress event rates and reduce statistical power.

Given this uncertainty, the most defensible positioning strategy is to favor companies with multiple near-term catalysts and low embedded expectations. Amgen fits this profile: its Lp(a) program is one of several pipeline assets, and the company does not rely on a single readout. MRK and ALNY, which the report identifies as best positioned for upside from large cardiovascular product cycles, also offer diversification. By contrast, pure-play names like Ionis and NAMS carry higher event risk but also higher potential upside for those willing to take a binary bet.

For observers evaluating the HORIZON readout specifically, the key decision point is not whether Lp(a) lowering reduces risk—that is increasingly accepted—but whether the magnitude of risk reduction in the overall population is large enough to justify broad commercial use. A narrow win in the high-risk subgroup only would be a pyrrhic victory: it validates the target but limits the market. A clean win across both populations would be transformative for the field, but even then, the commercial ramp will be slow until testing rates improve and longer-acting competitors validate their own profiles.

For the ZEUS readout, the interpretive challenge is whether a 12-15% MACE benefit is clinically meaningful. If Novo Nordisk frames the result as proof of concept for inflammation reduction as a cardiovascular strategy, the market reaction could be muted but the readthrough to BioAge and other anti-inflammatory programs could be significant. If the result is below 12%, the entire inflammation-CV thesis will face a credibility challenge that could take years to recover from.

The third quarter of 2026 will thus define the next phase of cardiovascular drug development. Two trials, two mechanisms, and one overarching question: can novel biology translate into clinically meaningful outcomes in a therapeutic area where the standard of care is already excellent? The answer will determine not just company valuations, but the direction of research investment for the rest of the decade.

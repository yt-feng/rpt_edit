Ahead of luxury results season kicking off this week, we frame the key investor debate facing the sector over the next 12-24 months: is the slowdown of the past two years a cyclical air-pocket that normalises as comps ease and China stabilises, or is the sector undergoing a genuine structural reset in its growth drivers? Cyclical or a structural reset? ## Why bulls see this as cyclical

\- Comps are genuinely easier. 2026 laps two years of destocking/demand normalisation in China and a soft US-election-adjacent comp base; bulls argue this alone should flatter growth optically.

## Why bears see this as structural

\- The industry lost real customers, not just spending intensity. Per Bain-Altagamma, the luxury customer base fell from >400m in 2022 to \~330m in 2025: an \~80m customer loss that management teams such as Prada have themselves acknowledged as roughly 1-in-5 consumers lost over a few years. \- Pricing power looks like it has been used up, not just paused. Hero-item price hikes ( $>+100\%$ on some Chanel styles, $>+75\%$ for most luxury handbags between 2019-2025) outpaced improvements in perceived value. Once trust in price-to-value is damaged, bears argue elasticity is structurally higher and hard to reverse.

This could put further pressure on demand, if consumers struggle to justify such expensive purchases.

The global resale market has grown to an estimated €50bn (+4-6% growth), providing a permanent lower-price alternative; in China specifically, the resale market (\~10% of primary market size, trending toward Japan's \~30%) and the rapid, socially-acceptable rise of manufacturer-sourced "dupes" (píng tì) represent genuinely new competitive dynamics that did not exist in prior slowdowns (e.g., 2013-2016).

\- China has shifted from a growth engine to a harder-to-underwrite pillar. Company-level organic growth series show much sharper amplitude through the cycle than investors previously assumed (the 2020-2022 boom/bust being only the most extreme example), raising the risk that any near-term stabilisation is itself just another turn of a more volatile cycle rather than a return to a stable structural growth rate.

As highlighted by Bain at our luxury conference recently, luxury spend is undergoing a structural shift towards “experiential indulgence”, with consumers increasingly reallocating wallet share from products to experiences linked to wellness, longevity and self-reward. However, the impact is highly segment-dependent: aspirational consumers are increasingly forced to switch spend away from goods towards experiences, exacerbating weakness in entry-level luxury, while HNWIs/UHNWIs retain the capacity to spend across both categories, with experiences acting as a complement rather than a substitute, we believe.

## China: still the swing factor

Both sides of the debate ultimately collapse into a China argument. Bulls point to early, tentative green shoots (equity-market-driven wealth effects, some returning mall queues, firming second-hand prices) as evidence that the worst is behind us and that China can again become a (partial) tailwind.

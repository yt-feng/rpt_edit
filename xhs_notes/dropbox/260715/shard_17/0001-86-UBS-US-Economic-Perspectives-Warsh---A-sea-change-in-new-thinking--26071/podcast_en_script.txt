EN_A: So we just read a fascinating research note on Fed Chair Warsh’s first big testimony. What’s the one thing you think people should take away from it?
EN_B: The big idea is that Warsh is trying to reshape the Fed from the inside out. Not by changing interest rates, but by changing how the Fed thinks about data, culture, and strategy. He called it a “sea change in new thinking.”
EN_A: That sounds almost like a corporate turnaround. What does that mean in practice?
EN_B: He’s setting up task forces. One on data, one on productivity and jobs. He basically said the Fed’s current inflation measures are outdated. He even said he wants to know the average price at a big box retailer, but none of the existing indicators capture that. So he’s going back to first principles.
EN_A: That’s pretty radical for a central bank. What about the balance sheet? The report mentioned he sounded more cautious than expected.
EN_B: Right. Before he became chair, people thought he’d move fast to shrink the balance sheet. But in the testimony, he said any changes will be “deliberate, public, and gradual.” He even reminded everyone that it took 18 years to build the balance sheet, so you can’t unwind it overnight.
EN_A: So that’s a dovish signal on liquidity?
EN_B: You could read it that way. But he also said the balance sheet is still an active tool. So here’s the thing the report doesn’t fully answer: if inflation picks up again, will he use rates or the balance sheet first? That’s a key question for the full report.
EN_A: And on inflation itself? The CPI came out that morning, and he had to comment.
EN_B: He called it “one data point” and said “mission accomplished” is not his view. Core PCE is still above 3%, so there’s plenty of work. But he also said the data was “positive relative to expectations.” So he’s not panicking, but he’s not declaring victory either.
EN_A: So no rate cuts anytime soon?
EN_B: Probably not. But he also brought up AI-driven productivity, like the 1990s internet boom, which could be disinflationary. He said “I think this could be that opportunity, but I can’t say it for certain.” That’s another open question the report explores more.
EN_A: And what about the Fed’s role? Did he say anything about staying in its lane?
EN_B: Absolutely. He was very clear: the Fed should stay out of fiscal policy. No buying assets with a fiscal flavor, no opining on things that aren’t its business. He’s positioning himself as a technocrat, not a politician.
EN_A: So what’s the big picture takeaway for investors?
EN_B: Warsh is trying to change the Fed’s operating system. He said most of the job is “left of the decimal point” — getting the culture, strategy, and organization right. If you only watch CPI and payrolls, you might miss the bigger shift. The full report has more detail on those task forces and what they mean.

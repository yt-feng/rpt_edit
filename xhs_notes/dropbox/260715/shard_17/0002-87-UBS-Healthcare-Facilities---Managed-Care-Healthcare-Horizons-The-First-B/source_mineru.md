# Healthcare Facilities & Managed Care Healthcare Horizons: The First Baby Boomer Hits 80 This Year, So What?

With this research note, we are introducing a new thematic report which we are calling "Healthcare Horizons" We plan to use these reports to look at broader evolving themes in healthcare services. This first edition examines the implications of the first baby boomer reaching 80 years of age in 2026.

## The Baby Boomer Demographic is Set to Boost the "Over 80" Age Cohort for the Next 15 Years

The year 2026 represents a pivotal demographic milestone, as the first members of the Baby Boomer generation (born 1946–1964) reach 80 years of age. Although population aging has been an enduring trend, numerous demographic analyses highlight that outcomes, healthcare utilization, and expenditures undergo significant shifts once individuals reach their 80s, setting this stage apart from the broader 65+ demographic. Brookings Institution research projects that the U.S. population aged 80 and older will approximately double between the mid 2020s and mid 2040s, rising from roughly 15 mln to nearly 30 mln individuals. U.S. Census Bureau data similarly shows older population cohorts are expected to grow at a faster rate than younger age cohorts. More specifically, the Census Bureau projects that adults aged 85 and older are expected to reach \~14 mln by 2040, representing a 72% increase over the roughly 6 mln adults aged 85 older as of 2022. From the standpoint of healthcare services, this transition is significant not solely due to population expansion, but also because medical care requirements, length of care, and dependence on support services escalate considerably beyond age 80, altering both the composition and magnitude of healthcare demand.

## Healthcare Spending Increases Significantly with Age

National Health Expenditure data shows that healthcare costs increase significantly with age and escalate further among the oldest age cohorts. For instance, in 2020, personal healthcare spending per capita for individuals aged 65 and older amounted to \$22,356, which is over five times the spending level for children and approximately 2.5 times that of working-age adults. Despite representing just 17% of the population, this age group accounted for \~37% of total healthcare expenditures in 2020. CMS data also reveals that per capita healthcare spending for adults aged 85 and older is even higher, averaging around \$36,000, exceeding the spending levels for younger seniors. This data underscores the notion that the demographic shift toward an aging population, particularly those aged 80 and above, creates a structural tailwind for healthcare spending, which is anticipated to further accelerate moving forward.

## However, The Type of Care Healthcare Utilized Changes with Increasing Age, Particularly at the 80+ Mark

As the population ages from 65 toward 80+, healthcare utilization shifts meaningfully from preventative and elective services toward more complex chronic disease management and higher-acuity care, driven by rising multi-morbidity and greater care coordination needs across settings. While younger seniors (65–74) remain in a “maintenance phase,” with spend concentrated in outpatient, physician, and drug utilization, the 75–84 cohort sees a notable step-up in inpatient admissions, specialist intensity, and post-acute utilization. This trend accelerates further in the 85+ population, where spending increasingly skews toward duration-based services such as skilled nursing, home health, hospice, and long-term services and supports (LTSS). Against this backdrop, aging demographics—already consuming healthcare at a multiple of the general population—should continue to drive incremental demand across the system, even as care delivery structurally shifts. ## Spending Rises Sharply with Advanced Age

With the increasing prevalence of chronic conditions, frailty, need for daily living assistance, etc., it is no surprise that healthcare spending increases with age. National Health Expenditure data provides clear evidence that healthcare spending rises steeply with age and accelerates further at the oldest ages. For example, personal healthcare spending for individuals aged 65 and older totaled \$22,356 (per capita) in 2020, more than five times the level for children and roughly 2.5 times that of working age adults. The age cohort also accounted for the smallest portion of the population (e.g. 17%), but accounted for \~37% of all healthcare spending in 2020. CMS tables show that per capita healthcare spending for adults aged 85 and older is even more elevated, reaching \~\$36,000, substantially higher than for younger seniors. Although individuals 85+ represent a relatively small share of the total population, they account for a disproportionately large share of total personal healthcare expenditures. This data supports the premise that the aging of the population into the 80+ cohort creates a structural spending tailwind, which we expect to accelerate moving forward. While not the only contributor to expectations for increased healthcare costs in the U.S., we do believe the aging baby boomers are one of the drivers of the growing total of healthcare expenditures in the country. CMS's National Healthcare Expenditure data forecasts healthcare expenditures growing to \~\$9 trillion by 2034, representing a 5.4% CAGR (2025-2034). CMS notes that this growth is projected to outpace that of average GDP growth (\~4%), resulting in an increase in the health spending share of GDP from \~18% to \~21% by 2034.

## Seniors Healthcare Utilization Changes as They Age

As seniors transition from age 65 toward 80 years of age, healthcare utilization shifts materially from preventative care and elective procedures toward the intensive management of chronic disease and higher-acuity treatment. This reflects the growing burden of multi-morbidity, which increases both the frequency and complexity of care encounters, driving higher utilization across primary, specialty, and coordinated care settings. Industry commentary underscores that aging cohorts require progressively greater chronic disease management and higher-intensity interventions, and this dynamic is amplified by the fact that individuals aged 65+ already utilize healthcare services at a multiple of the general population. As a result, the aging demographic will only serve to increase demand of healthcare resources and services in the U.S. At the same time, care delivery continues to migrate toward outpatient and ambulatory settings in earlier stages of the aging curve, reflecting both clinical preference and improved outcomes in lower-acuity environments.

Importantly, the increase in spending with age is not purely a volume story but reflects a fundamental rotation in the mix of care delivered. In the 65–74 cohort, spending is still dominated by outpatient, physician, and drug utilization consistent with a “maintenance phase” of chronic disease management, with limited reliance on institutional care. By ages 75–84, rising multi-morbidity drives a step-up in inpatient admissions and specialist intensity, alongside a growing role for post-acute services as more patients are discharged to rehabilitation and skilled nursing settings. The most pronounced shift occurs in the 85+ cohort, where spending becomes increasingly concentrated in post-acute and supportive care categories, including skilled nursing facilities (SNFs), home health, and hospice. At this stage, cost growth is driven less by discrete clinical interventions and more by duration-based utilization, including SNF days, home health episodes, and end-of-life care, as well as long-term services and supports (LTSS).

Not only is the US population growing older, but seniors increasingly show a preference to stay home and receive care in what is coined "aging in place". AARP's latest Home and Community Preferences Survey showed that older adults (60+) overwhelmingly want to stay in their homes (75%) and communities (73%). This is above the \~5.4% CAGR for total healthcare expenditures and the \~4.8% CAGR for hospitals, and \~4% for nursing care facilities and continuing care retirement facilities.

Personal Care Services: While many seniors remain active at 65, the demand for help with activities of daily living (ADLs) increases significantly by age 80. According to Addus, approximately 90% of adults aged 65 and older prefer to "age in place" for as long as possible. Personal care services are able to meet this demand by providing non-medical assistance with tasks such as bathing, dressing, meal preparation, and housekeeping, which are essential for seniors who are at increased risk of hospitalization or institutionalization. The need for assistance with ADLs increases with age, and with the increasing volume of older seniors, the demand for services to assist with ADLs will only increase. According to a 2018 study, roughly 20% of adults aged 85 or older needed help with ADLs, which is more than double the 8.7% of adults aged 75-84 that needed help with ADLs and compares to the 3.7% for the 65-74 age group.

## Hospice Volumes Should Also Benefit from Aging Demographics

Hospice volumes benefit from significant structural support driven by demographic aging, as the demand for end-of-life care is closely tied to age and increases disproportionately within the 75+ age group. With rising life expectancy, a larger portion of the population reaches advanced age categories where the prevalence of chronic, progressive conditions—such as cancer, dementia, and cardiovascular disease—escalates, expanding the pool of patients eligible for hospice services. According to the Caring Hospice Institute, the average age at hospice admission is \~80 years old, and the largest single age bracket is 85 and older. From a volume perspective, the growth of the elderly population not only increases the total number of decedents but also extends the duration of functional decline prior to death, supporting earlier referrals and longer lengths of stay, which collectively drive total patient days. Additionally, heightened awareness, evolving physician referral practices, and Medicare support have contributed to steadily increasing hospice utilization rates, indicating that demographic expansion is being complemented by secular penetration gains. According to the National Alliance for Care at Home, 1.9 mln Medicare beneficiaries were enrolled in hospice care for one day or more in CY24, representing a 4.4% increase versus CY23. In addition, of all Medicare decedents in CY24, 53.1% received at least one day of hospice care and were enrolled in hospice at the time of death, and marks the third year of improvement.

## Senior Housing and Assisted Living: Demand Rising, Supply Constrained

Senior housing data similarly reflect rising demand aligned with demographic aging. The National Investment Center for Seniors Housing & Care (NIC) reports that U.S. senior housing occupancy reached approximately 88–89% in 2025, marking more than four consecutive years of quarterly occupancy increases. Data as of 1Q26 from NIC, shows occupancy rates at 89.5%, up 40 bps Q/Q. NIC has noted that the strengthening demand is being driven by the aging Baby Boomer population, with limited supply of new inventory, with growth in inventory below 1% in 4Q25 and marked the third consecutive quarter of less than 1% growth in inventory. NIC projects that senior housing occupancy rates could exceed 90% by the end of 2026, particularly in independent and assisted living categories, as demand continues to outpace new construction. We look for demand for senior housing and assisted living to only increase from here given the demographic trends expected in the U.S.

## Deathcare Volumes to also Benefit from Aging Demographics

The aging of the Baby Boomer cohort into advanced ages is also relevant for deathcare services, including funeral homes, cemeteries, and cremation providers. Mortality rises sharply with age, particularly after age 80, making this demographic milestone a positive volume driver over time. In fact, SCI, one of the largest funeral and cemetery operators in the U.S. has noted that we are only in the early innings of a decade long expected tailwind from the aging Baby Boomer population. U.S. life tables from the CDC and Social Security Administration show that annual mortality probabilities increase exponentially in advanced age cohorts, despite incremental improvements in life expectancy at older ages. The National Funeral Directors Association (NFDA) projections call for the number of deaths to rise through 2045, anticipating a 19% increase by 2045 compared to 2022. That said, revenue growth is moderated somewhat by continued shifts in consumer preferences. NFDA forecasts cremation rates to increase significantly, reaching 82.3% by 2045, from \~63% in 2025, while burial rates are expected to fall to 13.3% by 2045. Cremation services generally carry lower average revenue per service than traditional burial, creating mix pressure for at need funeral revenues even as volumes rise. NFDA notes that families' increased preference for cremation is influenced by cost considerations, environmental concerns, fewer religious prohibitions and a desire for simpler funeral ceremonies, while the increase in the number of individuals without a religious affiliation has also contributed to the decline in traditional funerals and the rise in cremation. Cemetery operators and pre-need businesses are also expected to benefit from an aging population. Pre-need sales increase with age, as individuals entering their 70s and 80s are more likely to prearrange funeral and cemetery services. These contracts generate deferred but relatively predictable future revenue streams.

## Hospitals

While we have pointed out the fact that the aging demographic shifts will be beneficial for post-acute settings, we think hospitals will see a benefit as well from this trend. Hospitals are expected to benefit from population aging as older patients drive disproportionately higher acute care utilization, particularly in the 75+ and 80+ cohorts. Utilization data consistently show that the 65+ population uses hospital services at materially higher rates than younger adults, reflecting both a higher burden of chronic disease and greater incidence of acute events (e.g., cardiovascular episodes, infections, falls). For example, according to Vizient, 72% of emergency department visits in 2024 for adults aged 65+ were classified as emergent, higher than the 65% rate for adults aged 18-64. Over the next decade, Vizient projects emergent visit volume for the 65 and older population cohort to grow by 28%, while the 75-84 year old age group is expected to see a 45% increase. As the population ages, this should translate into continued growth in inpatient admissions, emergency department visits, and overall hospital volumes. In addition, the expansion of the Medicare-eligible population—particularly the continued growth of Medicare Advantage—provides a large and relatively stable payer base tied to aging demographics. According to projections by Vizient, volumes are expected to grow across all sites of care for the 65+ age cohort. This includes an expected 24% increase (from 2024-2034) in emergency department volumes for the 65+ population, a 17% increase in inpatient discharges, 22% increase in inpatient days, and 30% increase in outpatient volume. For reference, these are all below the 32% increase expected by Vizient for post-acute care volumes.

Interestingly, hospital inpatient volumes do not see any material decline in utilization as seniors age. According to CMS data on Medicare Fee-for-Service hospital inpatient stays, the discharges per 1,000 Medicare Part A enrollees is the lowest for the 65-74 age cohort at 147 discharges, then increases to 275 for 75-84 year olds and then again to 461 for the 85-94 year old cohort.

## MCOs Could See Pressure from Rise in Senior Member Utilization, but Look for MCOs to Steer Members to Lower Cost Settings

The aging population in the U.S. has already served to benefit Managed Care Organization's (MCOs) Medicare Advantage (MA) membership totals, which in turn has helped grow premiums. With the increasing age of MA members, there could in theory be increased utilization as members encounter incremental medical episodes given the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases amongst seniors. However, it is important to realize that members with higher disease burdens generally come with higher risk scores, and therefore higher payments to MCOs from CMS to care for these sicker patients. As it relates to the risk model and age cohorts, there is a natural step up in the CMS-HCC model coefficient based on age. However, the age groupings are in 4-year increments (75-79, 80-84, 85-89, etc). As such, assuming a member that has unchanged HCC codes but sees a change in age to the next age bracket, this would create a tailwind to revenue, all else equal. With that, if the health status of the member were to deteriorate quickly after hitting 80, the MCO would not be able to capture the codes until the following year. This in turn would cause a lower revenue level and higher medical cost expense until the member's risk score is appropriately captured and scored. We believe MCOs will look to better manage this aging population cohort through things like greater utilization of value-based care contracts and looking to send members to lower cost site of care settings, such as home health.

## Workforce and Caregiver Constraints as a Limiting Factor

Although demographic trends indicate rising demand, growth in volumes is constrained by workforce availability. According to the Bipartisan Policy Center, the U.S. has experienced a persistent shortage of direct care workers for over 20 years, with nearly 9 mln direct care job openings projected between 2022 and 2032. The report highlights that staffing shortages have compelled many home health providers to decline referred patients and have contributed to delays in hospital discharges. Concurrently, unpaid caregiving has emerged as a crucial component of the care system. AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's 2025 report estimates that 63 mln Americans provided unpaid caregiving, representing an almost 50% increase since 2015. Additionally, the Administration for Community Living observed that limited availability of direct care professionals restricts access to community-based services for older adults and individuals with disabilities. These trends point to a growing imbalance of demand for care given the aging population of the U.S., with a limited supply of caregivers to meet this demand.

## HOLT Scorecard and Factor Analysis

The UBS HOLT Scorecard is a systematic framework that ranks a company on three core dimensions: Operational Quality, Momentum and Valuation. These three pillars are weighted equally and combined into an Overall Scorecard Rank that helps investors identify attractive and unattractive stocks and has historically been associated with long-term shareholder returns. Based on HOLT's corporate performance and valuation metrics, the HOLT Scorecard provides a unique, differentiated and tractable factor set. Factors are ranked on a scale from 0 (weakest) to 100 (strongest) relative to a defined peer universe.

The HOLT Operational Quality factor assesses the relative attractiveness of a company based on its historical level and variability of CFROI. The HOLT Momentum factor provides a gauge of market sentiment, inclusive of 13-week CFROI Revisions, price momentum, and liquidity. The HOLT Valuation factor evaluates the relative attractiveness of a company's stock, and considers its HOLT Warranted Upside/Downside, Market Implied Yield, Economic P/E, HOLT P/B, and Dividend Yield.

We include Descriptive Factor Peer Ranks, which do not contribute to the Overall Scorecard Rank but highlight firms that may warrant specific portfolio positioning based on their growth potential or defensive characteristics. The HOLT Growth factor gauges the extent to which a company is likely to generate higher or lower future cash flow growth relative to its peers. The HOLT Low Volatility factor measures the variability of a company's stock price and its sensitivity to movements in its regional market. ## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

Risk to the managed care group include higher than expected utilization, elevated disenrollment, and government regulation. We use a multiple based approach based on PE to arrive at our price targets for the managed care group. Risks to the provider sector include: 1) slower volumes, 2) deteriorating payer mix, 3) integration and execution issues, 4) increased government regulation, 5) higher-than-expected operational expenses, and 6) any unexpected adverse macro and regulatory developments. We use a multiple-based approach to arrive at our price targets.

The HOLT methodology does not assign ratings or a target price to a security. It is an analytical tool that involves use of a set of proprietary quantitative algorithms and warranted value calculations, collectively called the HOLT valuation model, that are consistently applied to all the companies included in its database. The HOLT valuation model is a discounted cash flow model. Third-party data (including consensus earnings estimates) are systematically translated into a number of default variables and incorporated into the algorithms available in the HOLT valuation model. The source financial statement, pricing, and earnings data provided by outside data vendors are subject to quality control and may also be adjusted to more closely measure the underlying economics of firm performance. These adjustments provide consistency when analyzing a single company across time, or analyzing multiple companies across industries or national borders.

The default scenario that is produced by the HOLT valuation model establishes a warranted price that represents the expected mean value for a security based upon empirically derived fade algorithms that forecast a firm's future return on capital and growth rates over an extended period of time. As the third-party data are updated, the warranted price updates automatically. A company's future achieved return on capital or growth rate may differ from HOLT default forecast.

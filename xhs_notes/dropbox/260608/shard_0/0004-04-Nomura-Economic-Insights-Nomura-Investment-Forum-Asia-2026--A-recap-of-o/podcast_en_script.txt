EN_A: So there’s this new report from a major investment bank that came out of their Asia forum. It’s packed with macro views and even audience polls. What’s the big takeaway?
EN_B: The big picture is a growing divergence in the global economy. The US is clearly outperforming, while many emerging markets are getting squeezed by energy and chip prices. And crucially, this inflation cycle is different from 2022, so central banks probably won’t hike as much as markets expect.
EN_A: Divergence, okay. Why is the US doing so well?
EN_B: A lot of it is the AI boom. The report estimates AI infrastructure investment is adding a full percentage point to US GDP growth. Plus, the US is a net oil exporter and has its own natural gas market, so the Iran war hurts it much less than other developed economies.
EN_A: What about Europe and Asia then?
EN_B: Europe is facing stagflation—weaker growth and stronger inflation. The report says the ECB might only do modest tightening because markets are more worried about the war hurting growth than pushing up prices. In Asia, there’s a north-south split. North Asian economies benefit from AI, while South and Southeast Asia get hammered by high energy costs.
EN_A: The report mentions a “triple shock” of energy, chips, and food. What does that mean?
EN_B: It means global supply chain stress is almost as bad as during the pandemic. And even if the Strait of Hormuz reopens soon, the report warns there’s a lot of inertia. Historically, inflation peaks about six months after supply chain pressures do, so we might only be halfway through.
EN_A: So central banks are in a tough spot. One poll in the report shows 79% of respondents think the Fed will fall behind the curve this year. What’s your take?
EN_B: That’s a huge number. And most people expect the Fed to stay on hold through end of 2026. If they do move, it’s more likely a hike than a cut. The key insight is that the “stag” part of stagflation is worse this time because there’s no pent-up demand or big fiscal stimulus like after COVID. So central banks will hike less than the market thinks.
EN_A: What about Japan? The BOJ seems to be on a tightening path.
EN_B: Japan is the exception. The report expects the BOJ to hike in June and December this year, and again next June, taking rates to 1.5%. Wage hikes are becoming more sustainable, and inflation could peak at 3.6% by early 2027. But interestingly, only 69% of poll respondents think the BOJ will fall behind the curve—lower than for the Fed.
EN_A: Another poll result caught my eye: 64% of people think China’s 10-year bond yield will go even lower from its current record low of 1.72%. That’s pretty bearish.
EN_B: It reflects deep pessimism about China’s growth. The report spends a lot of time on China’s “K-shaped” divergence—the AI boom happening alongside a property bust. AI only benefits a handful of “smart cities,” while 14 million migrant workers lost construction jobs and wealth in lower-tier cities collapsed. This K-shape could further weaken overall demand.
EN_A: So AI isn’t going to fix China’s problems?
EN_B: Exactly. The report says policymakers can’t assume the AI economy will heal the wounds from the property bust. Beijing may still need to step up cleanup in the property sector and push fiscal reforms to support the left-behind cities.

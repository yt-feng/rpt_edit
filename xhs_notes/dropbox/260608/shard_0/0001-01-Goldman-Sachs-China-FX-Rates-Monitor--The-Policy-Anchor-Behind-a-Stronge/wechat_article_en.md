# The PBOC Is Not Cutting Rates, but the Bond Market Is Already Priced for a Liquidity Regime Shift

The most confounding development in Chinese financial markets over the past quarter is not the resilience of the renminbi against a stronger dollar, nor the acceleration of exporters' FX conversion. It is the simultaneous rally in China government bonds and decline in interest rate swaps at a time when imported inflation is rising, the PBOC has not cut policy rates, and overnight repo rates have already bounced off the lower bound of the corridor. These moves appear to violate standard pricing logic. Yet they are not irrational. They reflect a structural change in the demand side of China's fixed-income market that has decoupled yields from both inflation expectations and the central bank's policy rate stance. The bond market is not forecasting a rate cut. It is pricing in a liquidity regime shift driven by weak credit demand, constrained offshore investment channels, and an explicit PBOC blessing of bank bond buying as a legitimate form of money creation. Understanding this new anchor is essential for any investor positioning in China rates or FX over the next 12 months.

The April data miss has triggered debate about whether broad easing is imminent. Exports remain the headline growth driver, but domestic demand softened notably. Retail sales and fixed asset investment both slowed. Some of this weakness is technical or policy-related: residual seasonality, slower fiscal spending after a strong start to the year, fading consumer goods trade-in subsidies, and the increase in EV purchase taxes. These drags may fade. But the case for broad monetary easing is complicated by oil-led reflation. The reopening risk around the Strait of Hormus keeps imported inflation elevated, making policy rate cuts difficult. Instead, the PBOC is likely to accommodate fiscal implementation through low-profile easing: keeping interbank liquidity ample, keeping repo rates below the OMO target, and expanding targeted relending at lower rates. This is not the kind of easing that bond markets normally rally on. Yet the rally has been substantial.

The central argument of this article is that the PBOC's policy anchor has shifted from a single-minded focus on inflation and growth to a more complex framework that explicitly incorporates the asset-allocation consequences of weak credit demand. The bond market has priced this shift ahead of the central bank's own communication. The result is a market that is simultaneously pricing low yields and low probability of rate cuts, a combination that cannot persist indefinitely but may persist longer than most models suggest.

## A Stronger Renminbi Is Not a Surprise but a Policy Signal, and the Pace of Appreciation Matters More Than the Level

The renminbi has appreciated against the dollar by more than 3% year-to-date as of late May, and this has occurred during periods of renewed dollar strength. In March, when the Middle East energy shock triggered broad dollar strength, USD/CNY only partially retraced its earlier decline and stabilized around 6.90 from around 6.82 before the shock. The appreciation trend resumed later, with USD/CNY spot breaking below 6.80 in late May despite a stronger dollar since mid-May. This resilience is not an accident of market forces. It is a deliberate policy outcome.

The PBOC's daily fixing mechanism provides the clearest signal. The countercyclical factor remained elevated at above 400 pips in May, indicating the central bank's tendency to slow renminbi appreciation against the dollar. This is not a signal of resistance to appreciation. It is a signal of a desired pace. In our view, the PBOC is comfortable with orderly and gradual CNY appreciation against the USD at a pace of around 4% annualized. This pace is fast enough to offset carry costs for foreign investors but slow enough to keep the drag on export competitiveness and inflation limited. The policy anchor behind CNY appreciation is therefore not a target level but a target trajectory.

The implications for investors are strategic rather than tactical. A 4% annualized appreciation path means that USD/CNY spot could reach 6.50 in 12 months, with some near-term overshooting risk. Periods of dollar strength may reduce near-term downside pressure, but the medium-term direction is set by policy intent, not by the dollar cycle. The more important question is what happens when appreciation expectations become one-sided. We estimate that exporters have converted around 150 billion dollars more FX proceeds than normal since last December, against our earlier estimate that excess corporate USD hoarding reached around 500 billion dollars by mid-2025. This makes CNY appreciation more choppy and event-driven, with fixing changes easily read as policy signals. The risk is not that the PBOC abandons the appreciation path but that the pace becomes self-reinforcing and overshoots the desired trajectory.

## The Bond Rally Is Not a Forecast of Rate Cuts but a Consequence of a Structural Demand Shift That the PBOC Has Endorsed

The recent rally in China government bonds and decline in interest rate swaps has puzzled many investors, especially as oil-shock reflation has pushed inflation expectations higher. Our model-implied fair value for 10-year CGB yields has risen to above 2%, mostly reflecting higher one-year-ahead CPI inflation expectations, while the 10-year CGB yield has fallen to around 1.70%. The front end also looks difficult to square with a no-cut baseline: 1-year IRS is trading only around 3 basis points above the 1.4% OMO policy rate, while the 1-year CGB yield is much lower at around 1.15%. Meanwhile, expectations for high-profile monetary policy easing such as policy rate cuts are low. Overnight repo rates have also traded close to the lower bound of the interest rate corridor in April and even risen somewhat in May.

Taken together, these developments would normally suggest limited downside to bond yields and IRS rates. The missing factor is the asset-allocation pressure created by weak credit demand and limited alternative investable assets onshore. Soft loan demand leaves banks with more balance-sheet capacity for bond investment. In previous episodes of very low CGB yields and strong bank demand for bonds, the PBOC tended to warn against idle funds circulating within the financial system. This time, the PBOC's Q1 monetary policy report explicitly stated that banks' bond investment, like credit extension, is an important channel of financing the real economy and money creation. At the same time, limited room for domestic institutions to increase overseas investment keeps demand concentrated in onshore fixed-income assets.

The result is a market where yields can fall below model-implied fair value even without policy rate cuts. This is not a temporary anomaly. It is a structural feature of a financial system where credit demand is weak, the central bank has endorsed bond buying as legitimate money creation, and capital outflows are constrained. The bond market is not forecasting a rate cut. It is pricing in a liquidity regime where banks are forced to buy bonds because they have nowhere else to put their money. The PBOC has signaled that it will not stand in the way of this process, as long as it does not generate financial stability risks.

## A Sustained Selloff in Bonds Requires a Willingness to Push Repo Rates Above the OMO Target, Which Is a High Bar

The risk of a correction in China rates is real, but the conditions for a sustained selloff are narrow. We still expect China rates to remain low and see room for yields to move lower in the medium term if growth weakness becomes clearer or the liquidity anchor stays in place. That said, correction risks could rise into late June, given quarter-end liquidity demand and the market sensitivity to recent OMO operations. The PBOC's zero 7-day reverse repo operation earlier this week was read by markets as a warning of potential liquidity tightening. However, OMO injections resumed shortly after, and demand for OMO funding may be low given its relatively high cost compared with interbank repos.

In our view, the room for a sustained rates selloff should also be limited unless the PBOC is willing to repeat the 2020 or 2022 episodes of pushing repo rates above the OMO target. This looks like a high bar for several reasons. First, repo rates are currently not far below the OMO rate, which was not the case in 2020 or 2022. Second, domestic demand and credit demand remain soft, making it difficult for the PBOC to justify tightening. Third, uncertainty around the Middle East energy shock is still elevated, and the PBOC is unlikely to add to financial conditions tightening when the external environment is already uncertain.

The decision framework for investors is therefore straightforward. If repo rates remain below the OMO target, the liquidity anchor stays in place, and bond yields will likely remain low or move lower. If the PBOC pushes repo rates above the OMO target, the liquidity regime shifts, and a sustained selloff becomes possible. The probability of the latter is low, but it is not zero. The trigger would be a sharp improvement in domestic demand or a sustained decline in imported inflation that gives the PBOC room to normalize policy. Neither condition is present today.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Threshold for a PBOC Policy Reversal and the Role of Fiscal Policy

The report leaves several meaningful open questions that are critical for investors. First, what is the precise threshold for the PBOC to shift from its current low-profile easing stance to a more aggressive easing or tightening posture? The report identifies a sharper export slowdown or a more persistent deterioration in domestic demand as necessary triggers for broader policy response, but it does not quantify these triggers. How much of a slowdown in exports is needed? How persistent must the domestic demand weakness be? Without clear thresholds, investors are left to interpret every data point as a potential signal, which increases volatility.

Second, the report does not fully address the interaction between fiscal policy and the liquidity anchor. If government bond issuance re-accelerates and policy financial instruments provide additional support, as the report suggests, this could absorb some of the excess bank liquidity that is currently driving bond demand. The report acknowledges that fiscal support may catch up later this year, but it does not model the impact on bond yields. A large increase in government bond supply could push yields higher even if the liquidity anchor stays in place, because banks would be absorbing government bonds rather than accumulating CGBs. This is a second-order implication that the report touches on but does not resolve.

Third, the report's analysis of CNY appreciation assumes that the PBOC's desired pace of 4% annualized remains stable. But what happens if appreciation expectations become self-reinforcing and the pace accelerates? The report notes that exporters' FX conversion has already exceeded normal levels by 150 billion dollars, and that excess corporate USD hoarding reached 500 billion dollars by mid-2025. If appreciation expectations become one-sided, the PBOC may need to intervene more aggressively to slow the pace, which would drain FX reserves and tighten domestic liquidity. The report does not model this scenario in detail.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Anchors to Monitor

For investors trying to navigate the China rates and FX market, the key is to monitor three anchors rather than trying to predict the next data point or policy announcement.

The first anchor is the PBOC's tolerance for repo rates relative to the OMO target. As long as repo rates remain below the OMO target, the liquidity anchor for bonds is intact. The risk of a sustained selloff is low. If repo rates move above the OMO target, the regime shifts, and investors should reduce duration exposure. The signal to watch is the PBOC's OMO operations and the spread between DR001 and the OMO rate.

The second anchor is the pace of CNY appreciation relative to the PBOC's desired trajectory. If the pace is around 4% annualized, the PBOC is likely comfortable. If the pace accelerates above 5% or decelerates below 3%, the PBOC may adjust its fixing or intervene. The signal to watch is the countercyclical factor in the daily fixing and the CNH/CNY basis.

The third anchor is the interaction between fiscal policy and bank balance sheets. If government bond issuance accelerates and absorbs bank liquidity, bond yields may rise even if the PBOC does not tighten. The signal to watch is the pace of government bond issuance and the volume of bank bond holdings relative to loan growth.

These three anchors provide a framework for positioning that is more robust than trying to forecast the next data point or policy announcement. The market is not irrational. It is pricing a liquidity regime shift that the PBOC has endorsed. The question is not whether this regime will persist but what conditions would cause it to break.

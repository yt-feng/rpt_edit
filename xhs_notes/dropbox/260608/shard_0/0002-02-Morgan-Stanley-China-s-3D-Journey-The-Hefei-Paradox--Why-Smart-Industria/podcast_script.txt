ZH_A: 最近有一份关于合肥模式的研报，说合肥虽然打造了世界级的产业集群，但依然没能逃过产能过剩和通缩。这听起来有点反直觉，你觉得这份报告真正想回答的核心问题是什么？

ZH_B: 它的核心问题是：更聪明的产业政策和更强的出口，能不能从根本上解决中国经济的供需失衡。合肥是一个完美的案例——它被认为是中国产业政策最成功的模板之一，但即使如此，报告发现，它只是改变了“投资的方向”，并没有改变“投资过量的根本动力”。

ZH_A: 所以意思是，合肥模式只是把资源投到了更先进、更有竞争力的行业，但并没有解决地方政府的投资冲动问题？

ZH_B: 对，就是这个意思。报告里用一个词叫“合肥悖论”——它能造出世界级的企业，比如京东方、蔚来、长鑫存储，但它的消费却跑输全国，工业品通缩比全国平均更严重。这说明，好的产业政策可以提升投资质量，但无法修复由地方政府激励机制导致的供给过剩。

ZH_A: 那这个“地方政府激励机制”具体指什么？为什么这么难改？

ZH_B: 核心是三个东西：官员考核仍然看重产出和投资增长；税收体系以增值税为主，天然鼓励生产而不是消费；地方之间的竞争导致大家都去抢同一个赛道。报告说，就算北京现在推“全国统一大市场”和“反内卷”，如果没有改变这些底层激励，产能过剩只是从一个行业转移到另一个行业。

ZH_A: 那出口呢？最近几年中国的出口很强劲，尤其在全球AI和能源转型的资本开支大周期里，出口能不能帮忙消化掉过剩产能？

ZH_B: 报告认为出口能缓解一部分，但有两个硬约束。第一，现在的出口产品结构更偏向资本密集型，就业乘数比过去弱，所以出口增长对国内居民收入的传导效果更差。第二，靠外需来吸收国内过剩产能，本身就很脆弱——一旦全球周期转向或者贸易摩擦升级，风险就会暴露。

ZH_A: 所以报告预测的是一个典型的K型经济，新经济很强，但旧经济很弱？

ZH_B: 没错。他们预测2026年实际GDP增长4.8%，2027年4.7%，主要靠AI和新能源相关的出口和制造业投资拉动。但家庭消费会持续偏弱，因为劳动力市场疲软，房地产调整还没结束。新经济虽然增长快，但太窄、太资本密集，没办法创造出足够广泛的收入增长。

ZH_A: 那通胀呢？最近PPI好像有转正的迹象。

ZH_B: 报告认为这次PPI转正主要是输入性的，来自能源成本和AI相关需求的拉动，不是国内需求驱动的。他们预计2026年PPI到1.5%，但2027年随着能源脉冲消退，又会回到负值。真正全面的再通胀，需要像2015到2017年那样的大规模需求刺激，但这轮还看不到。

ZH_A: 那报告给出的“出路”是什么？

ZH_B: 他们的结论很明确：只有果断的经济再平衡改革才能解决问题。具体包括改变官员考核方式，从看重产出转向看重居民收入和消费；改革税收结构，减少对增值税的依赖，增加直接税；以及加强社会保障，降低居民的高预防性储蓄。

ZH_A: 听起来这些改革方向大家都有共识，但为什么进展这么慢？

ZH_B: 报告里也暗示了这一点——地缘政治压力可能让北京更倾向于维持供给端的思路。而且，十五五规划里关于消费的表述还是偏定性、偏软性的，没有硬性约束。所以，更聪明的产业政策和更强的出口，可以改善增长的质量和节奏，但无法消除结构性的过剩。

ZH_A: 那如果我们想更深入地了解，合肥模式到底是怎么运作的，它具体投了哪些项目，回报如何，是不是要去读完整报告？

ZH_B: 对，完整报告里有一个非常详细的案例分析，包括合肥怎么用财政资金做“耐心资本”，怎么通过市场化退出实现收益循环，以及为什么它的成功很难被其他城市复制。这些细节非常值得一看。

ZH_A: 好的，那我们就引导听众去获取完整报告，里面还有更多关于官员考核改革和财政体系改革的深度讨论。感谢你的分享。

ZH_B: 谢谢，我们下期再聊。

EN_A: So there’s this new report on Hefei’s model that says even the poster child of China’s smart industrial policy couldn’t escape overcapacity and deflation. That feels counterintuitive. What’s the big question the report is really trying to answer?

EN_B: The big question is whether smarter industrial policy and stronger exports can actually fix China’s supply-demand imbalance. Hefei is the perfect test case—it’s widely seen as the most successful example of ecosystem-driven industrial policy. But the report finds that even Hefei only shifted what China invests in, not how much it invests. The supply-side incentives remain intact.

EN_A: So you’re saying the Hefei model can build world-class champions like BOE, NIO, and CXMT, but it can’t fix the fundamental problem of overinvestment?

EN_B: Exactly. The report calls it the Hefei paradox. The city outperformed in industrial output and exports, but its consumer sales lagged and its deflation was worse than the national average. That tells you that better industrial policy improves allocation, but it doesn’t change the local government incentives that drive excess supply.

EN_A: And those incentives—what are they exactly?

EN_B: Three things. Cadre evaluation still prioritizes output and investment growth. The tax system is heavily VAT-based, which rewards production over consumption. And local governments compete to attract the same frontier industries. The report says that even with the new unified national market initiative and the anti-involution campaign, without changing these underlying incentives, overcapacity just shifts from one sector to another.

EN_A: Okay, but what about exports? China’s export machine is running hard, especially with the global AI and energy transition capex super-cycle. Can’t that absorb some of the excess?

EN_B: It helps, but the report points to two hard limits. First, the product mix is more capital-intensive now, so the employment multiplier is weaker—meaning exports don’t translate into broad income gains as effectively. Second, relying on foreign demand to absorb domestic oversupply makes the economy vulnerable to global cycle shifts and trade frictions.

EN_A: So the report is predicting a K-shaped economy—strong on the new economy side, weak on consumption?

EN_B: Yes. They see real GDP growth at 4.8% in 2026 and 4.7% in 2027, driven by exports and manufacturing capex related to AI and energy transition. But household consumption stays soft because the labor market is weak and property is still adjusting. The new economy is too narrow and too capital-intensive to generate self-sustaining domestic demand.

EN_A: And inflation? We’ve seen PPI turning positive recently.

EN_B: The report says that’s mostly imported—driven by energy costs and AI-related demand, not domestic demand. They expect PPI to hit 1.5% in 2026, but then turn negative again in 2027 as the energy impulse fades. For a genuine reflation, you’d need a demand stimulus comparable to the 2015-2017 cycle, and that’s not in sight.

EN_A: So what’s the way out according to the report?

EN_B: Decisive rebalancing reforms. That means shifting cadre evaluation from output to household income and consumption, reducing reliance on VAT in favor of direct taxes, and strengthening social safety nets to lower precautionary savings. Without those, better industrial policy and stronger exports can lift the composition of growth, but they can’t resolve the structural surplus.

EN_A: Why is progress so slow on these reforms?

EN_B: The report hints that geopolitical tensions may entrench Beijing’s supply-side approach. And the 15th Five-Year Plan’s consumption push is still qualitative and non-binding. So the core problem remains.

EN_B: Absolutely. The full report has a detailed case study on Hefei’s patient capital approach, its market-based exits, and why its success is hard to replicate. It’s worth reading for anyone trying to understand the real constraints on China’s growth model.

EN_A: So China’s FX reserves surprised to the upside again in May. What’s driving this?
EN_B: Yeah, reserves went up by 31.7 billion to 3.44 trillion, beating both the bank’s forecast and consensus. The main drivers are stronger exports and likely softer imports, plus capital outflows staying pretty muted at around 20 billion.
EN_A: So what does that mean? Is China’s economy actually picking up steam?
EN_B: Not exactly. The export beat partly reflects price effects — AI chip prices surged, and the “three new” sectors like EVs, solar, and batteries saw price gains. But the bigger story is that capital outflows are very subdued. That’s a big shift from a few years ago when we saw hundreds of billions flowing out.
EN_A: And the PBOC seems to be buying more gold. What’s that about?
EN_B: Right. In May, they accelerated gold purchases to 320,000 ounces, compared to just 3,000 on average in the second half of last year. Meanwhile, China’s U.S. Treasury holdings dropped by 41 billion in March. So clearly, they’re shifting reserve composition away from dollar assets and toward gold.
EN_A: That ties into a bigger question. We’ve seen a flurry of cross-border tightening measures recently — the CSRC crackdown on illegal marketing, the new outbound investment rules. Is China really clamping down on capital outflows?
EN_B: The report’s key takeaway is: don’t overreact. These measures are not about blocking outflows entirely. They’re about cleaning up gray-area channels and steering flows toward compliant routes like Stock Connect or QDII. Think of it as channel cleanup, not a blanket ban.
EN_A: But the new State Council regulations on outbound investment look pretty comprehensive. Are they really that benign?
EN_B: They do create a more complete legal framework. But the report argues the goal is to standardize governance, not to curb investment. In fact, the regulations include a protection clause — if Chinese firms face discriminatory barriers overseas, authorities can take countermeasures. So it’s about supporting high-standard opening, not closing doors.
EN_A: Interesting. And the renminbi has been strengthening lately. The CFETS basket was up 1.5% in May. Is that connected to all this?
EN_B: Partly. But the report points out that a stronger RMB basket actually helps ease trade tensions. Last year, the real effective exchange rate weakened a lot, and China faced accusations of “intentional depreciation.” A firmer basket now supports RMB internationalization goals under the 15th Five-Year Plan.
EN_A: But customer behavior seems mixed. Both the settlement ratio and the sales ratio dropped in April. What’s going on?
EN_B: Exactly. The settlement ratio fell from 58.2% to 50.2%, and the sales ratio dropped from 55% to 49.8%. So the net settlement narrowed to just 0.4 percentage points. That means both companies and individuals are sitting on the sidelines. They’re not eager to convert dollars into RMB, but they’re also not rushing to buy dollars. It suggests uncertainty about where the currency is headed next.

EN_B: That’s exactly what the full report explores further. It notes that the near-term policy focus is actually on avoiding too rapid an appreciation.

# China’s FX Reserves Surprise Is Not About Luck — It Signals a Structural Shift in Capital Management

China’s May FX reserves beat expectations for the second consecutive month, rising USD 31.7 billion to USD 3,442.2 billion against a consensus estimate of USD 3,400 billion. The immediate narrative will focus on firmer exports, softer imports, and valuation effects. But the deeper story is more strategic: China is simultaneously managing a stronger trade-weighted currency, accelerating gold accumulation, and rolling out a comprehensive governance framework for outbound investment — all while implied capital outflows remain remarkably muted. This is not a random data point. It is a deliberate policy signal.

The timing matters. In a global environment where trade tensions are resurging, the RMB is under scrutiny for alleged undervaluation, and capital flow management is once again a geopolitical flashpoint. China’s May reserve print and accompanying policy actions suggest a coordinated approach: defend the currency’s credibility, reduce exposure to US dollar assets, and codify a rules-based system for outbound capital that prioritizes compliance over restriction. Investors who dismiss this as a one-month export beat risk missing the structural pivot.

The question is not whether China can sustain reserve accumulation. The question is what the composition and governance of those reserves reveal about Beijing’s long-term strategy for financial integration, currency internationalization, and geopolitical risk management.

## The Reserve Beat Reflects Export Resilience and a Shift in Price Dynamics, Not Just Valuation Luck

The headline reserve increase of USD 31.7 billion in May exceeded both the JPM estimate of USD 3,405 billion and the broader consensus of USD 3,400 billion. The straightforward explanation points to a wider current-account surplus, estimated at USD 64.0 billion, combined with a valuation loss of USD 12.3 billion from a rising US dollar index (from 98.1 to 98.9). Implied capital outflows remained muted at just USD 20 billion.

But the composition of the surplus is more telling. Port tracking data shows some moderation in departing container-ship deadweight tonnage, but bulk shipping was lifted by a month-end jump. More importantly, price effects appear to have turned supportive: AI memory chip and module prices surged, the “three-new” sectors — EVs, solar, and batteries — saw price increases, and rising PPI pressures are now passing through to other manufactured goods. This suggests that China’s export outperformance is no longer solely a volume story. Price recovery in key industrial and technology segments is beginning to contribute to the trade surplus.

The implication is significant. If China’s export pricing power is recovering, the argument that the RMB is structurally undervalued loses some of its force. A firmer currency basket becomes more sustainable because it is backed by improving terms of trade, not just policy intervention. The May data point to a self-reinforcing cycle: stronger export prices support reserve accumulation, which in turn provides the policy space to allow the CFETS basket to strengthen.

## The PBOC Is Accelerating Gold Purchases and Reducing US Treasury Holdings — A Deliberate De-Dollarization Signal

The reserve composition is shifting in a way that cannot be explained by valuation alone. The PBOC accelerated gold purchases in May, adding 0.32 million ounces versus a 2H2025 average of just 0.03 million ounces. Meanwhile, China’s US Treasury holdings dropped USD 41 billion to USD 652.3 billion in March, the latest month for which data is available.

This is not a tactical adjustment. The pace of gold accumulation is accelerating at a time when the US dollar index is rising, making dollar-denominated assets more expensive to hold in local-currency terms. The PBOC is effectively paying a premium to reduce its exposure to US sovereign debt. The strategic rationale is clear: gold provides a geopolitical hedge, diversifies reserve risk, and supports the long-term objective of RMB internationalization by signaling that China is building a reserve base less dependent on the US financial system.

The open question is whether this trend is sustainable. At current purchase rates, China’s gold holdings would surpass those of several major European central banks within two years. But the opportunity cost is real: US Treasuries offer yield and liquidity that gold cannot match. The PBOC is trading financial return for strategic autonomy. Investors should watch whether this pattern continues through periods of US dollar weakness, when the relative cost of holding gold becomes even higher.

## The CFETS Strengthening Is a Deliberate Policy Choice, Not a Market Accident

In May, the CFETS basket rose about 1.5% and the REER increased around 1%, bringing the real effective exchange rate back to early-2025 levels. The CFETS has more than fully retraced the 2025 depreciation. This is happening while USD/CNY gains are partly capped by renewed US dollar strength, meaning the RMB is appreciating on a trade-weighted basis even as the bilateral rate remains relatively stable.

The policy rationale is twofold. First, a stronger CFETS basket helps ease geopolitical frictions. Last year’s CNY TWI weakness drew pushback from non-US trading partners amid China’s record trade surplus, reviving accusations of “intended” depreciation. A firmer basket reduces the risk of retaliatory trade measures. Second, a stronger RMB supports the 15th Five-Year Plan’s objective of RMB internationalization. No major reserve currency has ever achieved global status while persistently weakening on a trade-weighted basis.

But the domestic implications are more nuanced. The banks’ FX settlement ratio — a proxy for customers’ willingness to convert foreign currency into RMB — fell from 58.2% in March to 50.2% in April, weaker than typical seasonality. The FX sales ratio also declined. The net FX settlement ratio narrowed to just 0.4 percentage points from 3.1 points in March. This suggests that while the PBOC is pushing the basket higher, domestic market participants are not fully convinced. They are holding foreign currency rather than converting into RMB, indicating lingering uncertainty about the sustainability of the appreciation.

The policy tension is clear: the authorities want a stronger basket to achieve external objectives, but they must manage domestic expectations to avoid a self-reinforcing cycle of capital outflows. The May reserve data provides the ammunition to continue this policy, but the customer behavior data serves as a warning that credibility must be earned, not declared.

## The New Outbound Investment Regulations Are About Governance, Not Capital Controls — But the Distinction Matters

The State Council’s June 1 Regulations on Outbound Investment have been widely interpreted as a tightening of capital outflow restrictions. That reading is too simplistic. The regulations establish a comprehensive legal framework for outbound investment by China-domiciled enterprises, organizations, and resident individuals, covering direct or indirect overseas investments that confer ownership, control, or management rights. They include approval and filing requirements, enhanced national-security reviews, and penalties for non-compliance.

But the report’s analysis is clear: the objective is not to curb outbound investment per se, but to standardize governance and tighten enforcement around activities that already sit within existing rules. The CSRC-led campaign targets illegal cross-border marketing, account opening, and trade execution, while steering investors toward compliant channels like Stock Connect, QDII, and Wealth Management Connect. This is channel cleanup, not a blanket ban.

The distinction is critical for investors. A governance framework that codifies rules and enforcement mechanisms is fundamentally different from ad hoc capital controls. It provides predictability. Firms that comply with the new regulations can proceed with outbound investments. Those that relied on grey-zone practices will face increasing friction. The regulations also include a protection clause: if Chinese firms face discriminatory restrictions abroad, authorities may take corresponding measures to safeguard Chinese investors’ lawful rights and interests.

The unanswered question is how the regulations will be implemented in practice. The framework takes effect July 1, 2026, but detailed implementation measures have yet to be released. The gap between the broad legal framework and the specific rules will create a period of uncertainty. Investors should monitor whether implementation is consistent with the stated objective of governance standardization or whether it drifts toward de facto restriction.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Muted Capital Outflows

The report documents that implied capital outflows remained muted at USD 20 billion in May. It attributes this to a combination of a wider current-account surplus, valuation effects, and the impact of cross-border tightening measures. But it does not fully explain whether this is a structural shift or a cyclical lull.

Several factors could disrupt the current calm. First, the acceleration of gold purchases and reduction in US Treasury holdings represents a form of capital outflow through reserve composition. If this trend continues, it will show up in the capital account data with a lag. Second, the customer behavior data — declining settlement and sales ratios — suggests that domestic participants are cautious. A sudden shift in sentiment, triggered by geopolitical events or a sharp move in the USD, could reverse the current pattern.

Third, the new outbound investment regulations may initially suppress capital outflows as firms wait for clarity on implementation rules. Once the rules are finalized, there could be a pent-up wave of compliant outbound investment. The report does not model this scenario, but it is a plausible risk.

The most important unresolved question is whether China can maintain a stronger CFETS basket while keeping capital outflows muted. Historically, a stronger currency has been associated with capital inflows, not outflows. The current configuration — stronger basket, muted outflows — is unusual and may reflect temporary factors: export price recovery, regulatory tightening, and cautious market sentiment. Investors should stress-test their assumptions for a scenario in which any of these factors reverses.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask About China’s Reserve and Capital Flow Strategy

For investors trying to position for the next 12 to 18 months, the report’s data and analysis suggest three strategic questions:

First, how sticky is the export price recovery? If AI memory chips, EVs, solar, and batteries continue to command higher prices, the trade surplus will remain wide, supporting reserve accumulation and providing policy space for a stronger basket. If price effects fade, the reserve story weakens, and pressure to manage the currency more actively returns.

Second, is the PBOC’s gold accumulation a signal of permanent de-dollarization or a tactical hedge? If gold purchases continue at the May pace, China’s reserve composition will shift materially within two years. This would have implications for US Treasury demand, gold prices, and the broader geopolitics of reserve management. If purchases slow, the signal is weaker.

Third, will the outbound investment regulations become a tool for capital management or a framework for compliance? The answer depends on implementation. Investors should track the pace and content of the detailed rules, enforcement actions against non-compliant firms, and the behavior of approved outbound investment channels like QDII quotas. If the rules are applied consistently and transparently, they will support the stated objective of high-standard opening-up. If they become a vehicle for discretionary restriction, the risk premium on Chinese assets will rise.

These questions do not have definitive answers yet. But they define the analytical agenda for anyone serious about understanding China’s financial trajectory.

# The Energy Storage Industry Is Entering a New Phase: Scale Alone No Longer Guarantees Success

The global energy storage industry has reached an inflection point. For the past several years, the dominant narrative has been about capacity expansion—who could build the most gigawatt-hours, who could secure the largest supply contracts, and who could achieve the lowest unit costs. But the data and corporate actions now point to a different reality. The industry is transitioning from a volume-driven growth model to a value-driven one, and the companies that succeed in the next phase will be those that master integration, safety, and lifecycle economics, not just manufacturing scale.

This shift is visible across multiple dimensions. Cell costs for LFP batteries have stabilized around $44 per kilowatt-hour at the spot level, with pack costs at $59 per kilowatt-hour. These figures represent a remarkable compression from just a few years ago, but they also signal that the easy cost reductions have been achieved. Further gains will come from system-level optimization, not cell chemistry improvements alone. Meanwhile, the industry is seeing a surge in activity around second-life applications, thermal safety, and grid integration—areas that were peripheral to the core manufacturing focus of previous years.

Why now? Three forces are converging. First, the rapid buildout of renewable energy capacity, particularly in China and Europe, is creating an urgent need for storage that goes beyond simple peak shaving. Second, the emergence of AI-driven data center demand is introducing a new class of electricity consumer with unique requirements for reliability and power quality. Third, regulatory frameworks in Europe and North America are tightening, forcing companies to invest in certification, safety, and supply chain transparency. These forces are reshaping the competitive landscape in ways that many market participants have not fully appreciated.

The week's news flow, captured in a recent industry report, reveals a pattern that is easy to miss when reading individual press releases. Taken together, the announcements from companies across the battery value chain tell a coherent story about where the industry is heading. This article unpacks that story and offers a strategic framework for investors and executives navigating this transition.

## The Battery Value Chain Is Fragmenting into Specialized Niches, and Generalist Manufacturers Will Face Margin Pressure

One of the most striking developments in recent weeks is the proliferation of specialized battery companies targeting specific applications rather than competing across the entire market. This is not a temporary phenomenon. It reflects a structural shift in how value is created and captured in the energy storage industry.

Consider the contrast between two types of announcements. On one hand, we see companies like SK On unveiling next-generation ESS platforms with specific safety features and configurations tailored to the North American market. On the other hand, we see partnerships like the 10 GWh agreement between Xingchen Energy and Chuneng, which is fundamentally about supply chain coordination and delivery efficiency. The former is a product differentiation play; the latter is an operational scale play. Both can succeed, but they require different capabilities and serve different market segments.

The fragmentation is happening along several axes. First, by geography: companies are increasingly designing products for specific regional regulatory environments, such as the EU Battery Regulation certification that Wasion Energy Storage recently obtained for its containerized ESS. Second, by application: flow batteries for long-duration storage, second-life batteries for stationary storage, and solid-state batteries for high-performance applications are all attracting dedicated investment. Third, by technology: the emergence of silicon anode materials, graphene-based thermal management, and advanced diagnostics (EIS) is creating new value pools that require deep technical expertise.

The implication for generalist battery manufacturers is clear. The era when a single product could serve multiple markets with minor modifications is ending. Companies that try to be everything to everyone will find themselves squeezed between specialized competitors on one side and scale-driven commodity producers on the other. The winners will be those that make explicit choices about which niches to serve and build the corresponding capabilities.

## Second-Life Batteries Are Moving from Niche Experiments to Industrial-Scale Operations, Creating a New Asset Class

The battery industry has long talked about second-life applications for retired EV batteries, but the volume of actual deployments has been modest. That is changing rapidly. Recent announcements from Waymo, Moment Energy, and others signal that second-life battery systems are crossing the threshold from pilot projects to commercial-scale operations.

The Waymo-B2U partnership is particularly instructive. By deploying hundreds of megawatts of second-life battery capacity starting in Texas and California, this project addresses two critical challenges simultaneously: the disposal of retired robotaxi batteries and the need for grid-scale storage. The economics are compelling. Second-life batteries avoid the raw material and manufacturing costs of new batteries, while still offering adequate performance for stationary applications where weight and size are less constrained than in vehicles.

Moment Energy's new facility in Vancouver, with an annual capacity of 1 GWh, demonstrates that second-life operations can achieve industrial scale. The company's ability to secure over $100 million in total funding suggests that institutional investors are beginning to view second-life battery assets as a legitimate asset class with attractive risk-adjusted returns. The key question is no longer whether second-life batteries will work technically, but how to optimize the logistics of collection, testing, repurposing, and deployment.

This development has significant implications for the broader battery ecosystem. It creates a new revenue stream for EV manufacturers and fleet operators, potentially reducing the total cost of ownership for electric vehicles. It also introduces a new competitor to the stationary storage market, one that can undercut new battery systems on price while offering similar performance for many applications. The emergence of second-life batteries will compress margins for new battery systems in the stationary storage segment, accelerating the pressure on generalist manufacturers.

## The Convergence of AI Computing and Energy Storage Is Creating a New Demand Category That Existing Storage Solutions Are Not Designed to Serve

The rapid growth of AI computing is not just increasing electricity demand; it is changing the nature of that demand in ways that have profound implications for energy storage. The report highlights expert warnings that China's AI computing boom is testing the limits of green power, with 24/7 renewable matching proving difficult. This is not a China-specific problem. It is a global challenge that will define the next phase of energy storage innovation.

AI data centers have unique power requirements. They need high reliability, stable voltage and frequency, and the ability to handle rapid fluctuations in computing load. They also operate continuously, meaning that the typical four-hour duration of lithium-ion storage systems is insufficient for many applications. The Flexbase project in Switzerland, which combines an 800 MW flow battery with an AI data center and district heating, points to the solution: longer-duration storage integrated with computing infrastructure.

The implications for battery manufacturers are twofold. First, the demand for longer-duration storage (8 hours or more) will grow faster than the broader storage market. This favors technologies like flow batteries, which can scale duration independently of power, and second-life batteries, which offer low-cost capacity. Second, the integration of storage with data centers creates opportunities for companies that can provide system-level solutions rather than just battery cells. The ability to manage power quality, thermal loads, and grid interaction will become a competitive differentiator.

This convergence also introduces a new set of stakeholders into the energy storage ecosystem. Cloud providers, data center operators, and AI companies are becoming direct buyers of storage solutions, and their procurement criteria differ from those of traditional utilities or project developers. They prioritize reliability, uptime, and integration over pure cost. Companies that can adapt their products and sales strategies to serve this customer base will capture disproportionate value.

## The Report's Analysis Leaves Unanswered a Critical Question: How Will the Industry Manage the Transition from First-Life to Second-Life at Scale?

While the report provides extensive coverage of second-life battery developments, it does not fully address the operational and economic challenges of scaling this model. The question is not whether second-life batteries can work, but whether the industry can build the infrastructure to support them at the volumes that will become available as EV fleets mature.

Consider the logistics. Retired EV batteries come in different form factors, chemistries, and states of health. Testing, sorting, and repurposing them requires specialized equipment and expertise. The current capacity for this work is measured in gigawatt-hours, but the volume of retired batteries will reach hundreds of gigawatt-hours within a decade. The industry needs to invest in automated testing lines, standardized repurposing protocols, and efficient collection networks. These are capital-intensive activities with uncertain returns.

There is also the question of liability. When a second-life battery fails, who is responsible? The original manufacturer, the repurposer, or the system integrator? The legal and insurance frameworks for second-life batteries are still developing, and this uncertainty may slow investment. The report notes that Moment Energy has secured over $100 million in funding, but this is a small fraction of what will be needed to build a truly scaled second-life industry.

Finally, there is the competitive dynamic between second-life and new batteries. As new battery costs continue to decline, the economic advantage of second-life systems may narrow. If LFP pack costs fall to $50 per kilowatt-hour or below, the cost savings from using retired batteries may not justify the additional complexity and risk. The industry needs to develop a clear framework for when second-life makes sense and when it does not.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Energy Storage Transition

For executives and investors trying to make sense of these developments, a structured decision framework is essential. The following questions can help assess where value will be created and captured in the next phase of the energy storage industry.

First, what is your primary source of competitive advantage? If it is manufacturing scale and cost leadership, you need to invest in automation, supply chain integration, and volume commitments. The winners in this category will be companies like CATL and the major Korean cell manufacturers that can produce at gigawatt-hour scale with consistent quality. If your advantage is technology differentiation, you need to focus on specific applications where performance matters more than price, such as solid-state batteries for aerospace or high-power systems for grid frequency regulation.

Second, which market segment are you targeting? The industry is splitting into at least four distinct segments: utility-scale storage (large, long-duration, low-cost), commercial and industrial (medium-scale, moderate duration, high reliability), residential (small-scale, integrated with solar, consumer-friendly), and data center/AI (high-reliability, long-duration, integrated with computing). Each segment has different customer requirements, competitive dynamics, and margin structures. Trying to serve all four with a single product strategy is a recipe for mediocrity.

Third, how will you manage the lifecycle of your batteries? The emergence of second-life applications means that battery manufacturers can no longer think only about first-life performance. They need to design batteries that are easy to test, repurpose, and eventually recycle. They also need to develop business models that capture value across the full lifecycle, whether through leasing arrangements, take-back programs, or partnerships with repurposers. Companies that treat end-of-life as an afterthought will leave money on the table and may face regulatory penalties.

Fourth, what is your exposure to regulatory risk? The EU Battery Regulation is setting a global standard for safety, lifecycle management, and environmental compliance. Companies that can certify their products early and build regulatory expertise will have a significant advantage in European markets. Those that lag may find themselves locked out of the fastest-growing segment of the global storage market.

## The Next Phase of the Industry Will Be Defined by Integration, Not Just Innovation

The energy storage industry has made remarkable progress in reducing costs and increasing scale. But the easy gains are behind us. The next phase will require a different set of capabilities: the ability to integrate storage with renewable generation, computing infrastructure, and grid operations; the ability to manage battery lifecycles from production through repurposing; and the ability to navigate an increasingly complex regulatory environment.

The companies that succeed will be those that recognize that storage is not a standalone product but a component of larger energy and computing systems. They will invest in software, services, and partnerships that enhance the value of their hardware. They will build business models that capture value across multiple stages of the battery lifecycle. And they will make explicit strategic choices about which markets to serve and which to leave to competitors.

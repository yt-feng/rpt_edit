# The AI Infrastructure Build-Out Is Entering a New Phase: System-Level Complexity, Not Just Chip Performance, Will Determine the Winners

The narrative that AI infrastructure is merely about faster GPUs and more power is becoming dangerously incomplete. A detailed analysis of the latest Computex revelations, drawn from a major global investment bank's booth tour, reveals a far more intricate reality: the next generation of AI data centers is being defined by a cascade of interdependent engineering choices—from backplane signal integrity and high-voltage power distribution to modular assembly cycles and the rise of co-designed, multi-rack architectures. The stakes are not merely technical; they are strategic for every company in the supply chain. The core insight is this: the era of simple scaling is over, and the winners will be those who can navigate the systemic complexity of the Rubin platform, where a single decision on a cable cartridge versus an optical switch can reshape multi-billion-dollar TAMs for power supplies, baseboard management controllers (BMCs), and cooling equipment. This is not a story of a single technology trend; it is a story of a system of systems undergoing simultaneous and often conflicting transformations.

The urgency of this analysis stems from the fact that the decisions being made today—or rather, the decisions that are *not yet made*—will define the competitive landscape for the next three years. The report points to a critical uncertainty around NVIDIA's Kyber GPU rack, a potential delay linked to fundamental signal-integrity challenges on the backplane. This is not a minor component issue; it is a structural bottleneck that forces a choice between two radically different design paths for the Rubin Ultra NVL144. This indecision creates a window of risk and opportunity for suppliers of everything from copper-clad laminates to co-packaged optics. Simultaneously, the report highlights a surge in order visibility for general servers driven by agentic AI, a trend that is already changing the demand profile for BMCs and creating a potential supply crunch. To understand the next phase of AI, one must look past the headline GPU specifications and into the engineering trade-offs that will dictate the pace and shape of the entire build-out.

## The Kyber Rack Decision Is a Pivotal Uncertainty That Cascades Through the Power and Optical Supply Chains

The most consequential strategic question to emerge from Computex is not about a new chip, but about the physical architecture of the Rubin Ultra NVL144 rack. The report identifies a potential delay or uncertainty in the mass production of NVIDIA's Kyber GPU rack, currently targeted for the second half of 2027, due to backplane signal-integrity challenges. This is a classic, high-stakes engineering dilemma. To solve it, NVIDIA is evaluating two alternative designs: a "back-to-back" configuration of two NVL72 racks interconnected via cable cartridges, or a move to NVLink co-packaged optics (CPO) switching. This is not a trivial choice. The cable-cartridge solution is likely a lower-risk, more conventional path that leverages existing copper and connector technology, but it may face its own scaling limitations. The CPO switch path is a more radical, performance-oriented solution that could set a new standard for inter-rack connectivity, but it depends on the maturity of a technology that is still being qualified.

The "so what" here is profound. The decision, which is still roughly 1.5 years away from mass production, acts as a giant swing factor for multiple industries. A decision favoring the cable-cartridge path would be a positive signal for high-speed copper cable and connector vendors, but it would delay the ramp for CPO technology. Conversely, a push toward CPO switches would accelerate the timeline for optical component suppliers and could fundamentally change the architecture of future data centers. Furthermore, this uncertainty directly interacts with the adoption of high-voltage direct current (HVDC) power. The report notes that while some industry participants have lowered their expectations for the VR200 chip's TDP, the rack-level AC/DC power supply configuration is expected to remain unchanged. However, the report's analysis suggests potential upside to HVDC adoption, moving from a prior assumption of 15% to a potential 20% in the VR200 generation. The key debate, as the report frames it, is whether a configuration change in the VR Ultra platform—likely influenced by the Kyber decision—could impact HVDC penetration. The conclusion is that customers will continue to adopt HVDC even without Kyber, but that penetration will not reach 100% due to rising costs of power components like silicon carbide (SiC). This means the Kyber decision will not just affect optical or copper suppliers; it will determine the volume and value of SiC content in power systems for years to come.

## Agentic AI Is Forcing a Structural Re-Rating of the Server and BMC Demand Curve

While the GPU rack debates capture the headlines, a quieter but equally powerful force is reshaping the demand landscape: agentic AI. The report provides a striking data point: ASPEED management is seeing unusually long order visibility into 2027, compared to a typical three-month horizon. This is attributed directly to accelerating agentic AI-related server build plans. This is not a cyclical uptick; it is a structural shift in how enterprises and hyperscalers are planning their compute infrastructure. Agentic AI, which requires more complex, multi-step reasoning and action loops, demands a richer and more distributed server ecosystem than the simple inference workloads of the past. This means more servers, more nodes, and consequently, more BMCs to manage them.

The implications for ASPEED are clear. The report outlines a path to sequential BMC revenue growth through the first half of 2027, supported by resilient server demand, an expanding total addressable market for servers, and the ramp of the AST2700 BMC, which is expected to achieve 20-30% penetration by the end of 2027. There is also potential upside from further pricing actions if supply tightness persists. This is a powerful narrative of volume and value growth. But the story goes deeper. The report connects this trend directly to NVIDIA's "Extreme Co-design" Rubin platform. The Rubin architecture is not a single rack; it is a complex ecosystem comprising Vera Rubin NVL72 racks, BlueField-4 storage racks, Vera CPU racks for orchestration, Groq LPU racks for inference, and a full suite of scale-up and scale-out switches. This modular, multi-rack system dramatically increases the number of BMCs per deployment. The report estimates 99, 53, and 75 BMCs per Vera CPU, LPU, and storage rack, respectively. This is a step-change in content per AI server, creating a powerful tailwind for ASPEED that is independent of the overall GPU shipment volume. The key open question, which the report does not fully answer, is how this co-design architecture will affect the custom server designs of major cloud service providers. While the report notes that CSPs may deploy customized designs, the broader ecosystem build-out and rising system complexity should continue to support higher BMC attach rates. This suggests that even if hyperscalers try to optimize, the baseline complexity of the Rubin architecture will force them to use more BMCs than in previous generations.

## The Shift to High-Voltage Power and SiC Is Not a Future Trend; It Is an Active, Complex Supply Chain Battle

The transition to 800V HVDC power racks is often discussed as a future trend, but the Computex evidence shows it is an active, multi-front engineering and supply chain battle. The report provides granular detail on the components driving this shift. Silicon carbide (SiC) is no longer a "maybe"; it is likely a must-have for 800V HVDC racks, appearing in AC/DC PSUs, e-Fuse modules, and 800V-to-54V DC/DC conversion stages. The report explicitly states that NVIDIA is likely qualifying multiple international SiC suppliers—Infineon, STMicroelectronics, Wolfspeed, and potentially Onsemi—to secure supply. This is a classic dual-sourcing strategy for a critical, high-cost component. The report also notes that SiC content per PSU could be multiple times that of conventional silicon-based power semiconductors. This is a massive value-add opportunity for the SiC supply chain, but it also introduces a new set of risks related to cost, yield, and supply security.

The battle is not just about SiC vs. silicon. The report also highlights the qualification of Gallium Nitride (GaN), which is still under evaluation for these high-voltage use cases and may be limited to lower-voltage conversion due to voltage constraints. This creates a clear hierarchy: SiC for the high-voltage, high-power stages, and GaN for the downstream, lower-voltage conversion. The report also introduces a new, specific component that is gaining importance: e-Fuse modules, which act as circuit breakers for 800V safety. This is a new line item in the power system BOM that will directly benefit power semiconductor and module vendors like Delta. The report estimates that data centers could contribute $400-500 million or less to the SiC device TAM by 2028. While this is a relatively small portion of the overall SiC market (which is dominated by EVs and could reach $4.0-4.5 billion in 2026), the growth rate is high. The data center contribution is expected to rise from a low single-digit percentage to a mid-to-high single-digit percentage within two to three years. This is a meaningful growth vector for SiC players who can navigate the qualification process and secure design wins.

## The Mechanical and Power Architecture of the Rack Is Becoming a Source of Competitive Advantage and TAM Expansion

The report's most granular insights come from the physical design of the rack itself, revealing that the "how" of building a rack is becoming just as important as the "what" goes inside it. A standout example is the dramatic reduction in assembly cycle time for the VR200 NVL72 compute tray. Jensen Huang noted that production time has improved to roughly five minutes per tray, down from two hours for a GB300 tray. This is a 24x improvement, driven by a more modular, cableless architecture. The use of a midplane (solely supplied by FIT) to replace PCIe cables reduces cable count, shortens assembly time, and improves yield. The modular design, featuring hot-swappable HPM modules, a midplane, and CX9/OSFP modules, simplifies both assembly and maintenance. This has direct implications for the supply chain: it implies a faster ramp for the VR200 and is broadly supportive for key ODMs like Hon Hai, Quanta, and Wistron, potentially improving their yields and reducing working capital requirements. This is a powerful example of how design-for-manufacturing can create a competitive moat.

Beyond assembly, the report reveals a significant expansion in the bill of materials for power stability. To maintain power stability and improve transient response under increasingly demanding AI workloads, capacitor content in PSUs is rising. The report states that NVIDIA will include 10x or more aluminum electrolytic capacitors in its upcoming 3RU 110kW power shelf. This is a direct boon for traditional capacitor vendors like Nippon Chemi-Con, Nichicon, and TDK. Concurrently, some hyperscalers are exploring independent supercapacitor shelves to optimize space utilization, creating a parallel opportunity for a different set of suppliers. This trend is also extending to backup power. The report highlights that current BBUs support only about 1-2 minutes of ride-through for graceful shutdown, compared to about 5 minutes for a UPS. Any move by end customers to extend this backup duration would mechanically require higher BBU capacity and content. The report notes that some customers are evaluating standalone BBU racks, which would benefit system integrators like Delta and Lite-On Technology, as well as battery pack makers like Dynapack and AES. This is a clear example of how a seemingly minor operational requirement (a few extra minutes of backup power) can translate into a significant expansion of the TAM for a specific component.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Unresolved Questions on Customization, Pricing, and the AI PC Mirage

For all its depth, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved, which is precisely why a deeper dive into the full analysis is necessary. The most significant is the tension between NVIDIA's "Extreme Co-design" reference architecture and the desire of hyperscalers for customized deployments. The report acknowledges that CSPs may deploy customized designs, but it does not quantify the extent to which this will happen. Will hyperscalers adopt the full Rubin reference architecture, or will they cherry-pick components and build their own, potentially less BMC-intensive, systems? The answer will determine the actual upside for ASPEED and other component suppliers. The report's view is that the "broader ecosystem build-out and rising system complexity should continue to be supportive of higher BMC attach rates," but this is a directional view, not a quantified one.

Another unresolved question is the pricing dynamic for the AMD Helio system. The report notes that the system uses an ORW form factor, which implies lower space efficiency, but that this could be "partially offset by lower system pricing." The key word is "partially." Will AMD's pricing be aggressive enough to win significant share from NVIDIA in the scale-out inference market, or will the space inefficiency be a fatal flaw for cost-conscious hyperscalers? The report's expectation of mass production in the first half of 2027, with potential small volumes in late 2026, suggests a slow ramp, but the ultimate market impact remains unclear. Finally, the report is notably skeptical about the immediate impact of NVIDIA's Arm-based AI PC, the RTX Spark. The report draws a direct parallel to Qualcomm's AI PC efforts in late 2024, which failed to generate sustained momentum. The core issue, as the report frames it, is the lack of a "killer application" that requires a locally-run device, combined with lingering software compatibility issues and a potentially high price point. This suggests that the AI PC hype cycle may be ahead of the actual use case, and that the rally in related stocks could fade. This is a cautionary note that investors should take seriously.

## A Decision Framework for the Next 12-18 Months: Focus on the Interdependencies, Not the Individual Components

For readers trying to navigate this complex landscape, a simple, linear view of the supply chain is insufficient. The key is to understand the interdependencies. The following framework can help assess the strategic position of any company in the AI infrastructure ecosystem.

First, identify the company's primary exposure to the three major architectural decisions: the Kyber rack architecture (cable cartridge vs. CPO), the HVDC adoption rate (15% vs. 20%+), and the modular assembly cycle time. A company whose fortunes are tied to the success of the cable-cartridge path is in a very different position than one betting on CPO. The report's data suggests that the CPO path is real and accelerating, with Wistron and Accton showcasing 102.4T CPO switches and Hon Hai expecting shipments to more than double next year. However, the Kyber decision could alter this trajectory.

Second, evaluate the company's exposure to the "content per rack" expansion. The report is clear that the Rubin architecture is a multi-rack system that drives higher attach rates for BMCs, capacitors, and power components. Companies that can capture more content per rack, such as ASPEED, Delta, and the capacitor vendors, are in a structurally stronger position than those whose revenue is tied to a simple unit volume of a single component.

Third, assess the company's position on the power semi value chain. The shift to 800V HVDC and the qualification of multiple SiC suppliers creates a bifurcated market. Established players like Infineon and STMicroelectronics are likely to secure initial design wins, but the report's mention of potential qualification for Onsemi and Wolfspeed suggests that the market is still open. The key question is whether the data center SiC TAM will grow fast enough to support multiple profitable suppliers, or if it will become a commoditized, low-margin business.

Finally, watch the "assembly time" metric. The report's highlight of the 5-minute assembly cycle for the VR200 tray is a powerful signal of NVIDIA's focus on manufacturing efficiency. Any company that can help its customers reduce assembly time and improve yield—whether through modular designs, cableless architectures, or advanced connectors—will have a significant competitive advantage. This is a non-obvious but critical success factor that the report has brought to light.

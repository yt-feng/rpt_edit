# The Token Budgeting Debate Is a Distraction: AI Consumption Is Scaling Faster Than Corporates Can Manage

The recent headlines about Uber and Walmart imposing caps on employee AI token usage might appear to signal a cooling of enterprise AI adoption. They do not. These stories, when read against the actual consumption data, tell the opposite story: corporate AI usage is accelerating so rapidly that internal governance mechanisms are scrambling to catch up. The real strategic question for investors and enterprise leaders is not whether AI demand is real, but how the inevitable shift from unfettered experimentation to structured budgeting will reshape the software landscape over the next twelve to eighteen months.

A global investment bank report tracking software sector developments highlights a paradox that is easy to miss on first reading. On one hand, major corporations are publicly announcing token budget restrictions. Uber is limiting employees to $1,500 per month per AI coding tool after its CTO revealed that the company burned through its entire 2026 AI coding budget in just four months. Walmart has capped usage on its internal AI tool amid high demand. On the other hand, data from OpenRouter, a platform that routes AI traffic across multiple large language models, shows token consumption running at an expected 34.7 trillion tokens per week as of early June 2026, up from just 2.1 trillion tokens per week in early June 2025. That is a more than 16x increase in twelve months.

The tension between these two data points is the central analytical challenge for anyone trying to understand where the enterprise software market is headed. The report suggests that these token budgeting stories are largely noise, and that rapid consumption of tokens should be read as technology validation rather than as a sign of waning interest. This interpretation is worth taking seriously, but it also raises deeper questions about how the economics of AI will evolve as enterprises move from the experimentation phase into a phase of disciplined deployment.

## Token Budgeting Headlines Reflect Governance Immaturity, Not Demand Saturation

The instinct to interpret token caps as bearish signals is understandable but analytically lazy. In the early phase of any transformative technology deployment, usage often outpaces the administrative infrastructure designed to manage it. The report notes that corporate IT budget growth is running at only about 3.7% year-over-year, and that AI spending is still largely being sourced from within those constrained budgets. When a technology is consuming budget at a rate that far exceeds planning assumptions, the natural response is to install guardrails. That is what Uber and Walmart are doing.

What matters is not that they are imposing caps, but that the caps are set at levels that still imply enormous usage. Uber's $1,500 per month per employee for AI coding tools is not a small number when multiplied across thousands of developers. The fact that the company needed to impose any cap at all is evidence that usage was significantly higher than anticipated, not that developers were finding the tools unhelpful. The report correctly frames this as a validation problem: users are consuming tokens because they find value in the tool, and the enterprise response is to manage cost, not to reduce value.

The more important structural point is that this governance immaturity creates an opening for software vendors who can help enterprises understand, predict, and optimize their token consumption. The market for AI cost management and observability tools is likely to emerge as a meaningful sub-sector over the next two years, much as cloud cost management tools became necessary once enterprises realized that unlimited cloud consumption was not financially sustainable. The companies that can provide the analytics layer for AI token spend will be well positioned, regardless of which foundation models ultimately win.

## SAP's Cloud Conversion Is Hitting an Inflection Point That Changes the Valuation Debate

While the AI token story captures headlines, the more consequential development for European software investors may be what is happening at SAP. The report uses new disclosures to unpack the company's cloud growth, and the conclusions challenge the prevailing narrative that SAP's cloud migration story is maturing. On the contrary, the data suggests that FY25 marked an inflection point, with the rate of migration tripling compared to the prior four-year run rate.

The implication is significant. SAP has a maintenance support base of approximately 10.5 billion euros that is still being converted to cloud subscriptions, and migrations typically carry a 2x or greater revenue uplift. If the conversion rate has indeed tripled, and if the opportunity is only 20-25% penetrated, then the cloud growth runway extends well beyond what current market pricing appears to reflect. The report estimates that S/4HANA cloud revenue reached roughly 8.3 billion euros in FY25 and grew approximately 50% year-over-year in constant currency, despite already running at major scale.

This matters because the bear case on SAP has centered on two arguments: that the cloud migration is approaching maturity, and that AI monetization is not yet visible enough to sustain the valuation. The report's analysis directly undermines the first argument. If the migration is only one-quarter complete, and if the conversion rate is accelerating, then the core thesis remains intact even without any contribution from AI. The second argument, about AI monetization, remains an open question, but it becomes less urgent if the base business has more room to run than previously assumed.

The valuation context is worth noting. European software stocks, as represented by the report's coverage universe, have seen their forward P/E multiples compress meaningfully from the elevated levels of 2021-2025. As of mid-2026, the group is trading at roughly 15x forward earnings, well below the historical mean of 20x. This compression creates a setup where positive surprises on growth, particularly from a large-cap name like SAP, could drive multiple expansion rather than just earnings revision.

## The Edge Computing Agent Layer Represents a Structural Shift in How AI Economics Will Work

The report's coverage of Microsoft's partnership with NVIDIA to launch RTX Spark PCs is easy to overlook as a product announcement, but it carries implications that extend far beyond the hardware. The strategic logic is clear: Microsoft is extending AI inference from the cloud toward the edge, placing agentic AI capabilities directly on Windows PCs. This is not merely a feature enhancement. It represents a fundamental shift in the architecture of enterprise AI.

The economic logic of edge inference is compelling. Cloud-based AI inference is expensive, latency-sensitive, and dependent on network connectivity. If a significant portion of AI workloads can be moved to local devices, the cost structure of enterprise AI changes dramatically. The token budgeting problem that Uber and Walmart are grappling with becomes less acute when a large share of token consumption occurs on local hardware rather than on cloud infrastructure that bills per token.

The report frames this as a step toward an agent-driven computing experience, where users interact through natural language and AI workflows rather than launching individual applications. That vision is plausible, but the nearer-term impact is likely to be on the economics of AI deployment. If enterprises can shift even 20-30% of their inference workloads to edge devices, the total cost of AI ownership declines meaningfully, and the ROI calculation becomes easier to justify. This dynamic may actually accelerate enterprise AI adoption, not slow it, because it reduces the budgetary friction that is currently generating headlines.

The open question is whether the edge AI layer will be dominated by the platform vendors, as Microsoft clearly intends, or whether it will create opportunities for specialized middleware providers. The report does not answer this question, but it is the kind of second-order implication that investors should be tracking. If edge AI becomes the primary consumption model for enterprise agents, then the competitive dynamics of the software market shift toward those who control the device layer and the agent runtime.

## Legal AI Reveals a Structural Advantage for Incumbents That General-Purpose Tools Cannot Easily Replicate

The report's expert call on legal AI provides a useful counterweight to the narrative that large language models will commoditize specialized software. The Chief Innovation Officer at a US law firm noted that while Harvey, a general-purpose AI tool, is preferred for generic use cases, Westlaw and Lexis remain the preferred platforms for complex legal research. The reason is instructive: these incumbents have built sophisticated legal infrastructure over more than a century, including citator systems that track whether cases have been followed, distinguished, or overruled.

The expert estimated that Harvey's output would be good enough or better for the same query approximately 50% of the time. That is a striking number, but the implication is not that Harvey is winning. The implication is that for the other 50% of queries, the specialized tools provide materially better answers, and that the legal profession's risk tolerance makes the specialized tools the default choice for anything that matters. The expert also expressed concern that Harvey's comparability on the easy half of queries might give attorneys a false sense of confidence about its capabilities on the hard half.

This dynamic has broader implications for the software sector. In domains where accuracy, provenance, and institutional trust matter, general-purpose AI tools face a structural disadvantage that is not easily overcome by better models alone. The incumbents have data assets, workflow integration, and credibility that cannot be replicated quickly. The report's coverage of RELX and Thomson Reuters in this context is not accidental. These companies have durable moats that AI may actually strengthen if they can effectively integrate AI capabilities into their existing platforms.

The strategic question for enterprise buyers is whether to bet on general-purpose tools that are improving rapidly or on specialized tools that are already good enough and carry lower risk. The answer will vary by use case, but the legal AI example suggests that in high-stakes domains, the incumbents will retain pricing power and customer loyalty for longer than the commoditization narrative assumes.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered Questions About How Token Budgeting Will Ultimately Reshape Enterprise IT Spending

For all its analytical depth, the report leaves several meaningful questions unresolved, and these are precisely the questions that investors should be exploring further. The first is about the unit economics of token consumption. The report notes that corporate IT budgets are growing at roughly 3.7% year-over-year, and that AI spend is largely being funded from within those budgets. If token consumption is growing at a rate that far exceeds budget growth, something has to give. Either token prices must decline, or enterprises must reallocate budget from other categories, or the growth in token consumption must moderate.

The report's data shows token consumption growing from 2.1 trillion tokens per week to 34.7 trillion tokens per week over twelve months. If that growth rate continues, the implied token consumption by mid-2027 would be staggering, and the budget implications would force difficult trade-offs. The report acknowledges that the discussion may evolve into a comparison between token spend and incremental employee hires, but it does not model what that trade-off would look like for a typical enterprise.

The second unresolved question is about the competitive dynamics of the token market itself. OpenRouter's data shows aggregate consumption, but it does not reveal which models are winning or at what prices. If token prices are declining rapidly, then the revenue implications for model providers are very different than if prices are stable. The report does not address pricing trends, and this is a critical gap for anyone trying to value the AI infrastructure layer.

The third question is about the lag between token consumption and enterprise software revenue. If enterprises are consuming tokens at a 16x growth rate, but software vendor revenue is growing at a much more modest pace, then there is a conversion problem. Either enterprises are getting enormous value for very low cost, which would be good for them but bad for vendors, or the consumption is happening on platforms that do not yet monetize effectively. The report does not resolve this tension.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the AI Consumption Paradox

For investors trying to make sense of these cross-currents, a structured decision framework is more useful than a directional bet. The framework should start with the recognition that AI token consumption is a leading indicator, not a lagging one. When token consumption accelerates, it signals that enterprises are finding real use cases and are willing to spend. The budgeting headlines are noise; the consumption data is signal.

The second layer of the framework should distinguish between infrastructure and application layer bets. The token consumption data is most directly relevant to infrastructure providers, including cloud platforms and model vendors. For application layer companies, the relevant question is whether they can capture a share of the value being created by AI usage. The legal AI example suggests that incumbents with domain-specific data and workflow integration are well positioned. The SAP example suggests that companies with large installed bases and migration tailwinds can generate growth regardless of AI monetization.

The third layer should focus on the governance gap. If enterprises are struggling to manage token consumption, then the companies that provide the tools for cost management, observability, and optimization have a clear opportunity. This is analogous to the cloud cost management market that emerged after the first wave of cloud adoption. The same pattern is likely to repeat in AI, and the companies that solve this problem will benefit regardless of which AI models or platforms ultimately win.

The fourth layer should incorporate valuation. The European software sector is trading at historically compressed multiples relative to its own history. If the AI consumption data is validated by enterprise software revenue growth over the next two to four quarters, the multiple compression may prove to have been an opportunity. If revenue growth disappoints despite high token consumption, then the thesis that AI is unmonetizable will gain credibility, and further multiple compression is possible.

The report provides the raw material for this analysis, but it does not synthesize it into a clear investment framework. That is the work that each investor must do, and the unanswered questions in the report are precisely the areas where original analysis can generate the most insight.

# The Diabesity Market Is Not a Winner-Take-All Battle: The Strategic Question Is Which Players Will Dominate Which Patient Segments

The $190 billion diabesity market is often framed as a single contest—a race for the most potent molecule, the broadest label, and the largest market share. This framing is misleading. The data emerging from this year's ADA conference, particularly the full Phase 3 readouts for Eli Lilly's Retatrutide and Pfizer's Berobenatide, reveal a more nuanced reality: the market is fragmenting along lines of efficacy, tolerability, dosing frequency, and patient phenotype. The critical strategic question is not which company will "win" obesity, but rather which companies will own specific patient segments, and how quickly the market will stratify into distinct therapeutic niches.

Three forces are driving this fragmentation. First, the efficacy bar has been raised decisively. Retatrutide's 28.3% weight loss at 80 weeks in the TRIUMPH-1 trial, with continued trajectory through 104 weeks in high-BMI patients, establishes a new ceiling. Second, tolerability profiles are diverging in ways that will shape real-world prescribing. Third, the emergence of monthly dosing options, oral small molecules, and combination-ready amylin agonists is creating new axes of competition that do not align neatly with the current GLP-1 paradigm. Investors who treat this as a simple linear race risk missing the most important strategic dynamics.

The timing of this analysis is critical. Lilly has guided for Retatrutide filing in obesity in the second half of 2026, with type 2 diabetes filing following in early 2027. Pfizer is targeting a 2028 launch for weekly Berobenatide, with monthly dosing to follow. Structure Therapeutics presented full Phase 2 data for its oral GLP-1. These timelines mean that the competitive landscape is about to undergo its most significant shift since tirzepatide's launch. Understanding the emerging segmentation now is essential for positioning.

## Retatrutide Has Established a New Efficacy Frontier, But Its Commercial Role Remains Strategically Ambiguous

The full TRIUMPH-1 and TRANSCEND-T2D-1 data represent a genuine scientific milestone. The 28.3% weight loss in the 12mg arm, with the efficacy estimand, compares favorably to tirzepatide's approximately 23% in SURMOUNT-1. The pre-specified extension showing 30.3% weight loss at 104 weeks in patients with baseline BMI of 35 or greater is unprecedented. The ADA discussant's characterization of this as "opening a new frontier" is not hyperbole.

Yet the strategic implications are not straightforward. The academic endocrinologist hosted at the conference articulated a critical tension: Retatrutide's efficacy is best-in-class, but its tolerability profile—particularly the elevated vomiting rates and the novel signals of dysesthesia and urinary tract infections—creates a risk-benefit calculus that will vary by patient. The discontinuation rates due to adverse events on the 12mg arm (11.3% in TRIUMPH-1) are higher than typical injectable obesity therapies, where rates below 10% are standard. This is not a disqualifying issue, but it shapes positioning.

The more important strategic question is where Retatrutide fits in the treatment algorithm. The KOL's view was instructive: in obesity, Retatrutide could be first-line therapy, particularly for patients with high BMI who need maximal efficacy. In type 2 diabetes, however, he saw it as unlikely to move ahead of tirzepatide, given the similar HbA1c reductions and the potential glucagon-mediated counter-regulatory effect. His payer perspective was even more pointed: he would force patients to step through oral GLP-1s or semaglutide/tirzepatide before reaching Retatrutide, which would cap utilization well below tirzepatide's levels.

This creates a strategic paradox for Lilly. Retatrutide is arguably the most impressive molecule in the pipeline, but its optimal commercial positioning may be as a premium second- or third-line option for the most refractory obesity patients, not as a broad first-line agent. The TRIUMPH-5 head-to-head data against tirzepatide, expected in the second half of 2026, will be critical for resolving this question. If Retatrutide shows meaningfully superior weight loss with acceptable tolerability in a direct comparison, the commercial calculus changes. If the advantage is marginal, the step-therapy dynamic the KOL described will likely prevail.

## The Tolerability Debate Is Shifting from Efficacy to Real-World Adherence, and This Rewrites Competitive Dynamics

A subtle but important shift occurred at this year's ADA. The conversation around Pfizer's Berobenatide was not about whether it works—the efficacy data are competitive, with the KOL describing it as "similar to Zepbound" on weight loss and HbA1c reduction. The debate was about tolerability, specifically the transition from weekly to monthly dosing and the vomiting profile.

This shift matters because it signals that the market is moving beyond the "efficacy arms race" phase. Once multiple agents can deliver 20-25% weight loss, the differentiating factors become tolerability, dosing convenience, and the ability to individualize therapy. The KOL's comment that "vomiting is the most important side effect to compare and monitor between drugs" is a strategic insight disguised as a clinical observation.

For Pfizer, the tolerability question is existential. Berobenatide's potential competitive hook is monthly dosing, which would be a genuine innovation in a market dominated by weekly injectables. But the KOL was clear: monthly dosing only works if tolerability is comparable to weekly agents. Patients will not accept a worse side effect profile for the convenience of fewer injections. The Phase 2 data raised questions on this front, and the tolerability comparison to Amgen's Maritide—another long-acting candidate with a different mechanism—will be a defining feature of the next phase of competition.

For Lilly, the tolerability question is about portfolio optimization. Retatrutide's elevated vomiting rates at higher doses, combined with the dysesthesia and UTI signals, create an opening for tirzepatide to remain the workhorse agent for the broadest patient population. The low-dose Retatrutide (4mg) data, which the CSO suggested could be a "workhorse" for lower-BMI patients, offer a potential solution, but the commercial logic of launching a drug at multiple dose levels with different risk-benefit profiles is complex.

The deeper implication is that the market is developing a tolerability hierarchy that may not align with the efficacy hierarchy. The drug that is most tolerable across the broadest patient population may capture more market share than the drug with the highest peak efficacy. This is a classic pharmaceutical market dynamic, but it is often underappreciated in the current narrative, which focuses heavily on efficacy head-to-heads.

## The Amylin Opportunity Is Real, But Its Strategic Role Depends on Combination Flexibility

The amylin data presented at ADA, particularly for Lilly's Eloralintide, represent one of the most strategically interesting developments in the pipeline. The Phase 2 monotherapy data showing approximately 12% weight loss at the 3mg dose, with a placebo-like tolerability profile and no titration requirement, is genuinely differentiated. The KOL's enthusiasm was notable: he explicitly preferred amylin as a standalone therapy that could be combined with other agents, because it allows individual titration.

This preference reveals a critical strategic insight. The future of obesity treatment is likely combinatorial, with patients receiving multiple agents targeting different pathways. In this framework, the ability to individually titrate each component is valuable. Fixed-dose combinations, while convenient, limit flexibility. Eloralintide's profile as a well-tolerated, titratable monotherapy that can be layered onto GLP-1 or triple-agonist therapy positions it as a potential "add-on" agent for patients who need additional weight loss after maximal GLP-1 therapy.

The timeline is important here. Lilly has five Phase 3 trials in progress for Eloralintide and plans to report Phase 2 combination data with tirzepatide in the second half of 2026. If those data show meaningful additive weight loss with acceptable tolerability, the strategic case for Eloralintide becomes compelling. It would allow Lilly to address the "incomplete responder" population—patients who lose significant weight on tirzepatide or Retatrutide but still have a BMI above target.

The KOL's view that amylins are not first-line therapy, given the outcomes data and safety established for GLP-1s, is a realistic assessment. But as a second-line or adjunctive agent, the amylin class could carve out a meaningful niche. The open question is whether Novo Nordisk's Amycretin, which the KOL characterized as "incremental" and "maybe better than CagriSema," can match Eloralintide's profile. The competitive dynamic in the amylin space is still early, but Eloralintide's selectivity for the amylin receptor 1 over receptor 3 appears to be a genuine advantage.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Payer and Real-World Evidence Questions

For all the depth of the clinical data presented at ADA, the report leaves several critical strategic questions unresolved. The most important is the payer perspective. The KOL offered a payer-adjacent view when he described forcing patients to step through oral GLP-1s or semaglutide/tirzepatide before reaching Retatrutide. But this is one academic's opinion, not a systematic analysis of formulary placement, rebate dynamics, or step-therapy protocols.

The reality is that payer management of the diabesity market is still evolving. The $190 billion market size projection assumes broad access and utilization, but the actual trajectory will depend on how payers manage the proliferation of options. Will they create preferred tiers based on net price, favoring the most aggressively priced agents? Will they require failure on cheaper oral options before covering injectable triple agonists? Will they limit access based on BMI thresholds or comorbidity requirements? These questions are not answerable from clinical data alone.

A second unresolved question is the real-world adherence dynamic. Clinical trial discontinuation rates, while informative, do not capture the messy reality of patient behavior outside controlled settings. The fact that 16% of placebo patients who dropped out of another obesity trial independently went on GLP-1 therapy is a telling data point: patients are actively seeking treatment, and the market is demand-driven. But adherence to chronic injectable therapy is notoriously poor, and the impact of tolerability differences on real-world persistence is not yet clear.

Third, the report does not fully address the competitive threat from oral agents. Lilly's Foundayo (oral GLP-1) is early in its launch, but the KOL's comment that he has "not heard any chatter on Foundayo DDIs being an issue" is a positive signal. If oral agents achieve meaningful efficacy with good tolerability and no food/water restrictions, they could capture a significant portion of the market, particularly in the lower-BMI and earlier-line segments. The scalability advantage of a small molecule over a peptide is real.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Mapping the Competitive Landscape Across Patient Segments

The report's data can be synthesized into a decision framework organized around patient segments rather than company narratives. This framework has three dimensions: patient phenotype, treatment line, and dosing preference.

**Segment 1: High-BMI obesity patients seeking maximal efficacy.** This is Retatrutide's natural market. The 30% weight loss at 104 weeks in patients with BMI over 35 is unmatched. The key risk is tolerability, particularly vomiting and the novel dysesthesia/UTI signals. Investors should monitor the TRIUMPH-5 head-to-head data against tirzepatide and the TRIUMPH-3 cardiovascular outcomes data to assess whether the efficacy advantage justifies the tolerability trade-off.

**Segment 2: Broad obesity population seeking a balance of efficacy and tolerability.** This is tirzepatide's fortress. The approximately 23% weight loss with well-characterized tolerability, combined with the established outcomes data, makes it the default first-line injectable for most patients. The threat from Retatrutide is real but likely contained to the highest-efficacy segment unless the head-to-head data show a dramatic advantage.

**Segment 3: Patients who prioritize dosing convenience.** This is where Pfizer's Berobenatide could compete, if monthly dosing is approved with acceptable tolerability. The KOL's skepticism about tolerability is a warning sign, but the monthly dosing hook is genuine. Investors should focus on the Phase 3 tolerability data and the comparison to Amgen's Maritide.

**Segment 4: Patients who need additive therapy after maximal GLP-1 treatment.** This is the amylin opportunity, particularly for Lilly's Eloralintide. The Phase 2 combination data with tirzepatide, expected in the second half of 2026, will be the key catalyst. If the combination shows 5-10% additional weight loss with good tolerability, the addressable market is substantial.

**Segment 5: Earlier-line and lower-BMI patients who prefer oral therapy.** This is the oral GLP-1 opportunity, currently led by Lilly's Foundayo and Structure Therapeutics' aleniglipron. The KOL's characterization of Foundayo's profile as "better than oral Wegovy" is a positive signal, but the real question is whether oral agents can achieve sufficient efficacy to compete with injectables in the higher-BMI segments.

This framework suggests that the market is not a single contest but a series of overlapping niches. The winners will be companies that can address multiple segments with differentiated products, not necessarily the company with the single best-in-class molecule.

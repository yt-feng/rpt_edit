# China: Three things in China

Three quick highlights from China:

State Council’s cross-border investment regulations: On June 1, the State Council released a 34-item decree on regulating cross-border investment. In our view, the timing likely relates to the government’s recent blocking of the Meta-Manus deal. Amid the US-China tech race and as Chinese companies expand overseas operations and acquire foreign assets, most of the 34 items appear aimed at establishing frameworks to regulate cross-border technology transfers, support Chinese companies going global, and manage the operational and geopolitical risks associated with Chinese businesses and assets abroad. Although individual cross-border investment was briefly mentioned, the decree only states that specific measures “shall be formulated by the investment authorities and commerce authorities under the State Council”. In our view, policymakers’ primary concern is to closely monitor and manage cross-border technology and capital flows in an increasingly complex geopolitical environment, rather than simply raise tax revenue or reduce capital outflow pressure. After all, exporter conversion ratios have risen, the RMB has appreciated meaningfully this year, and the PBOC’s daily fixing suggests an intention to slow the pace of RMB appreciation. That said, we do see a structural trend toward stricter enforcement of rules and regulations, including those related to tax collection and capital flows.

Signals from latest high-frequency data: We highlight two observations from our latest high-frequency data tracking. First, daily transactions in both new and existing homes have remained broadly stable. Recent policy easing remains localized (e.g., Guangzhou's buyback program) and is focused on expanding the use of housing provident funds. Second, higher energy prices continue to affect the Chinese economy: passenger flight numbers are down, flight cancellations are up, and coal consumption has risen significantly above year-ago levels. However, the net impact appears less dramatic, judging by indicators such as the daily traffic congestion index and weekly steel production and demand. Taken together, these data point to greater flexibility in the Chinese economy in adapting to higher oil prices.

EN_A: So you were at SNEC this year. What was the vibe like?
EN_B: Honestly, it was a tale of two industries. Distributed energy storage was buzzing, but the solar main value chain felt pretty subdued.
EN_A: Distributed storage was the bright spot? What makes you say that?
EN_B: Just look at the booth sizes. DESS companies had much bigger display areas this year. Meanwhile, most leading module makers downsized their booths, and some Tier 2 and 3 players didn't even show up.
EN_A: So the big question this report tries to answer is: which sector is actually recovering?
EN_B: Exactly. The core is to figure out whether the DESS demand is sustainable and where the bottom is for solar.
EN_A: Let's start with storage. Demand looks strong, but can it last?
EN_B: This time it's different. In the past, growth was often driven by a single market, like European residential storage. But in Q2 this year, orders grew 60 to 70 percent quarter over quarter, and that growth came from multiple regions: Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Eastern Europe, Australia, and Africa.
EN_A: So what does that mean? A more diversified market is healthier?
EN_B: Exactly. Management believes this multi-market driven recovery is more sustainable. And leading DESS companies are already running at full capacity. Further order upside in the second half depends on their own capacity expansion.
EN_A: But lithium carbonate prices are rising. Won't that squeeze margins?
EN_B: The report says the impact is manageable. Battery density is improving. For example, upgrading from 180Ah to 340Ah cells can cut unit production costs by about 20 percent. New products are also designed with more cost-efficient structures to absorb the battery cost inflation.
EN_A: Now, what about solar? If the booths were smaller, does that mean the industry hasn't bottomed yet?
EN_B: On the demand side, there are actually positive signals. Several module makers have raised their China solar installation estimates for 2026 from 200 gigawatts at the start of the year to 220 to 240 gigawatts. The impact from Document 136 has been milder than expected, and distributed solar economics are improving.
EN_A: Then why did they downsize their booths? That seems contradictory.
EN_B: The issue is pricing. While Tier 1 players are trying to hold module prices steady, some Tier 2 players have started aggressive price cuts again to win orders, especially overseas. Their Middle East projects are basically break-even.
EN_A: So the problem isn't demand, it's profitability?
EN_B: Right. And the pressure is worst at the upstream polysilicon segment, because they need to destock inventory, and Tier 1 producers might restart production from June, adding new supply.
EN_A: It sounds like storage and solar have completely different investment stories. Does the report have a clear preference?
EN_B: Yes. Within the solar value chain, the report prefers Cell and Module players, with a Buy on Longi. But it's a Sell on Glass and Polysilicon names like Flat Glass and Tongwei.
EN_A: Are there any big questions the report doesn't fully answer yet?
EN_B: Two key ones. First, can the DESS capacity expansion actually deliver on time? If not, order growth could be capped. Second, when will the solar price war truly end? How long will Tier 2 players keep cutting prices, and could that force an industry shakeout? The full report dives much deeper into these.

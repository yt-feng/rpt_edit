# China’s New Cross-Border Brokerage Regulation Rewrites the Earnings Trajectory for FUTU and TIGR, Forcing a Fundamental Reassessment of Growth

The May 22 announcement from the China Securities Regulatory Commission is not a mere extension of the December 2022 restrictions. It is a structural break that demands a complete revaluation of online brokers exposed to mainland Chinese clients. For the first time, the regulator has imposed a two-year rectification period with explicit penalties, forcing FUTU and TIGR to remediate non-compliant mainland accounts and absorb fines totaling RMB 1.85 billion and RMB 412 million, respectively. The market’s initial 25-28% sell-off reflects only the most visible cost. The deeper, more consequential shift lies in what this regulation means for client acquisition economics, asset growth trajectories, and the long-term viability of the cross-border brokerage model. A global investment bank report now projects 2026 net profit reductions of 25% for FUTU and 60% for TIGR relative to prior estimates, with target P/E multiples compressed to 10x and 9x, respectively. These numbers are not the end of the story; they are the starting point for a more difficult strategic question: can these firms replace lost mainland revenue without destroying their margin structure?

The timing of this regulatory action is critical. It arrives at a moment when both companies had been enjoying a post-pandemic recovery in trading volumes and client acquisition, particularly in Hong Kong and Singapore. The 2022 regulation had already closed the door to new mainland clients. The 2026 regulation now forces the door shut on existing ones, creating a two-year window during which only one-way sell transactions and fund withdrawals are permitted. After that, all services to mainland-resident clients must cease entirely. This is not a fine and forget event. It is a permanent restructuring of the addressable market.

The implications extend well beyond the immediate earnings hit. The report identifies four structural pressures that will shape the next 12-18 months: the pace of client base replacement, the rising cost of acquiring new clients outside mainland China, the decline in average AUM per client as higher-value mainland accounts are remediated, and the uncertain trajectory of revenue from accounts in transition. Each of these factors individually would warrant a valuation discount. Together, they create a compound effect that the current consensus estimates have not fully captured.

## The Regulatory Penalty Is a Known Cost, but the Revenue Loss from Remediated Accounts Is the Larger Unknown

The fines are large but finite. FUTU’s RMB 1.85 billion penalty and TIGR’s RMB 412 million penalty represent 19% and 35% of their respective 2025 net profits. These are one-time charges that, while painful, can be modeled with reasonable precision. The more analytically challenging variable is the revenue loss from the 13% of FUTU’s paying clients and 10% of TIGR’s client AUM that are now classified as non-compliant mainland accounts.

The report estimates that remediating these accounts will reduce FUTU’s net profit by an additional USD 121 million and TIGR’s by USD 5 million, on top of the penalty impact. But these estimates depend on assumptions about the AUM of non-compliant accounts, which neither company has fully disclosed. FUTU has only provided the number of accounts, not their asset values. The report uses previous company guidance to estimate the AUM, creating a sensitivity where every 5% of total AUM from non-compliant mainland accounts reduces 2026 EPS by approximately 3% for both firms.

This uncertainty is not a minor detail. It is the central analytical problem. If the actual AUM in non-compliant accounts is higher than the report’s baseline estimate, the earnings impact could be significantly worse. Conversely, if clients maintain cash balances in these accounts during the two-year transition period and those balances generate interest income, there may be a partial offset. But the report argues persuasively that such income is unlikely to be recognized as revenue given the regulatory constraints. The accounts can only sell, not buy or deposit. They generate no trading commissions. The interest income, if any, is not expected to flow through the income statement in a meaningful way.

The implication for investors is clear: the current consensus estimates for 2026 earnings are too high. The report’s own forecasts are 27% and 75% below Bloomberg consensus for FUTU and TIGR, respectively. As the market digests the full cost structure of this regulation, further downward revisions are likely.

## Replacing Lost Mainland Clients Will Require a Fundamentally Different, More Expensive Acquisition Strategy

The report reduces FUTU’s and TIGR’s client base projections by 467,000 and 128,000 clients, respectively, representing 13% and 10% of estimated first-quarter 2026 paying clients. To fill this gap, both companies must acquire new clients in non-mainland markets. This is not simply a matter of scaling up existing marketing efforts. The economics of client acquisition outside mainland China are structurally different and more challenging.

The report identifies three constraints. First, the pool of mainland clients with Hong Kong work visas is too small to drive meaningful replacement. Between 2023 and 2025, the Hong Kong government issued approximately 230,000 work visas to mainland individuals, or roughly 70,000 per year. Even if every single one of these individuals became a client of FUTU or TIGR, it would represent annual client growth of only 2.3% for FUTU and 6.1% for TIGR. This is a trickle, not a flood.

Second, growth in core markets like Hong Kong and Singapore is already showing signs of deceleration. Both companies have achieved significant market share in these regions. FUTU now serves an estimated 30% of Hong Kong adults aged 25-54, and its Singapore penetration has reached 13% of the population aged 15-64. While there is still room to migrate clients from traditional banks like HSBC and DBS, the report notes that further gains will require progressively higher acquisition costs. The low-hanging fruit has been picked.

Third, international expansion into new markets such as South Korea, Japan, Australia, and New Zealand will demand significant upfront investment. The report raises its 2026-2027 average client acquisition cost estimates by 8% for both FUTU and TIGR, reflecting increased spending on marketing, compliance, and localization. These are not optional investments. They are necessary to build the client base that will replace the lost mainland accounts. But they will compress margins in the near term.

## The Quality of New Clients Will Be Lower Than the Clients They Replace

The report makes a critical observation that is easy to overlook in the noise of headline numbers: mainland clients historically generate higher AUM per paying client than clients from other regions. Management guidance indicates that average AUM per paying client in Greater China is several times higher than in international markets. As non-compliant mainland accounts are remediated, the overall AUM per client will decline. The report lowers its AUM per client estimates by 6% for FUTU and 3% for TIGR.

This is not a temporary phenomenon. It is a structural shift in the client mix. The clients being removed are the highest-value clients. The clients being added are lower-value, at least initially. Even if the companies successfully replace the lost account numbers, the revenue per client will be lower. This dynamic will persist until the new clients grow their assets over time, which is a multi-year process.

The combination of higher acquisition costs and lower AUM per client creates a double squeeze on profitability. The companies will have to spend more to acquire clients who generate less revenue. This is the opposite of the virtuous cycle that drove their growth in the previous decade. The business model is not broken, but its unit economics have fundamentally changed.

## Why the Consensus Has Not Yet Fully Priced In the Downside

The report’s scenario analysis provides a useful framework for understanding the range of possible outcomes. Under the base case, FUTU’s target price is USD 102, implying 14% upside. Under the bear case, the target price drops to USD 77. For TIGR, the base case target of USD 4.10 implies 6% downside, while the bear case goes to USD 3.

What is striking is that even the base case valuations imply P/E multiples of 10x for FUTU and 9x for TIGR, well below the 19x and 10x multiples that the report previously assigned. This compression reflects not just the earnings impact but also the increased uncertainty around the business model. The market is being asked to price in a future where growth is slower, margins are thinner, and the regulatory environment is more hostile.

Yet the consensus estimates have not fully adjusted. The report’s 2026 net profit forecasts are 27% and 75% below consensus for FUTU and TIGR, respectively. This gap suggests that many analysts have not yet incorporated the full cost of client remediation, the higher acquisition costs, and the lower AUM per client. As companies report quarterly results and provide more granular disclosure on remediation progress, the consensus will likely move toward the report’s estimates. The question is whether the market has the patience to wait for that convergence.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Regulatory Overhang

For investors trying to form a view on FUTU and TIGR, the report suggests a structured approach based on three variables that will determine the outcome over the next 12 to 18 months.

First, track the pace and cost of remediation. The two-year rectification period creates a window of uncertainty. Companies have not announced their own remediation timelines, and management has noted that closing accounts is ultimately a client decision. The speed at which non-compliant accounts are resolved will determine how quickly the earnings impact is recognized. Faster remediation means a larger near-term hit but less uncertainty. Slower remediation spreads the pain but prolongs the overhang.

Second, monitor client acquisition costs in international markets. The report’s 8% upward revision to CAC estimates is a starting point, not a ceiling. If FUTU’s entry into South Korea or TIGR’s push into Australia requires more spending than currently modeled, the margin compression will be worse. Conversely, if these companies can replicate the product-led growth they achieved in Hong Kong and Singapore, the CAC estimates may prove conservative.

Third, watch the trajectory of AUM per client. The report’s assumption of a 6% decline for FUTU and 3% for TIGR is based on the current client mix. If the new clients acquired in international markets prove to be higher-quality than expected, or if existing clients in Hong Kong and Singapore increase their asset allocation, the AUM per client could recover faster than modeled. This is the most optimistic scenario, but it requires execution that has not yet been demonstrated.

The report also leaves open a question that it does not fully answer: what happens after the two-year rectification period ends? The regulation states that overseas institutions must completely shut down domestic websites, trading software, and supporting servers. But it is not clear whether this applies to all operations or only those serving mainland-resident clients. The distinction matters because FUTU and TIGR have legitimate businesses in Hong Kong and Singapore that serve local clients. If the regulation is interpreted broadly, it could create additional operational complexity. If it is interpreted narrowly, the impact may be contained to the remediation period.

## The Strategic Crossroads for Cross-Border Brokerage Models

The regulation that triggered this revaluation is not an isolated event. It is part of a broader pattern of Chinese financial regulators asserting control over cross-border capital flows and the platforms that facilitate them. The December 2022 regulation stopped new client acquisition. The May 2026 regulation addresses the existing stock. The logical next step would be to tighten the definition of what constitutes a legitimate client, potentially restricting even clients with Hong Kong visas or other work permits.

This regulatory trajectory raises a fundamental question that goes beyond the current earnings impact: can a cross-border brokerage model that depends on mainland Chinese clients for a meaningful portion of its revenue and profitability survive in its current form? The answer is not obvious. Both FUTU and TIGR have strong franchises in Hong Kong and Singapore, and they are building positions in other Asian markets. But the mainland Chinese client base has been a source of high-margin, high-AUM revenue that will be difficult to replace.

The report does not answer this question definitively, and it should not. The evidence is still being gathered. What the report does provide is a rigorous framework for thinking about the trade-offs. The fines are known. The remediation costs are estimable. The client acquisition challenges are identifiable. What remains unknown is whether the companies can execute a pivot to international markets with the same efficiency they showed in their home market.

That is the question that will determine whether today’s depressed valuations are a buying opportunity or a value trap. The next 12 months will provide the answer.

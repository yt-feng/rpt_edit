# Engineering Services Demand Is Resilient, But the Market Is Rewarding Execution Over Exposure

The first quarter of 2026 delivered a paradox for the civil engineering services sector. Customer demand held up broadly, order backlogs grew, and most companies beat quarterly revenue and EBITDA expectations. Yet the share price reactions on results day ranged from an 18% gain for Arcadis to a 12% decline for AECOM, with several other firms posting negative moves despite beating consensus. This divergence tells a story that goes beyond quarterly performance. The market is no longer rewarding firms simply for being in the right end-markets. It is rewarding those that can convert demand into margin expansion and penalizing those that cannot, regardless of the strength of their pipeline.

The underlying data from a global investment bank report covering the sector reveals a critical inflection point. Organic sales growth for the peer group averaged 2.8% year-on-year, broadly in line with recent quarters. Order intake grew, and backlogs reached record levels at several firms. But the correlation between these top-line metrics and share price performance has broken down. The reason is that investors are now focused on conversion efficiency, cost discipline, and the ability to extract value from a pipeline that, while healthy, is no longer accelerating. For firms like Arcadis, which the report covers with an Outperform rating, the challenge is not demand. It is operational execution.

This article unpacks the key themes from the quarter, examines why beats on estimates rarely translated into estimate revisions, and provides a decision framework for investors trying to navigate a sector where the easy growth is behind us. The central argument is this: the engineering services sector is entering a phase where strategic positioning matters less than operational leverage, and the winners will be those that can improve margins while maintaining revenue growth, not those that simply ride tailwinds.

## Customer Demand Remained Resilient, But The Composition Of Growth Shifted Toward A Narrower Set Of Sectors

The headline numbers for the quarter ending March 2026 were reassuring. Organic revenue growth across the peer group averaged 2.8%, with several firms reporting beats on both revenue and EBITDA. Arcadis itself posted organic sales growth of 0.8%, a sequential improvement from the negative growth in the prior quarter. More importantly, order intake and backlog growth remained positive, suggesting that the demand environment has not deteriorated despite the operational disruption caused by the conflict in the Middle East that began in late February.

However, the composition of that growth reveals a more nuanced picture. The strongest demand was concentrated in three sectors: data centers, life sciences, and nuclear. These are not new themes, but their acceleration in the quarter was notable. Jacobs, for instance, reported that AI-related activity now represents approximately 10% of group revenue, with data centers contributing 3-4% and growing at over 40% year-on-year. The AI data center pipeline increased 400% year-on-year, with visibility extending into fiscal 2027 and 2028. WSP Global similarly cited data center activity as a key driver, alongside energy transition and climate resilience work.

The implication is clear: the demand that exists is increasingly specialized and project-specific. Firms with exposure to residential construction, commercial buildings, or traditional industrial end-markets are seeing stagnation or decline. Sweco, for example, reported weakness in residential and commercial buildings, along with parts of the pharmaceutical and chemicals sectors. Tetra Tech saw US Commercial revenue fall 2% year-on-year due to weakness in renewables. The broad-based demand recovery that some had hoped for is not materializing. Instead, growth is being driven by a handful of secular trends that favor firms with specific technical capabilities in areas like data center design, nuclear engineering, and water infrastructure.

This concentration of demand creates a strategic challenge. Firms that are well-positioned in these high-growth segments can sustain above-market growth, but they also face capacity constraints and pricing pressure as competitors pile in. Firms that are not well-positioned face the risk of stagnation, regardless of their overall backlog. The market is beginning to price this divergence, which explains why the share price reactions were so mixed. Investors are not just asking whether demand is growing. They are asking whether each firm has the right mix of end-market exposure to sustain that growth.

## Beats On Quarterly Estimates Rarely Translated Into Full-Year Estimate Revisions, Signaling A Market That Has Already Priced In The Upside

One of the most telling findings from the quarter is that even when companies beat consensus on revenue and EBITDA, full-year consensus estimates moved only marginally, and in some cases declined. Exhibit 7 in the report shows this clearly. Tetra Tech beat quarterly sales by 10% and EBITDA by 7%, yet full-year sales estimates were revised down by 2%. AECOM met sales expectations and beat EBITDA by 3%, but full-year estimates fell by 4%. Sweco missed on both revenue and EBITDA, and its full-year estimates were revised down by 2% on sales.

This pattern suggests that the market has already built in a relatively optimistic view of the sector's growth trajectory. The beats that occurred were not large enough to change the narrative, and in some cases, they were offset by concerns about the second half of the year. The report notes that the main movements in full-year estimates were relatively small, and that the positive quarterly results "rarely translated into significant upward revisions."

Why does this matter? Because it indicates that the sector is trading on expectations that are already elevated. Any incremental positive news is unlikely to drive material re-ratings, while negative surprises could trigger significant de-ratings. This is a classic late-cycle dynamic. The easy money has been made by those who positioned early in the data center and energy transition themes. Now, the burden of proof is on management teams to deliver on those expectations, not just in terms of revenue growth, but in terms of margin expansion and free cash flow generation.

The report's analysis of Arcadis reinforces this point. Despite the sequential improvement in organic sales growth, the report's investment thesis is cautious on revenue growth, forecasting only 3.5% growth over 2027-2036 with a peak at 4.3% in fiscal 2029. The challenge for Arcadis is not demand, but operational performance: converting leads into signed contracts, accelerating backlog conversion, and increasing consultant utilization rates. The market appears to agree, which is why the share price reaction on results day was positive, but the long-term outlook remains constrained.

For investors, the key question is whether the current valuation of the sector already reflects the best-case scenario. If it does, then the risk-reward is skewed to the downside, and the focus should shift to firms that have the most room for operational improvement, not those with the strongest top-line growth.

## M&A Activity Accelerated, But The Strategic Logic Varies Widely, And Integration Risk Is Rising

M&A was a dominant theme in the quarter, with most firms in the peer group announcing or completing acquisitions. WSP Global was the most active, finalizing the acquisition of TRC Companies for $3.2 billion and maintaining M&A as its top capital allocation priority. Jacobs consolidated PA Consulting for $1.7 billion. Stantec benefited from the earlier acquisition of Page, which contributed significantly to revenue and backlog growth. AtkinsRéalis, Sweco, and Tetra Tech all made bolt-on acquisitions, while AECOM entered the Norwegian market with the acquisition of Consigli AS.

The pattern is clear: firms are using M&A to gain exposure to high-growth end-markets, acquire technical capabilities, and expand geographically. But the strategic logic varies widely, and so does the risk. WSP's acquisition of TRC gives it a strong position in the US power and energy market, which is benefiting from electrification and data center demand. Jacobs' acquisition of PA Consulting adds consulting and advisory capabilities that complement its engineering services. Stantec's acquisition of Page strengthens its position in the US, but integration costs weighed on free cash flow in the quarter.

For investors, the critical question is whether these acquisitions will generate returns above the cost of capital. The report notes that Arcadis has remained behind its peers on M&A, focusing instead on its operational turnaround. This is a deliberate strategic choice, and it may prove to be the right one if the integration risks for the more acquisitive peers prove to be higher than expected. The track record of large-scale M&A in professional services is mixed, and the current environment of rising interest rates and economic uncertainty increases the execution risk.

The report does not provide a definitive answer on which firms will succeed or fail in their M&A strategies, but it raises an important analytical question: in a sector where organic growth is slowing and valuations are elevated, is M&A a source of value creation or a sign that management teams are running out of organic options? The answer likely varies by firm, but investors should be skeptical of acquisitions that are justified primarily by cost synergies or that involve significant cultural integration challenges. The firms that will create the most value are those that use M&A to acquire scarce technical talent and capabilities that are in high demand, not those that use it to buy revenue growth.

## The Report Raises An Unanswered Question: Can The Sector Sustain Its Current Growth Trajectory Without A Broad-Based Economic Recovery?

The report provides a detailed and valuable analysis of the quarter, but it leaves one critical question unresolved. The demand growth that exists is concentrated in a narrow set of end-markets: data centers, life sciences, nuclear, and water infrastructure. These are driven by secular trends that are unlikely to reverse in the near term. But they are also capital-intensive, long-cycle projects that require significant upfront investment from clients. If the broader economic environment deteriorates, or if interest rates remain elevated, there is a risk that some of these projects are delayed or scaled back.

The report notes that the peer group's backlog continues to grow, which provides visibility into future revenue. But backlog is not the same as revenue. It can be delayed, cancelled, or renegotiated. The report also notes that several firms highlighted "significant uncertainty surrounding growth due to external factors beyond their control." This is not a new concern, but it is one that the report does not fully address.

The unanswered question is whether the secular demand from data centers and energy transition is sufficient to offset a potential slowdown in other segments, or whether the sector is vulnerable to a broader economic downturn. The answer has significant implications for valuation. If the secular trends are strong enough to sustain growth regardless of the macro environment, then the current valuations may be justified. If not, then the sector is priced for perfection, and the downside risk is substantial.

Investors should watch for leading indicators of project delays, such as changes in client capital expenditure plans, rising financing costs for large infrastructure projects, or a slowdown in data center construction starts. These would be early warning signs that the backlog is not as resilient as it appears.

## A Decision Framework For Investors: Focus On Conversion Efficiency, Not Just Revenue Growth

Given the dynamics described above, investors need a framework for evaluating engineering services firms that goes beyond top-line growth and backlog trends. The following decision framework is derived from the patterns observed in the quarter and is designed to help identify which firms are best positioned for the current environment.

First, assess conversion efficiency. The most important metric is not organic sales growth, but the ratio of backlog to revenue and the speed at which backlog is converted into recognized revenue. Firms that can accelerate backlog conversion without sacrificing margins are the ones that will generate the strongest earnings growth. The report notes that Arcadis's challenge is to "accelerate the conversion of backlog into revenue." This is the right focus, and it should be the primary lens through which investors evaluate all firms in the sector.

Second, evaluate margin trajectory. Revenue growth that comes at the expense of margins is not value-creating. The firms that beat estimates in the quarter generally did so because of margin expansion, not just revenue beats. Jacobs, for example, saw adjusted EBITDA margins expand 70 basis points to 14.1%. WSP saw margins expand 80 basis points to 16.8%. These are the firms that are demonstrating operational leverage. Firms that are growing revenue but seeing margins compress are destroying value, even if their top-line numbers look good.

Third, assess end-market exposure, but with a critical eye. Exposure to data centers and nuclear is clearly positive in the current environment, but investors should ask whether that exposure is already priced in. The report shows that Jacobs and AtkinsRéalis have the highest organic sales growth rates, and they also have the strongest exposure to these high-growth segments. But their valuations likely reflect that. The question is whether there is upside to estimates that the market has not yet priced.

Fourth, evaluate M&A discipline. The report shows that M&A activity is accelerating, but not all M&A is created equal. Investors should favor firms that are making small, bolt-on acquisitions that add specific technical capabilities, over firms that are making large, transformational acquisitions that carry significant integration risk. The firms that are most disciplined on M&A are likely to generate the highest returns on invested capital over time.

Finally, monitor estimate revision trends. The fact that beats on quarterly estimates rarely led to full-year estimate revisions is a warning sign. It suggests that the market is already positioned for the good news. Investors should look for firms where there is room for estimates to move higher, either because the firm is under-earning relative to its potential or because the market has not fully appreciated the strength of its end-market exposure.

# The EM Credit Curve Is Flattening — But Not Because Markets Are Confident

The aggregate EM USD 10s30s spread curve has compressed by 7 basis points over the past year, from roughly 75 to 68. Over the same period, the UST 10s30s yield curve has moved in the opposite direction, steepening by roughly 10 basis points. This divergence is not a statistical anomaly. It is a signal that the EM credit market is pricing a fundamentally different set of assumptions about duration, credit risk, and the economic cycle than the US Treasury market.

The conventional explanation for curve flattening in credit is that investors expect improving fundamentals and lower uncertainty. But the data tells a more nuanced story. The EM aggregate 10s30s spread curve now sits near the bottom of its one-year range of 58 to 79. At the same time, absolute spread levels on the 10-year point have compressed only modestly. The EM aggregate 10-year spread is currently 178 basis points, down from roughly 185 a year ago. This means the flattening is not being driven by a compression of long-end spreads relative to short-end spreads in a straightforward bull-steepening scenario. Instead, the long end is being repriced relative to the belly in a way that suggests investors are demanding less term premium for extending duration in EM credit, even as they remain cautious on the absolute level of risk.

The critical question for portfolio managers is whether this flattening reflects a genuine improvement in EM credit quality or a crowding into long-duration positions driven by yield-seeking behavior in a world where US Treasury yields have become more volatile. The answer has direct implications for whether the current curve slope is sustainable or vulnerable to a sharp reversal.

## The EM Aggregate Curve Is Flattening While HY Curves Are Steepening — A Telling Divergence

The most instructive pattern in the current data is the divergence between investment-grade and high-yield curve behavior. The EM IG 10s30s spread curve has flattened by 11 basis points over the past year, from 72 to 61. In contrast, the EM HY 10s30s curve has steepened by 2 basis points over the same period, moving from 81 to 83. This is not a trivial difference. It means that the aggregate flattening is entirely concentrated in the IG segment, while HY curves have actually become steeper.

This divergence matters because it reveals the underlying driver. IG flattening in EM is consistent with a "reach for yield" narrative in which institutional investors, constrained by liability-driven mandates and low absolute yields in developed markets, extend duration in higher-quality EM names to capture incremental carry. This is a demand-driven phenomenon, not a fundamental improvement in the credit outlook for EM broadly. If the flattening were driven by improving fundamentals, we would expect to see it across both IG and HY, with HY flattening more aggressively as credit risk recedes. Instead, HY curves are steepening, which is the typical pattern when investors are demanding more compensation for extending duration in lower-quality names.

The regional data reinforces this interpretation. EMBIG Asia, the highest-quality regional sub-index by spread level, has seen the most aggressive flattening, with the 10s30s curve compressing by 24 basis points over the past year to 35. This is the flattest curve in the EM complex. Meanwhile, EMBIG CEEMEA, which includes several higher-spread sovereigns, has steepened by 10 basis points to 81. The pattern is clear: the higher the credit quality, the more aggressive the flattening. This is not a broad-based credit improvement story. It is a story of capital flowing into the safest EM duration.

## The Slope-Spread Relationship Inverts Across Regions — A Structural Shift or a Tactical Anomaly?

One of the most revealing charts in the report is the relationship between the 10s30s spread curve slope and the 10-year spread level, broken down by region. In Asia, the relationship is positive: as 10-year spreads increase, the curve slope also increases. In CEEMEA and Latin America, the relationship is negative: higher spreads are associated with flatter curves.

This inversion of the slope-spread relationship across regions is not widely discussed, but it has significant implications for relative value analysis. In a textbook credit model, higher spread levels should be associated with steeper curves because investors demand more term premium to hold longer-duration bonds from riskier issuers. Asia conforms to this textbook pattern. CEEMEA and Latin America do not.

What explains the negative relationship in CEEMEA and Latin America? One possibility is that these regions contain a higher proportion of quasi-sovereign and state-owned enterprise issuers whose curves are influenced by the sovereign's yield curve dynamics rather than by idiosyncratic credit risk. Another possibility is that the negative relationship reflects a "crowding out" effect in which the most distressed issuers in these regions have very flat curves because their long-dated bonds trade at distressed levels that compress spreads mechanically. But the data does not allow us to distinguish between these hypotheses with certainty.

For investors, this means that a simple rule of thumb — buy steepeners in high-spread names and flatteners in low-spread names — may not work uniformly across EM regions. The relationship between slope and spread level is region-specific, and applying a global heuristic could lead to significant curve positioning errors.

## The Corporate-Sovereign Curve Gap Is Widening — And It May Signal a Regime Change

Another pattern that deserves more attention than it typically receives is the growing gap between sovereign and corporate curve slopes. The EMBIG sovereign 10s30s curve is currently 72, while the CEMBI corporate curve is 55. This 17-basis-point gap is near the wide end of its historical range. One year ago, the gap was roughly 10 basis points. Two years ago, it was roughly 5 basis points.

The widening gap suggests that corporate credit curves are flattening faster than sovereign curves. This could reflect a compositional effect: the corporate index has a higher proportion of investment-grade issuers than the sovereign index, and as we have already established, IG curves are flattening faster than HY curves. But it could also reflect a more structural shift in the way investors price duration risk in corporate versus sovereign EM debt.

If the gap continues to widen, it may create opportunities for relative value trades that exploit the mispricing between sovereign and corporate curves. For example, an investor who believes the gap is too wide could buy sovereign steepeners and corporate flatteners, betting on convergence. But the report does not provide enough data to assess whether this gap is mean-reverting or trending. This is one of the open questions that the full report may address.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Role of UST Curve Dynamics and Carry

The report documents the EM curve data comprehensively, but it does not fully disentangle the influence of UST yield curve dynamics on EM spread curves. The UST 10s30s yield curve slope has steepened from roughly 30 basis points one year ago to 51 basis points currently. All else equal, a steeper UST curve should pull EM spread curves steeper as well, because the risk-free rate component of EM bond yields becomes more back-loaded. Yet EM spread curves have flattened.

This suggests that EM credit spreads are compressing on a duration-adjusted basis even more aggressively than the raw curve data shows. If we strip out the UST curve effect, the "pure credit" component of the EM 10s30s spread curve may have flattened by significantly more than 7 basis points over the past year. The report does not provide this decomposition, but it would be a valuable extension for investors who want to understand whether the flattening is driven by credit fundamentals or by UST curve dynamics.

Another unresolved question is the role of carry. The report shows that the EM aggregate 10s30s spread curve is 68 basis points, but it does not tell us the carry advantage of owning the 30-year relative to the 10-year. In a world where carry is a primary driver of total return, the carry-adjusted slope may be more relevant than the raw spread slope. An investor who is long the 30-year versus the 10-year earns the spread differential plus the rolldown. If the carry is attractive enough, it can justify holding a flattening position even if the curve is expected to flatten further.

## A Decision Framework for Curve Positioning in EM Credit

For portfolio managers who need to translate this analysis into actionable positioning, the following framework may be useful. It is not a recommendation but a structured way to think about curve trades given the current data.

First, assess the credit quality of the specific issuer or index you are trading. The data shows that IG curves are flattening and HY curves are steepening. If you are trading an IG name, the dominant trend is flattening, and fighting that trend requires a strong conviction that the flattening has gone too far. If you are trading an HY name, the dominant trend is steepening, and flatteners are contrarian.

Second, consider the regional slope-spread relationship. In Asia, higher spreads are associated with steeper curves, so flatteners work best when spreads are low. In CEEMEA and Latin America, higher spreads are associated with flatter curves, so steepeners work best when spreads are high. This is counterintuitive but supported by the data.

Third, monitor the corporate-sovereign curve gap. If the gap continues to widen, relative value trades between sovereign and corporate curves may become attractive. But the direction of the trade depends on whether you believe the gap is driven by fundamentals or by technicals. If it is fundamentals, the gap may persist. If it is technicals, it may reverse.

Fourth, decompose the UST curve effect. If you believe the UST curve will continue to steepen, then EM spread curves should steepen mechanically, and flatteners are a bet against the UST curve. If you believe the UST curve will flatten, then EM flatteners have a tailwind.

Finally, consider the carry. The raw spread slope is 68 basis points, but the carry-adjusted slope may be higher or lower depending on the rolldown. If the carry is strong enough, it can absorb a modest amount of adverse curve movement.

## The Open Questions That Deserve a Deeper Look

The report raises several questions that it does not fully answer, and these are precisely the questions that make the full report worth reading.

Why is the EM aggregate 10s30s spread curve near the bottom of its one-year range while the UST 10s30s yield curve is near the top of its range? If both were driven by the same macro factors, they should move together. The divergence suggests that EM credit investors are behaving differently than UST investors, but the report does not fully explain why.

Is the flattening in IG and steepening in HY a sustainable regime or a tactical anomaly? If it is sustainable, then IG flatteners and HY steepeners are the right trades. If it is tactical, then the current positioning may be crowded and vulnerable to a reversal.

Why does the slope-spread relationship invert across regions? Is this a structural feature of EM credit markets, or is it a temporary artifact of the current cycle? The answer has significant implications for regional allocation.

What is the carry-adjusted slope, and how does it compare to history? The report provides the raw spread slope but not the carry, which is a critical input for any curve trade.

These questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are the natural result of a data-rich analysis that focuses on what can be measured rather than what can be speculated. For investors who want to go deeper, the full report provides the historical data and methodology needed to answer these questions.

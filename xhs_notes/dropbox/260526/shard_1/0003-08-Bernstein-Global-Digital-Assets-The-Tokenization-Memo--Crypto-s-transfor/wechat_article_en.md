# Tokenization Is No Longer a Crypto Thesis — It Is the Infrastructure Layer for Institutional Finance

The tokenization of real-world assets has crossed $51 billion in market capitalization, growing 42% year-to-date while the broader crypto market has declined roughly 15%. This divergence is not a statistical anomaly. It is a signal that a structural shift has begun — one in which blockchain infrastructure is being adopted not by retail speculators but by the most conservative institutions in global finance.

The conventional framing of tokenization as a "crypto use case" is now misleading. What we are witnessing is the quiet construction of a parallel financial plumbing system. Private credit, U.S. Treasuries, commodities, and equities are being digitized, fractionalized, and traded on distributed ledgers. The asset managers, transfer agents, exchanges, and clearinghouses that have dominated post-trade infrastructure for decades are not fighting this trend. They are building it.

This article argues that tokenization has moved beyond proof-of-concept and into early-stage production. The strategic question for institutional investors is no longer whether tokenization will matter. It is which asset classes will migrate first, which platforms will capture the economics, and where the regulatory and operational risks remain unaddressed.

The report from a global investment bank that underpins this analysis provides the data and industry developments necessary to assess these questions. But the implications extend well beyond the numbers. The following sections unpack the key trends, expose the gaps in current thinking, and offer a framework for decision-making.

## Private Credit Has Become the Anchor Asset Class for Tokenization, but the Real Story Is Infrastructure, Not Volume

Private credit accounts for approximately 44% of the total tokenized RWA market cap, making it the dominant category by a wide margin. At first glance, this seems intuitive. Private credit markets are large, illiquid, and inefficient — precisely the conditions where blockchain-based tokenization can add value through fractionalization, faster settlement, and programmatic compliance.

But the dominance of private credit is not simply a function of market demand. It is the result of one company's infrastructure bet. Figure, the fintech and blockchain platform, has tokenized $5 billion in consumer loans year-to-date as of April 2026, with an all-time high monthly volume of $1.3 billion. Figure's blockchain marketplace for credit, known as Connect, contributed 56% of total loan volumes in the first quarter of 2026. The company has built a network of over 380 loan originators and 15 blue-chip institutional private credit investors.

The strategic insight here is not that Figure is growing fast. It is that Figure has solved a structural problem that has plagued private credit markets for years: the inability to efficiently match originators with capital providers across a fragmented, bilateral market. By creating a blockchain-based marketplace that records ownership, automates payments, and enables real-time settlement, Figure has effectively built a new primary issuance and secondary trading infrastructure for consumer credit.

The "Democratized Prime" offering — a blockchain alternative to traditional warehouse lines that allows originators to access DeFi and institutional capital pools at rates like SOFR plus 200 basis points — is particularly telling. It represents the first credible bridge between decentralized finance liquidity and regulated credit origination. If this model scales, it could fundamentally alter the economics of consumer lending.

What remains unanswered is whether Figure's model is replicable across other credit verticals — commercial real estate, corporate loans, trade finance — or whether it is uniquely suited to the standardized, high-volume nature of consumer loans. The report notes that non-HELOC loans are expected to contribute roughly 20 percent of Figure's total loan volumes by 2027. That target will test the platform's flexibility.

## Equity Tokenization Is Moving through the Back Door, and the SEC's Silence May Be the Green Light

Tokenized equities remain a small category — roughly $1.5 billion outstanding and $3 billion in monthly transfer volumes. But the infrastructure buildout happening beneath the surface suggests this figure could scale rapidly in the next 12 to 24 months.

The critical regulatory development is not a new rule or exemption. It is the SEC's continued delay of the "innovation exemption" that would have established a clear framework for third-party issued digital stock tokens. On its face, this delay appears to be a setback. In practice, it has forced the industry to adopt an alternative model that may prove more durable: the issuer-led model.

Under the issuer-led approach, tokenized equities are not synthetic derivatives or IOUs. They are actual equity in the company, with tokenholders registered as shareholders with the company's transfer agent. This means tokenholders are entitled to voting rights, dividends, and corporate actions. The model fits within existing SEC rules, which means it does not require new legislation or regulatory relief to operate.

The industry moves that follow from this are significant. Bullish has entered into a definitive agreement to acquire Equiniti, a global transfer agent serving approximately 3,000 issuer clients. This acquisition allows Bullish to operate a unified ledger that records ownership for both traditional and tokenized shares. The target client base is already there: 3,000 issuers who could be offered tokenization as an additional service.

The DTCC is collaborating with more than 50 financial services firms — including Citi, JPM, Charles Schwab, and the Canton Network — to launch tokenization services for DTCC-custodied assets, with a target launch date of October 2026. The NYSE has signed a memorandum of understanding with Securitize to develop digital transfer agent infrastructure for its upcoming tokenized securities platform. Figure has listed OpenWorld's securities on the OPEN network, marking the second equity issuance on that platform.

What is striking about these developments is that they are not being led by crypto-native firms alone. The DTCC, NYSE, and major banks are actively participating. This is not disruption from the outside. It is evolution from within.

The open question is whether the issuer-led model can achieve sufficient scale to matter. Tokenized equities today represent a fraction of the $100 trillion-plus global equity market. For the model to become relevant, it needs to attract not just small issuers but large-cap companies. The report does not address whether any of the 3,000 Equiniti clients are Fortune 500 firms, or whether the DTCC's initiative will initially focus on less liquid securities. These are the details that will determine whether equity tokenization remains a niche or becomes a mainstream channel.

## Hyperliquid's RWA Derivatives Growth Shows Where Demand Is Real — and Where It Is Speculative

One of the most surprising data points in the report is the rapid growth of real-world asset derivatives on Hyperliquid. The platform's HIP-3 upgrade, which allows developers to list perpetual futures referencing equities, commodities, and currencies, has scaled to represent approximately 35 percent of total Hyperliquid perpetuals volume in less than eight months since launch. In April 2026, RWA derivatives contributed $65 billion in trading volume, led by commodities and indices.

This is a genuinely new phenomenon. Onchain derivatives markets have historically been dominated by crypto-native assets like Bitcoin and Ethereum. The fact that traders are now using blockchain-based perpetuals to gain exposure to gold, oil, and equity indices suggests that the demand for onchain access to traditional assets is real and growing.

But a note of caution is warranted. Perpetual futures are synthetic instruments. They do not involve the ownership or transfer of the underlying asset. The growth in Hyperliquid's RWA derivatives volume does not necessarily translate into growth in the tokenization of those same assets. It is possible that traders are using these instruments simply because they offer leverage, 24/7 trading, and lower barriers to entry — not because they value the onchain infrastructure itself.

The report does not address the extent to which Hyperliquid's RWA derivatives are backed by actual tokenized assets or whether they are purely cash-settled synthetic positions. This distinction matters. If the derivatives are predominantly speculative, the volume growth may not be a reliable signal of institutional adoption. If, however, the derivatives are increasingly used for hedging by institutions that hold the underlying tokenized assets, then the growth is more structurally significant.

## Asset Managers Are Entering through the Treasury Door, and the Implications for Fund Distribution Are Profound

Tokenized money market funds and Treasury products have become the entry point for blue-chip asset managers to launch onchain funds. BlackRock's BUIDL fund has grown to over $2.5 billion in assets under management, and the firm is planning to launch two additional tokenized funds on blockchain. Standard Chartered and OKX now allow clients to post BUIDL as trading margin without forfeiting yield, effectively integrating tokenized collateral with trading markets.

The operational advantages are clear. Tokenization enables fund managers to offer instant subscriptions and redemptions, atomic settlement, and programmable yield distribution. This replaces the traditional T+1 or T+2 NAV cycle with real-time onchain execution. For institutional investors who manage cash positions across multiple counterparties, the ability to move in and out of money market funds instantly — and to use those fund shares as collateral — represents a meaningful efficiency gain.

But the strategic implications go deeper. Once a fund is tokenized, it can be distributed through any blockchain-based platform without the need for additional intermediaries. This could fundamentally alter the economics of fund distribution. Asset managers that tokenize their funds gain direct access to a global network of wallets, DeFi protocols, and trading venues. The traditional gatekeepers — custodians, transfer agents, and distribution platforms — may find their role reduced.

The report does not address whether BlackRock's new tokenized funds will be available to retail investors or restricted to institutional and qualified purchasers. It also does not specify the blockchains on which these funds will be issued. These details will determine the pace and breadth of adoption. If the funds are limited to permissioned networks and accredited investors, the impact will be incremental. If they are issued on public blockchains and accessible to a broad investor base, the impact could be transformative.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Fragmentation Risk and the Liquidity Trap

The report provides a comprehensive dashboard of tokenization activity, but it leaves several critical questions unresolved.

First, the fragmentation of tokenization platforms is a growing concern. Ethereum and Provenance host approximately 75 percent of onchain RWA activity, but there are at least a dozen other platforms with meaningful market share. Each platform has its own standards, smart contract languages, and governance models. For institutional investors, this fragmentation creates operational complexity. If a tokenized Treasury on Ethereum cannot be used as collateral on a Provenance-based lending protocol, the network effects that drive adoption are weakened.

Second, the liquidity of tokenized assets remains untested under stress. The report shows growing volumes and asset holder counts, but it does not provide data on bid-ask spreads, depth of order books, or the time required to execute large trades. Tokenized private credit, in particular, may face significant liquidity challenges. If the secondary market for these assets is thin, the promise of "instant settlement" may be less valuable than it appears.

Third, the regulatory framework remains incomplete. The SEC's delay on the innovation exemption for tokenized stocks is a reminder that the regulatory environment is uncertain. The report does not address the potential for regulatory reversals in other jurisdictions, nor does it analyze the implications of the U.S. midterm elections for digital asset policy.

These gaps are not criticisms of the report. They are the natural boundaries of any analysis that attempts to forecast the trajectory of a rapidly evolving market. But they are the questions that serious investors must answer before committing capital.

## A Decision Framework for Institutional Investors

For readers who are evaluating whether and how to engage with tokenization, the following framework may be useful. It is derived from the patterns identified in the report and from the strategic logic that underlies them.

**First, distinguish between infrastructure plays and asset-class plays.** Figure, Securitize, and Bullish are building infrastructure that can support multiple asset classes. The value of these platforms is not tied to the performance of any single asset class. By contrast, tokenized Treasury funds or commodity tokens are asset-class specific. Infrastructure plays offer diversification and optionality. Asset-class plays require a conviction view on the underlying market.

**Second, prioritize asset classes where tokenization solves a clear operational problem.** Private credit and money market funds are strong candidates because they involve frequent transactions, multiple counterparties, and settlement delays. Equities and commodities are weaker candidates in the near term because existing infrastructure is already efficient. The report's data supports this prioritization: private credit and Treasuries account for roughly 74 percent of tokenized RWA market cap.

**Third, assess regulatory risk by jurisdiction and by asset class.** The issuer-led model for equity tokenization appears to be on solid regulatory ground in the United States, but the same cannot be said for third-party issued tokenized stocks or for tokenized securities in other jurisdictions. Investors should favor platforms and asset classes that operate within existing regulatory frameworks rather than those that depend on future regulatory clarity.

**Fourth, monitor the integration of tokenized assets with traditional market infrastructure.** The DTCC's tokenization initiative and the NYSE's partnership with Securitize are more significant signals than any single tokenization deal. When the incumbent infrastructure providers begin to adopt blockchain-based systems, the probability of widespread adoption increases substantially.

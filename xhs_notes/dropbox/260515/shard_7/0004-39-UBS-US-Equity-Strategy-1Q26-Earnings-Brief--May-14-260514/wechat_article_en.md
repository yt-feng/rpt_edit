# The 1Q26 Earnings Season Reveals a Market That Is No Longer a Single Story

The first quarter of 2026 is delivering a stark message to investors: the S&P 500 is not a monolith, and treating it as one is a strategic error. With 84% of the index's market capitalization having reported, aggregate expectations show revenue growth of 11.2% and EPS growth of 28.4%. But these headline numbers conceal a more consequential reality. The divergence between the technology-driven segment of the market and virtually everything else has reached a magnitude that demands a fundamental reassessment of portfolio construction.

This is not a normal earnings cycle. The 28.4% EPS growth figure is heavily distorted by a single group. TECH+ alone is expected to deliver 59.6% EPS growth, while the rest of the S&P 500 ex-TECH+ is tracking at just 11.1%. The six largest TECH+ companies are projected to grow earnings at 61.2% versus 17.9% for the rest of the market. When the gap between the top performers and the median is this wide, the aggregate number becomes misleading for anyone trying to understand the true health of corporate America.

What makes this moment strategically important is not just the magnitude of the divergence, but what it implies about the sustainability of current market leadership and the risks embedded in consensus positioning. The data from this earnings season provides a rare window into which sectors have genuine pricing power, which are riding transitory tailwinds, and which are structurally impaired. The task for the serious investor is to separate signal from noise.

## The TECH+ Earnings Machine Is Running at Full Throttle, But the Dispersion Within the Group Is Growing

The headline numbers for TECH+ are striking. The group is expected to post 59.6% EPS growth, driven by a combination of revenue expansion at 25.1% and dramatic margin expansion of 34.1%. The six largest names in the group--Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet, Apple, and NVIDIA--are projected to deliver 61.2% EPS growth as a collective. This is not merely a cyclical recovery; this is structural compounding at a rate that has historically been unsustainable.

But a closer look at the individual company data reveals a more nuanced picture. The expected growth rates for the Big 6 vary enormously. NVIDIA is projected to grow EPS by 115.1%, a figure that, if realized, would confirm that the AI infrastructure buildout remains in full swing. At the other end of the spectrum, Alphabet is expected to show negative EPS growth of 7.2%, and Meta is projected at just 5.5%. The actual results already reported tell an even more dramatic story: Amazon reported 81.1% actual EPS growth against a 6.6% estimate, while Alphabet reported 80.8% against a negative 7.2% estimate.

This pattern matters because it reveals that consensus estimates for these names are systematically unreliable. The magnitude of the beats suggests that the market is consistently underestimating the earnings power of the largest technology platforms, particularly those with exposure to cloud computing, digital advertising, and AI-related demand. The question this raises is whether the underestimation is a function of analyst conservatism or whether it reflects a genuine inability to model the nonlinear returns from AI investments.

The more important strategic implication is that the dispersion within TECH+ is widening. If NVIDIA delivers on its 115% growth estimate while Alphabet struggles, the group's aggregate performance will be increasingly driven by a narrowing set of names. This creates concentration risk for any investor who is simply indexing into the technology sector. The days of a uniform technology trade are over; stock selection within TECH+ has become the primary determinant of relative performance.

## The Rest of the Market Is Growing at a Pace That Justifies Tactical Rotation, Not Strategic Abandonment

The S&P 500 ex-TECH+ is expected to grow EPS at 11.1%, a figure that looks modest next to the technology sector but is actually quite healthy in historical context. Financials are expected to deliver 24.7% EPS growth, driven by margin expansion of 13.7% and revenue growth of 9.7%. Materials are expected to grow at 42.2%, the second-highest growth rate in the entire index after TECH+. Cyclicals ex-Energy are tracking at 15.7%.

The financial sector's performance deserves particular attention. With 76 out of 76 companies having reported, the data is complete. Banks are growing EPS at 21.8%, insurance at 47.8%, and diversified financials at 19.2%. This is not a sector in distress. The margin expansion of 13.7% suggests that financial institutions are successfully navigating the interest rate environment and capturing operating leverage. For investors who have avoided financials due to concerns about credit quality or regulatory headwinds, this earnings season provides a strong counterargument.

The materials sector is a more complex story. The 42.2% EPS growth is impressive, but it is heavily weighted toward a small number of companies. The median EPS growth for materials is just 8.5%, compared to the weighted average of 42.2%. This means that a few large companies are driving the aggregate number, while the typical materials company is growing at a much more modest pace. For an investor considering a materials overweight, the critical question is whether the leaders can sustain their outperformance or whether mean reversion is likely.

Non-cyclicals, by contrast, are a source of concern. The group is expected to grow EPS at just 1.7%, with health care actually declining by 3.8%. Even stripping out Merck, health care grows at only 8.9%. Staples are growing at 7.0%, and REITs at 4.1%. These are not recessionary numbers, but they are clearly below trend. The margin contraction in non-cyclicals of 6.2% suggests that pricing power is eroding and cost pressures are mounting. For a portfolio that relies on defensive exposures for stability, this earnings season raises uncomfortable questions about whether traditional defensive sectors are actually defensive anymore.

## The Beat Rate Is Strong, But the Market Is Punishing Misses With Unprecedented Severity

One of the most important data points from this earnings season is not the level of earnings but the market's reaction to surprises. The aggregate beat rate stands at 78% of companies topping projections, with earnings beating estimates by 17.3% on average. This is a strong beat rate by any historical standard. But the price action data reveals a market that is asymmetric in its punishment of disappointments.

Companies that beat on both earnings and revenue see an average price increase of 1.2% in the window from one day before to one day after the report. Companies that miss on both see an average decline of 2.8%. This 4 percentage point spread is wider than the historical average. In the technology sector, the asymmetry is even more extreme. TECH+ companies that beat on earnings but miss on revenue see an average price increase of 4.5%, while those that miss on earnings but beat on revenue see a decline of 8.4%. A company that misses on both metrics in TECH+ sees an average decline of 11.9%.

This pattern suggests that the market is rewarding clarity and punishing ambiguity. When a company delivers a clean beat, the reward is modest. When a company disappoints, the penalty is severe. This is consistent with a market that is priced for perfection and has little tolerance for negative surprises. For portfolio managers, the implication is clear: the risk-reward of holding names with uncertain earnings trajectories has shifted. The expected value of a position in a high-conviction name is positive, but the downside risk of a miss has become disproportionately large.

The data also reveals something about the market's information processing. The fact that revenue misses are punished more severely than earnings misses in certain sectors suggests that investors are looking through accounting adjustments and focusing on top-line sustainability. A company that can grow revenue despite margin pressure is viewed more favorably than one that hits earnings through cost cuts alone. This is a subtle but important signal about what the market values at this point in the cycle.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Margin Expansion and the Role of Buybacks

The earnings data presented in this report is rich, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important of these concerns the sustainability of margin expansion. The S&P 500 is showing margin expansion of 16.4% in aggregate, with TECH+ at 34.1% and materials at 31.4%. These are enormous numbers. In any other context, they would raise immediate questions about cyclical peaks and mean reversion.

The report does not provide a framework for assessing whether these margin gains are structural or cyclical. Are technology companies benefiting from genuine operating leverage as AI-related revenues scale, or are they benefiting from a temporary reprieve in input costs and labor market tightness? For materials, is the margin expansion driven by commodity price tailwinds that could reverse, or by cost restructuring that is permanent? Without answers to these questions, it is impossible to know whether current earnings levels are sustainable.

A second unanswered question concerns the role of share buybacks. The data shows that buybacks are contributing only 0.8% to EPS growth for the S&P 500 as a whole. This is a surprisingly small contribution given the level of buyback activity in recent years. The implication is that the vast majority of EPS growth is coming from genuine earnings improvement rather than financial engineering. But the report does not break this down by sector or company. If buyback activity is concentrated in a few large companies, the aggregate number may be misleading.

The third open question is about the trajectory of earnings through the rest of 2026. The report provides consensus estimates for the full year, showing S&P 500 EPS growth of 22.2%. But this is based on current consensus, which may be stale. The historical pattern of estimate revisions through the reporting season suggests that the final number could be higher. The report notes that EPS is on pace for 28.6% assuming historical revision trends, but it does not offer a view on whether this pace is sustainable or whether a deceleration is more likely.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Second Quarter

For the serious investor, the data from this earnings season can be translated into a practical decision framework. The framework has three dimensions: conviction, concentration, and catalyst.

First, conviction. The earnings data provides a clear signal about which sectors have genuine, demonstrable earnings momentum. TECH+ and financials are delivering. Non-cyclicals and health care are not. The investor should have a higher conviction threshold for positions in sectors where the earnings data is weak and a lower threshold for sectors where the data is strong. This does not mean avoiding weak sectors entirely, but it does mean requiring a stronger idiosyncratic thesis to justify an overweight.

Second, concentration. The dispersion within sectors is as important as the dispersion between sectors. In TECH+, the range of expected growth rates is enormous, from negative 7.2% for Alphabet to positive 115.1% for NVIDIA. In materials, the weighted average growth rate is five times the median. An investor who buys a sector ETF is making a bet on the weighted average, not on the typical company. The data suggests that active stock selection within sectors has a higher potential payoff than sector rotation alone.

Third, catalyst. The market's asymmetric punishment of misses means that the timing of earnings reports has become a portfolio risk factor. An investor who holds a name with an uncertain earnings trajectory into a reporting date is implicitly taking a leveraged bet. The data suggests that the expected value of this bet is negative for names with weak fundamentals. The framework should include a explicit assessment of whether a position should be held through earnings or reduced ahead of the report.

The most important insight from this framework is that the current market environment rewards precision. Broad bets on the market or on sectors are unlikely to generate excess returns. The earnings data has created a clear separation between winners and losers, and the market is rewarding those who can identify the difference.

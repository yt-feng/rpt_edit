# The AI Hardware Cycle Is Far From Over, But The Software Narrative Is Shifting Underneath

The artificial intelligence investment thesis has entered a new phase, and the distinction between hardware and software winners is becoming sharper than most market participants realize. The expansion of coding AI has triggered a structural surge in demand for tokens and inference compute, and the fundamentals of AI data centre-related businesses remain robust. Growth in AI-related hardware demand is likely to continue across semiconductors, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, electric power, and high-performance packaging. Yet the market is currently pricing many of these names as if the cycle is mature. It is not.

The real story of this earnings season in Japan is not about a peak in AI capital expenditure. It is about a reordering of preferences within the technology conglomerate and semiconductor universe. The report from a global investment bank makes a clear argument: the hardware cycle is still accelerating, but the software layer is facing structural headwinds that will reshape which companies benefit and which do not. The "death of SaaS" narrative is not just noise; it is a real risk for certain business models. Meanwhile, the most interesting opportunities are emerging in names where the market has not yet fully priced in the next leg of AI inference demand.

This is not a moment for blanket sector exposure. It is a moment for stock-specific conviction. The report identifies SoftBank and Sony as the top picks among tech conglomerates, and Tokyo Electron and Renesas as the standout semiconductor names. The reasoning behind these calls reveals a deeper strategic logic about where value is being created and destroyed in the current AI cycle.

## The AI Demand Structure Is Shifting From Training To Inference, And That Changes Everything

The first-order driver of the current cycle has been training demand for large language models. That wave is not over, but a second wave is building. The rapid expansion of coding AI tools from multiple players including Anthropic, OpenAI, and Chinese competitors like Kimi has created a step-change in token consumption. Every new coding assistant rollout means more inference compute, more power consumption, and more demand for high-performance packaging.

This is structurally different from the training wave. Training demand is lumpy, concentrated among a handful of hyperscalers, and subject to periodic pauses as new model architectures are developed. Inference demand, by contrast, is distributed, growing steadily, and tied to user adoption curves that are still in their early innings. The report highlights that AI inference demand is likely to be the primary driver of growth for ARM, which in turn drives SoftBank's equity value. This is a critical insight because it shifts the lens from the current quarter's capex numbers to a multi-year adoption cycle.

The implication is clear: hardware companies exposed to inference compute, power infrastructure, and packaging have a longer runway than the market currently discounts. The risk is not that AI spending peaks. The risk is that investors misjudge which part of the value chain is still early cycle.

## SoftBank And Sony Offer Asymmetric Exposure To AI Inference And Content Monetisation

Among the Japanese tech conglomerates, the report is most constructive on SoftBank and Sony. For SoftBank, the thesis hinges on ARM's ability to capture AI inference demand. This is not a speculative bet on ARM's architecture winning in data centres; it is a bet on the structural shift toward edge and mobile inference where ARM already dominates. The report also highlights the growing business value of OpenAI as coding AI accelerates monetisation. If OpenAI's revenue trajectory continues to steepen, SoftBank's stake becomes a more material driver of NAV.

For Sony, the narrative is more nuanced. The most concerning issue heading into full-year results was the impact of DRAM price hikes on Sony's cost structure. The company has managed to offset that through price increases, which signals pricing power that the market may be underestimating. More importantly, the report argues that Sony's current share price excessively reflects concerns about content value erosion due to AI. This is a critical distinction. The market is pricing in a worst-case scenario where AI destroys the economics of content creation and distribution. But if Sony can navigate that transition, the current valuation offers a margin of safety.

The common thread between SoftBank and Sony is optionality. Both companies have core businesses that are undervalued because the market is focused on the wrong risks. For SoftBank, the risk is ARM's competitive position. For Sony, the risk is content disruption. In both cases, the report suggests the market has overcorrected.

## Tokyo Electron And Renesas Are The Semiconductor Picks, But Valuation Discipline Matters

Within the semiconductor equipment and component space, the report draws a sharp distinction between names that offer upside and names that are fully priced. Tokyo Electron stands out because its price hike was not anticipated by the market. This is a powerful signal. When a semiconductor equipment supplier can raise prices, it indicates pricing power, capacity constraints, and a favourable supply-demand balance. The report expects market share and profit margins to expand going forward. That is a fundamental improvement in the business model, not just a cyclical tailwind.

Renesas presents a different but equally compelling case. The company has delivered six consecutive quarters of sequential sales growth, and the trajectory continues into April-June. Overseas competitors have implemented further price hikes following those in March-April, which suggests that Renesas will also see margin improvement. The upcycle is being reconfirmed, not questioned.

But the report is not indiscriminate. Advantest and Disco are seeing solid trends from AI demand, yet the report explicitly states that their multiples are excessively high. This is a crucial reminder that in a market where AI enthusiasm has pushed valuations to extreme levels, not every good business is a good investment at the current price. The distinction between a great company and a great stock is as relevant now as ever.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered Questions About The Software Layer And The Domestic IT Market

One of the most valuable aspects of this report is what it does not fully answer. The report acknowledges that concerns about software-related businesses, including the "death of SaaS" narrative, are likely to persist. In the domestic IT services market, the greatest risk factor appears to be the shift by customers toward in-house development. This is a structural threat to the traditional IT services model that deserves deeper analysis.

The open question is whether this shift is cyclical or secular. If customers are moving development in-house because coding AI makes it easier, then the impact on IT services companies could be permanent. If it is a temporary response to cost pressures, the cycle could reverse. The report does not resolve this tension, and that ambiguity creates an opportunity for investors who can develop a proprietary view.

Another unresolved question is how the competitive dynamics in coding AI will evolve. The report mentions multiple product launches from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Chinese competitors, but it does not take a definitive stance on which platform will dominate. This matters because the distribution of inference demand depends on which models gain adoption. If a single platform captures the majority of coding AI usage, the hardware demand profile could become more concentrated and more volatile.

## A Decision Framework For Navigating The Japanese Tech Landscape

For investors trying to translate this analysis into actionable portfolio decisions, a structured framework is essential. The report suggests three dimensions for evaluating Japanese tech stocks.

First, assess the exposure to AI inference versus AI training. Companies with direct exposure to inference demand have a longer and more predictable growth runway. This favours ARM, data centre power infrastructure, and high-performance packaging. Companies tied primarily to training demand face more lumpiness and potential disappointment.

Second, evaluate pricing power. The ability to raise prices is the single clearest signal of a favourable competitive position. Tokyo Electron's price hike and Sony's ability to pass through DRAM costs are both positive signals. Companies that cannot raise prices in this environment are likely facing structural headwinds.

Third, separate valuation from narrative. Advantest and Disco have strong businesses, but the report questions whether their multiples leave room for upside. A great business at a high multiple is often a worse investment than a good business at a reasonable multiple. The market is not efficient at distinguishing between these cases in the current AI frenzy.

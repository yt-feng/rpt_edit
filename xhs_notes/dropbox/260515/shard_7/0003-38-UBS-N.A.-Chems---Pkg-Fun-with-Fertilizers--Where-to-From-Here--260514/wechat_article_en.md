# Fertilizer Markets Have Entered a Regime of Divergent Nutrient Economics — and the Winners Are Not Who You Think

The global fertilizer complex is no longer moving as one market. For the past two years, investors have treated nitrogen, phosphates, and potash as a single cyclical story, driven by the same macro forces of crop prices, farmer incomes, and trade flows. That assumption is now breaking down. The Middle East conflict has exposed structural differences in how each nutrient responds to supply disruption, input cost inflation, and demand destruction — differences that are creating starkly divergent outcomes for producers. The nitrogen complex is experiencing a temporary but powerful earnings windfall driven by a widening premium to the cost curve, even as spot prices begin to ease. Phosphates are trapped in a cost squeeze that is destroying margins and forcing plant shutdowns, with no clear path to recovery. Potash sits in between, benefiting from modest price support but facing a medium-term overhang of new supply that caps the upside for producers.

This is not a sector where a single view works. The strategic question for investors is not whether fertilizers are attractive, but which exposure, at what valuation, and under what scenario. The data from a recent global investment bank report makes clear that the market is pricing in a base case of moderate disruption duration — but the range of outcomes is wide, and the asymmetry of risk and reward differs sharply across the three nutrients.

Why now matters: the second quarter of 2026 is likely to be the peak pricing quarter for the cycle, particularly for nitrogen. The seasonal demand surge from the Northern Hemisphere growing season is passing, and spot markets are already showing signs of softening. Yet the structural tightness in supply — from the Strait of Hormuz closure, Iranian outages, and curtailed phosphate production — means that the decline may be shallower and slower than historical norms. The report's analysis suggests that we are moving from a phase of price discovery to a phase of regime differentiation. The next six months will determine which companies have the balance sheet, cost position, and nutrient mix to weather the normalization, and which are left exposed.

The investment implications are not obvious from headline price moves. A producer with strong nitrogen exposure may look like a peak-cycle earnings story, but the report's valuation work suggests that much of this is already discounted. A phosphate-heavy producer may look cheap on book value, but the margin compression is structural, not cyclical. And a diversified potash player may offer stability, but the medium-term supply growth story raises questions about whether current earnings are sustainable. The answer lies not in a single trade, but in a framework that maps each company's exposure to the specific dynamics of each nutrient.

## Nitrogen Producers Are Enjoying a Temporary Cost Curve Premium That Is Already Priced Into Stocks

The most striking feature of the current nitrogen market is not the absolute level of prices, but the size of the premium above what input costs would normally justify. Ammonia, urea, and nitrates benchmarks are up roughly 25 to 60 percent since the onset of the Middle East conflict. But natural gas — the primary input for nitrogen production — has moved far less. U.S. and Canadian gas prices have shown a muted response to the conflict, and European gas benchmarks, while elevated, remain well below the peaks seen during the Russia-Ukraine crisis in 2022. The result is that nitrogen pricing now carries a wider-than-normal premium to the global cost curve. This premium is a direct function of supply disruption, not of underlying demand strength.

The report estimates that ammonia pricing has increased by roughly $100 per metric ton more than what the move in gas would imply. For urea and UAN, the excess premium is approximately $35 and $140 per ton, respectively. This is the earnings windfall that nitrogen-heavy producers are currently capturing. But it is also the source of the risk. The report's analysis of spot trends shows that urea pricing has already begun to soften, and the Tampa ammonia contract settled below expectations. The second quarter is shaping up to be the peak for nitrogen pricing, and the trajectory beyond that is downward — modestly in the near term as seasonal demand fades, and more meaningfully if trade flows normalize.

The report's valuation work on a leading nitrogen producer illustrates the tension. The report's 2026 and 2027 EBITDA estimates are 17 and 21 percent above consensus, respectively. Yet the stock is already trading at a level that the report considers fair value under a mid-cycle earnings assumption. The implied base case already prices in several more quarters of excess free cash flow. The upside scenario — around $140 to $145 per share — requires the disruption to persist well into 2027. The downside scenario — a correction to the mid-cycle base — could see the stock fall to the $105 level. The risk-reward is balanced, but the asymmetry is not obviously favorable at current prices.

The strategic implication is that nitrogen exposure is a bet on the duration of the disruption, not on the level of earnings. Investors who believe the Strait of Hormuz will reopen and Iranian production will restart within months should avoid nitrogen-heavy names. Those who see a prolonged disruption lasting into 2027 may find the current valuations reasonable. But the report's analysis suggests that the easy money has already been made. The premium to the cost curve is likely to narrow, not widen, from here.

## Phosphates Are in a Cost Squeeze That Is Destroying Margins and Forcing Curtailments, with No Clear Catalyst for Recovery

If nitrogen is a story of windfall premiums, phosphates is a story of margin compression. The mechanism is straightforward but brutal. Phosphate prices have moved higher since the conflict began, driven by supply constraints from OCP in Morocco, Mosaic in Brazil, and disruptions from Russia and the Strait of Hormuz. U.S. phosphate prices are up $100 to $140 per ton since pre-conflict levels, and export prices are up roughly 20 percent. But the input costs for phosphate production — particularly sulfur and ammonia — have risen even more. Sulfur pricing has reached $900 to $1,150 per ton in key markets, compressing stripping margins by up to $120 per ton in offshore regions.

The result is that producers are losing money on every ton of phosphate they produce at current market prices. This is not a theoretical risk. The report documents that Mosaic has announced 50 percent curtailments at its Louisiana and Bartow facilities, representing roughly 1.7 million tons of capacity. OCP has implemented production cuts of up to 30 percent in the second quarter. PhosAgro in Russia is also facing disruptions from drone strikes. The market is responding to margin compression with supply cuts, which theoretically should support prices. But the report raises a critical question: can the market bear those higher prices? Demand destruction in phosphates is already more severe than in potash, with affordability concerns driving reduced applications in both the U.S. and Brazil.

The report's earnings estimates for a leading phosphate-heavy producer show the scale of the problem. The report's 2026 EBITDA estimate is 17 percent below consensus. The combined phosphate and fertilizers EBITDA is expected to decline by more than 50 percent in 2026, while potash earnings are roughly flat. The company is trading near $23 per share, which the report estimates is roughly in line with a mid-cycle EBITDA of $1.85 billion at a 6.0x multiple. But the free cash flow generation is being depressed by both lower earnings and higher working capital, with a free cash flow yield of only 0.2 percent in 2026 and 2.0 percent in 2027.

The strategic question for phosphate exposure is whether the current margin compression is cyclical or structural. If sulfur and ammonia costs normalize over the next twelve months, stripping margins could recover sharply, and the stocks would look very cheap. But if input costs remain elevated and demand destruction caps the ability to pass through higher prices, the earnings downturn could be deeper and longer than the market expects. The report's bias is toward the negative scenario, but it acknowledges that the outcome depends on factors that are difficult to forecast — the trajectory of sulfur supply, the speed of trade flow normalization, and the elasticity of farmer demand at higher price levels.

## Potash Offers Modest Support but Faces a Medium-Term Overhang That Limits the Upside for Producers

Potash has been the most resilient of the three nutrients during the current disruption, but the support is more fragile than headline prices suggest. Pricing has moved higher since the conflict began, driven by peak Northern Hemisphere demand, increased buying from Brazil, and higher freight costs. Spot prices are tracking $15 to $30 per metric ton higher quarter over quarter. Demand destruction has been limited to an estimated 5 to 15 percent, far less severe than in phosphates. Supply from Israel and Jordan has remained largely uninterrupted, and potash has been less exposed to Strait of Hormuz disruptions than nitrogen or phosphates.

Yet the report's analysis raises a critical caveat: producers may not benefit as much from the price increases as the headline numbers suggest. A portion of the price move is attributable to higher freight costs, which do not flow through to producer margins. And the medium-term supply outlook is challenging. The report estimates that incremental supply will exceed demand growth through the later part of the decade, creating a structural headwind for pricing. This is the central tension in the potash story: near-term support from strong demand and trade disruptions, but medium-term pressure from capacity additions.

The report's valuation work on a diversified potash and nitrogen producer illustrates the risk. The report's 2026, 2027, and 2028 EBITDA estimates are 1 percent above, 3 percent below, and 8 percent below consensus, respectively. The medium-term outlook for potash is the key point of disagreement. The bulls expect potash prices to increase into 2027 on the back of better crop prices and farmer profitability, with tighter supply-demand dynamics than the report assumes. The report's base case is that potash pricing will decline modestly as new supply comes online. The difference matters: if potash prices remain flat at 2025 levels, the report estimates it would add $300 to $450 million to mid-cycle EBITDA, implying a fair value of $70 to $72 per share. That is roughly where the stock is currently trading.

The strategic takeaway is that potash exposure is a bet on the supply-demand balance over the next two to three years, not on the current pricing environment. The near-term support is real, but it is largely priced in. The upside risk comes from a scenario where crop prices continue to rise, farmer incomes improve, and potash demand surprises to the upside. The downside risk comes from new supply hitting the market faster than expected, particularly from projects in Russia and Belarus that have been delayed but not canceled. The report leaves this question open, and it is the most important variable for investors considering potash exposure.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Demand Destruction Will Interact with Supply Constraints in a Normalization Scenario

The most important analytical gap in the report is the interaction between demand destruction and supply recovery. The report documents that demand destruction is already occurring in phosphates, with reduced applications in the U.S. and Brazil driven by affordability concerns. It also notes that nitrogen demand is softening as buyers shift to lower-cost alternatives. But it does not fully model the feedback loop between price normalization and demand recovery. If trade flows normalize and supply constraints ease, will demand snap back quickly, or will the damage to farmer confidence and planting decisions persist into the next season?

This question is critical for the medium-term outlook. If demand destruction is temporary and reversible, then the current margin compression in phosphates and the premium in nitrogen are both cyclical phenomena that will correct as supply and demand rebalance. But if the higher price environment has permanently altered farmer behavior — for example, by accelerating the adoption of precision agriculture or shifting crop rotation patterns — then the recovery may be slower and more modest than historical analogs suggest.

The report also does not fully address the implications of the Brazil import data. Brazil fertilizer imports are up 3 percent year to date, but the composition is shifting. Potash imports are up 17 percent, while phosphate and nitrogen imports are down. This suggests that farmers are making active choices about which nutrients to apply, favoring potash where affordability is less of a constraint. The report notes that the full impact of the Middle East disruption on Brazil imports may not be visible until May and June data, given the 6 to 8 week shipping lag. But the early signals are consistent with the thesis that demand destruction is concentrated in the most expensive nutrients.

Investors should watch the Brazil import data closely over the next three months. If phosphate and nitrogen imports recover as the peak season approaches, it would suggest that demand is more resilient than the report assumes. If they continue to decline, it would validate the thesis that affordability constraints are structural, not temporary. This is the single most important data point for differentiating between the bull and bear cases across the sector.

## A Decision Framework for Fertilizer Investors: Map Each Company to the Nutrient Dynamics That Matter Most

The report's analysis suggests a simple but powerful framework for evaluating fertilizer companies. Investors should assess each company based on three variables: nutrient exposure, cost position, and balance sheet strength. The nutrient exposure determines the direction of earnings momentum. The cost position determines the resilience of margins during periods of input cost inflation. The balance sheet determines the ability to withstand a prolonged downturn or to invest through the cycle.

For nitrogen-heavy companies, the key variable is the duration of the supply disruption. The earnings windfall is real but temporary, and the stock prices already reflect a significant amount of good news. The upside scenario requires the disruption to persist into 2027. The downside scenario is a sharp correction as the premium to the cost curve narrows. Investors should size their positions based on their conviction about the trajectory of Middle East trade flows, not on the level of current earnings.

For phosphate-heavy companies, the key variable is the trajectory of input costs. The margin compression is severe, and the supply curtailments are a symptom of financial distress, not of strategic discipline. The recovery scenario requires sulfur and ammonia costs to normalize, which depends on factors outside the control of any single producer. Investors should demand a significant margin of safety before investing in phosphate exposure, and should be prepared for the possibility that the earnings downturn is deeper and longer than consensus expects.

For potash-focused companies, the key variable is the medium-term supply-demand balance. The near-term support from strong demand and trade disruptions is largely priced in. The upside risk comes from higher crop prices and tighter supply. The downside risk comes from new supply hitting the market. Investors should focus on the project pipeline for new potash capacity and on the trajectory of crop futures, particularly corn and soybeans, which have already moved higher since the conflict began.

The report's ranking within neutral-rated names — CF ahead of LXU ahead of Mosaic — reflects this framework. CF has the strongest nitrogen exposure and the best cost position, making it the most attractive in a prolonged disruption scenario. LXU has similar nitrogen exposure but a less favorable cost position. Mosaic has the most challenging setup, with phosphate margin compression offsetting any benefit from potash. The sell-rated names — NTR and IPI — face medium-term headwinds from potash oversupply and, in NTR's case, declining nitrogen earnings as the cycle turns.

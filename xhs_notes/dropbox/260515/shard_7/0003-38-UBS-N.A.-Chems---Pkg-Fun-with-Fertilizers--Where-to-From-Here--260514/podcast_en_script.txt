EN_A: Fertilizer markets have been all over the place lately. A new deep dive from a global investment bank just came out. What’s the big takeaway?
EN_B: The big idea is that the fertilizer market is completely fragmented right now. Nitrogen, phosphates, and potash are all driven by very different forces. You can’t treat them as one sector.
EN_A: So what’s the main question the report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It’s trying to figure out whether the price spike we’ve seen since the Middle East conflict is temporary or structural. And which fertilizer actually benefits, and which one gets squeezed.
EN_A: And what’s the answer?
EN_B: For nitrogen, the peak is likely behind us. The report says the second quarter of this year is probably the high point for this cycle. Prices should start to ease seasonally from here. But phosphates are in a completely different place. They are in a classic “prices up but margins down” situation.
EN_A: How can prices go up and margins go down at the same time?
EN_B: Because producing phosphate requires sulfur and ammonia. And sulfur prices have exploded to over nine hundred dollars a ton in some regions. The increase in phosphate prices isn’t enough to cover that cost spike. So producers are cutting output. OCP in Morocco, Mosaic in Brazil and the U.S. — some plants are running at only fifty percent capacity.
EN_A: So what does that mean? Doesn’t less supply usually support prices?
EN_B: It should, in theory. But the report raises a really tough question. If prices keep going up, will demand just collapse? Farmers can’t afford it. So you end up in this loop where cutting supply to protect prices just destroys demand. That tension is most acute in phosphates.
EN_A: What about potash?
EN_B: Potash is more stable. Prices are up, but a lot of that is driven by higher freight costs, not a real supply shortage. Producers don’t get to keep all that gain. And supply from the Middle East has been largely uninterrupted. The report estimates demand destruction risk for potash is only about five to fifteen percent, much lower than for phosphates.
EN_A: So the report has very different views on different companies.
EN_B: Exactly. It’s positive on CF and LXU within the neutral bucket. But it has sell ratings on NTR and IPI. For NTR, the concern is that potash supply will grow faster than demand over the next few years, which puts pressure on prices.
EN_A: What about Mosaic? It has both phosphate and potash.
EN_B: Mosaic is rated neutral, but the report sees a tough year ahead. Its phosphate business is being crushed by high input costs. The report estimates that combined phosphate and Brazil fertilizer EBITDA could drop by more than fifty percent in 2026. But there is a twist.
EN_A: A twist?
EN_B: The report says that if potash pricing stays strong and phosphate raw material costs come down over the next couple of years, Mosaic could see a real recovery. But it calls that scenario less likely.
EN_A: It sounds like the report leaves a lot of questions open.
EN_B: It does. One key open question is how long the nitrogen premium can last. Right now, nitrogen prices are well above what the cost of natural gas would suggest. That premium is held up by supply disruption. If the situation in the Middle East de-escalates, that premium could shrink fast. But the report also notes that even if the strait reopens, it would take at least three months for product to start flowing again. And that timing overlaps with Brazil’s peak import season.
EN_A: So the real value of this report is helping investors navigate all that uncertainty.
EN_B: Exactly. It doesn’t give a simple buy or sell call. It maps out the risks for each nutrient, the logic for each stock, and the key variables to watch. If you’re interested in fertilizer investing, the full report has much more detail on valuation and scenario analysis.

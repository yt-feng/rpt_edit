# The Luxury Market Is No Longer Democratising: Brands Must Choose Between Heritage and Hype

The era of luxury for the masses is ending. For the better part of a decade, the global luxury industry pursued a strategy of democratisation, opening its doors to aspirational consumers through accessible entry-level products, social media campaigns, and rapid brand expansion. That strategy worked spectacularly until it did not. Today, the market is undergoing a correction that is not cyclical in the traditional sense but structural. The trend-sensitive, aspirational consumer who drove the post-pandemic boom has slowed their spending, while the core high-net-worth client remains engaged but has shifted their preferences toward rarity, craftsmanship, and relationships. The result is a bifurcated market where the rules of success are being rewritten.

This is not a temporary slowdown that will reverse when consumer confidence returns. It is a repricing of what luxury actually means, and the implications for brand strategy, product architecture, and investor positioning are profound. The secondary luxury market, which has matured from a niche curiosity into a core component of the luxury ecosystem, provides an unusually clear lens into these shifts. Resale prices, inventory flows, and buyer-seller dynamics reveal which brands hold genuine desirability and which are merely riding a wave of fashion-driven demand.

The central argument of this analysis is straightforward: the luxury brands that will outperform in the coming years are those that can successfully "scale back" their focus from broad market capture to deep client relationships, from volume to quality, from hype to heritage. The brands that fail to make this transition will face persistent volatility and margin compression. The secondary market is already signalling which brands are on which trajectory.

## The Secondary Market Is No Longer a Side Show; It Is the Canary in the Coal Mine for Brand Health

Resale was once dismissed as a fringe activity, a place where past-season goods went to die or where bargain hunters found deals. That view is obsolete. The expert call underlying this analysis makes clear that resale has become a core part of the luxury ecosystem, enabling circular consumption and, critically, supporting the primary market. This is not a threat to luxury brands; it is a feedback mechanism that reveals the true state of demand.

What the secondary market is telling us right now is uncomfortable for many brands. Pricing dynamics are sharply bifurcated. Iconic, timeless items continue to command premiums near or above retail, while trend-driven and limited-edition pieces have seen their secondary multiples normalise materially after reaching peak levels during the pandemic. The speculative froth that characterised the post-pandemic period, when status-driven buyers snapped up anything with a logo and a story, is dissipating. The buyers who remain are more discerning, more knowledgeable, and more focused on what the expert describes as "rarity and craftsmanship."

This shift has a direct consequence for inventory availability. The expert noted that supply on resale platforms has improved structurally, reflecting reduced stigma around buying pre-owned luxury and stronger relationships with VIP consignors. Yet some sellers are holding back inventory, waiting for a rebound in secondary multiples that may not come. This is a classic sign of a market in transition: the participants who understood the old regime are reluctant to accept the new pricing reality.

For investors, the implication is clear. Brands that rely on hype-driven, limited-edition drops to sustain momentum are facing a structural headwind. The secondary market is no longer forgiving of overproduction or overexposure. Brands that maintain disciplined product cycles and invest in enduring design language are seeing their secondary pricing hold firm. The resale channel has become a real-time, unvarnished report card on brand equity.

## Hard Luxury Is Winning Because It Is the Ultimate Expression of Trading Up Within the Category

Within the broader luxury landscape, a clear pattern has emerged. Core segments such as handbags and ready-to-wear continue to perform, but the notable acceleration is in fine jewellery and watches. This is not a coincidence. It reflects a specific consumer behaviour that the report terms "trading up within the categorial pyramid."

The logic is as follows. A repeat luxury consumer who began their journey with an entry-level leather good, say a canvas tote or a small wallet, eventually seeks a more enduring expression of status and taste. They do not leave the category; they move upward within it. Hard luxury, in the form of a fine watch or a diamond necklace, represents a permanent store of value, a piece that can be worn daily, passed down, and resold with minimal depreciation. In an environment where the aspirational consumer is pulling back, the repeat consumer is doubling down on pieces that signal permanence rather than trend.

This has profound implications for brand portfolio strategy. Luxury conglomerates that have invested in hard luxury, whether through organically grown watch divisions or through acquisitions of heritage jewellers, are better positioned to capture this trading-up behaviour. Brands that remain anchored in soft luxury, particularly in trend-sensitive categories, face a more volatile demand profile.

The expert's observation that pricing for iconic items remains highly resilient while trend-driven pieces have normalised is consistent with this thesis. Iconic items, whether a Cartier Tank watch or a Van Cleef & Arpels Alhambra necklace, function as quasi-currency. Their secondary pricing is anchored by decades of desirability and a deep pool of informed buyers. Trend-driven pieces, by contrast, are at the mercy of the fashion cycle, and the fashion cycle is currently turning against them.

## The Polarisation Across Brands Mirrors the Polarisation Across Consumers, and the Winners Are Already Visible

The brand-specific dynamics described in the report offer a granular view of which strategies are working and which are not. Chanel, which remains privately held and thus less exposed to quarterly earnings pressure, is cited as an outperformer. The renewed interest in its leather goods following the appointment of Matthieu Blazy is a case study in how creative direction can re-energise a brand's core category without resorting to volume expansion.

Within the publicly traded universe, the picture is more mixed. Louis Vuitton continues to show stable momentum, driven by strong performance of classic vintage monograms. This is significant because it demonstrates that even a mega-brand can maintain desirability when it leans into its heritage rather than chasing every trend. The latest campaign's focus on classic monograms, rather than on experimental collaborations or celebrity-driven drops, is a strategic choice that aligns with the broader market shift toward authenticity.

Dior, by contrast, tells a more complicated story. Secondhand demand for Dior is selective, concentrated on iconic eras such as the John Galliano years. This suggests that the brand's current creative direction may not be generating the same level of enduring desirability. For investors, this raises a question that the report does not fully answer: can Dior recapture the cultural resonance of its archival peaks, or is it destined to be a brand that lives on past glory?

Gucci presents the most volatile case. The brand is described as "more volatile and exposed to fashion cycles after prior overexposure." This is a polite way of saying that Gucci's aggressive push into trend-driven products and broad market appeal has left it vulnerable. The re-emerging archival demand for the Tom Ford-inspired Demna era is telling: consumers are looking backward for the Gucci they remember, not forward to the Gucci they are being offered. The strategic challenge for Gucci is whether it can transition from a brand defined by fashion moments to a brand defined by enduring desirability.

Prada and Miu Miu are the clear winners in the current environment. Their secondary prices are close to retail, a remarkable signal of strength. The expert attributes this to "broad client relevance," which is a function of consistent creative vision and disciplined product strategy. Prada does not chase every trend, but it also does not rest on its archival laurels. It occupies a sweet spot that few brands achieve: it is both of the moment and timeless.

Finally, the growing interest in The Row and Phoebe Philo's independent label signals a deeper shift in consumer taste toward discretion and craftsmanship. These are brands that do not shout; they whisper. In a market that is moving away from logo-heavy, status-driven consumption, quiet luxury is not a trend but a structural preference.

## Hermès Is the Anchor, But Even It Is Not Immune to Normalisation

Hermès occupies a unique position in the luxury landscape. It is often treated as a category unto itself, a brand whose desirability is so deeply entrenched that it is immune to cycles. The report's findings confirm that Hermès remains the anchor of the resale market, but they also reveal a more nuanced picture.

Demand for Hermès is still structurally strong, but it is increasingly concentrated among VIP clients seeking rare and less recognisable pieces. The standard Kelly and Birkin bags, which have long been the brand's most visible symbols, are seeing demand that is solid but not spectacular. The real action is in limited-edition and highly customised pieces that only the most informed buyers know to seek out.

The normalisation of Hermès resale premiums is perhaps the most telling data point in the entire report. During the post-pandemic speculative frenzy, pre-loved Hermès bags traded at approximately two to two-and-a-half times retail value. That premium has now normalised to approximately one to one-and-a-half times retail. This is not a collapse; it is a reversion to a more sustainable level. But it does indicate that even the most iconic brand is not immune to the fading influence of speculative, status-driven buying.

For investors, the Hermès story is a cautionary tale about the limits of brand power. Even when a brand has everything going for it, the market will eventually price out the speculative excess. The question for Hermès, and for every other brand in the luxury space, is what happens when the speculative buyers leave and only the true clients remain. The answer, based on the current data, is that the true clients are more discerning, more demanding, and more focused on what the brand stands for beyond its most famous products.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Unresolved Question of Scale

The report provides a compelling diagnosis of the current state of the luxury market, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important of these is the question of scale. The expert's central recommendation is that brands need to "scale back" and focus on the core to drive growth. But what does scaling back look like in practice for a publicly traded luxury conglomerate that is under pressure to deliver quarterly earnings growth?

The tension between brand health and financial performance is not new, but it is becoming more acute. A brand like Louis Vuitton, which generates billions in revenue, cannot simply shrink its way to higher margins. It must find a way to maintain its scale while also protecting its exclusivity. The report offers no clear framework for how this can be achieved, and the historical record is not encouraging. Most attempts by luxury brands to broaden their appeal while maintaining exclusivity have ended in one of two ways: either the brand becomes too accessible and loses its cachet, or it becomes too exclusive and fails to grow.

A second unresolved question concerns the role of creative directors. The report notes that certain creative appointments, such as Matthieu Blazy at Chanel, have generated renewed interest. But the relationship between creative direction and commercial success is notoriously unpredictable. The report does not offer a systematic way to evaluate which creative appointments are likely to succeed and which are not.

A third question is geographic. The report focuses on global trends, but the luxury market is heavily dependent on Chinese consumers, whose behaviour is shaped by factors that are distinct from those in Europe or the United States. The report does not address how the polarisation trend plays out in China, where the aspirational consumer segment is larger and the relationship between primary and secondary markets is less developed.

These are not criticisms of the report; they are the natural limits of any single piece of analysis. But they are the questions that investors and brand strategists need to grapple with as they make decisions in this uncertain environment.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Polarised Luxury Market

For readers who are trying to translate the report's insights into actionable decisions, a structured framework is essential. Based on the analysis above, we propose a three-part framework for evaluating luxury brands and positioning portfolios.

The first dimension is product architecture. Brands should be evaluated on the ratio of iconic to trend-driven products in their revenue mix. A high ratio of iconic products, defined as items that have been in the collection for five years or more and that maintain stable secondary pricing, is a positive signal. A high ratio of trend-driven products, defined as items that are launched with a specific seasonal or cultural hook, is a risk factor. The report's data on secondary pricing provides a direct, quantifiable way to assess this dimension.

The second dimension is client concentration. Brands that derive a significant share of revenue from a small number of high-net-worth repeat clients are better positioned than brands that rely on a broad base of aspirational one-time buyers. The expert's emphasis on "relationship-driven" sourcing and selling in the secondary market is a proxy for this dynamic in the primary market. Brands that invest in client relationships, through personal shopping, exclusive events, and direct communication, are building a moat that trend-driven brands cannot replicate.

The third dimension is creative consistency. Brands that maintain a consistent creative vision across multiple seasons, and across multiple creative directors, are more likely to build enduring desirability. The report's contrast between Prada, which has maintained broad client relevance through consistent vision, and Gucci, which has suffered from volatility after creative changes, illustrates this point. Investors should look for brands with a clear creative identity that transcends any single designer.

This framework does not provide easy answers, but it does provide a systematic way to ask the right questions. The brands that score highly on all three dimensions are likely to outperform in the polarised market. The brands that score poorly on one or more dimensions face structural headwinds that no amount of financial engineering can overcome.

## Conclusion: The Market Is Sending a Signal, and Only the Prepared Will Hear It

The luxury market is not in crisis, but it is in transition. The era of easy growth, driven by aspirational consumers and trend-driven products, is over. The brands that will thrive in the coming years are those that understand the value of heritage over hype, of relationships over reach, of craftsmanship over content.

The secondary market, once a niche corner of the luxury ecosystem, has become the clearest signal of which brands are building lasting value and which are merely generating noise. The data is unambiguous: iconic items hold their value, trend-driven pieces do not. The consumer is voting with their wallet, and they are voting for quality, rarity, and authenticity.

For investors, the opportunity is to identify the brands that are making the transition from hype to heritage before the market fully prices it in. The risk is to assume that the current correction is temporary and that the old playbook will work again.

# The Earnings Divide Is Not Temporary: Why 1Q26 Confirms a Structural Market Bifurcation

The 1Q26 earnings season is delivering a verdict that many investors are still reluctant to accept. With 84% of the S&P 500's market capitalization having reported, the data reveals not a cyclical fluctuation but a structural divergence in corporate profitability that is reshaping the landscape for capital allocation. The headline number is striking: aggregate earnings per share is on pace for 28.6% year-over-year growth, the strongest quarterly performance in years. But this aggregate masks a deeper truth. The growth is almost entirely concentrated in a narrow cluster of technology-related companies, while large swaths of the index are delivering single-digit or flat earnings expansion.

This is not the typical late-cycle pattern where earnings breadth eventually broadens. What we are observing is a persistent, self-reinforcing bifurcation driven by differential exposure to artificial intelligence investment cycles, margin expansion capacity, and market power. The implications for portfolio construction are profound. Investors who continue to treat the S&P 500 as a diversified bet on the U.S. economy are effectively making a concentrated bet on fewer than ten companies, and they may not realize it.

The data from the current reporting period forces a strategic reassessment. The question is no longer whether the market is expensive in aggregate. The question is whether the premium you are paying for the rest of the market is justified by the earnings trajectory, or whether the concentration risk has become the dominant variable in equity returns.

## The Aggregate Growth Story Is Almost Entirely a TECH+ Story

The most important single fact from this earnings season is the magnitude of the gap between the technology-related segments of the market and everything else. The TECH+ group, which encompasses the largest technology platforms, semiconductor companies, and related hardware and software firms, is expected to deliver EPS growth of 59.6% year over year. The Big 6 companies within that group are projected to grow at 61.2%. In contrast, the S&P 500 excluding TECH+ is expected to grow at only 11.1%.

This is not a marginal difference. It is a factor of more than five to one. When you remove the TECH+ impact from the index, the aggregate EPS growth rate drops from 28.4% to 11.1%. The entire narrative of a booming earnings recovery is dependent on a single sector cluster. What makes this structural rather than cyclical is the source of the growth. The TECH+ group is not merely benefiting from a cyclical economic upturn. Its earnings expansion is being driven by margin improvement of 34.1% year over year, compared to just 2.6% for the rest of the index. These companies are generating profit growth through operating leverage, pricing power, and the capitalization of AI infrastructure spending. The rest of the market is struggling to expand margins at all.

The implication is straightforward. If you believe the earnings trajectory of the S&P 500 is healthy, you are implicitly betting that the TECH+ group will continue to outperform the broader economy indefinitely. That may be a reasonable thesis, but it is a thesis, not a diversified portfolio strategy.

## The Surprise Data Reveals a Market That Rewards Precision and Punishes Ambiguity

The earnings surprise data from 1Q26 offers a second critical insight about market behavior. On aggregate, companies are beating earnings estimates by 17.3%, with 78% of companies topping projections. The median surprise is 5.7%. These are strong numbers by historical standards. But the price action associated with these surprises tells a more nuanced story.

When a company beats both revenue and earnings estimates, the average price reaction over the sUBSequent trading days is positive 1.2%. When a company beats earnings but misses on revenue, the price reaction turns negative, at minus 0.5%. When a company misses on both, the penalty is severe, at minus 2.8%. The market is not rewarding earnings beats indiscriminately. It is rewarding companies that demonstrate top-line momentum alongside bottom-line discipline.

This pattern is even more pronounced within the TECH+ group. Companies that beat earnings but miss revenue see a positive price reaction of 4.5%, which is counterintuitive until you examine the composition. The market appears to be giving technology companies significant latitude on revenue as long as the earnings beat is large enough. But the penalty for a combined miss is extreme, at minus 11.9% for TECH+ companies that miss on both metrics. The market is pricing in a winner-take-most dynamic where the bar is high, and the consequences for falling short are disproportionate.

For portfolio managers, this means that the traditional approach of buying broad earnings momentum is insufficient. The market is now discriminating between quality of earnings, not just quantity. Companies that are growing through genuine revenue expansion and margin improvement are rewarded. Companies that are relying on cost-cutting or financial engineering to hit earnings targets are being penalized, especially if revenue growth is absent.

## The Health Care and Energy Sectors Are Sending Contrarian Signals That Deserve Attention

Not all the news is about technology dominance. Two sectors stand out for the wrong reasons, and both may offer strategic opportunities for investors willing to look beyond the consensus.

Health Care is expected to deliver negative EPS growth of 3.8% in 1Q26, making it the worst-performing sector in the index. However, this aggregate figure is heavily distorted by a single company, Merck. Excluding Merck, Health Care EPS growth is 8.9%, which is still below the index average but far less alarming. The margin picture is worse. Health Care margins are expected to decline by 11.7% year over year, the largest margin contraction of any sector. This suggests that the sector is facing genuine cost pressures, possibly from labor, regulatory changes, or patent cliffs. But the median EPS growth for Health Care is 9.6%, which is higher than the weighted average. This dispersion implies that the weakness is concentrated in a few large names, while the broader sector is holding up reasonably well.

Energy is a different story. The sector is expected to deliver zero EPS growth year over year, with margins declining by 3.8%. For a sector that typically benefits from inflation and supply constraints, this is a surprisingly weak performance. The implied message is that energy companies are facing headwinds from moderating commodity prices, increased capital expenditure requirements, or both. However, the median EPS growth for Energy is 16.0%, significantly higher than the weighted average of zero. This suggests that smaller energy companies are outperforming the large integrated majors, which may be a signal about where the value lies in the sector.

The contrarian implication is that both Health Care and Energy may be priced for worse outcomes than the data supports. If the median company in these sectors is growing earnings at 9.6% and 16.0% respectively, while the index is pricing in a 28.4% growth rate, there may be a valuation disconnect. The market is extrapolating the weakness of a few large names to the entire sector. That is often a recipe for mean reversion.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Margin Expansion Beyond TECH+

For all the detail in the 1Q26 earnings data, there is a critical question that remains unresolved. The TECH+ group is delivering margin expansion of 34.1%. The rest of the market is delivering margin expansion of 2.6%. The gap is enormous. But the report does not provide a framework for assessing how long this margin divergence can persist.

There are plausible arguments on both sides. On one hand, the TECH+ companies are investing heavily in AI infrastructure, which is a capital-intensive activity that may compress margins in future quarters before the revenue benefits materialize. The current margin expansion may be a temporary phenomenon driven by the timing of revenue recognition versus capital expenditure. On the other hand, these companies have demonstrated an ability to generate operating leverage at scale that is unmatched in the rest of the economy. If AI adoption accelerates, the revenue growth may outpace the investment cycle, sustaining margin expansion for several more years.

The report also does not address the question of what happens when the comparison base normalizes. The 28.4% EPS growth in 1Q26 is being compared against a relatively weak 1Q25, which had 10.6% growth. As the base effects become more challenging in the second half of the year, the year-over-year growth rates may decelerate even if absolute earnings continue to rise. The full-year 2026 consensus estimate of 22.2% EPS growth is already lower than the 1Q26 run rate, suggesting that the market is pricing in some deceleration. But the magnitude of that deceleration and its sector distribution are not fully explored in the data presented.

This analytical gap is not a flaw in the report. It is an invitation for investors to do their own work. The question of margin sustainability is the single most important variable for equity returns over the next 12 to 18 months, and it requires a forward-looking view that goes beyond the current earnings season.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in a Two-Speed Market

Given the evidence from 1Q26, investors need a systematic approach to portfolio construction that accounts for the structural earnings divergence. The following framework is designed to translate the earnings data into actionable decisions.

First, assess your exposure to the TECH+ group not as a sector bet but as a concentration bet. If your portfolio is benchmarked to the S&P 500, your effective exposure to the Big 6 TECH+ companies is likely higher than you realize, because their weight in the index has grown alongside their earnings outperformance. The earnings data confirms that this concentration is justified by fundamentals, but it also means that you are taking on single-factor risk. A regulatory shock, a shift in AI investment sentiment, or a margin compression event in the TECH+ group would have an outsized impact on your portfolio. Consider whether you need to hedge this concentration through options, factor tilts, or explicit underweighting.

Second, evaluate the rest of the market through the lens of margin trajectory, not just earnings growth. The data shows that sectors with negative or near-zero margin expansion, such as Health Care, Energy, and Non-Cyclicals, are being penalized by the market regardless of their revenue growth. If you are considering adding exposure outside of TECH+, prioritize sectors where margins are expanding. Financials, with margin expansion of 13.7% and EPS growth of 24.7%, offer a compelling combination of growth and relative value. Materials, with margin expansion of 31.4% and EPS growth of 42.2%, are even more attractive on a pure earnings basis, though the cyclicality of that sector requires careful timing.

Third, use the surprise data as a real-time signal for position sizing. The price action around earnings announcements reveals that the market is punishing combined misses more severely than in past cycles. This means that the downside risk of holding a stock through its earnings report is higher than the historical average, especially for TECH+ names. If you are constructing a portfolio with a low turnover mandate, consider whether you want to hold positions through earnings season or whether it is more prudent to reduce exposure ahead of reports for companies with high valuation multiples and low revenue visibility.

Fourth, pay attention to the median versus weighted growth rates. In four out of eleven sectors, the median company is expected to outgrow the cap-weighted index. This is a classic signal that the largest companies in those sectors are dragging down the average. In Energy, the median EPS growth is 16.0% while the weighted average is zero. In Health Care, the median is 9.6% while the weighted average is negative 3.8%. This dispersion suggests that there are attractive opportunities in the mid-cap and small-cap segments of these sectors that are being overlooked by a market that focuses on the largest names.

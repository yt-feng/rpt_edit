# The Coming $410 Billion Passive Demand Wave from Big Tech IPOs Will Reshape Index Composition, Not Mag7 Dominance

The arrival of three large private AI companies to public markets represents the most significant shift in equity supply and demand dynamics since the dot-com era. A global investment bank report estimates that once lock-up periods expire, likely in the first half of 2026, passive funds will need to deploy approximately $410 billion to accommodate index inclusion and rebalancing. This is not a story about dilution or insider selling creating downward pressure on stock prices. It is a story about mechanical index mechanics, the resilience of passive fund inflows, and a structural transformation in how equity indices will be composed. The controlling insight is this: the magnitude of passive buying required is so large that it will be absorbed not by forced selling of existing holdings, but by a combination of companies releasing repurchased shares, non-strategic investors transacting with passive funds, and the continued strong inflow of capital into passive equity vehicles. The real strategic question for investors is not whether the market can handle these IPOs, but how the rebalancing will redistribute index weight away from the largest technology incumbents and toward these new entrants, and what that means for relative performance.

Why now matters. The report's analysis comes at a moment when equity futures flows show a persistent rotation away from Chinese technology and toward Korean and Taiwanese tech, while equity ETF flows show only tentative signs of a pause. Commodities ex-gold positioning sits at the 90th percentile, and Japan equities positioning is at the 90th percentile as well. Against this backdrop of stretched positioning in certain asset classes, the prospect of a single passive demand event worth over $400 billion introduces a powerful stabilizing force for equity markets broadly, but also creates specific winners and losers within index constituents. The report's data on free float evolution after IPOs, combined with its estimates of index inclusion demand, provides a framework for understanding why this event is fundamentally different from previous large IPOs.

## The Post-IPO Free Float Jump Is Primarily a Mechanical Reclassification, Not a Signal of Insider Selling

The most common misinterpretation of IPO aftermarket dynamics is that the sharp increase in free float shares after lock-up expiry reflects insiders and early investors rushing to sell. The report's analysis of ten of the largest IPOs since 2010 demonstrates that this conclusion is largely incorrect. The average free float share across these IPOs hovers around 45% for the first 100 trading days, then jumps to approximately 75% around day 150, which corresponds to the typical six-month lock-up period. Yet the average insider share declines only modestly, from about 7% at listing to around 5.3% after 250 trading days. The divergence between the two trends is telling.

The jump in free float is driven by index providers reclassifying holdings that were previously restricted. Once lock-up periods expire, the stakes of non-insiders—non-strategic investors with stakes below 10%, and employees not classified as insiders—are counted toward the free float regardless of whether those holders sell or retain their positions. This is a mechanical reclassification, not a supply event. The practical implication for the three anticipated AI IPOs is critical: the initial free float at listing will be very low, likely around 4% according to the report's assumptions, but the eventual free float after lock-up expiry will converge toward 30% to 50%. The increase is largely a function of index methodology, not shareholder behavior.

The so-what for investors is that fears of a massive overhang of insider selling depressing post-IPO stock prices are overstated. The report's historical data shows considerable heterogeneity at the company level. Meta's free float rose from 65% to 95% over 250 days, while Lyft's jumped from 10% to 70%. But insider shares for many companies, including Coinbase and Palantir, showed little reduction over the same period. This suggests that the three AI companies, which have likely already provided opportunities for non-insiders to sell through structured tender offers or secondary market transactions before listing, may see even less insider selling pressure than the historical average. The companies themselves may also repurchase shares ahead of listing to release them after lock-up expiry, further absorbing any potential supply.

## The $410 Billion Passive Demand Estimate Is Conservative and Anchored to a Specific Free Float Assumption

The report's central quantitative estimate deserves close scrutiny. Assuming the three IPOs have a combined market capitalization of approximately $3.5 trillion, and that at listing only 4% of shares count toward free float, initial passive rebalancing demand would total around $40 billion. After the six-month lock-up period, assuming free float rises to 40%—the midpoint of the 30% to 50% range—additional passive demand would reach $370 billion, bringing the cumulative total to $410 billion.

This estimate is built on three assumptions that investors should question. First, the combined market cap of $3.5 trillion may prove conservative or aggressive depending on how the IPOs are priced and how the companies perform between now and listing. Second, the free float assumption of 40% after lock-up is a midpoint estimate; if the actual free float settles at 30%, passive demand would be lower, and if it reaches 50%, demand would be higher. Third, the timeline of inclusion matters. The report notes that index providers may follow an expedited timeline for these IPOs, which could compress the passive buying into a shorter window and amplify its market impact.

The report's framework for how this demand is accommodated is equally important. The $410 billion in passive buying does not require an equivalent amount of new equity issuance. Instead, it can be satisfied by companies releasing previously repurchased shares back into the market, and by non-strategic investors selling their stakes to passive funds. This is a transfer of ownership between holders, not a net increase in equity supply. The report's earlier analysis of zero net equity issuance in 2025 remains intact, because the IPOs themselves do not change the aggregate supply-demand balance—they simply shift the composition of who holds the shares.

## Index Rebalancing Will Reduce Mag7 Weights, But Strong Inflows May Prevent Forced Selling

A natural concern for holders of the Magnificent Seven stocks is that index rebalancing to accommodate the new IPOs will force passive funds to sell these large-cap technology names. The report's analysis provides a nuanced answer. At the index level, the increased weights of the three new companies will come at the expense of the companies they replace and the largest tech incumbents. However, the report argues that this rebalancing does not necessarily require actual selling of Mag7 stocks by passive funds.

The key variable is the continued pattern of strong and steady inflows into passive equity funds. If inflows remain robust, passive fund managers can deploy a disproportionate amount of new capital into the newly listed companies to reach their increased index weights, without needing to sell existing positions. The report's flow data supports this possibility: the four-week average for all equity fund flows stands at $11.6 billion, and the 2025 average is $8.1 billion, with US equities alone averaging $3.5 billion year-to-date. If these inflow trends persist through 2026, the $410 billion in passive demand can be largely funded by new money rather than portfolio churn.

The so-what is that Mag7 holders should not automatically assume that index rebalancing will create downward pressure on these stocks. The risk is asymmetric: if inflows slow or reverse, the rebalancing could force selling, but the base case suggested by current flow momentum is that new money will absorb the demand. The report's cross-asset positioning data also shows that technology sector positioning is at the 79th percentile, indicating that the market is already positioned for tech outperformance. The addition of three large AI companies could reinforce this positioning rather than undermine it.

## The Historical Free Float Convergence Between Nasdaq and S&P 500 Offers a Cautionary Tale

The report's Figure 5, showing the free float to total market cap ratios for the S&P 500 and Nasdaq composite from 2006 to 2026, reveals a long-term convergence. The Nasdaq's free float ratio has risen from about 89.5% in 2006 to approximately 93.8% in 2026, while the S&P 500's ratio has declined slightly from 95.5% to 93.8%. This convergence reflects the maturing of the technology sector, increased institutional ownership, and the growing overlap between the two indices as the S&P 500 has become more tech-heavy.

However, the report notes that this convergence has not been linear. From 2020 to late 2021, the Nasdaq's free float ratio declined significantly amid a tech IPO and SPAC boom that brought many newly public companies with large founder and venture capital stakes. From 2022 to 2024, the trend reversed as lock-up periods expired and strategic investors sold or were reclassified. The inclusion of the three anticipated IPOs could cause another temporary decline in index free float ratios, as these companies enter equity indices with lower free float ratios than the current index average.

The implication for portfolio construction is that investors should expect a period of elevated index-level free float volatility. This is not a structural problem but a transitional one. Over time, as the new companies mature and their free float ratios converge toward the index average, the free float ratio of the Nasdaq and S&P 500 will likely resume its upward trend. The report's analysis suggests that the free float ratio of the Nasdaq has already risen to match the S&P 500, which is a remarkable structural shift that underscores how deeply technology has penetrated the broader market.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Sequencing of Insider Liquidation

While the report effectively debunks the myth that free float jumps are driven by insider selling, it leaves several important questions unresolved. First, the report acknowledges considerable heterogeneity in insider share evolution at the company level, but does not provide a framework for predicting which of the three AI companies will see more or less insider selling. The data shows that for some IPOs like Coinbase and Palantir, insider shares barely changed over 250 days, while for others like Snap, insider shares declined from 30% to 24%. What explains this variation? The report does not offer a systematic answer, leaving investors to guess whether the three AI companies will behave more like Coinbase or more like Snap.

Second, the report's analysis of non-insider selling is limited. The mechanical reclassification of non-insider stakes into free float after lock-up expiry does not tell us whether these holders will actually sell. The report notes that non-insiders have had opportunities to sell before listing through structured tender offers, but it does not quantify how much of their stakes have already been monetized. If a significant portion of non-insider shares have already been sold in secondary markets, the post-lock-up supply could be smaller than the free float increase suggests.

Third, the report does not address the potential for active fund managers to front-run the passive rebalancing. If active managers anticipate the $410 billion in passive demand, they may buy the three stocks ahead of index inclusion, creating price appreciation that could alter the market capitalization assumptions underlying the passive demand estimate. This dynamic could create a self-fulfilling prophecy where the stocks rise in anticipation of passive buying, which in turn increases the required passive buying.

Fourth, the report's analysis of regional flow rotation—the shift from Chinese tech toward Korean and Taiwanese tech—is mentioned but not integrated into the main IPO thesis. How does this regional rotation interact with the AI IPO demand? If capital is flowing out of Chinese equities and into other Asian tech markets, does that create a more favorable environment for the three AI IPOs, or does it divert attention and capital away from them?

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Pre-IPO and Post-IPO Phases

For institutional investors, the report's analysis translates into a four-phase decision framework.

Phase one, the pre-IPO period, requires investors to assess the initial free float and the likely passive demand at listing. The report estimates $40 billion in initial passive demand based on a 4% free float assumption. Investors should monitor the actual free float disclosed in the IPO prospectuses and adjust their passive demand estimates accordingly. If the initial free float is higher than 4%, the initial passive demand will be larger, but the post-lock-up demand will be smaller, all else equal.

Phase two, the listing to lock-up expiry period, is the window where the free float is artificially low and the stocks may trade at premiums to their intrinsic value due to scarcity. The report's historical data shows that free float shares increase only slowly until lock-up expiry, meaning that supply is constrained. Investors who want to build positions before the passive demand wave should consider the risk that they are buying into a scarcity premium that will dissipate after lock-up expiry.

Phase three, the lock-up expiry event itself, is the moment of maximum uncertainty. The report argues that the free float jump is mechanical, but the market may not fully internalize this distinction. There is a risk of a temporary sell-off as traders anticipate insider selling that does not materialize. The report's historical data on insider shares suggests that the actual selling is modest, but the market's perception may differ. Investors should be prepared for volatility and potentially attractive entry points.

Phase four, the post-lock-up period, is where the $370 billion in additional passive demand is deployed. The report's key insight is that this demand can be absorbed without forced selling of Mag7 stocks if passive inflows remain strong. Investors should monitor weekly equity fund flow data, particularly US equity flows, as a leading indicator of whether the rebalancing will be orderly or disruptive. If inflows slow, the rebalancing could create downward pressure on Mag7 stocks that offsets the upward pressure from the new IPOs.

The report's cross-asset positioning data provides additional context. Equity positioning is at the 72nd percentile, suggesting that the market is already leaning bullish. Commodities ex-gold at the 90th percentile and Japan equities at the 90th percentile indicate that some asset classes are stretched. The AI IPOs could provide a catalyst for a rotation out of these stretched positions and into the new listings, but that rotation would depend on the relative attractiveness of the IPOs versus existing high-conviction positions.

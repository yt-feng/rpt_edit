# Data Center Markets Are Not Created Equal: Why Location Strategy Determines Which Builders Will Win

The most important question in data center investing today is not whether demand will grow, but where that demand will materialize and who is positioned to capture it. The answer is not uniform, and the market is rapidly bifurcating into three distinct tiers—Major Metros, Minor Metros, and Rural markets—each with fundamentally different economics, risk profiles, and competitive dynamics. Investors who treat all data center exposure as equivalent are making a category error.

This distinction matters now more than ever because the narrative around data centers has become dangerously monolithic. Daily headlines about power delays, NIMBYism, and supply chain constraints create the impression of a single industry facing a single set of challenges. In reality, the experience of building and operating in Northern Virginia bears almost no resemblance to building in rural Ohio. The constraints that dominate headlines are concentrated in the most desirable markets, and those constraints are actually a moat for the incumbents who already own land, permits, and interconnection infrastructure there.

A global investment bank report provides the most granular framework we have seen for understanding this market stratification. The report breaks the U.S. data center landscape into three archetypes, then dives deep into three specific markets—Northern Virginia, Phoenix, and Atlanta—to show how the framework plays out in practice. The core insight is that the established public data center companies, particularly Digital Realty and Equinix, are overwhelmingly concentrated in the markets that are hardest to enter and therefore most valuable. Their footprints are not an accident; they are a strategic asset that new entrants cannot easily replicate.

The strategic question for investors is whether to bet on the incumbents who own scarce urban capacity or on the developers pushing into cheaper, faster-to-build rural markets. The report's data suggests the answer depends on time horizon, risk tolerance, and conviction about the evolution of AI workloads. But the framework itself is clear: market selection is the single most important determinant of risk and return in data center investing.

## The Three Archetypes of Data Center Markets Are Defined by a Single Trade-Off: Latency Versus Power Versus Cost

The data center market is not a monolith. It is a spectrum defined by how developers and customers trade off three variables: latency sensitivity, power availability, and cost. Major Metro markets optimize for proximity to users and enterprise customers. Rural markets optimize for scale and cheap power. Minor Metro markets sit in between as an equilibrium zone that captures overflow from both sides.

Major Metros—typically the top eight to ten markets in North America—are the most important for latency-sensitive use cases, including AI inferencing and enterprise colocation. These markets are well established and extraordinarily difficult to build in. Power queues run four to seven years. Build costs range from $12 billion to $15 billion per gigawatt. Hyperscale pricing runs $120 to $150 per kilowatt per month, while colocation pricing reaches $160 to $230. Cap rates are tight, around 4.5 to 6.5 percent for hyperscale and 5.5 to 6.5 percent for colocation. Vacancy rates in the top markets are below 1 percent. Existing capacity and in-progress, permitted builds are therefore extremely valuable.

Minor Metros represent the overflow zone. These markets have marginally less latency sensitivity and marginally more price sensitivity. Build costs are lower, at $9 billion to $12 billion per gigawatt, and power queues are shorter, at three to five years. Pricing is slightly below Major Metros, but still attractive. The report notes significant growth in these markets over the last five years and anticipates that growth continuing as hyperscalers exhaust available capacity in primary markets.

Rural markets are a different animal entirely. They are optimized for AI training workloads that are latency-insensitive and highly price-sensitive. These markets offer the fastest power availability—one to three years—and the lowest build costs, at $7 billion to $9 billion per gigawatt. Hyperscale pricing drops to $70 to $120 per kilowatt per month. But there is a trade-off: rural markets have less clear visibility to sustained demand, more speculative builds, and less experienced developers. Cap rates are higher, at 6.5 to 9 percent or more, reflecting this uncertainty.

The report's data on planned capacity is revealing. Rural markets have 37 gigawatts of planned capacity, compared to 25.7 gigawatts for Major Metros and 26.1 gigawatts for Minor Metros. This suggests that the industry is betting heavily on rural expansion. But the question is whether that demand will materialize as expected, or whether the scarcity premium in Major Metros will continue to widen.

## Northern Virginia, Phoenix, and Atlanta Illustrate Three Distinct Competitive Dynamics Within the Same Framework

The report's deep dive into three specific markets shows how the archetype framework plays out in practice. Northern Virginia is the dominant global hub, with approximately 8 gigawatts of existing supply and 5 gigawatts under construction. Power queues are reportedly in the seven-year range. Pricing has only gone up over the last several years, currently around $217 per kilowatt per month. Vacancy is stable below 1 percent. This is a market where incumbency is everything. The barriers to entry—land acquisition, permitting, power procurement, community relations—are so high that new entrants face years of delays and billions in costs.

Phoenix represents a different model: a power-advantaged growth market. It has 2.1 gigawatts of existing capacity and 2.2 gigawatts planned. Pricing is lower than Northern Virginia, at roughly $175 per kilowatt per month, but vacancy is also below 2 percent. Phoenix benefits from relatively lower costs, more available land, and faster power timelines than Northern Virginia. It is increasingly important as an alternative for hyperscalers who cannot get capacity in primary markets. But it has less network density and ecosystem depth than Northern Virginia, which limits its appeal for latency-sensitive enterprise colocation.

Atlanta sits in between as a rapidly scaling secondary hub. It has approximately 2 gigawatts of existing capacity and over 3 gigawatts planned. Pricing is similar to Phoenix, around $175 per kilowatt per month, with vacancy around 2 percent. Atlanta combines proximity to enterprise demand with improving power access and economics. The report notes that both Digital Realty and Equinix have spoken about large hyperscale-focused builds outside Atlanta, which the report views favorably given their experience in Major Metros.

The data on net absorption is striking. Atlanta saw 706 megawatts of net absorption in the second half of 2024 alone, more than any other period in any of the three markets. Northern Virginia saw 1,102 megawatts in the first half of 2025. These numbers confirm that demand is not slowing; it is accelerating. But the distribution of that demand is shifting, and the winners will be those who have positioned themselves in the right markets at the right time.

## The Report Leaves Several Critical Questions Unanswered, Particularly Around the Sustainability of Rural Demand

The report is unusually clear about what it does and does not claim. It provides a rigorous framework for comparing market types and a detailed snapshot of three specific markets. But it leaves several important questions open, and investors should be aware of these gaps.

The most significant open question is whether the massive planned capacity in rural markets will be absorbed. The report shows 37 gigawatts of planned capacity in rural markets, compared to 25.7 gigawatts in Major Metros. This implies that the industry expects the majority of future growth to come from rural areas. But the report also notes that rural markets have "not as clear visibility to sustained demand." The facilities are larger, the builds are more speculative, and the developers are less experienced. If AI training workloads shift toward inferencing, which is more latency-sensitive, the demand for rural capacity could soften. If power costs rise or renewable energy mandates change, the economics could shift.

A second open question is how NIMBYism will evolve. The report notes an increase in community pushback in Northern Virginia and Phoenix, driven in part by rising power costs. This is an emerging risk for established builders, but the report does not quantify how severe it could become or whether it could spread to other markets. If NIMBYism becomes a material constraint on supply growth, the scarcity premium in Major Metros could widen further. If it accelerates permitting delays, it could push even more demand into rural markets.

A third question is about the competitive dynamics between hyperscalers and data center REITs. The report shows that Amazon and Microsoft have the most ambitious capacity expansion plans, each with 5 to 6 gigawatts slotted. Digital Realty has 317 megawatts of future capacity in the top markets, compared to Amazon's 5,047 megawatts. This raises the question of whether hyperscalers will increasingly bypass third-party data center providers and build their own capacity, particularly in rural markets where land and power are easier to secure. The report does not address this directly, but it is a critical consideration for investors in Digital Realty and Equinix.

## Investors Need a Decision Framework That Maps Their Conviction on AI Workload Evolution to Market Exposure

The report's framework is most useful when translated into a decision framework for investors. The key variable is conviction about the evolution of AI workloads. If you believe that AI will remain heavily dependent on training for the next several years, and that latency-sensitive inferencing will be a smaller share of demand, then rural markets look attractive. The faster power availability, lower costs, and higher cap rates could generate strong returns, particularly if hyperscalers continue to build their own capacity.

If you believe that inferencing will become the dominant workload as AI becomes more embedded in enterprise applications and consumer products, then Major Metros are the place to be. Latency matters for inferencing in a way it does not for training. Enterprise colocation customers will not move to rural Ohio to save on power costs if it means slower response times for their customers. In this scenario, the incumbents who own capacity in Northern Virginia, Silicon Valley, and other Major Metros have an irreplaceable asset.

The middle ground is Minor Metros and secondary hUBS like Atlanta and Phoenix. These markets offer a balance of growth and risk. They are easier to build in than Major Metros but have more demand visibility than rural markets. They also capture overflow from Major Metros as those markets become constrained. The report's data suggests that these markets will see significant growth over the next five years, and the established builders like Digital Realty and Equinix are well positioned there.

The report's valuation framework is worth noting. The report values Digital Realty at 27 times its 2027 estimated adjusted funds from operations, and Equinix at 25 times. These multiples reflect the scarcity value of their portfolios, but they also imply that the market is pricing in significant growth. If the growth does not materialize, or if it shifts to rural markets where these companies have less exposure, the multiples could compress.

## The Most Valuable Insight From This Report Is That Market Selection Is a Strategic Choice, Not a Passive Outcome

The data center industry is often discussed as if it were a single asset class, but the report makes clear that it is actually three distinct asset classes with different risk-return profiles. The established public companies have chosen to concentrate in Major Metros and Minor Metros, accepting longer timelines and higher costs in exchange for better visibility and pricing power. The new entrants and hyperscalers are pushing into rural markets, accepting higher uncertainty in exchange for faster execution and lower costs.

Neither strategy is obviously superior. The answer depends on time horizon, risk tolerance, and conviction about how AI workloads will evolve. But the framework itself is invaluable. It forces investors to ask the right questions: Which markets does this company operate in? How hard are those markets to enter? What is the balance of supply and demand? How much of the projected growth is in rural markets versus Major Metros?

The report's analysis of Northern Virginia, Phoenix, and Atlanta shows that even within the same tier, markets have distinct dynamics. Northern Virginia is the gold standard, but it is also the most constrained. Phoenix offers faster growth but less ecosystem depth. Atlanta is the up-and-comer, benefiting from proximity to enterprise demand and improving power access. Each requires a different investment thesis.

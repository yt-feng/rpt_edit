# The Data Center Market Is Not One Market: Location Strategy Now Determines Which Builders Will Win

The data center industry is being discussed in aggregate terms that obscure the most important strategic distinction in the sector today. Headlines about power delays, NIMBY opposition, and supply chain bottlenecks create the impression of a single market under strain. This framing is misleading. The US data center market is not one market. It is three distinct markets operating under fundamentally different economic, operational, and risk profiles. The companies that understand this differentiation and position their capital accordingly will separate themselves from the pack over the next five years.

The most important strategic question for investors and operators is not whether data center demand is real. It is whether a given player's footprint is concentrated in the markets that will sustain pricing power, absorb new supply, and withstand regulatory friction. The answer to that question explains why two of the largest publicly traded data center companies continue to deliver on their build projections while many developers face mounting delays and cost overruns. It also explains why the next wave of capacity growth will not be distributed evenly across the country.

This article draws on a detailed analysis of the U.S. data center landscape, comparing market archetypes and providing deep dives into three of the top markets: Northern Virginia, Phoenix, and Atlanta. The argument is straightforward: Major Metro markets are the only segment where pricing power, demand visibility, and barriers to entry align to create durable value. Everything else is a trade-off that must be evaluated on its own terms.

## The Three Archetypes of Data Center Markets Are Defined by a Single Trade-Off: Latency Versus Power Versus Cost

Every data center location decision comes down to how a customer balances three variables: how close the facility needs to be to end users, how much power it requires, and how much the customer is willing to pay. These three forces sort the market into three distinct archetypes that operate with different economics, different risk profiles, and different competitive dynamics.

Major Metro markets are optimized for latency-sensitive workloads. These are the top eight to ten urban hUBS in North America, where inference processing, enterprise colocation, and financial services applications require proximity to dense population centers and network interconnection points. The pricing in these markets is the highest in the industry, with hyperscale leases ranging from $120 to $150 per kilowatt per month and colocation pricing reaching $160 to $230 per kilowatt per month. But the barriers to entry are correspondingly severe. Build costs run $12 billion to $15 billion per gigawatt of capacity. Power queues stretch four to seven years. Zoning battles and local opposition are structural features, not temporary annoyances.

Minor Metro markets sit in the middle. These are smaller Cities or the far outskirts of Major Metros, where customers accept slightly higher latency or slightly less interconnection density in exchange for lower pricing and faster power availability. Hyperscale pricing ranges from $90 to $130 per kilowatt per month. Build costs are lower at $9 billion to $12 billion per gigawatt. Power queues run three to five years. These markets have seen significant growth over the last five years, and that trend is likely to continue as overflow demand from Major Metros seeks viable alternatives.

Rural markets are the most distinct category. They are built for training workloads that are latency-insensitive and highly price-sensitive. These facilities tend to be much larger, often speculative, and developed by less experienced operators. The positives are real: the lowest build costs at $7 billion to $9 billion per gigawatt, the fastest power availability at one to three years, and hyperscale pricing as low as $70 to $120 per kilowatt per month. But the negatives are equally significant. There is no clear visibility to sustained demand. Cap rates are the highest in the industry, ranging from 6.5 percent to 9 percent or more. And colocation is essentially absent. These markets carry the highest execution risk and the least pricing power.

The so what is this: the industry is not experiencing a uniform supply constraint. It is experiencing a bifurcation in which the most valuable capacity is becoming harder to build, while the easiest capacity to build is becoming less valuable. This divergence has profound implications for which companies and which assets will generate superior returns.

## The Public Data Center Companies Are Insulated From Market Noise Because Their Footprints Are Concentrated in the Markets That Matter Most

The two largest publicly traded data center companies, Digital Realty and Equinix, have footprints that are overwhelmingly concentrated in Major and Minor Metro markets. This is not an accident. It is the result of years of deliberate capital allocation that prioritized location quality over scale for its own sake. The consequence is that these companies are among the most insulated players in the industry from the market noise that dominates headlines.

Consider the dynamics in Northern Virginia, the largest data center market in the United States. The region has approximately 8 gigawatts of existing supply, roughly 5 gigawatts under construction, and power queues that reportedly extend seven years. Pricing has only increased over the last several years, currently around $217 per kilowatt per month. Vacancy is stable below 1 percent. This is a market where existing capacity and in-progress, permitted builds are extraordinarily valuable. The barriers to entry are so high that new competition cannot easily emerge. The established builders who already have land, power allocations, and community relationships in place hold a structural advantage that is difficult to replicate.

The same logic applies to Atlanta, another top market that is growing quickly. Atlanta currently has roughly 2 gigawatts of existing capacity and approximately 3 gigawatts under construction. Pricing is $175 per kilowatt per month with vacancy around 2 percent. Both Digital Realty and Equinix have spoken about large hyperscale-focused builds outside Atlanta. This strategy makes sense because market dynamics are favorable, both companies are experienced builders in Major Metros, and execution risk is relatively limited compared to building in rural markets with no track record of sustained demand.

The insight here is that location strategy is not just about where demand exists today. It is about where the barriers to entry are high enough to protect pricing power and where the development ecosystem is mature enough to manage the inevitable friction of zoning, power procurement, and community relations. The public data center companies have built their portfolios around this principle, and it is why they continue to deliver on their build projections while others struggle.

## Northern Virginia, Phoenix, and Atlanta Illustrate Three Distinct Strategic Positions Within the Major Metro Category

Even within the Major Metro category, there are meaningful differences that investors need to understand. Northern Virginia, Phoenix, and Atlanta represent three distinct archetypes of Major Metro market, each shaped by a different balance of power availability, cost, and ecosystem maturity.

Northern Virginia is the dominant global hub. It has unmatched scale, dense interconnection, and the highest concentration of hyperscale customers in the world. The pricing is premium, the cap rates are the lowest in the industry at 4.5 percent to 6 percent for hyperscale and 5.5 percent to 6.5 percent for colocation, and the supply conditions are the tightest anywhere. But the trade-off is that power timelines are the longest and barriers to entry are the highest. This is a market for incumbents, not newcomers.

Phoenix is a power-advantaged growth market. It attracts large hyperscale deployments because of relatively lower costs and more available land. The ecosystem is less dense than Northern Virginia, but the power availability is better and the development risk is lower. Phoenix is increasingly important as an alternative to constrained Tier 1 hUBS, but it lacks the same network density and interconnection depth.

Atlanta sits in between. It is a rapidly scaling secondary hub that combines proximity to enterprise demand with improving power access and economics. Atlanta is increasingly capturing spillover from primary markets while maintaining a more balanced profile across pricing, growth, and development risk. The vacancy rate has declined significantly from over 13 percent in late 2023 to approximately 2 percent today, and net absorption has surged.

The pattern across all three markets is the same: vacancy rates have declined to remarkably low levels, available capacity has shrunk, and pricing has risen. In Northern Virginia, vacancy is below 1 percent. In Phoenix, it is around 1.3 percent. In Atlanta, it is approximately 2 percent. These are not markets where new supply is going to destroy pricing power. They are markets where the existing operators have pricing power that is likely to persist.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question It Does Not Fully Answer: How Will the Market Resolve the Tension Between Hyperscale Demand and Power Availability?

For all the detail in the analysis, there is a question that remains open. The report documents the scale of planned capacity across the top markets, with Northern Virginia and Chicago each planning roughly 5 gigawatts of new capacity and Atlanta planning over 3 gigawatts. It also documents that the largest providers, Amazon and Microsoft, each have 5 to 6 gigawatts of future capacity slotted. But the report does not fully resolve how these ambitious plans will be executed given the power constraints that are already evident.

The power queues in Major Metros are four to seven years. Build costs are rising. NIMBY opposition is increasing, particularly in Northern Virginia and Phoenix, driven in part by rising power costs that affect residential customers. The report notes that the established builders will manage this appropriately, but it also identifies this as an emerging risk.

The open question is whether the power and regulatory constraints will ultimately force a larger share of demand into Minor Metro and Rural markets than the current data suggests. If power availability in Major Metros becomes the binding constraint, then the overflow into secondary markets could accelerate faster than expected. That would benefit developers with exposure to those markets, but it would also introduce more uncertainty about sustained demand and pricing power.

Alternatively, the hyperscale customers themselves may solve this problem by investing directly in power generation and transmission infrastructure. Several of the largest technology companies have already begun to pursue this strategy. If that trend accelerates, it could change the competitive dynamics of the market in ways that the current analysis does not fully capture.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating Data Center Investments Based on Market Archetypes

For investors trying to make sense of this landscape, the most useful framework is to evaluate every data center investment against three criteria: market type, builder quality, and demand visibility.

First, determine which market archetype the asset belongs to. Is it a Major Metro with high pricing power and high barriers to entry? A Minor Metro with moderate pricing and moderate execution risk? Or a Rural market with low pricing and high demand uncertainty? The answer to this question determines the appropriate valuation multiple and the key risks to monitor.

Second, assess the quality of the builder. The report makes clear that experience matters in Major Metro markets. The established players have teams that understand zoning, power procurement, and community relations. They have relationships with utilities and local governments. They have track records of delivering on their build projections. Less experienced developers face higher execution risk, particularly in markets where the regulatory environment is complex.

Third, evaluate the visibility of demand. In Major Metros, demand visibility is high because vacancy rates are low and absorption is strong. In Rural markets, demand visibility is low because the facilities are often speculative and the customer base is concentrated. The best investments are those where demand visibility is high and the builder has a demonstrated ability to execute.

The so what is this: the data center market is not a homogenous asset class. It is a collection of distinct submarkets with different economics, different risks, and different return profiles. The companies that understand this and allocate capital accordingly will generate superior risk-adjusted returns. The companies that treat all data centers as the same will underperform.

# AI Transceiver Demand Is Not Just Strong—It Is Structurally Outstripping Supply, and the Market Is Underestimating How Long This Will Last

The most important signal for investors in the AI infrastructure buildout is not a revenue forecast or a product launch. It is a single sentence from the CEO of Lumentum in April 2026: the company expects to be "sold out through 2028" within two quarters. That statement, coming from the leader in indium phosphide (InP) optical components, is not a projection of demand. It is a confession of structural supply inadequacy. The implication is clear: the AI transceiver cycle is not peaking. It is accelerating into a multi-year shortage regime that the consensus has not yet fully priced in.

The evidence is mounting across the supply chain. Multiple vendors reported large orders, product launches, and technology demonstrations in March and April 2026, all pointing to continued ramps in 800G, accelerating adoption of 1.6T, and active development toward 3.2T. A global investment bank's research team has responded by raising its AI transceiver shipment forecasts dramatically: from 53 million units to 73 million units in 2026, from 71 million to 141 million in 2027, and from 80 million to 150 million in 2028. The total addressable market for AI transceivers is now projected to grow from $18 billion in 2025 to $102 billion in 2028—more than a fourfold increase in three years.

Yet the most critical insight from this data is not the magnitude of the upward revision. It is the persistence of the supply-demand imbalance. Even after these aggressive forecast increases, component shortages remain the key risk to volume growth. The market may be asking the wrong question. Instead of "How much demand is there?" the right question is "How long can supply constrain demand before the architecture of AI data centers changes?"

This article makes the argument that the AI transceiver cycle is entering a phase where supply, not demand, will determine the winners. The strategic implications for investors, technology buyers, and hyperscaler planners are profound—and the full report contains charts and data that make this case visually unassailable.

## The Industry Leader's "Sold Out Through 2028" Statement Signals That Demand Is Not Cyclical but Structural

When the CEO of the leading InP optical component supplier says his company could fill its entire order book through 2028 within two quarters, the market should stop treating AI transceiver demand as a cyclical uptick. Lumentum's commentary, echoed by Coherent's plans to scale InP capacity by 100% or more over two years, indicates that the bottleneck is not willingness to buy but ability to produce.

The data supports this. Lumentum projects an approximately 85% compound annual growth rate in InP optical lane volume demand through 2030. Their capacity model shows a widening gap between lane volume demand and lane capacity forecast across every calendar year from 2026 through 2030. In 2026, demand is roughly double available capacity. By 2030, demand is projected to be nearly three times capacity. This is not a temporary mismatch. It is a structural deficit that will define the competitive dynamics of the entire AI transceiver ecosystem.

The so-what is straightforward: companies that control InP capacity or have secured long-term supply agreements will enjoy pricing power and margin expansion that the consensus does not yet reflect. Companies dependent on spot procurement will face volume constraints and compressed margins. The strategic question for investors is not which transceiver vendor has the best product roadmap. It is which vendor has the best access to InP sUBStrates, EML lasers, and the other constrained components that Fabrinet recently identified as limiting datacom revenue.

## The Forecast Revision Magnitude Reveals That Consensus Is Still Playing Catch-Up, Not Leading

The global investment bank's upward revision to 2027 and 2028 shipments is not an incremental adjustment. It is a near-doubling of the 2027 forecast from 71 million to 141 million units, and an 87% increase to the 2028 forecast. The revision is overwhelmingly driven by 1.6T demand, where the 2027 forecast was raised by 233% and the 2028 forecast by 226%.

These numbers are sUBStantially more bullish than consensus. The bank notes that compared to LightCounting's 65% growth forecast for 2026, its own estimate for 800G plus 1.6T shipment growth exceeds 200%. For 2027, it forecasts shipment growth of 93%, also well above the industry research firm's estimate.

The pattern here is important. Consensus forecasts have consistently lagged actual demand in this cycle. The bank's own revision cycle—from 53 million to 73 million for 2026, and from 71 million to 141 million for 2027—suggests that even these new numbers may prove conservative if supply constraints ease faster than expected or if hyperscaler capex continues to accelerate. The top 11 cloud players are now estimated to spend approximately $735-795 billion on cash capex in 2026, representing roughly 60% year-over-year growth. Amazon alone is expected to devote approximately $200 billion to capex. These are not numbers that suggest a near-term peak.

The so-what for readers is that relying on consensus estimates for AI transceiver demand is likely to produce systematically conservative views. ## Silicon Photonics Is Accelerating Not Because It Is Better, but Because EML Shortages Are Forcing the Market to Accept Alternatives

According to LightCounting, silicon photonics' share of the optical transceiver market rose from 10% in 2018 to 33% in 2024 and is expected to become the dominant technology in 2026 for the first time. The conventional narrative is that this reflects superior performance. The more accurate interpretation is that it reflects a supply crisis.

EML (electro-absorption modulated laser) shortages are the key driver of silicon photonics adoption. When the industry cannot produce enough of the preferred laser technology, customers accept alternatives. This is not a vote of confidence in silicon photonics as a superior technology. It is a pragmatic response to a binding constraint. The bank's analysis notes that estimates for continuous-wave lasers as a percentage of 1.6T vary from 30% to over 50%, indicating that even within the industry, there is no consensus on how quickly the technology mix will shift.

The strategic implication is that silicon photonics vendors may benefit from a temporary tailwind that is not sustainable once EML capacity catches up. The risk for investors is mistaking a shortage-driven adoption cycle for a structural technology shift. The opportunity is identifying which silicon photonics players have the manufacturing scale and customer relationships to retain share even after the shortage eases. ## The CPO Debate Is Real, but Its Impact on Near-Term Transceiver Demand Is Overstated

Co-packaged optics (CPO) has generated significant investor attention, particularly after Nvidia's investment in Corning and the analyst day commentary about fiber and optics access. The bank's CPO estimates, however, tell a more measured story. Under the base case, CPO switch volume could grow from 4,712 units in 2025 to 103,889 units in 2028. Under the bull case, it reaches 50,000 units by 2028. Under the bear case, it reaches 250,000 units.

These numbers are not trivial, but they are small relative to the overall transceiver market. Corning's opportunity roadmap, detailed in the full report, shows that the bulk of the CPO impact starts in 2028. Views differ on CPO penetration by 2030, with estimates ranging from 10% to 50%. That is a wide range that reflects genuine analytical uncertainty.

The so-what is that CPO is a 2028-2030 story, not a 2026-2027 story. For the next two years, the dominant technology will remain pluggable transceivers at 800G and 1.6T. Investors who over-rotate toward CPO winners risk missing the much larger and more certain opportunity in the current generation of transceivers. ## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Elasticity of Hyperscaler Demand Under Supply Constraints

The report is exceptionally clear on the supply side. It documents capacity expansions from Lumentum, Coherent, Sumitomo, and Broadcom. It identifies the specific components in shortage: lasers, memory, and certain ASICs. It quantifies the gap between InP demand and capacity through 2030.

What the report does not fully answer is how hyperscalers will respond if supply constraints persist. Will they accept lower volumes and ration available transceivers to their highest-priority workloads? Will they accelerate their own investments in alternative technologies, including CPO or even custom silicon photonics? Will they accept higher prices, effectively transferring the shortage rent to transceiver vendors?

These questions matter because they determine whether the current pricing power enjoyed by transceiver companies is temporary or structural. If hyperscalers have inelastic demand—if they must have the transceivers to complete their AI data center buildouts regardless of price—then the shortage creates a pricing super-cycle that could drive margins well above historical norms. If hyperscalers have elastic demand and can sUBStitute or delay, then the pricing power is capped.

The evidence from Lumentum's commentary and the capex guidance from Amazon, Google, and Meta suggests inelastic demand. But the report does not make this argument explicitly. It leaves the question open, and that is precisely why readers who want to form their own view should examine the original data and charts.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Positioning in the AI Transceiver Cycle

Based on the report's analysis, investors should evaluate their AI transceiver exposure through three lenses.

First, assess supply chain access. Which companies have secured long-term agreements with InP suppliers? Which are dependent on spot markets? The difference will determine who ships volume and who does not. The report's price target increases for Eoptolink and Accelink reflect this logic, but the same framework applies across the ecosystem.

Second, evaluate technology mix exposure. The revision is overwhelmingly driven by 1.6T. Companies with strong 1.6T roadmaps and customer qualifications will benefit disproportionately. Companies still dependent on 800G will see slower growth and potentially margin compression as the technology lifecycle progresses.

Third, monitor the CPO timeline. For the next two years, CPO is a narrative, not a revenue driver. The risk is not that CPO disrupts the pluggable market. The risk is that investors overpay for CPO exposure and underweight the current cycle. The report's estimates provide a useful baseline for calibrating expectations.

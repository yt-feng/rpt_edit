# The Gas Turbine Cycle Is Not Peaking: Record Orders Signal a Structural Shift, Not a Cyclical Blip

The first quarter of 2026 delivered 29 GW of global gas turbine orders, a 39% year-over-year increase and the largest first-quarter total on record. This is not a temporary spike. It is the clearest signal yet that the power generation landscape is undergoing a structural re-rating driven by data center demand, AI infrastructure buildout, and the limits of intermittent renewables. The conventional wisdom that natural gas is a bridge fuel in terminal decline is being overturned by hard order data, OEM capacity expansions, and project economics that now favor gas-fired generation at a scale not seen in decades. Investors who treat this as a cyclical upswing risk missing the most consequential capital cycle in power equipment since the shale revolution.

The numbers are unambiguous. First-quarter 2026 orders nearly doubled the 15 GW recorded in the same period two years ago. North America alone accounted for 18.3 GW, with the United States representing 17.2 GW of that total. The US market is not just leading; it is dominating. The structural driver is the insatiable power demand from hyperscale data centers, which require firm, dispatchable baseload capacity that solar and wind alone cannot provide. The data center order from Quanta Services for 1.1 GW of Baker Hughes turbines, and Doosan's record 3.8 GW quarterly intake including a 2.7 GW US data center order, are not anomalies. They are the leading edge of a multiyear procurement wave.

The implication for investors is strategic, not tactical. The gas turbine supply chain is tightening, pricing is rising, and the Big Three OEMs are racing to expand capacity. Yet the market's pricing of these stocks still reflects a degree of cyclical skepticism. That gap between order momentum and valuation is where the opportunity lies, provided one understands which companies are positioned to capture margin expansion and which face execution risk.

## The US Market Is No Longer Just a Growth Engine; It Is the Structural Anchor of Global Gas Turbine Demand

The data shows that North America represented roughly 63% of global gas turbine orders in the first quarter. The US alone accounted for 59% of the global total. This concentration is unprecedented in the modern gas turbine cycle. Historically, the Middle East and Asia have been the primary growth regions, driven by petrochemical investment and industrial expansion. Today, the US is the dominant market because of a single, powerful driver: the need for 24/7 power to support data centers and the broader electrification of the US economy.

This shift matters for three reasons. First, US power markets have a different risk profile than emerging markets. Projects are financed with lower cost of capital, regulatory timelines are more predictable, and the offtake agreements are often backed by investment-grade technology companies. This reduces the cancellation risk that has historically plagued large gas turbine orders in volatile regions. Second, the US market favors advanced-class turbines, specifically H and J class machines, which command higher pricing and margins. In the first quarter, advanced-class turbines represented 12.4 GW of global orders, a share that is likely to grow as US data center developers prioritize efficiency and lower emissions per megawatt. Third, the US market creates a natural competitive advantage for GE Vernova, which holds approximately 45% of the North American market share since 2023. This regional dominance is a durable moat, not a temporary advantage.

The strategic takeaway is that the US-centric demand profile reduces the cyclical volatility of the gas turbine order book. Investors should expect less dramatic swings between boom and bust quarters, and more sustained, compounding growth. The question is whether the OEMs can scale capacity fast enough to capture this demand without eroding pricing discipline.

## OEM Capacity Expansion Plans Are Aggressive but Carry Divergent Risk-Reward Profiles

The report estimates that global OEM capacity will reach approximately 85 GW by 2028, up from current levels that are already strained. Each of the Big Three OEMs has announced expansion plans, but the credibility and execution risk vary significantly.

GE Vernova has laid out the most concrete and measurable plan. The company initially announced a capacity expansion to 70-80 heavy-duty units by the second half of 2026, representing roughly 20 GW. At its December 2025 investor event, it added another 4 GW, bringing heavy-duty capacity to approximately 24 GW by 2028, with total capacity including aeroderivative units reaching 26 GW. GE Vernova has repeatedly emphasized that efficiency gains at existing factories could drive additional upside beyond these targets. For a company that already commands the largest installed base and the strongest service revenue stream, this measured but deliberate expansion is a sign of disciplined capital allocation. The risk is not that GE Vernova overbuilds, but that it underbuilds relative to demand and cedes share to competitors.

Siemens Energy's plan is more ambitious but also more opaque. The company targets moving from roughly 17 GW in fiscal 2024 to over 30 GW by fiscal 2028-2030. However, this target is stated in combined-cycle terms, which includes steam turbines and HRSGs. Converting to simple-cycle capacity requires assumptions about configuration mix, and the company has not provided enough granularity for investors to assess the true capacity uplift. The risk with Siemens Energy is that the headline number overstates the actual gas turbine output, and that execution challenges in scaling its supply chain could lead to delivery delays or cost overruns.

Mitsubishi Heavy Industries presents the most speculative case. The CEO has made public comments about potentially increasing manufacturing throughput by 100%, but the company has not provided a formal capacity target. The report assumes a gradual 50% capacity increase by 2028 based on historical order averages. This is the most conservative assumption, but also the one with the most upside if MHI executes on its stated ambition. However, MHI's market share has been declining in recent quarters, and its reliance on the Asian market, where demand growth is less pronounced, raises questions about utilization rates if it does expand.

For investors, the key distinction is between capacity that is already funded and in execution, and capacity that remains aspirational. GE Vernova's expansion is largely in the former category. Siemens Energy and MHI are in the latter. The divergence in execution risk will become a central valuation differentiator over the next 12 to 18 months.

## Project Cost Inflation Is Reshaping the Competitive Landscape and Creating a New Pricing Floor

The report's proprietary pricing tracker reveals that median CCGT capex costs for projects with 2030 commercial operation dates have risen approximately 200% compared to 2025 completion dates. For simple-cycle plants, 2028 COD costs are up roughly 50% versus 2025. More strikingly, channel checks indicate that CCGT project costs for early-2030s CODs are now pushing toward $2,800 to $3,000 per kilowatt, with some regional conversations above $3,000 per kW.

This cost inflation is not a temporary phenomenon driven by commodity prices or supply chain bottlenecks. It is structural, driven by three factors: labor shortages in power plant construction, rising costs for balance-of-plant equipment beyond the turbine itself, and the scarcity of sites with adequate water and grid interconnection capacity. The implication is that the economics of new gas-fired generation are changing in a way that benefits incumbent OEMs with established supply chains and long-term service contracts.

Higher project costs also create a natural hedge against order cancellations. Developers who commit to projects at $3,000 per kW have a strong incentive to see them through, because the sunk cost is high and alternative technologies face their own cost pressures. This is fundamentally different from the 2010s, when low project costs made it easy to walk away from orders when power price forecasts weakened.

The pricing data also points to an underappreciated dynamic: the gap between CCGT and simple-cycle costs is widening. CCGT costs have risen 63% for 2028 CODs compared to 2025, while simple-cycle costs have risen only 50% in the same period. This suggests that the incremental complexity of combined-cycle configurations is becoming more expensive relative to the base turbine. For developers focused on quick-to-market data center power, simple-cycle plants may become the preferred option, which would benefit OEMs with strong aeroderivative and small-frame turbine portfolios.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Data Center Demand Curve

The report makes a compelling case that data center demand is the primary driver of the current order cycle. Doosan's 2.7 GW data center order and Baker Hughes' 1.1 GW order with Quanta Services are cited as evidence. But the report does not fully address the question of sustainability. How many of these orders are for confirmed data center builds with signed offtake agreements, and how many are speculative procurement by developers who are securing turbine slots before they have secured end customers?

This distinction is critical. If a significant portion of the order book represents speculative capacity reservation, then the risk of order delays or cancellations increases as interest rates remain elevated and technology companies reassess their AI infrastructure spending plans. The report notes that Doosan's 2.7 GW order is expected to ship in 2029 and reach operation in 2030. That is a long lead time, and the probability of scope changes or delays is non-trivial.

Furthermore, the report does not disaggregate data center demand by end customer. Are the hyperscalers Amazon, Microsoft, and Google placing these orders directly, or are they being placed by independent developers who will then sell capacity to the hyperscalers? The difference matters for contract quality and cancellation risk. Direct orders from investment-grade hyperscalers are essentially bulletproof. Developer orders are more vulnerable to financing disruptions.

Investors should monitor the ratio of direct hyperscaler procurement to developer procurement as a leading indicator of order book quality. The report provides the raw order data but does not offer this granularity. It remains an open analytical question that will determine whether the current cycle has a five-year runway or a two-year peak.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Gas Turbine Cycle

The report provides extensive data but does not offer a structured framework for investment decisions. Based on the material, the following framework emerges for investors evaluating exposure to gas turbine equities.

First, assess regional exposure. Companies with dominant market share in North America, particularly GE Vernova, benefit from the highest-quality demand pool with the lowest cancellation risk. Companies overly reliant on the Middle East or Asia face more episodic demand and greater geopolitical risk. The report shows that Siemens Energy holds 51% of the Middle East market and 42% of Europe, while MHI holds 50% of Asia. These are strong positions, but the underlying demand drivers are less durable than the US data center cycle.

Second, evaluate capacity expansion credibility. Investors should distinguish between capacity that is already under construction or funded, and capacity that is merely announced. GE Vernova's expansion is the most credible because it is incremental, factory-based, and supported by efficiency gains. Siemens Energy's expansion is ambitious but lacks granularity. MHI's expansion is the most speculative. The market will reward companies that deliver on capacity targets and penalize those that miss.

Third, monitor pricing power. The report's pricing tracker is the single most important leading indicator for margin expansion. If CCGT costs continue to rise toward $3,500 per kW, OEMs will have significant pricing leverage in new contracts. If costs stabilize or decline, pricing power will erode. The trajectory of project costs over the next four quarters will be the key variable determining whether OEM margins expand or contract.

Fourth, consider the service revenue tailwind. Gas turbines have long service lives, and the current installation wave will generate decades of aftermarket revenue for OEMs with large installed bases. GE Vernova's installed base advantage is a structural competitive moat that is not fully reflected in near-term order metrics. Investors should value service revenue streams at a premium to new equipment sales.

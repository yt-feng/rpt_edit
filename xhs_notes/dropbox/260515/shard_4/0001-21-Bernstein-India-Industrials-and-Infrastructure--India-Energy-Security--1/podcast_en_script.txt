EN_A: So today, we are talking about something that sounds super technical but is actually a huge strategic shift for India. Coal gasification. What is it, and why does it matter now?
EN_B: Great question. The core argument of this global investment bank report is pretty simple. India has massive coal reserves, but it imports most of its natural gas to make fertilizers and chemicals. Coal gasification could change that, and the government is now putting serious money behind it.
EN_A: But wait, India is also pushing hard on renewables. Why go back to coal?
EN_B: That is exactly the tension the report highlights. India has the fifth largest coal reserves in the world, but only about 2.4 million tons per year of coal gasification capacity. Meanwhile, it imports 90 percent of its methanol, 100 percent of its ammonia, and 20 percent of its urea. That is a huge forex drain.
EN_A: So the logic is, use your own coal instead of importing gas?
EN_B: Exactly. And the numbers are big. The report estimates India's LNG import bill could reach 2.85 trillion rupees by 2028. Fertilizer sUBSidies peaked at 2.5 trillion rupees in 2023, which was about 6 percent of the entire net budget. If you can shift that cost to domestic coal, you keep that money inside the economy.
EN_A: But coal gasification is not the same as burning coal, right? How does it actually work?
EN_B: Right. It is a chemical process. You take coal, subject it to high temperature and pressure with steam and a limited amount of oxygen. Instead of burning, the coal breaks down into syngas, which is hydrogen and carbon monoxide. That syngas can then be turned into methanol, urea, or even used in steelmaking.
EN_A: And has any country done this at scale?
EN_B: Two great examples in the report. South Africa, because of oil sanctions in the 1980s, built the world's largest coal-to-chemicals complex. Today, 29 percent of its liquid fuel comes from coal gasification. And then there is China.
EN_A: China is the big one.
EN_B: Yes. China went from almost zero coal gasification to about 340 million tons per year. Today, 78 percent of its urea comes from coal, not natural gas. The report highlights a key moment during the Iran crisis, when international urea prices surged 70 percent, but China's domestic price stayed at one third of the global level because of its coal-based production.
EN_A: That is a powerful case study. So why has India not done this already?
EN_B: The report outlines three main barriers. First, technology. Indian coal has very high ash content, around 35 percent, which makes it harder to gasify. BHEL is working on a local solution. Second, the upfront capital cost is huge, and without long-term offtake agreements, banks are hesitant to lend.
EN_A: So the chicken-and-egg problem. No plants without demand certainty, no demand certainty without plants.
EN_B: Exactly. But the third factor is government support, and that is changing. The government first set a target of 100 million tons per year in 2020. Then they announced 85 billion rupees in viability gap funding. Just yesterday, they announced another 375 billion rupees in capital sUBSidies.
EN_A: That is serious money. So does the economics work now?
EN_B: The report runs a breakeven analysis. Assuming 80 percent plant utilization and a rupee-dollar rate of 95, making urea from coal gasification costs the equivalent of 11.7 dollars per million British thermal units of LNG. Spot LNG is currently at 17 dollars, and the three-year average is 12.6 dollars.
EN_A: So at current prices, it is cheaper?
EN_B: It is competitive. But the report warns that the biggest risk, the lack of long-term offtake agreements, is still unresolved. Without that, project financing remains difficult.
EN_A: So who benefits if this takes off? Any companies named?
EN_B: Within the report's coverage, L&T is a beneficiary on the equipment side, as it won a downstream construction package for one of the plants. NTPC has a target to produce 5 to 10 million tons of syngas by 2029, and the new capital support could accelerate that. Also, lenders like PFC and REC could see a new lending opportunity.
EN_A: So there are plays across the value chain. But what about the elephant in the room? How does coal gasification compete with green ammonia and green hydrogen?
EN_B: That is a very good question, and the report acknowledges it. It says green ammonia economics are improving over time. But the key point is that coal gasification can be deployed now, while green technologies still need time to scale. The report essentially says, in energy security, the color of energy does not matter anymore.
EN_A: So India is going to pursue both paths?
EN_B: Exactly. The report leaves that competition as a cliffhanger. It says the full analysis of how coal gasification stacks up against green alternatives over the long term is something you need to read the complete report to get.

EN_B: Yes. There is a lot more detail on how South Africa used state guarantees and how China used state-owned enterprises to absorb early losses. Those lessons are directly relevant for India. That is where the full report really adds value.

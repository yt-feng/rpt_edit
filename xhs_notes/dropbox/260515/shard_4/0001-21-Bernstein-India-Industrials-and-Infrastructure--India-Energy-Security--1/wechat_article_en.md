# India's Coal Gasification Push Is No Longer Optional—It Is a Fiscal and Strategic Imperative

India faces a structural vulnerability that no amount of renewable energy investment can solve on its own. The country imports roughly 90 percent of its methanol, 100 percent of its ammonia, and 20 percent of its urea, while sitting on 400 billion tonnes of coal reserves—the fifth-largest in the world. This asymmetry between resource endowment and import dependence is not a market anomaly; it is a policy failure that has persisted for decades. The Middle East crisis has now made that failure untenable.

Coal gasification is the most direct, commercially scalable solution to reduce India's liquefied natural gas import bill, which the International Energy Agency projects will reach 2.85 trillion rupees by fiscal year 2028. It also offers a path to convert the government's open-ended fertilizer sUBSidy—which peaked at 2.55 trillion rupees in fiscal year 2023, roughly 6 percent of the entire net budget—into a fixed, rupee-denominated cost insulated from global gas price volatility.

The case is not hypothetical. South Africa built the world's largest coal-to-chemicals complex under oil embargoes in the 1980s using high-ash coal similar to India's. China scaled coal gasification from virtually nothing to approximately 340 million tonnes per year through sustained state intervention, and now produces 85 percent of its ammonia and 75 percent of its methanol from coal. India has just 2.4 million tonnes per year of coal gasification capacity today.

The government has announced 375 billion rupees in capital sUBSidies, effectively covering 15 percent of the expected capital expenditure toward a 100-million-tonne-per-year target. But capital support alone will not solve the deeper structural barriers. The question is whether India can replicate the Chinese and South African playbook without the same institutional mechanisms for absorbing risk and underwriting long-term off-take.

## Coal Gasification Is Not a Technology Problem—It Is a Contracting Problem

The most frequently cited obstacle to coal gasification in India is the high ash content of domestic coal, which averages 35 percent compared to South Africa's 25 percent. This is a real engineering challenge, but it is not the binding constraint. Jindal Steel and Power has already proven the technology at its Angul plant in Odisha, and Bharat Heavy Electricals is developing an indigenous fluidized bed gasifier specifically designed for low-grade, high-ash coal. The technology exists. The question is whether it can be deployed at scale.

The real barrier is the absence of long-term off-take agreements. Coal gasification plants require massive upfront capital expenditure—the eight projects currently underway represent approximately 880 billion rupees in committed investment. No private developer will commit that capital without assurance that the output will be purchased at a predictable price over the plant's economic life. This is not a technology risk; it is a contracting risk.

South Africa solved this problem through government-guaranteed off-take contracts and a strategic fuel fund levy that sUBSidized domestic synfuels. China solved it through state-owned enterprises that absorbed years of accounting losses, state-bank debt at below-commercial rates, and mandatory grid off-take of coal-derived synthetic natural gas. India has announced capital sUBSidies but has not yet created the demand-side mechanisms that made the South African and Chinese models work.

The government's recent announcement of regulated coal pricing for gasification projects is a step in the right direction, but it addresses input cost certainty, not output price certainty. Without blending requirements for coal-derived methanol in the transport fuel pool or mandatory procurement of coal-based urea by the fertilizer ministry, the demand side remains speculative. The capital sUBSidy reduces the cost of building the plant, but it does not guarantee that the plant will operate at 80 percent utilization—the assumption underlying the break-even analysis in the report.

## The Fiscal Arithmetic Is Compelling, But Only If Utilization Is Guaranteed

The report provides a break-even analysis that is worth examining closely. At a plant utilization rate of 80 percent and a foreign exchange rate of 95 rupees per U.S. dollar, the break-even price for producing urea via coal gasification is $11.7 per million British thermal units of LNG Japan Korea Marker price. This compares favorably to the current spot price of roughly $17 per MMBtu and the three-year average of $12.6 per MMBtu.

The implication is straightforward: at current LNG prices, coal-based urea production is significantly cheaper than gas-based production. Even at the three-year average LNG price, coal gasification is marginally cheaper. The economics improve further if one factors in the foreign exchange depreciation of approximately 3.3 percent per year that the report assumes, which would make imported LNG even more expensive in rupee terms over time.

But these economics are contingent on the utilization assumption. If the plant operates at 60 percent utilization instead of 80 percent, the break-even price rises to approximately $15.6 per MMBtu, eroding most of the cost advantage. If utilization falls to 50 percent, coal-based urea becomes more expensive than the three-year average LNG price. This is not a hypothetical risk. The report notes that the only large operational coal gasification plant in India is Jindal Steel and Power's facility at Angul. The other eight projects worth 880 billion rupees are still underway. There is no track record of high utilization at scale in the Indian context.

The government's capital sUBSidy of 375 billion rupees improves the project economics by reducing the initial capital outlay, but it does not address the utilization risk. A plant that is built but underutilized is worse than a plant that is not built at all, because the capital is sunk and the debt service obligations remain. This is why the absence of long-term off-take agreements is the critical missing piece.

## The Green Ammonia Competition Is Real and Getting Stronger

One of the most significant risks to the coal gasification thesis is competition from green ammonia, which is produced using renewable energy to split water into hydrogen and then combining it with nitrogen. The economics of green ammonia are improving rapidly as the cost of solar and wind power continues to decline, and as electrolyzer manufacturing scales globally.

The report acknowledges this risk but does not fully explore its implications. If green ammonia reaches cost parity with coal-based ammonia within the next five to seven years, then the 100-million-tonne-per-year coal gasification target could become a stranded asset halfway through its construction cycle. This is not a distant possibility. Several large-scale green ammonia projects are already under development in India, including those backed by the government's production-linked incentive scheme for green hydrogen.

The counterargument is that green ammonia economics remain dependent on the cost of capital, the utilization rate of electrolyzers, and the availability of firm renewable power. Coal-based ammonia, by contrast, benefits from a well-established supply chain, existing infrastructure, and a regulatory framework that already supports coal production. But this advantage is eroding. The cost of electrolyzers has fallen by approximately 40 percent over the past three years, and the government's green hydrogen mission includes 174.9 billion rupees in incentives.

The strategic question for investors is whether coal gasification represents a bridge technology to a green future or a lock-in to a carbon-intensive past. The answer depends on the time horizon. Over the next decade, coal gasification offers a clear path to import sUBStitution and fiscal savings. Over a 20-year horizon, the risk of technological obsolescence is real. The report does not resolve this tension, and investors should demand clarity on how the government plans to manage the transition from coal-based to green chemicals without stranding capital.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Answer Before Committing Capital

The report provides detailed analysis of the opportunity, but it does not offer a structured framework for investment decision-making. Based on the data and risks presented, investors should evaluate coal gasification opportunities through three lenses.

First, what is the off-take structure? The most important determinant of project viability is not the capital sUBSidy or the technology choice, but the certainty of demand. Projects that have secured long-term procurement agreements with the fertilizer ministry or with state-owned fuel distributors are fundamentally different from projects that rely on spot market sales. Investors should prioritize projects with government-backed off-take commitments, even if the headline returns are lower, because the utilization risk is sUBStantially reduced.

Second, what is the technology pathway? The report identifies three types of gasifiers: entrained flow, fixed bed, and fluidized bed. The fluidized bed gasifier is most suitable for Indian coal, but it is still under development by Bharat Heavy Electricals. Projects that use proven international technology from South Africa or China may have lower technology risk, but they may also face higher capital costs and longer lead times. Investors should assess whether the technology provider has a track record of operating at scale with high-ash coal, and whether the plant design includes redundancy for critical components.

Third, what is the exit option? Coal gasification plants have long construction periods and even longer payback periods. If the green ammonia transition accelerates, or if global LNG prices decline structurally, the economics of these projects could deteriorate rapidly. Investors should evaluate whether the plant can be retrofitted to use alternative feedstocks, whether the syngas can be redirected to higher-value products, and whether the site has access to carbon capture and storage infrastructure that could extend its economic life.

The report covers only Larsen and Toubro and NTPC among the key players. Larsen and Toubro is positioned as an equipment and construction beneficiary, having won the downstream construction package for one of the planned plants. NTPC targets 5 to 10 million tonnes per year of syngas production by 2029, leveraging its existing coal resources. Both companies have diversified business models that reduce their exposure to any single project outcome. For investors considering exposure to the broader ecosystem, Power Finance Corporation and REC could see a new lending opportunity as coal gasification fits with their long-term infrastructure lending focus.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Political Economy of Implementation

The report makes a compelling case for coal gasification on economic and strategic grounds, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. These are not analytical gaps; they are structural uncertainties that will determine whether the 100-million-tonne-per-year target is achievable.

The most important unanswered question is whether the Indian government has the institutional capacity to replicate the Chinese model of state-directed industrial policy. China's coal gasification scale-up was driven by state-owned enterprises that could tolerate years of accounting losses, access state-bank debt at below-commercial rates, and rely on mandatory off-take from state-owned grid operators. India's public sector enterprises operate under different constraints. Coal India, GAIL, and NTPC are all subject to dividend payout requirements, return-on-equity targets, and regulatory oversight that limit their ability to absorb losses for strategic purposes.

The second unanswered question is whether the government will impose blending requirements for coal-derived methanol in the transport fuel pool. The NITI Aayog's Methanol Economy program has discussed this possibility, but no mandate has been announced. Without a blending requirement, the demand for coal-derived methanol remains uncertain, and the economics of methanol production are less attractive than urea production. The report notes that the government's focus is on liquid fuels, but it does not specify what policy instruments will be used to create demand.

The third unanswered question is how the government will manage the competition between coal gasification and green hydrogen for policy attention and fiscal resources. Both technologies are competing for the same pool of capital sUBSidies, tax incentives, and regulatory support. If the government prioritizes green hydrogen over coal gasification, the 100-million-tonne-per-year target could be scaled back. If it prioritizes coal gasification, it risks locking in carbon-intensive infrastructure that could become stranded as green technology costs decline.

These are not questions that can be answered by financial analysis alone. They require an understanding of the political economy of Indian energy policy, the institutional capacity of public sector enterprises, and the trajectory of global technology costs.

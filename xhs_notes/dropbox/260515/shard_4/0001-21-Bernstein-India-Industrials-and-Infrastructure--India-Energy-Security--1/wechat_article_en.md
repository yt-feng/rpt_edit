# India's Coal Gasification Push Is No Longer Optional — It Is a Strategic Imperative for Energy Security and Fiscal Stability

India faces a structural vulnerability that few in the market fully appreciate. The country imports nearly 90 percent of its methanol, 100 percent of its ammonia, and roughly 20 percent of its urea, while sitting on the world's fifth-largest coal reserves. This asymmetry between domestic resource endowment and import dependence is not merely an industrial inefficiency — it is a growing fiscal and strategic risk. As global LNG prices remain volatile and the rupee continues its structural depreciation, India's annual LNG import bill is projected to reach nearly INR 2.85 trillion by FY2028, up from INR 1.45 trillion in FY2023. The fertilizer sUBSidy alone peaked at INR 2.5 trillion in FY2023, representing roughly 6 percent of the entire net budget. These numbers make one thing clear: the economics of coal gasification, long dismissed as uncompetitive, have shifted decisively in India's favor.

The global context has accelerated this calculus. The Middle East crisis, disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, and the lingering effects of the Russia-Ukraine conflict have repeatedly demonstrated that reliance on imported LNG and ammonia exposes India to supply shocks and price spikes beyond its control. China, facing a similar resource profile two decades ago, scaled its coal gasification capacity from virtually nothing to roughly 340 million tonnes per annum through sustained policy intervention. Today, China produces approximately 85 percent of its ammonia and 75 percent of its methanol from coal, making it a net exporter of fertilizers. India, by contrast, has just 2.4 million tonnes per annum of coal gasification capacity. The gap is not just a missed opportunity — it is a liability that compounds every year.

The Indian government has begun to respond. After announcing a target of 100 million tonnes per annum of coal gasification capacity in 2020, it has progressively strengthened the policy framework: viability gap funding of INR 85 billion, regulated coal pricing allocations, guaranteed returns on equity for select government plants, and most recently, a capital sUBSidy of INR 375 billion — effectively covering roughly 15 percent of the expected project capex. These measures signal that the government is moving from aspiration to implementation. But the real question for investors is not whether coal gasification will happen — it is which companies are positioned to capture value across the value chain, and what risks remain unaddressed.

The argument of this article is straightforward: coal gasification in India is transitioning from a decades-long discussion to a genuine, policy-backed deployment cycle. The strategic imperative is clear, the economics are improving, and the government is putting capital behind its commitment. However, the path to scale is not linear. It depends on resolving technical challenges around high-ash coal, establishing long-term off-take agreements, and managing competition from green ammonia. For investors, understanding where the value accrues — equipment providers, asset owners, or lenders — and where the risks remain underestimated is the critical analytical task.

## The Global Playbook Is Clear: Sustained State Intervention Is the Only Path to Scale

South Africa and China have already demonstrated what it takes to build a coal gasification industry from scratch. Their experiences are not just historical case studies — they are the template India is following, and they reveal the conditions necessary for success.

South Africa's Sasol built the world's largest coal-to-chemicals complex in the 1980s under apartheid-era oil embargoes. At the time, crude oil was available at USD 3 to 5 per barrel, making coal gasification economically suboptimal. The project was sustained by state ownership, a Strategic Fuel Fund levy on imported petroleum that sUBSidized domestic synfuels, and government-guaranteed long-term off-take contracts that insulated project economics from price volatility. By the 1990s, as oil prices rose to USD 20 to 30 per barrel, the economics flipped. Today, Sasol's coal-to-liquids operations provide roughly 29 percent of South Africa's locally refined liquid fuel requirements, saving the nation billions in foreign exchange annually. Critically, South Africa proved that high-ash coal — with ash content around 25 percent — can be gasified at commercial scale. India's coal has even higher ash content, averaging 35 percent, but the technological precedent exists.

China's scale-up was even more aggressive and deliberate. From the 2000s onward, state-owned enterprises such as Shenhua and Sinopec deployed coal-to-chemical plants using preferentially priced captive coal allocations, state-bank debt at below-market rates, mandatory grid off-take of coal-derived synthetic natural gas, and a tolerance for years of accounting losses absorbed by state-owned enterprise balance sheets. The result was transformative. China's coal gasification capacity grew from roughly 50 million tonnes per annum before 2000 to approximately 340 million tonnes per annum today. The payoff became visible during the Iran conflict, when urea prices outside China surged by about 70 percent due to shipping disruptions through the Strait of Hormuz. China, with ample domestic coal-based urea production, maintained stable stocks and kept domestic prices roughly one-third of international benchmarks. China is now among the largest exporters of fertilizers, shipping more than USD 13 billion worth last year.

The lesson for India is unambiguous. Coal gasification does not scale through market forces alone. It requires sustained state intervention — in capital allocation, fuel pricing, off-take guarantees, and tolerance for initial losses — to bridge the gap between high upfront costs and long-term import sUBStitution benefits. India's recent policy announcements follow this playbook, but the question is whether the scale and duration of support will match what South Africa and China deployed.

## India's Fiscal Arithmetic Makes Coal Gasification a Budgetary Necessity, Not Just an Energy Strategy

The most compelling argument for coal gasification in India is not energy security in the abstract — it is the concrete impact on the government's fiscal position. India's fertilizer sUBSidy is an open-ended, LNG-contingent liability that fluctuates with global gas prices and exchange rates. Coal gasification offers the possibility of converting this variable cost into a fixed, rupee-denominated expense that stays within the domestic economy.

Consider the numbers. India's LNG import bill has been driven by three compounding forces: absolute volume growth as industrial gas demand expands with GDP, structural rupee depreciation at roughly 3.3 percent per year, and periodic LNG price spikes. The IEA projects that India's total LNG import bill will grow at a 26.2 percent CAGR to INR 2.85 trillion by FY2028. Fertilizers consume approximately 40 percent of India's imported gas annually. This means that a significant portion of the fertilizer sUBSidy — which peaked at INR 2.5 trillion in FY2023 — is effectively a transfer to foreign LNG suppliers.

Coal gasification changes this equation. Using the announced project capex, a plant utilization rate of 80 percent, and a rupee-dollar exchange rate of roughly 95, the breakeven for producing urea via coal gasification is approximately USD 11.7 per MMBtu of LNG on a JKM price basis. For context, current LNG prices are around USD 17 per MMBtu, and the three-year average LNG price is approximately USD 12.6 per MMBtu. At current prices, coal gasification is already economically viable. Even at the three-year average, the margin is thin but positive. The key variable is not whether coal gasification can compete with LNG at today's prices — it can. The risk is that LNG prices could fall to levels that undermine the economics before projects are fully amortized.

This is precisely why long-term off-take agreements are critical. The government's willingness to provide capital sUBSidies and guaranteed returns on equity for specific plants addresses part of the risk, but the absence of a guaranteed demand-side contract remains the single biggest obstacle to project financing. Without a long-term offtake agreement that locks in a minimum price or volume, private capital will be reluctant to commit the sUBStantial upfront investment required.

## The Value Chain Is Not Uniform: Equipment Providers, Asset Owners, and Lenders Face Different Risk-Reward Profiles

Coal gasification creates value across three distinct segments: equipment and construction providers, asset owners who operate the plants, and lenders who finance the projects. The risk-reward profile differs significantly across these segments, and investors need to differentiate accordingly.

On the equipment side, companies such as Larsen & Toubro and Thermax are positioned to benefit from the construction and engineering contracts associated with new plants. L&T has already won the downstream construction package for one of the planned projects, and Thermax is in discussions with Coal India and the Ministry of Coal to serve as an equipment provider. For these companies, the exposure is project-based and relatively low-risk — they earn fees for construction and installation without taking on the operational or price risk of the plant itself. The upside comes from the volume of projects that reach financial close, not from the long-term performance of any single plant.

For asset owners such as NTPC, the calculus is different. NTPC targets producing 5 to 10 million tonnes per annum of syngas by 2029, leveraging its existing coal resources and infrastructure. As an asset owner, NTPC takes on construction risk, operational risk, and off-take risk. However, as a state-owned enterprise with access to low-cost capital and government support, NTPC is better positioned than private players to absorb these risks. The government's latest capital sUBSidy announcement accelerates NTPC's plans by reducing the upfront equity requirement and improving project-level returns.

The lending opportunity is perhaps the most underappreciated segment. Companies such as Power Finance Corporation and REC, which have a long history of financing power sector infrastructure, could see a new lending opportunity in coal gasification. These projects fit naturally with their long-term infrastructure lending focus, and the government's capital sUBSidy reduces the risk profile of the loans. For PFC and REC, the question is not whether coal gasification lending will happen, but at what scale and with what risk-adjusted returns.

## The Unresolved Questions: High-Ash Coal, Green Ammonia Competition, and the Missing Off-Take Guarantee

Despite the compelling strategic case and growing policy support, three critical risks remain unresolved, and investors should not underestimate them.

The first is the technical challenge of gasifying high-ash Indian coal. India's coal has an average ash content of roughly 35 percent, compared to 25 percent in South Africa and lower levels in China. High-ash coal is more difficult to gasify because the ash can cause slagging, fouling, and reduced efficiency in the gasifier. BHEL is currently developing an indigenous fluidized bed gasifier designed specifically for high-ash coal, but the technology is not yet proven at commercial scale. If BHEL's gasifier does not perform as expected, India may need to import technology or accept lower conversion efficiencies, both of which would reduce the economic viability of the projects.

The second risk is competition from green ammonia. As the cost of renewable energy continues to decline, the economics of green ammonia — produced using electrolysis powered by solar or wind — are improving. If green ammonia reaches cost parity with coal-based ammonia within the next decade, the long-term returns on coal gasification plants could be impaired. The government's policy framework will need to navigate this tension: supporting coal gasification as a near-term solution while not locking in carbon-intensive infrastructure that becomes stranded as green alternatives scale.

The third and most immediate risk is the absence of long-term off-take agreements. The government has provided capital sUBSidies and guaranteed returns on equity for specific plants, but it has not yet mandated that fertilizer companies or other downstream users commit to purchasing a minimum volume of coal-derived products at a predetermined price. Without such off-take guarantees, project financiers face the risk that plants will operate below capacity or sell output at prices that do not cover fixed costs. This is the single biggest gap in the current policy framework, and it is the issue most likely to determine whether India's 100 million tonne target is achieved or remains aspirational.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Evaluate Coal Gasification Exposure

For investors considering exposure to India's coal gasification theme, the analytical task is to assess which companies have the strongest risk-adjusted positioning across three dimensions: policy dependency, technology risk, and balance sheet resilience.

First, assess policy dependency. Companies with direct government backing — either through equity participation, guaranteed returns, or preferential coal allocations — face lower policy risk than purely private players. NTPC and GAIL, which are involved in most projects as equity holders or infrastructure providers, have a structural advantage. Private sector equipment providers such as L&T and Thermax have lower policy dependency but also lower upside — their revenue is tied to project awards, not plant performance.

Second, evaluate technology risk. Companies that rely on proven, licensed technology face lower execution risk than those developing indigenous solutions. The fluidized bed gasifier being developed by BHEL is the highest-risk technology bet in the sector. If it succeeds, BHEL could capture significant value as the exclusive equipment provider for high-ash coal gasification. If it fails, projects may face delays or cost overruns. Investors should monitor BHEL's pilot plant progress closely.

Third, consider balance sheet resilience. Coal gasification projects require sUBStantial upfront capital, and delays in construction or off-take can strain balance sheets. Companies with strong cash flows and low leverage — such as NTPC and L&T — are better positioned to absorb these risks than smaller or more leveraged players.

The investment implication is not binary. Coal gasification is not a single trade; it is a theme that will play out differently across the value chain. Equipment providers benefit from project announcements but face lumpy revenue streams. Asset owners benefit from long-term cash flows but take on operational risk. Lenders benefit from a new asset class but must underwrite project risk carefully. The most attractive risk-reward profiles are likely to be found in companies that combine policy support, proven technology, and strong balance sheets.

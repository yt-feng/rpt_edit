# Fortinet's OT Cybersecurity Surprise Is a Signal, Not an Anomaly — and It Will Reshape the Vendor Landscape

The most important earnings beat in cybersecurity this quarter was not about AI, cloud migration, or consolidation. It was about firewalls — specifically, the sudden surge in demand for Operational Technology (OT) security appliances. Fortinet’s Q1 2026 results delivered a revenue growth beat of +6.9% versus midpoint guidance, a magnitude not seen since the COVID demand bubble of 2021. That is double or triple the normal beat range of 2-3%. And while roughly one percentage point came from passing through higher memory costs, the remainder reflects something more structural: a wave of OT cybersecurity demand driven by geopolitical friction, new European regulations, and intensified U.S. government pressure on critical infrastructure operators.

This is not a Fortinet-specific story. It is a sector-wide signal that will flow through the earnings of multiple cybersecurity vendors in the coming quarters. The question is not whether OT demand will boost other companies — it is which architectures, product categories, and business models are best positioned to capture it. The answer will separate winners from laggards in a market that has been dominated by platform consolidation narratives and cloud-native security architectures.

The conventional wisdom has been that OT cybersecurity is a niche, dominated by specialized players like Dragos and Nozomi Networks, and that mainstream enterprise security vendors have only tangential exposure. Fortinet’s beat challenges that assumption. It suggests that the mainstream firewall market — long considered mature and commoditized — is experiencing a demand shock from an unexpected source. And because OT environments are fundamentally different from IT environments, the nature of this demand will favor certain product architectures over others.

The implications extend beyond firewall appliances. If OT demand is real and sustained, it will create tailwinds for endpoint security platforms that can secure unmanaged devices, for SASE/SSE architectures that can replace physical appliances, and for any vendor that can credibly bridge the IT-OT divide. But the distribution of these benefits will be uneven, and the next several earnings cycles will reveal which companies have genuine OT exposure and which are merely marketing it.

## The Fortinet Beat Reveals a Structural Demand Shift in OT Cybersecurity, Not a One-Off Event

Fortinet’s Q1 results were a data point, but they were not an outlier. The company explicitly attributed the upside to a “bubble of demand in firewall appliances to support Operational Technology cybersecurity.” The drivers are specific and verifiable: recent Middle East friction, nation-state hacks such as the Stryker incident, new European OT regulations, and U.S. government pressure on businesses with critical physical infrastructure. These are not transient factors. Geopolitical instability shows no signs of abating, regulatory frameworks are becoming more stringent, and the attack surface of OT environments is expanding as more industrial equipment becomes networked and software-defined.

The critical insight is that OT demand is not a sUBStitute for IT security spending; it is incremental. Companies that already have enterprise firewall deployments are now being forced to add dedicated OT security appliances, often in segmented networks that cannot be protected by existing IT infrastructure. This creates a new addressable market that did not exist in the same form two years ago. Fortinet’s beat suggests this market is larger and arriving faster than most models anticipated.

The question for investors is whether this is a pull-forward of demand or a sustained new growth vector. The report’s model implicitly assumes the elevated demand persists for several quarters, possibly into next year, as U.S. and European companies evaluate and address their OT exposure. That assumption is reasonable but unproven. The next two quarters of earnings from Fortinet and its peers will provide the evidence needed to determine whether this is a cyclical spike or a secular shift.

## Palo Alto Networks Is the Most Likely Direct Beneficiary of OT Firewall Demand, but Geography and Architecture Matter

If OT demand is boosting firewall appliance sales, the next logical candidate is Palo Alto Networks. According to Gartner, Fortinet and Palo Alto are effectively tied for the number one position in OT network security, with similar market shares. The logic is straightforward: if enterprises are buying OT firewalls, they are likely buying from one of these two leaders.

However, the magnitude of benefit for Palo Alto may be smaller than for Fortinet for two reasons. First, the regulatory push in Europe — which is more prescriptive and compliance-driven — is a significant driver of OT demand, and Fortinet over-indexes in European revenue by roughly 2x compared to Palo Alto. Second, Palo Alto’s business model is increasingly oriented toward its Next-Generation Security (NGS) ARR, which includes cloud-delivered services like endpoint protection and SASE. Firewall appliance revenue, while still material, is a smaller and less strategically emphasized portion of the business.

This creates an asymmetry. Fortinet’s hardware-heavy model is directly leveraged to OT appliance demand. Palo Alto’s model is more diversified, which reduces downside risk but also dilutes the upside from a single product category. The question for Palo Alto is whether OT demand spills over into its NGS offerings — for example, if enterprises buying OT firewalls also adopt Palo Alto’s endpoint or SSE solutions as part of a broader security transformation. That would be a more powerful signal, but it is not yet confirmed.

The upcoming Palo Alto earnings will be a critical test. A modest beat in appliance revenue would be consistent with OT tailwinds but not definitive. A beat that also shows acceleration in NGS ARR, particularly from OT-adjacent verticals like manufacturing and energy, would be a much stronger confirmation that the OT demand wave is broad-based.

## SentinelOne and CrowdStrike Have OT Endpoint Exposure, but the Revenue Impact May Be Smaller and Slower to Materialize

OT cybersecurity is not just about firewalls. The endpoint security layer is equally critical, particularly for the growing category of Internet of Things (IoT) and Industrial IoT (IIoT) devices that cannot run traditional security agents. These include medical devices, surveillance cameras, industrial control systems, and building automation systems — all of which are increasingly connected and vulnerable.

SentinelOne has positioned itself explicitly for this market. Its Singularity platform is designed to secure unmanaged, internet-connected devices, with broad OS support and the ability to operate in segmented networks without direct internet access. CrowdStrike has also made strides, with recent announcements of its own OT/IoT capabilities and historical relationships with partners like Dragos.

The thesis is compelling, but the revenue impact may be smaller and slower than for firewall vendors. OT endpoint security is a newer category, and adoption cycles are longer because OT environments are typically more conservative, have longer upgrade cycles, and require extensive validation before deploying new security tools. Moreover, the total addressable market for OT endpoint security is smaller than for OT network security, at least in the near term.

The report notes that SentinelOne’s stock has underperformed significantly, trading at a TTM relative performance of -47.4%. This suggests that the market has not priced in OT tailwinds. If SentinelOne’s upcoming earnings show even modest upside driven by OT-related deals, the re-rating could be sUBStantial. But the risk is that OT demand remains concentrated in firewall appliances for the next several quarters, leaving endpoint vendors waiting for a wave that has not yet arrived.

## SASE and SSE Vendors Are Trying to Make OT Relevance a Growth Story, but the Architecture Is Still Unproven

The most ambitious claim in the OT security narrative comes from the SASE/SSE vendors, particularly Zscaler and Cloudflare. Their argument is that physical firewall appliances are suboptimal for OT environments because they are difficult to deploy, manage, and scale across distributed industrial sites. Instead, they propose a cloud-delivered architecture that uses zero-trust principles to secure OT connectivity without the need for on-premises hardware.

Zscaler’s acquisition of Airgap Technologies in 2024 was a direct bet on this thesis. Cloudflare has taken a similar approach, with partners like Brixio deploying IT/OT converged cyber solutions. The logic is appealing, particularly for organizations with hundreds or thousands of geographically dispersed OT sites that cannot support a dedicated firewall appliance at each location.

However, the evidence for this thesis is thin. The report notes that Cloudflare’s OT focus was not discussed as a tailwind in its Q1 earnings, and that its emphasis seems “thinner” than competitors. Zscaler’s OT messaging is more developed, but it remains to be seen whether enterprises are willing to replace physical firewalls with cloud-based alternatives in critical infrastructure environments where latency, reliability, and air-gapped security are paramount.

The SASE/SSE OT thesis is a second-order implication of the Fortinet beat, not a direct beneficiary. If OT demand is real and sustained, it will eventually create opportunities for alternative architectures. But the initial wave of spending is likely to go to the incumbents — Fortinet and Palo Alto — whose products are already deployed, validated, and trusted by OT operators. SASE vendors may need to wait for the replacement cycle, which could be years away.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer — and Why the Next Two Quarters Are Critical

The report raises several important questions that it does not fully resolve. First, it is unclear whether the OT demand spike is a one-time adjustment or a permanent shift in spending. The geopolitical and regulatory drivers are structural, but the initial surge may reflect catch-up spending by organizations that were previously underinvested. Once they have deployed the necessary appliances, the incremental demand could fade.

Second, the report does not quantify the total addressable market for OT cybersecurity or the share that each vendor can realistically capture. The Fortinet beat provides a data point, but extrapolating from one company’s performance to the entire sector is risky. The upcoming earnings from Palo Alto, SentinelOne, CrowdStrike, and Zscaler will provide the cross-validation needed to assess the breadth and depth of the trend.

Third, the report does not address the competitive dynamics within OT cybersecurity. Fortinet and Palo Alto are leaders in OT network security, but specialized players like Dragos, Nozomi Networks, and Claroty have deep expertise and established relationships with industrial operators. It is possible that the mainstream vendors capture only a portion of the OT market, with the rest going to specialists that are not covered in this report.

Finally, the report does not explore the implications for OT cybersecurity spending in the broader context of enterprise IT budgets. If OT demand is incremental, it could come at the expense of other security categories, particularly if overall budgets are constrained. Alternatively, OT demand could be additive if regulators and governments are effectively mandating new spending. The answer to this question will determine whether the OT tailwind is a net positive for the sector or a zero-sum reallocation.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Evaluate OT Cybersecurity Exposure Across Vendors

For investors trying to position for the OT cybersecurity trend, the key is to distinguish between three types of exposure: direct, indirect, and aspirational.

Direct exposure applies to vendors whose core products are being purchased specifically for OT environments. Fortinet and Palo Alto fall into this category, with Fortinet having the stronger near-term leverage due to its hardware-heavy model and European revenue concentration. The metric to watch is firewall appliance revenue growth, particularly in verticals like manufacturing, energy, and healthcare.

Indirect exposure applies to vendors whose products are used in OT environments but are not the primary driver of purchasing decisions. SentinelOne and CrowdStrike fit here. Their endpoint platforms can secure OT devices, but the buying decision is often driven by broader enterprise security needs, with OT as an add-on rather than a primary use case. The metric to watch is OT-specific deal references in earnings calls and customer win announcements.

Aspirational exposure applies to vendors that are building OT capabilities but have not yet demonstrated meaningful revenue. Zscaler and Cloudflare are in this camp. Their SASE/SSE architectures offer a compelling long-term vision, but the near-term revenue contribution is likely negligible. The metric to watch is product development milestones and partner ecosystem announcements, not revenue.

The critical unknown is timing. If OT demand accelerates over the next two quarters, direct-exposure vendors will benefit first and most. If the demand is slower to materialize, indirect and aspirational vendors may underperform as the market waits for proof points. The next earnings season will be the most important in years for understanding whether OT cybersecurity is a genuine growth story or a temporary distraction.

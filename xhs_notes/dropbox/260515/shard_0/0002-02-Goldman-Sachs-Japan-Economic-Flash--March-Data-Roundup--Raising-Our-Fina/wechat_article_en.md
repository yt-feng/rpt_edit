# Japan’s Economy Is Holding Up Better Than Expected in Q1, But the Real Test Begins in Q2

The first quarter of 2026 has delivered a modest upside surprise for the Japanese economy, but the narrative is more nuanced than a simple upward revision. A global investment bank’s latest tracking estimate now places Q1 real GDP growth at +1.3% quarter-on-quarter annualized, up from a prior forecast of +0.7%. The revision is driven almost entirely by net exports, which have so far escaped the direct impact of the Middle East disruption that began in late February. Private consumption and capital expenditure, the core engines of domestic demand, are expected to post only modest gains. This is not a story of robust acceleration. It is a story of resilience within a narrow band, and the band is about to tighten.

The critical question is not whether Q1 beats expectations. It is whether the economy can sustain positive momentum once the delayed effects of the Middle East crisis fully materialize in supply chains, input costs, and consumer behavior. The data through March already shows cracks: industrial production fell for a second consecutive month, chemicals and petroleum products were hit hard, and consumer sentiment has deteriorated sharply. The real economy is beginning to absorb the shock, and the second quarter will reveal the true depth of the adjustment.

What makes this moment strategically important is the confluence of forces that rarely align in Japan. The Bank of Japan is grappling with the most divided board in recent memory, with three of nine members dissenting at the April meeting to push for a rate hike to 1.0%. The government is deploying a mix of reserve releases, supply security measures, and price controls, while a cross-party council debates a consumption tax cut. Meanwhile, the spring wage negotiations have delivered base pay rises in the mid-3% range, and equity markets are at all-time highs. Japan is simultaneously facing an external supply shock, an internal inflation dynamic, and a structural shift in labor and corporate behavior. The outcome of this combination is far from predetermined.

This article unpacks the Q1 data, the policy responses, the outlook for consumption and capex, and the unresolved questions that will define the next phase of Japan’s economic trajectory.

## The Q1 GDP Revision Is Largely a Statistical Artifact of Net Exports, Not a Sign of Underlying Strength

The upward revision to Q1 GDP tracking from +0.7% to +1.3% qoq annualized is significant, but its composition demands careful interpretation. The primary driver is net exports, which are now expected to contribute +0.4 percentage points to growth, the first positive contribution from external demand in three quarters. Real exports rose +3.0% qoq in Q1, accelerating sharply from +0.6% in Q4, while real imports turned up for the first time in three quarters at +1.0% qoq. The improvement in exports is broad-based: shipments to the United States rose for a second straight quarter, and those to mainland China turned positive for the first time in a year.

Yet this export recovery is fragile. The March data showed real exports declining -0.5% month-on-month, and the Middle East disruption has not yet fully fed through to trade flows. The Strait of Hormuz remains disrupted, and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry’s bias-adjusted production outlook for April calls for a -0.7% month-on-month decline in industrial output, with the risk of a larger drop. The Q1 export strength may prove to be a pull-forward effect, with orders placed before the crisis materializing in the data, while the real drag accumulates in Q2.

Private consumption and capex, by contrast, are expected to post only modest growth. The bank’s forecast for Q1 private consumption stands at +0.8% qoq annualized, supported by services spending, while capex is also pegged at +0.8%, slowing from strong growth in Q4. Neither component suggests broad-based momentum. The BOJ’s Consumption Activity Index showed month-on-month declines in both February and March, and consumer sentiment has deteriorated markedly since March. The upward revision to GDP is a welcome data point, but it does not change the underlying fragility of domestic demand.

## Consumer Sentiment Is Deteriorating Faster Than the Hard Data, Creating a Divergence That Will Resolve in Q2

The most concerning signal in the current data is the divergence between hard consumption metrics and sentiment. Nominal retail sales rose +1.7% month-on-month in March, beating expectations, and Q1 sales accelerated to +1.7% qoq from +0.5% in Q4. The BOJ’s Consumption Activity Index, which includes services, posted a +0.3% qoq increase for Q1. On the surface, consumption appears resilient.

But the breakdown tells a different story. Spending on durable goods declined sharply, while services spending remained high. The surge in Shinkansen passenger numbers during the Golden Week holidays suggests that consumers are still willing to spend on experiences and special occasions, even as sentiment sours. This is consistent with the pattern observed in other economies during periods of uncertainty: consumers pull back on big-ticket items but maintain spending on services that offer immediate utility or emotional reward.

The risk is that this pattern is not sustainable. Consumer sentiment has deteriorated significantly since March, driven by concerns about real income erosion from higher prices and the prolonged Middle East conflict. The bank expects GDP private consumption to slow from +0.8% qoq annualized in Q1 to +0.3% to +0.4% in Q2 and Q3. That deceleration is consistent with the sentiment data, but the magnitude of the slowdown will depend on how quickly the energy price shock translates into broader price increases for non-energy goods and services.

The April Tokyo CPI data offers a preview. The new core CPI, which excludes fresh food and energy, slowed to +1.9% year-on-year, below expectations, partly due to the negative contribution from the free childcare system. But energy price inflation rose, as the positive contribution from scaling back price controls on electricity and gas outweighed the negative contribution from the introduction of price controls on gasoline. The net effect is that headline inflation remains sticky, and the pass-through to consumer prices is still incomplete. If energy costs continue to rise, the drag on real disposable income will intensify, and the divergence between sentiment and spending will close with a downward adjustment in consumption.

## Capital Expenditure Faces a Temporary Headwind, But Structural Drivers and Order Backlogs Provide a Floor

Capex is often the first casualty of uncertainty, and the current environment is no exception. The bank expects GDP capex to slow from +0.8% qoq annualized in Q1 to +0.6% to +0.7% in Q2 and Q3, Citing the risk of postponement due to uncertainty about domestic and external demand, high costs, and reduced supply of petroleum-derived materials.

Yet the downside risk to capex is limited by two structural factors. First, corporate capex plans for FY2026 are off to a good start, similar to last year, according to the BOJ Tankan survey. The initial plans in March showed a similar trajectory to the previous year, and the June survey will be critical in determining whether companies revise down their intentions. Second, backlogs for construction and machinery orders have accumulated. Even if companies temporarily hold off on new orders, the execution of outstanding orders will sustain capex activity for several quarters.

Japan also has a track record of improving energy efficiency during periods of rising crude oil prices. In the current reflationary environment, corporate profits are historically high, and many companies cite decarbonization as a key driver of investment. The capex aimed at improving energy efficiency is likely to continue increasing, providing a structural tailwind that partially offsets the cyclical headwind from uncertainty.

The key variable to watch is the duration of the Middle East disruption. If the supply shock normalizes within a quarter, the capex drag will be limited, and the structural drivers will reassert themselves. If the disruption persists, the risk of a more pronounced pullback in investment plans will rise, particularly for energy-intensive industries.

## The Bank of Japan Faces Its Most Difficult Decision in Years, and the July Meeting Is a Tipping Point

The April Monetary Policy Meeting was a watershed moment for the BOJ. The decision to keep the policy rate on hold at 0.75% was expected, but the dissent from three of nine board members, who proposed a hike to 1.0%, was not. The Summary of Opinions reveals that most board members see the need for a rate hike, but they are divided on the urgency given the high uncertainty over the Middle East situation.

This division reflects a fundamental tension at the heart of BOJ policy. On one hand, the domestic economy is showing signs of strength: wages are rising, corporate profits are high, and inflation is above target. On the other hand, the external shock from the Middle East creates downside risks to growth and makes the inflation outlook more uncertain. The BOJ’s preferred approach is to wait for more data, particularly corporate surveys and other survey data, to confirm that the economy is not entering a major adjustment phase.

The bank’s base case is a rate hike in July. This is a reasonable assumption, but it is contingent on the Middle East situation not deteriorating further and on the Q2 data not showing a sharp slowdown. If the disruption persists and the economic data weakens, the BOJ may be forced to delay, which would create a credibility problem given the hawkish dissent in April. Conversely, if the data holds up and the disruption begins to normalize, a July hike would reinforce the BOJ’s commitment to normalization and signal confidence in the domestic outlook.

The implications for markets are significant. A July hike would push the policy rate to 1.0%, the highest level in Japan in over a decade, and would set the stage for further normalization in 2027. A delay would be interpreted as a sign of weakness and could trigger a selloff in the yen and a rally in JGBs. The BOJ is walking a tightrope, and the next two months of data will determine which direction it leans.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Transmission Mechanism of the Energy Shock to Domestic Demand

The report provides a thorough analysis of Q1 data and a reasonable baseline forecast for Q2 and Q3, but it leaves several important questions unresolved. The most critical is the transmission mechanism of the energy shock to domestic demand. The report notes that the Middle East disruption has not materially appeared in exports or imports in Q1, but that the impact is beginning to show in industrial production, particularly in chemicals and petroleum products. The question is how this supply-side shock will propagate through the economy.

There are three channels to watch. First, the direct channel: higher energy costs reduce real disposable income and increase input costs for businesses. The report acknowledges this, but the magnitude of the impact depends on the duration and severity of the price increase. Second, the indirect channel: supply disruptions in chemicals and petroleum products can create bottlenecks in downstream industries, from manufacturing to transportation. The report notes that capex could be delayed due to reduced supply of petroleum-derived materials, but it does not quantify the potential impact on production schedules across sectors. Third, the confidence channel: the deterioration in consumer sentiment could become self-reinforcing if it leads to a pullback in spending that further weakens the economy.

The report’s baseline assumption is that the Japanese economy will continue to post positive growth, even with a further deterioration in the terms of trade. This is a reasonable central case, but it leaves open the possibility of a more adverse scenario in which the energy shock triggers a sharper slowdown in consumption and a more pronounced pullback in capex. The report does not provide a detailed stress test or scenario analysis, which would be valuable for readers trying to assess the tail risks.

## A Decision Framework for Readers: How to Position for the Next Six Months

For investors and business leaders trying to navigate the current environment, the report’s data and analysis can be translated into a practical decision framework. The key is to focus on the variables that will determine the trajectory of the economy over the next two quarters, and to monitor them for signs of acceleration or deceleration.

The first variable is the Middle East situation. The duration and severity of the disruption will determine the magnitude of the energy price shock and the extent of supply chain disruptions. A quick resolution would remove the primary headwind and allow the structural drivers of consumption and capex to reassert themselves. A prolonged disruption would increase the risk of a sharper slowdown and could force the BOJ to delay normalization.

The second variable is consumer spending on services versus goods. The report shows that services spending is holding up, while durable goods spending is declining. This divergence is likely to persist as long as sentiment remains weak, but it is not stable. If the energy shock pushes up prices for services, or if the labor market weakens, the services sector could also soften. Monitoring the BOJ’s Consumption Activity Index and the monthly retail sales data for signs of a broader pullback will be critical.

The third variable is corporate capex intentions. The June Tankan survey will be the first major test of whether companies are revising down their investment plans in response to the uncertainty. If the survey shows a significant downward revision, it would signal that the capex floor is weaker than expected. If the survey holds up, it would reinforce the view that structural drivers are providing a buffer.

The fourth variable is BOJ policy. The July meeting is the next major inflection point. A rate hike would signal confidence and support the yen, but it would also tighten financial conditions at a time of uncertainty. A hold would signal caution and could weaken the yen, but it would also reduce the risk of a policy error. The market’s reaction to the decision will provide important information about how investors are interpreting the trade-off.

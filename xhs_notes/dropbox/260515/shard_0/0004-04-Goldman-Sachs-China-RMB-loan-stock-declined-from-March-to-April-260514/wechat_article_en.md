# China's Credit Contraction Is Not a Liquidity Problem—It Is a Demand Problem Disguised as a Policy Success

The April credit data from China landed with a thud that reverberated far beyond the consensus miss. New RMB loans to the real economy turned negative for the first time since July 2025, registering a net contraction of RMB 400 billion against a consensus expectation of RMB 300 billion in positive flows. Total social financing came in at roughly half the forecasted level. For analysts accustomed to interpreting Chinese credit data through the lens of policy calibration, this print demands a fundamental rethinking of the narrative.

The instinctive response is to attribute the weakness to policy tightening or seasonal distortions. That instinct is wrong. The data reveal something more structurally concerning: the sUBStitution of bank lending by bond issuance, while real in accounting terms, masks a deeper erosion of private-sector credit demand. The surge in bill financing—a short-term instrument banks use to fill unused loan quotas—is the tell. When the only growth in corporate lending comes from instruments designed to exhaust quota, the system is not experiencing a liquidity shortage. It is experiencing a borrower shortage.

This matters now because the stakes for 2026 are rising. The full-year total social financing growth forecast has been revised downward to 8.0 percent year-on-year from 8.5 percent, a meaningful downgrade that signals the analysts expect the softness to persist. The question for investors is not whether policy will respond—it will—but whether the response can address the structural nature of the demand weakness. The answer depends on understanding the precise mechanism at work, and that mechanism is more complex than a simple slowdown narrative suggests.

## The Negative RMB Loan Print Is Not an Anomaly—It Is the Logical Outcome of a Balance Sheet Recession in Key Sectors

The headline figure that demands attention is the RMB 400 billion net contraction in loans to the real economy in April. This is not a statistical quirk. It represents the first outright decline in the stock of RMB loans since mid-2025, and the magnitude of the miss relative to consensus is sUBStantial enough to warrant a reassessment of the underlying trend.

Breaking down the components reveals the pattern. Household loan stock declined by RMB 787 billion in April, significantly worse than the RMB 522 billion decline recorded a year earlier. This is consistent with a household sector that remains in deleveraging mode, where mortgage prepayments and consumer credit repayments continue to outpace new borrowing. The corporate side is more nuanced but equally concerning. Corporate loans expanded by RMB 390 billion in aggregate, but that headline masks a critical detail: the entire expansion was driven by a surge in bill financing, which reached RMB 1.24 trillion in April compared to RMB 834 billion a year earlier. Mid-to-long term corporate loans, the category that signals genuine investment appetite, declined by RMB 410 billion.

The bill financing surge is the analytical key. Banks use short-term bill financing to meet regulatory lending targets when genuine borrower demand is absent. It is a quota-filling exercise, not a reflection of productive credit deployment. When mid-to-long term lending is contracting while bill financing is expanding, the system is signaling that banks are willing to lend but borrowers are unwilling to take on term debt. This is the classic signature of a balance sheet recession: entities with access to credit are choosing to deleverage rather than invest, and the banking system responds by parking liquidity in short-term instruments that satisfy regulatory metrics without creating genuine economic stimulus.

The official media explanation—that bond issuance has sUBStituted for bank lending due to lower financing costs—is partially correct but insufficient. Yes, corporate bond issuance rose to RMB 1.74 trillion in April from RMB 1.43 trillion in March. And yes, lower bond yields make debt capital markets more attractive relative to bank loans. But sUBStitution explains only part of the story. If total credit extension across all channels were robust, the sUBStitution narrative would be reassuring. It is not. Total social financing flows were still well below expectations, indicating that the bond market pickup was insufficient to offset the broader credit weakness.

## The Bond Market SUBStitution Narrative Obscures a More Troubling Shift in the Composition of Credit Intermediation

The argument that stronger bond issuance has sUBStituted for bank lending is convenient for policymakers because it frames the data as a success story of capital market development rather than a credit demand problem. The data, however, suggest a more complex and less reassuring interpretation.

Consider the seasonally adjusted flows. Total social financing flows actually rose from March to April on a seasonally adjusted basis, driven by increased bond issuance. Government and corporate bond issuance combined reached RMB 1.74 trillion, up from RMB 1.43 trillion in March. But this increase was more than offset by the decline in bank loan extension, which fell to RMB 948 billion from RMB 1.12 trillion. The net result was that TSF stock growth edged down to 7.8 percent year-on-year from 7.9 percent.

The sUBStitution narrative works only if the bond market is absorbing the same borrowers at similar terms. In practice, the composition of bond issuance matters. Government bond issuance is largely predetermined by fiscal plans and does not reflect private-sector credit demand. Corporate bond issuance, while higher, is concentrated among state-owned enterprises and high-grade credits that have access to both bank and bond markets. The borrowers who are absent from both channels are the small and medium enterprises, the property developers, and the private-sector firms that form the backbone of employment and consumption.

This compositional shift has second-order implications for monetary policy transmission. When credit intermediation moves from bank lending to bond markets, the transmission mechanism changes. Bank lending is relationship-based and can be directed through administrative guidance. Bond markets are price-based and respond to yield differentials and credit spreads. A policy framework designed to stimulate bank lending may be ineffective if the marginal borrower is already priced out of both channels. The declining mid-to-long term corporate loans suggest that the borrowers who need credit most are not accessing it through either channel, while the borrowers who have access are sUBStituting one form of financing for another without increasing their total leverage.

## The M1 and M2 Divergence Reveals a Fiscal Transmission Problem That Policy Has Not Yet Addressed

The monetary aggregates offer a complementary lens through which to interpret the credit weakness. M1 growth edged down to 5.0 percent year-on-year from 5.1 percent, while M2 growth edged up to 8.6 percent from 8.5 percent. The divergence is modest but directionally significant.

The key driver of the M1 moderation is fiscal deposits. Fiscal deposits increased by RMB 739 billion in April, which was RMB 368 billion higher than the increase a year earlier. This implies a slower pace of fiscal spending relative to revenue collection. When the government collects taxes and issues bonds but delays spending, the funds sit in fiscal deposits rather than circulating in the economy. This directly suppresses M1, which measures money held in transaction accounts that is available for immediate spending.

The implication is that fiscal policy, while expansive on paper, is not translating into real-economy stimulus at the pace intended. The government bond issuance is occurring, but the proceeds are accumulating in deposits rather than being deployed into infrastructure projects, transfer payments, or procurement. This creates a timing mismatch that exacerbates the credit demand weakness. Fiscal stimulus works best when it puts money directly into the hands of households and businesses that will spend it. When fiscal deposits rise, the stimulus is delayed, and the multiplier effect is reduced.

The M2 acceleration, meanwhile, reflects the banking system's continued creation of deposits through bond purchases and other financial transactions. But M2 can grow even when the real economy is stagnant, as long as financial institutions are intermediating between savers and the government. The divergence between M1 and M2 growth is therefore a warning signal: the financial system is expanding, but the expansion is not reaching the transaction accounts that drive economic activity.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Mechanism of a Potential Policy Inflection

The report provides a clear diagnosis of the current weakness but leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important is the timing of the policy response. The full-year TSF growth forecast has been revised down to 8.0 percent from 8.5 percent, which implies that the analysts expect the softness to persist through the remainder of the year. But what would cause a reversal? The report does not specify the conditions under which credit demand would recover.

A second open question concerns the effectiveness of further monetary easing. If the problem is demand-driven rather than supply-driven, lowering interest rates or reserve requirements may not stimulate borrowing. The surge in bill financing suggests that banks have ample capacity to lend but cannot find creditworthy borrowers willing to take term loans. Lower rates could narrow net interest margins without increasing loan volumes. The report does not address whether the transmission mechanism is broken and, if so, what would fix it.

A third question relates to the property sector. The household loan decline is consistent with ongoing deleveraging, but the report does not disaggregate household loans into mortgages versus consumer credit. Understanding which component is driving the decline would help assess whether the weakness is concentrated in housing or is broader-based. Similarly, the corporate loan data by sector would clarify whether the contraction is concentrated in property developers or is spreading to manufacturing and services.

These open questions are not criticisms of the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of what can be inferred from aggregate data. The answers require granular data on borrower categories, sectoral credit flows, and the pipeline of approved but undrawn credit facilities. Until those data are available, investors must operate with a range of scenarios rather than a single forecast.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguishing Between Cyclical Softness and Structural Credit Regime Change

For investors, the key analytical task is to distinguish between a cyclical slowdown that will reverse with policy support and a structural shift in the credit regime that has longer-term implications for asset allocation. The report provides the data to make this distinction, but the interpretation requires a framework.

Consider three scenarios. In the first scenario, the weakness is cyclical and policy-responsive. The government accelerates fiscal spending, the People's Bank of China cuts rates further, and credit demand recovers in the second half of the year. In this scenario, the current weakness creates a buying opportunity in cyclical assets, particularly those linked to infrastructure and state-owned enterprise investment. The TSF growth forecast of 8.0 percent would prove conservative, and upward revisions would support risk assets.

In the second scenario, the weakness is structural but concentrated. The property sector and local government financing vehicles continue to deleverage, but the rest of the economy maintains access to credit through bond markets. In this scenario, credit growth stabilizes at a lower trend rate, and the composition of credit shifts permanently toward government and high-grade corporate bonds. Asset allocation would favor duration and credit quality over cyclical exposure.

In the third scenario, the weakness is structural and spreading. Private-sector borrowers across multiple sectors lose access to credit, and the banking system retreats into bill financing and other quota-filling instruments. In this scenario, the credit contraction becomes self-reinforcing as declining investment leads to declining income, which leads to declining credit demand. The policy response would need to be far more aggressive than current expectations, and asset allocation would favor cash, gold, and defensive equities.

The April data are most consistent with the second scenario, but the direction of travel is toward the third. The key variable to monitor is not the aggregate credit growth rate but the composition. If mid-to-long term corporate lending continues to decline while bill financing grows, the structural interpretation gains weight. If fiscal deposits continue to rise, the fiscal transmission problem deepens. If household loan contraction accelerates, the consumption outlook darkens.

Investors should build monitoring frameworks around these three variables rather than the headline TSF number. The headline will be noisy and subject to base effects. The composition will tell the real story. The April data are a warning, not a verdict, but they are a warning that should not be dismissed as a one-month anomaly.

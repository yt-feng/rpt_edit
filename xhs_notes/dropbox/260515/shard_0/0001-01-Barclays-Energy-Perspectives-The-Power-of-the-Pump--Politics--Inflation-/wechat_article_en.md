# The 2026 Gas Price Shock Is More Structurally Dangerous Than 2022—and Markets Are Not Pricing It Correctly

The oil market in 2026 is under more severe physical stress than it was during the Russia-Ukraine shock, yet inflation expectations and credit spreads are behaving as if this disruption will be short-lived. That divergence is the single most important analytical tension in macro markets today. Thirteen percent of global oil supply is currently offline, compared to just three percent in 2022. Gasoline prices are already eighteen cents higher than they were on the same date in 2022, and the rate of increase since the start of the Iran conflict has been thirty percent—matching the pace of the 2022 spike. The scale of the disruption is larger, the duration is extending, and the political calendar is unforgiving.

What makes this moment different is not merely the magnitude of the supply shock, but the structure of the response. In 2022, prices peaked and then began to fall by late June, giving voters and markets a de-escalation narrative before the midterm elections. That pattern is not repeating. The supply constraints this time are not a single geopolitical event with a clear off-ramp. They involve a combination of crude production losses, refining bottlenecks, and inventory draws that are compounding rather than resolving. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve has already been drawn down, spare capacity is limited, and the refining system is the binding constraint—a problem that policy tools designed for crude supply cannot easily address.

The market is discounting a benign outcome. Inflation expectations have not repriced to 2022 levels. Toll road and airport credit spreads remain tight, even for lower-rated issuers in tourist-dependent regions. This calm assumes that the price spike will fade before it becomes entrenched. That assumption deserves scrutiny. The persistence of higher gas prices, not the initial spike, is what drives both inflation pass-through and voter behavior. A ten percent persistent increase in gas prices boosts headline CPI by roughly 0.2 percentage points within one to two months. If prices remain elevated through the summer driving season and into the fall, the cumulative effect on inflation and on political sentiment will be materially larger than what current market pricing implies.

## The Duration of Elevated Gas Prices, Not the Peak, Will Determine the Inflation and Political Outcome

The most common analytical error in energy-driven macro scenarios is to focus on the headline number—will gas hit five dollars?—rather than on how long it stays there. In 2022, national average gas prices reached $5.01 per gallon in June, but they had fallen to $3.80 by Election Day in November. That decline allowed inflation to moderate and shifted voter attention to other issues. Exit polls showed that only 31 percent of voters cited inflation broadly as their top concern, and abortion ranked nearly as high. The price spike lasted roughly sixteen weeks and was receding before the political window opened.

The current trajectory is different. Prices have risen more rapidly from a lower base, and the supply-side drivers are more entrenched. The refining constraint means that even if crude prices stabilize, the pass-through to retail gasoline may remain elevated because the bottleneck is at the processing stage. Inventory data shows that US gasoline demand cover has fallen to the low end of the seasonal range, and total oil inventories are drawing at an accelerated pace. Historical analysis suggests that a fifty percent increase in retail prices will reduce demand by only three to four percent over time—far too small to rebalance the market given the scale of the supply disruption.

For the midterm elections, the relevant question is not whether gas prices hit five dollars, but whether they are still above four dollars in October. If the current trajectory holds, prices will have been elevated for twenty weeks or more by then. That duration transforms the political calculus. Congressional Republicans are the ones facing re-election, not the president. Their willingness to sustain political costs for a war policy that keeps gas prices high will erode as the election approaches. The polling data already shows that perceptions of gas price increases are deeply partisan, but that partisan buffer has limits. When prices stay high long enough, the gap between perception and reality narrows, and the political pressure becomes bipartisan.

## The Refining Bottleneck Makes This Shock Different from 2022 and Limits the Effectiveness of Conventional Policy Tools

The most underappreciated structural feature of the current crisis is that the binding constraint is not crude oil supply but refining capacity. Prompt-month RBOB futures have risen 74 percent since the start of the Iran conflict, compared to 51 percent for WTI crude. That divergence tells a clear story: the market is pricing a product shortage, not just a crude shortage. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve, spare capacity, and potential US supply increases are all designed to address crude availability. They do little to alleviate the refining bottleneck.

This distinction matters for policy. The administration has already deployed most of its conventional tools: SPR releases, sanctions waivers, a Jones Act waiver, and an EPA waiver to allow E15 sales during the summer driving season. These measures have helped at the margin, but they have not closed the widening spread between AAA retail gasoline and RBOB futures, which is currently over one dollar per gallon. That spread reflects the cost of converting crude into finished product under capacity constraints.

A federal gas tax holiday, which some have proposed, would provide only modest relief—an estimated $5 to $14 per capita over three months—while creating a significant fiscal problem. The Highway Trust Fund, which relies on federal gas tax revenues of $36 to $38 billion annually, is already projected to be exhausted by fiscal year 2028. A six-month suspension would accelerate that timeline to the first half of 2027. State-level gas tax holidays are more feasible, and several states have already implemented or proposed them, but these are geographically limited and do not address the national pricing dynamic.

Export restrictions on crude oil are another theoretical option, but they come with significant complications. US refineries are configured for heavy crude, while domestic production is primarily light crude. The infrastructure to move crude domestically in a cost-effective manner is inadequate. Restricting exports would not automatically increase domestic gasoline supply. The most effective policy lever would be reopening the Strait of Hormuz militarily or reaching a diplomatic resolution with Iran, but time for either option is running short.

## The Credit Market Implications Are Concentrated in Specific Sectors and Geographies, Not Broad-Based

The benign behavior of credit spreads so far masks a vulnerability that is sector-specific and geographically concentrated. Toll roads and airports, particularly those serving tourist-heavy markets, are the most exposed to sustained high gas prices. The logic is straightforward: higher fuel costs reduce discretionary travel, and the effect is amplified for lower-rated issuers whose revenue streams are more sensitive to volume changes.

The Spirit Airlines liquidation provides a cautionary precedent. When a low-cost carrier with a leisure-heavy route network fails, it signals that the travel demand elasticity is higher than many investors assumed. If gas prices remain elevated through the summer, the pressure on smaller and medium-sized airports that depend on leisure traffic will intensify. Their credit spreads have not yet repriced to reflect this risk, partly because the market is still treating the gas price spike as temporary.

Toll roads face a different but related risk. Higher fuel costs reduce vehicle miles traveled, and the effect is more pronounced for toll roads that serve discretionary trips rather than commuter routes. The revenue impact is lagged but cumulative. If gas prices stay high for three to four months, the traffic data will show a measurable decline, and credit spreads for the most exposed issuers will widen.

The inflation channel is equally important but operates on a different timeline. A persistent ten percent increase in gas prices feeds through to headline CPI within one to two months, but the pass-through to core inflation is smaller and takes longer. This means that if gas prices remain elevated through August, the September and October CPI prints will show an acceleration that the market is not currently pricing. One-year-forward one-year CPI swaps would need to move well above three percent to reflect this risk, which they have not done.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Mechanism of Demand Destruction

The report provides a clear framework for understanding the supply side of the equation, but it leaves several critical questions about demand unresolved. The analysis suggests that a fifty percent increase in retail prices will reduce demand by only three to four percent over time. That estimate is based on historical elastiCities, but the current environment may produce a different response for two reasons.

First, the post-pandemic travel patterns are still evolving. Remote work has reduced commuting demand but increased leisure travel. The elasticity of leisure travel to fuel prices may be higher than historical averages because households have more flexibility to postpone or cancel trips. Second, the cumulative effect of multiple price shocks over a short period may produce a nonlinear demand response. Consumers who absorbed higher prices in 2022 and again in 2026 may reach a threshold where they change behavior more dramatically than the historical data would predict.

The report also does not fully address the interaction between gas prices and broader consumer confidence. If sustained high gas prices coincide with a weakening labor market or declining real wages, the demand response could be amplified. The credit market implications would then extend beyond toll roads and airports to consumer-dependent sectors more broadly.

A third open question is the role of China. The report notes that lower demand from China is partially offsetting the supply disruption, but it does not explore how a Chinese demand recovery would change the calculus. If Chinese economic stimulus measures gain traction later this year, the demand side of the global oil market could tighten further, pushing prices higher than the report's base case.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios and Their Portfolio Implications

The most useful way to apply this analysis is to construct three scenarios that map to different outcomes for gas price persistence and their implications for inflation, credit, and political risk.

Scenario one is the benign normalization. In this scenario, diplomatic progress or a military resolution reopens the Strait of Hormuz within the next four to six weeks. Crude prices decline, the refining bottleneck eases as seasonal demand moderates, and gas prices fall below $4.00 by September. Inflation expectations stabilize, credit spreads remain tight, and the midterm elections are dominated by non-energy issues. In this scenario, current market pricing is broadly correct, and the main risk is opportunity cost for investors who hedged too aggressively.

Scenario two is the prolonged elevation. In this scenario, no diplomatic resolution emerges, but the supply disruption does not worsen. Gas prices remain in the $4.50 to $5.00 range through the summer and into the fall. The persistence of high prices gradually erodes consumer confidence and reduces discretionary travel. Toll road and airport credit spreads widen by 50 to 100 basis points for the most exposed issuers. Inflation expectations drift higher, and one-year-forward CPI swaps move above three percent. The political pressure on congressional Republicans intensifies, but the president maintains his position on the war. In this scenario, current market pricing is too complacent, and investors should be reducing exposure to tourist-dependent municipal credits and adding inflation hedges.

Scenario three is the escalation. In this scenario, the supply disruption deepens due to further conflict or infrastructure damage. Gas prices break above $5.00 and remain there for an extended period. Demand destruction accelerates as consumers change behavior in a nonlinear way. Credit spreads widen broadly as recession risk increases. The political calculus shifts dramatically, with congressional Republicans breaking with the president on war policy. In this scenario, current market pricing is severely mispriced, and the portfolio implications are defensive across asset classes.

Each scenario implies a different set of actions. The key analytical challenge is identifying which scenario is unfolding in real time. The most important leading indicator is not the headline gas price but the trajectory of gasoline inventories and the spread between retail and wholesale prices. If inventories continue to draw and the spread remains wide, scenario two or three becomes more likely. If inventories stabilize and the spread narrows, scenario one gains credibility.

## The Questions That Remain Open Should Drive Your Research Agenda

The report raises more questions than it settles, which is the mark of serious analysis. Why has the refining bottleneck persisted despite policy efforts to address it? Is this a temporary capacity issue or a structural problem that will take years to resolve? How will the demand response evolve if prices remain elevated through the end of the year? What is the threshold at which the political pressure on congressional Republicans becomes overwhelming? And what happens to the Highway Trust Fund if a federal gas tax holiday is enacted?

These are not academic questions. They have direct implications for portfolio positioning, risk management, and investment timing. The market is currently pricing a benign outcome. If that assumption proves wrong, the repricing will be sharp and concentrated in the sectors most exposed to sustained high gas prices. The time to prepare for that repricing is now, not after the data becomes undeniable.

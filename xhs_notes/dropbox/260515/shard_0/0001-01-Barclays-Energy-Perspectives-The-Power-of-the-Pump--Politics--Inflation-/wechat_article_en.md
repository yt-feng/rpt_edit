# The 2026 Oil Shock Is Structurally Worse Than 2022, Yet Markets Are Pricing the Opposite—That Divergence Will Not Last

The scale of the global oil supply disruption in 2026 has already surpassed the 2022 Russia-Ukraine shock by a wide margin, yet inflation expectations remain subdued and credit spreads in transportation-linked municipal bonds have not repriced. This disconnect is not a sign of resilience. It is a timing mismatch between the slow-moving nature of macroeconomic transmission and the sudden, concentrated nature of this supply crisis. Investors who treat the current benign market conditions as a signal of stability are likely underestimating the second-order effects that will compound over the coming months.

Thirteen percent of global oil supply is currently offline, compared to just three percent during the 2022 crisis. Gasoline prices today are already 18 cents higher than on the same date in 2022, and the rate of increase since the start of the Iran war has been 30 percent, faster than the 2022 trajectory. Retail gasoline at the national average is approaching $4.50 per gallon, and the gap to the psychologically critical $5 threshold is narrowing faster than most market participants appreciate. The prompt-month RBOB futures have risen 74 percent since the conflict began, dramatically outpacing the 51 percent rise in both WTI crude and AAA retail gasoline—a clear signal that refining capacity, not just crude supply, has become the binding constraint.

The critical insight is not that prices are high. It is that the duration of this price elevation is likely to exceed the 2022 episode, and that difference has profound implications for inflation, midterm elections, and municipal credit quality. The 2022 spike lasted approximately 16 weeks before prices began to fall. The current crisis shows no analogous relief valve. The strategic petroleum reserve has already been drawn down significantly. Sanctions waivers, Jones Act waivers, and EPA waivers for E15 sales have been deployed. What remains on the policy shelf is either politically untenable—a federal gas tax holiday that would cripple the Highway Trust Fund—or operationally ineffective, such as crude export restrictions that would do little to address the refining bottleneck. Time may have run out for a diplomatic resolution to reopen the Strait of Hormuz before the summer driving season locks in elevated prices through the autumn.

This is not a commodity story alone. It is a political and credit story whose dimensions are only beginning to emerge.

## The Perceptual Gap Between Voters and Prices Creates a Political Time Bomb That Will Detonate by Labor Day

Recent polling reveals a striking divergence: perceptions of gas price changes track political affiliation far more closely than they track actual price movements. In a May 2026 Economist/YouGov survey, 61 percent of US adult Citizens said gas prices had gone up "a lot," but the partisan split was extreme. Only 49 percent of Trump voters acknowledged a large increase, compared to 82 percent of Harris voters. Among self-identified MAGA supporters, the figure dropped to 39 percent. Even more telling, there is little correlation between a state's actual gas price increase and the share of its residents who report prices rising significantly. Republicans in states with the smallest price increases were nearly as likely to deny the severity as Republicans in states hit hardest.

This perceptual gap is not merely a sociological curiosity. It has direct political consequences. The 2022 midterm elections offer a cautionary precedent. Gas prices peaked at $5.01 per gallon in June 2022, but by the November midterms they had fallen to $3.80—only 20 cents above the pre-invasion level. That decline effectively neutralized gasoline as a dominant electoral issue. Exit polls showed inflation broadly and abortion as the top concerns, not fuel prices. The current trajectory is different. Prices are rising in May, not falling. The summer driving season will test whether the administration can keep the national average below the Biden-era peak of $5.01, adjusted for inflation to $5.58 in today's dollars.

The political calculus is asymmetric. Congressional Republicans face reelection in November 2026, while President Trump does not. If gas prices remain above $4.50 through August, the perceptual gap among Republican voters will begin to close. The partisan cushion that currently insulates the administration from political blowback will erode as actual price persistence overwhelms motivated reasoning. By Labor Day, if prices have not reversed, the political pressure on congressional Republicans to break with the administration's war policy will become intense. The few defections on War Powers Act resolutions to date suggest discipline is holding, but discipline has a price threshold.

## The Refining Bottleneck Means Demand Destruction Is the Only Near-Term Balancing Mechanism, and That Will Hit Muni Credit

The conventional narrative that this is a crude supply crisis is incomplete. The sharper increase in wholesale gasoline futures relative to crude indicates that refining capacity, not crude availability, is the marginal constraint. US gasoline inventories in days of demand have fallen to the low end of the seasonal range, and total oil inventories are drawing rapidly. The strategic petroleum reserve releases and potential US supply response are both skewed toward addressing the crude side of the equation. Refining capacity cannot be added quickly, and the existing capacity is operating near limits.

Historical analysis suggests that a 50 percent increase in retail gasoline prices will reduce demand by approximately 3 to 4 percent over time. That is far from sufficient to rebalance the market given the scale of supply disruption. The implication is clear: either prices must rise further to destroy more demand, or the supply situation must resolve. The base case Brent forecast of $100 per barrel for 2026 is skewed to the upside.

For municipal credit investors, the transmission mechanism is direct but lagged. Toll roads and airports, particularly those serving tourist-heavy markets and carrying lower credit ratings, are the most exposed. Higher fuel prices suppress discretionary travel, and the Spirit Airlines liquidation has already demonstrated that the airline industry's capacity to absorb cost shocks is limited. Credit spreads for these issuers have not yet widened materially, suggesting that the market is either discounting a near-term resolution or underestimating the persistence of elevated prices. If the 2022 analogy were to hold, one-year-forward one-year CPI swaps would be well above three percent, and tight spreads on lower-rated transportation munis would look vulnerable.

The mechanism is not instantaneous. Fuel price increases take one to two months to feed into headline CPI, with a smaller and slower pass-through to core inflation. But the cumulative effect of a 10 percent persistent increase in gasoline prices is approximately 0.2 percentage points on headline CPI. If prices remain elevated through September, the inflation data for August and September will reflect the full impact, arriving just as voters begin to focus on the midterms.

## The Policy Toolkit Is Exhausted, and What Remains Comes with Severe Trade-Offs

The administration has already deployed the most effective short-term tools: SPR releases, sanctions waivers, a Jones Act waiver, and an EPA waiver for summer E15 sales. Each of these measures provided marginal relief, but none addresses the structural refining constraint or the scale of the supply disruption. The remaining options are either politically costly, operationally limited, or both.

A federal gas tax holiday of 18.4 cents per gallon would provide modest relief—an estimated $5 to $14 per capita over three months, based on 2022 analysis—but would accelerate the depletion of the Highway Trust Fund, which the CBO already expects to exhaust by fiscal year 2028. If a holiday lasted six months or longer, the fund would likely run out of money in the first half of 2027, creating a separate fiscal crisis for surface transportation funding. Congress has never suspended the federal gas tax, and the political will to do so now is uncertain.

Crude oil export restrictions are legally available for up to one year, but they would not solve the problem. US refineries are configured for heavy crude, while domestic production is primarily light crude. The infrastructure to move crude domestically in a cost-effective manner is inadequate. Restricting exports would not meaningfully increase the supply of gasoline, because the bottleneck is refining, not crude availability. The administration has repeatedly stated that export controls are not under consideration, and for good reason.

State-level gas tax holidays offer some relief but are uneven and limited. Georgia, Indiana, and Utah have implemented partial or full suspensions. Approximately ten other states have proposals at various stages, but governors in several key states—including Florida, New York, and Ohio—have opposed such measures. California has already voted against a gas tax holiday, opting instead for targeted income-based payments and EV incentives. The aggregate impact of state actions is unlikely to move the national average significantly.

The only tool that could fundamentally change the supply picture is a military reopening of the Strait of Hormuz or a diplomatic resolution with Iran. Both appear increasingly unlikely in the near term. The administration's remaining lever is jawboning: public pressure on oil companies to moderate prices or refrain from reporting large earnings. This may influence investor expectations at the margin, but it will not close the $1 per gallon spread between AAA retail gasoline and RBOB futures.

## What the Report Does Not Yet Answer: The Demand Elasticity Uncertainty and the Political Feedback Loop

The analysis makes a compelling case that demand destruction of 3 to 4 percent is insufficient to rebalance the market, but it does not fully address the uncertainty around demand elasticity at these price levels. The 50 percent increase in retail prices since the start of the war is historically large, and the demand response may be nonlinear. If consumers begin to change behavior more aggressively—shifting to public transit, consolidating trips, or accelerating EV adoption—the demand adjustment could exceed historical estimates. Conversely, if the perceptual gap among Republican voters delays behavioral change, demand may prove more inelastic than modeled.

The political feedback loop is also underexplored. The report notes that congressional Republicans have remained supportive of the war, but it does not model the tipping point at which electoral self-preservation overrides party loyalty. If gas prices remain above $4.50 through August, and if polling among Republican-leaning independents begins to shift, the political calculus could change rapidly. A small number of defections could trigger a broader reassessment of the war's sustainability, with direct implications for the supply outlook.

Finally, the interaction between gas prices and broader inflation expectations remains uncertain. The benign inflation swap pricing today may reflect a market that has learned from 2022 that gasoline-driven inflation is transitory. But the duration of the current shock may be longer, and the pass-through to core inflation, while smaller, may accumulate over time. If inflation expectations begin to drift higher, the Federal Reserve's reaction function becomes a wild card, with implications for all risk assets, including municipal bonds.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios and Their Portfolio Implications

Investors should not treat the current benign market pricing as a forecast. They should construct a scenario framework and position accordingly.

Scenario One: Quick Resolution. A diplomatic breakthrough reopens the Strait of Hormuz within 60 days. Oil supply normalizes, gasoline prices fall below $4.00 by September, and the 2022 analogy holds: prices decline before voters focus on the midterms. In this scenario, current market pricing is validated. Toll road and airport muni spreads remain stable. CPI swaps revert to pre-crisis levels. The portfolio implication is to do nothing, but this scenario carries the lowest probability given the current trajectory of negotiations.

Scenario Two: Prolonged Elevation Without Breaching $5. Gasoline prices stabilize between $4.50 and $4.99 through the summer driving season, then begin a gradual decline in the autumn. The political impact is muted because the psychological $5 threshold is never breached, but the economic impact accumulates. Headline CPI drifts 0.2 to 0.3 percentage points higher by September. Muni credit spreads for lower-rated tourist-dependent airports widen 20 to 40 basis points. The portfolio implication is to reduce exposure to small and medium-sized airport bonds and tourist-heavy toll roads, and to increase duration in high-quality general obligation bonds.

Scenario Three: Breach and Persist. Gasoline prices exceed $5.00 by July and remain above that level through the midterms. The perceptual gap among Republican voters collapses. Political pressure forces either a policy response or a change in war strategy. Inflation expectations re-anchor higher. Credit spreads for transportation munis widen significantly. The portfolio implication is to move to a defensive posture: overweight cash and short-duration high-grade munis, underwrite exposure to discretionary travel-linked credits, and prepare for a volatility regime shift in the fourth quarter.

The key variable to monitor is not the absolute price level but the trajectory. If prices are still rising in August, the probability of Scenario Three increases sharply. If they have plateaued or begun to decline, Scenario Two becomes more likely. The data to watch is weekly EIA inventory reports and the AAA national average. The decision point is Labor Day.

# China’s Credit Contraction Is Not a Liquidity Problem—It Is a Structural Demand Crisis

The April credit data from China delivered a clear and uncomfortable message: the economy is not suffering from a shortage of money, but from a shortage of borrowers willing to deploy it. New aggregate financing came in at RMB621 billion, roughly half the consensus expectation of RMB1.25 trillion. Outstanding aggregate financing growth slipped to 7.8% year-on-year, the weakest reading on record. But the headline understates the severity. New RMB loans turned negative for the first time since July 2025, clocking in at minus RMB10 billion. This was not a seasonal blip. It was the lowest April reading on record, and it signals a fundamental breakdown in the transmission mechanism between policy intent and private-sector behavior.

The significance of this moment extends far beyond one month's data. For months, the prevailing narrative among market participants has been that China's economic weakness is a function of insufficient policy stimulus. The argument has been that if Beijing would only cut rates, lower reserve requirements, and accelerate fiscal spending, credit demand would rebound. The April data challenges that assumption head-on. The People's Bank of China has already been maintaining accommodative liquidity conditions. The interbank repo rate DR007 fell below the policy rate in April for the first time since August 2023. Money is cheap. The problem is that no one wants to borrow it.

This is the critical analytical distinction that investors must internalize. China is entering a phase where monetary policy is losing its effectiveness not because of transmission bottlenecks, but because of a structural collapse in credit demand from both households and corporations. The policy response required to address this is fundamentally different from the rate-cutting cycle that many have been expecting. And as this report will argue, the implications for asset allocation, sector selection, and macroeconomic forecasting are profound.

## The Collapse in Household Credit Reveals a Balance Sheet Recession That Is Deepening, Not Cyclical

The most alarming component of the April data is the household sector. New household loans fell to minus RMB787 billion in April, a sharp deterioration from minus RMB522 billion a year earlier. Both short-term and medium- to long-term components turned more negative. Medium- to long-term household loans, which primarily reflect mortgage borrowing, dropped to minus RMB341 billion from minus RMB123 billion. This is not a temporary pause in home buying. It is a sustained retreat from leverage.

What makes this particularly concerning is the context. The property sector has been in distress for years. Policy measures have been deployed repeatedly to stabilize it. Yet household borrowing continues to contract. The data suggests that households are not merely waiting for better conditions. They are actively deleveraging. New household deposits also fell sharply, dropping by RMB1.9 trillion in April compared to a decline of RMB1.4 trillion a year earlier. This combination—falling loans and falling deposits—points to a balance sheet recession dynamic. Households are not shifting their savings into other assets in a meaningful way. They are simply reducing both sides of their balance sheets.

There is one nuance worth watching. The report notes that deposits at non-bank financial institutions increased significantly, while household deposits declined. This suggests some migration of funds from bank deposits to investment products offered by NBFIs, likely driven by lower bank deposit rates and the maturation of time deposits. But this is not a sign of healthy risk appetite. It is a search for yield in a low-rate environment, not a vote of confidence in the economic outlook. The household sector remains cautious, and that caution is now embedded in the data for the fifth consecutive month.

## Corporate Credit Demand Is Being Masked by Bill Financing, and the Real Picture Is Worse Than It Appears

At first glance, the corporate sector data might seem less alarming. New corporate loans totaled RMB390 billion in April, down from RMB610 billion a year earlier, but still positive. However, this headline obscures a severe underlying weakness. Medium- to long-term corporate loans turned negative, falling to minus RMB410 billion from positive RMB250 billion a year earlier. Short-term loans were also deeply negative.

The only reason the overall corporate loan number is not even worse is a surge in bill financing. New bill financing reached a record high of RMB1.24 trillion in April, up from RMB834 billion a year earlier. Bill financing is not a genuine indicator of corporate credit demand. It is a short-term, often revolving instrument that banks use to meet lending targets without taking on genuine credit risk. When bill financing spikes while term loans collapse, it signals that banks are pushing credit onto corporate balance sheets that companies do not actually want. The real demand for productive investment capital is far weaker than the headline suggests.

There is also an important sUBStitution effect underway. New corporate bond financing under aggregate financing rose to RMB454 billion in April from RMB234 billion a year earlier. This suggests that companies that do need funding are shifting from bank loans to bond markets, likely because bond yields have fallen and the cost of capital is lower there. This is rational behavior, but it does not change the fact that overall corporate funding demand is weak. The bond market is capturing a larger share of a shrinking pie.

The implication is clear. The corporate sector is not investing. It is not expanding capacity. It is not taking on long-term debt to fund growth. The record bill financing is a canary in the coal mine, not a sign of vitality.

## The Policy Response Has Shifted, and the Absence of Rate Cuts in 2026 Is a Strategic Decision, Not a Mistake

Given the weakness in the data, one might expect the PBoC to accelerate monetary easing. The report explicitly states the opposite. Following the April Politburo meeting, the forecast for any reserve requirement ratio cut or policy rate cut in 2026 has been removed entirely. Previously, the expectation had been for a 50 basis point RRR cut in the second quarter and a 10 basis point policy rate cut in the fourth quarter. That is now off the table.

The reasoning is twofold. First, the Politburo's assessment of the economy and its wording on monetary policy showed no signs of near-term urgency for easing. The official language was consistent with recent PBoC communications, which have emphasized patience and precision over broad-based stimulus. Second, and more importantly, inflationary pressures are building. The ongoing global energy shock, compounded by the Strait of Hormuz disruption, has pushed domestic inflation notably higher in April. Import growth remained highly elevated at 25.3% year-on-year, with semiconductor imports making a notable contribution. The PBoC's first-quarter Monetary Policy Report explicitly warned about monitoring imported inflationary pressures.

This is a critical strategic pivot. The PBoC is choosing to hold its fire despite weak growth because it sees inflation risk as the more immediate threat. This is not a mistake born of conservatism. It is a deliberate calculation that the marginal benefit of further rate cuts would be low given the structural nature of the credit demand problem, while the marginal cost in terms of inflation and currency stability would be high. Investors who have been positioning for a dovish pivot need to reassess.

## One Critical Analytical Gap: The Report Does Not Fully Explain Why Fiscal Policy Is Not Filling the Void

The report notes that government bond issuance was modestly weaker than year-earlier levels in April, which it describes as slightly surprising. It expects government bond issuance to accelerate in coming months to support fixed asset investment. But this expectation raises more questions than it answers.

If the private sector is unwilling to borrow and spend, the obvious policy response is for the government to borrow and spend instead. Yet the data shows that net government bond issuance actually fell in April compared to the prior year. Fiscal deposits rose by RMB739 billion, suggesting that funds are being collected but not yet deployed. The gap between policy intent and execution remains wide.

The report does not fully explore why this gap exists. Is it due to project readiness constraints? Local government financing platform restrictions? Political caution ahead of key meetings? The answer matters enormously for the outlook. If fiscal acceleration is coming, it could stabilize credit growth in the second half of the year. But if the bottlenecks are structural rather than temporal, then the weakness in aggregate financing will persist regardless of how much the PBoC cuts rates or the Ministry of Finance issues bonds. This is the unresolved question that investors should be tracking most closely.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position When Credit Demand Is Broken and Policy Is Stuck

For portfolio managers and strategists, the April data demands a shift in analytical framework. The old playbook of buying cyclical exposure on expectations of policy easing no longer works. The following decision framework can help navigate this environment.

First, assess the direction of credit demand, not just credit supply. The PBoC can make liquidity available, but it cannot force households and corporations to borrow. Monitor the ratio of bill financing to total corporate loans. If it remains elevated, it confirms that underlying demand is weak regardless of what the headline loan number shows.

Second, watch household deposit and loan dynamics together. If both are declining, the economy is in a balance sheet repair phase. This favors defensive sectors and high-dividend stocks over growth and cyclical names. It also favors bonds over equities in the near term, as the demand for safe assets remains strong.

Third, track the pace of government bond issuance relative to expectations. If issuance accelerates meaningfully in the coming months, it could provide a floor for credit growth and support infrastructure-related sectors. But if issuance remains below expectations despite official commitments, it signals that the fiscal transmission mechanism is impaired, and the growth outlook will deteriorate further.

Fourth, monitor inflation data closely, especially import prices and semiconductor costs. If inflation continues to rise while growth remains weak, the economy enters a stagflationary phase that is particularly challenging for both equity and fixed income markets. In that scenario, cash and short-duration instruments become more attractive.

Finally, do not assume that the PBoC will eventually capitulate and cut rates. The removal of the 2026 rate cut forecast is a signal that the central bank is willing to tolerate slower growth to maintain price stability. Investors who bet against that resolve could be caught offside.

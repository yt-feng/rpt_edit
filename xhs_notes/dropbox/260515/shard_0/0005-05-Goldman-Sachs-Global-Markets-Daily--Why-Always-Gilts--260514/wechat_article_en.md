# Gilts Are Not Always the Problem: Why the UK Bond Market's Underperformance Is a Story of Global Forces, Domestic Structure, and a Shifting Demand Base

The 30-year Gilt yield recently traded above 5.8%, a level last seen in 1998. This is not just a number; it is a signal that the UK government's cost of long-term borrowing has entered territory that predates the modern inflation-targeting era. For investors who have watched Gilts underperform every other major developed-market bond since 2022, the instinct is to blame UK-specific dysfunction: weak growth, stubborn inflation, political chaos, and a pension system in crisis. That instinct is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The deeper truth is that Gilt underperformance is a compound story, and the most important driver today is not a uniquely British crisis but a global repricing of term premium that has hit the UK harder because of its structural vulnerabilities. The question is whether this repricing is permanent, and whether the factors that made Gilts a perennial underperformer are now fading or accelerating.

The conventional narrative holds that Gilts are always the first to break when bond markets turn. The 2022 liability-driven investment crisis, the Truss mini-budget, and the persistent inflation overshoot all support this view. But a careful analysis of term premium decomposition, supply-demand mechanics, and cross-market comparisons reveals a more nuanced picture. The UK term premium has indeed risen the most among G4 economies since 2022, but in recent quarters, on several relative measures, Gilts have actually shown improvement versus peers. The recent underperformance is less about a rising risk premium and more about the repricing of the Bank of England policy path. This distinction matters because it changes the forecast for where yields go next.

## The UK's Macro Uncertainty Premium Is Structural, Not Cyclical, and It Justifies a Higher Term Premium

The first and most fundamental reason for elevated Gilt yields is that the UK economy has delivered the worst combination of outcomes among major developed markets: the weakest growth and the highest inflation. This is not a temporary misfortune. It reflects deeper structural features of the UK economy, including a high dependence on natural gas in the energy mix, a relatively high degree of inflation indexation in wages and prices, and an economic model that has proven unusually vulnerable to supply shocks. When growth is weak and inflation is high, the correlation between bonds and growth falls, and the hedge value of bonds for risk-asset portfolios deteriorates. Investors demand a higher term premium to hold long-duration assets precisely when those assets are most needed as a diversifier.

The data on macro uncertainty supports this structural interpretation. Survey measures of growth forecast uncertainty and inflation forecast uncertainty are both higher in the UK than in the US, the euro area, or Japan. This is not a recent phenomenon that will automatically revert. The UK has the highest cross-sectional standard deviation of GDP forecasts among G4 economies, and the same is true for inflation forecasts. High uncertainty is not just a function of the post-pandemic shock; it is embedded in the UK's economic structure and its exposure to terms-of-trade shocks. The implication is that a portion of the Gilt term premium is permanent, or at least persistent, because it reflects a structural uncertainty premium that other economies do not command to the same degree.

## The Repricing of Gilt Yields in Recent Months Is a Policy Path Story, Not a Risk Premium Story

It is easy to assume that when Gilt yields rise faster than other bond yields, the UK-specific risk premium must be expanding. The data suggests otherwise. On several measures of relative bond pricing, including swap spreads and the 10s30s curve, Gilts are currently comparable to other major curves and have actually shown improvement on a relative basis in recent quarters. The 10-year OIS swap spread for the UK is now roughly in line with the US and the euro area, and the 10s30s curve steepness is not an outlier. If the UK risk premium were expanding aggressively, these relative measures would show persistent deterioration. They do not.

What has changed in recent months is the market's assessment of the Bank of England policy path. The realized volatility of front-end rates on BoE meeting days is the highest among G4 central banks, and the mean absolute change in 1-year OIS on meeting days is nearly double that of the Federal Reserve. This is not risk premium; it is policy uncertainty. The market is pricing larger and more volatile moves in front-end rates because the BoE's reaction function has become harder to predict. When the fundamental outlook for inflation shifts, as it has in recent months, the repricing of the policy path flows through to the long end. The risk premium has not increased much, but the expected path of rates has. This distinction is critical for investors trying to decide whether to fade the move or chase it.

## Supply and Demand Mechanics Have Permanently Altered the Gilt Market's Structure

The structural story of Gilt underperformance cannot be told without examining the dramatic shift in supply and demand. The Bank of England has swung from being a large buyer of duration to a seller through quantitative tightening, and the domestic pension system is no longer the reliable source of demand it once was. Pension fund holdings of Gilts as a share of total debt have fallen from over 30% in 2019 to around 15% today, and BoE holdings have fallen from a peak of 37% to roughly 23%. The combined effect is a massive increase in the duration-adjusted free-float of Gilts available to the private sector. The composition of demand has also shifted toward more price-sensitive holders. Net repo positioning data shows that insurers, pension schemes, and LDI funds have reduced their net borrowing significantly since 2022, while hedge funds have increased theirs. This means the marginal buyer of Gilts is now a more speculative, yield-sensitive investor rather than a structural, liability-driven buyer. When the composition of demand shifts toward price-sensitive holders, the risk premium becomes more volatile and more responsive to supply news. This is why ongoing fiscal debates about defence spending and fiscal rules can have outsized effects on Gilt yields. The market no longer has a captive buyer of last resort.

## The Report's Model Does Not Fully Capture the Feedback Loop Between Fiscal Pressure and Higher Rates

The term premium decomposition model presented in the analysis identifies supply factors, BoE quantitative tightening, and the global term premium as the main drivers of the UK term premium. Policy uncertainty plays a modest role, and the unemployment gap contributes little. But the model has a limitation that investors should understand: it treats fiscal deficits and bond supply as exogenous drivers, when in fact they are endogenous to the level of yields themselves. Higher Gilt yields increase the government's interest expense, which widens the fiscal deficit, which increases bond supply, which pushes yields higher. This feedback loop is not captured in a linear decomposition, and it may be the most important dynamic to watch in the near term.

The report estimates that a 1 percentage point increase in the medium-term deficit is worth roughly 30 to 40 basis points of higher Gilt yields. If the government faces pressure to increase defence spending or if growth disappoints, the deficit could widen further, and the feedback loop could intensify. The current level of yields is already creating fiscal pressure, and that pressure could become self-reinforcing. The model's decomposition is useful for understanding the past, but it may understate the risks embedded in the present because it cannot fully capture the nonlinear dynamics of fiscal dominance.

## A Decision Framework for Gilt Investors: Distinguish Between the Structural and the Cyclical

For investors trying to position in Gilts, the key is to separate the structural factors that will persist from the cyclical factors that will fade. The structural factors include the macro uncertainty premium, the shift in demand composition away from pension funds, and the higher free-float of duration-adjusted bonds. These factors will not reverse quickly. The cyclical factors include the current level of policy uncertainty, the pace of quantitative tightening, and the path of global term premium. These factors are likely to moderate over time.

The decision framework has three steps. First, assess the macro outlook. If the UK economy delivers data consistent with the Bank of England holding rates through 2026 and potentially cutting in 2027, as the report's economists forecast, then the cyclical pressure on yields should ease. Second, monitor the fiscal trajectory. Any sign that the government is willing to loosen fiscal rules or increase borrowing for defence spending would be a clear negative for Gilts, and the 30 to 40 basis point per percentage point of deficit estimate provides a rough rule of thumb for the magnitude of the move. Third, watch the global term premium. If the global repricing of long-end yields stabilizes or reverses, Gilts will benefit disproportionately because they have the highest sensitivity to global factors. The worst-case scenario is a combination of domestic fiscal loosening and a continued global repricing higher. The best case is a global decline in term premium combined with fiscal discipline and a BoE that eventually cuts.

## The Open Question That the Analysis Leaves Unanswered

The report makes a compelling case that the recent underperformance of Gilts is driven by policy path repricing rather than risk premium expansion, and that the structural factors are not worsening. But it leaves one critical question unanswered: what is the equilibrium level of the Gilt term premium in a world where the domestic pension system is no longer a structural buyer, the BoE is no longer a large holder, and the fiscal outlook remains uncertain? The model can decompose the current term premium into its drivers, but it cannot tell us whether the current level is justified by fundamentals or whether it reflects an overshoot. The answer depends on whether the new demand base proves resilient enough to absorb the increased supply at lower yields. That is a question that only time and data will answer, and it is the reason why investors should remain cautious even if the cyclical outlook improves.

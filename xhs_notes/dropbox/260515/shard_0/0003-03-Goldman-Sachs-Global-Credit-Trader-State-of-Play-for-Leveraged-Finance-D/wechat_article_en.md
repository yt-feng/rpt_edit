# The Default Cycle Has Peaked, But the Real Risk Has Shifted From Frequency to Structure

The headline numbers in leveraged finance defaults are finally providing some relief. After a prolonged period of elevated stress, particularly in the floating-rate broadly syndicated loan market, aggregate default activity appears to have crested. The US BSL default rate has fallen from a peak of 7.4% to 5.1%, and US high-yield defaults have been oscillating in a narrow 3-4% band. For investors who have been bracing for a wave of traditional bankruptcies, the data suggests the worst may be over.

But this conclusion is dangerously incomplete. It misses the most consequential transformation underway in credit markets: the structural shift in *how* defaults occur, not just how often. The peak in default *frequency* masks a more troubling trend in default *quality*. The mechanism of distress resolution has changed so fundamentally that the metrics investors have relied on for decades—trailing default rates, recovery averages, sector concentration—are becoming unreliable guides to portfolio risk.

The defining feature of this cycle is not the volume of defaults but the dominance of distressed exchanges and liability management exercises. These out-of-court restructurings now account for 70-80% of all default events across both US and European markets. This is not a temporary anomaly. It reflects a deliberate strategic choice by private equity sponsors and corporate management teams to preserve control, avoid court oversight, and delay the recognition of losses. The consequence is a market that appears calmer on the surface while accumulating structural fragility underneath.

Three themes demand the attention of any serious credit investor: the repeat defaulter phenomenon that erodes recovery values with each successive restructuring, the rise of loan-only capital structures that remove the traditional subordination cushion, and the growing divergence between US and European default trajectories driven by fundamentally different macroeconomic backdrops. Each of these forces is reshaping the risk-return calculus in ways that backward-looking models will fail to capture.

## Distressed Exchanges Have Become the Default Mechanism of Choice, But They Are Creating a Revolving Door of Repeat Defaulters

The shift toward distressed exchanges is not a minor procedural change. It represents a fundamental reengineering of how capital structure distress is resolved. In-court restructurings, for all their costs and delays, impose a finality that out-of-court deals often lack. They force a comprehensive resolution of the balance sheet, with clear seniority rules, creditor committee oversight, and judicial approval. Distressed exchanges, by contrast, are negotiated bilaterally between sponsors and select creditor groups, often leaving junior creditors with incomplete relief and unresolved claims.

The data is stark. When a company completes an in-court restructuring, the probability of a repeat default within three years is near zero. This holds true across US high-yield, US leveraged loans, and European markets. The process works because it addresses the full capital structure. Distressed exchanges tell a different story. Roughly 50% of companies that execute a distressed exchange will default again within three years. This is not a statistical curiosity. It is a structural feature of the mechanism itself.

Why do distressed exchanges fail so often? The answer lies in the incentives of the parties designing them. Private equity sponsors are motivated to keep portfolio companies alive, to avoid marking down fund valuations, and to preserve the option value of a future recovery. They design exchanges that provide just enough debt relief to avoid an immediate cash flow crisis, but not enough to restore the business to sustainable health. The result is a series of iterative restructurings, each one more costly and complex than the last, and each one systematically eroding recovery values for creditors.

For credit investors, this creates a difficult analytical challenge. A company that completes a distressed exchange is not "fixed." It is more accurately described as "still distressed, but with a different maturity profile." The traditional default rate metric treats a distressed exchange as a terminal event, which it rarely is. Investors who rely on trailing default rates as a measure of portfolio health are systematically underestimating the tail risk embedded in their holdings.

## Recovery Rates Are Declining Because the Capital Structure Has Changed, Not Because Companies Are Worth Less

The secular decline in recovery rates is one of the most important and least understood trends in leveraged finance. Over the past two decades, recovery values for senior unsecured bonds have fallen sUBStantially, and the pattern has accelerated in recent years. The conventional explanation points to weaker underwriting standards, higher leverage, and more aggressive covenant-lite structures. All of these are true, but they are not the full story.

The primary driver of declining recoveries is the structural shift in capital structures themselves. The percentage of loans issued by loan-only capital structures—companies that have no outstanding high-yield bonds subordinated to the loan—has risen from roughly 45% to over 70%. This matters because in a traditional mixed capital structure, the presence of subordinated bondholders provides a cushion for senior lenders. When distress occurs, the bonds absorb the first losses, protecting loan recovery values.

In a loan-only structure, there is no such cushion. The loan is the entire capital structure, and when distress hits, the loan bears the full weight of the loss. The recovery rate on a loan in a loan-only structure is lower than the recovery rate on the same loan in a mixed structure, all else equal. This is not because the underlying business is worth less. It is because the loss distribution has been concentrated on a single instrument.

This trend interacts dangerously with the rise of repeat defaults. Each successive restructuring in a loan-only structure chips away at recovery values further, because there are no junior creditors left to absorb incremental losses. The combination of loan-only structures and iterative distressed exchanges creates a downward spiral in recovery outcomes that is difficult to reverse.

For investors, this means that historical recovery rate averages are misleading. A 60% recovery on a senior secured loan might have been reasonable in 2010. In today's market, with loan-only structures dominating and repeat defaults proliferating, the same loan might recover only 40% or less. The risk premium embedded in loan spreads needs to reflect this structural deterioration, and there is strong evidence that it does not.

## The Sector Concentration of Default Risk Is Counterintuitive and Requires Active Reassessment

The prevailing market narrative around default risk has focused heavily on software and technology companies, driven by concerns about AI disruption, competitive obsolescence, and the repricing of growth expectations. This narrative is not supported by the data. The sector composition of defaults remains heavily skewed toward capital-intensive industries and consumer-facing businesses.

This should not be surprising. Capital-intensive businesses—manufacturing, chemicals, metals, transportation—carry high fixed costs, significant debt loads, and exposure to commodity price cycles. Consumer-facing businesses—retail, restaurants, consumer services—face margin pressure from inflation, shifting spending patterns, and the ongoing restructuring of physical retail. These are the sectors where leverage is highest, cash flow generation is most volatile, and the cushion for debt service is thinnest.

The software narrative, while compelling as a long-term theme, has not yet translated into measurable default activity. Software companies tend to have lighter capital structures, recurring revenue models, and greater financing flexibility. They are not immune to distress, but they are less vulnerable to the kind of cash flow crises that trigger defaults in capital-intensive industries.

The implication for portfolio construction is clear. A default risk model that overweights technology and underweights industrials and consumer cyclicals will produce systematically biased results. Investors need to align their sector exposure with the actual distribution of default risk, not with the headlines. This is particularly important in the current environment, where sector dispersion is widening and the gap between narrative and reality is growing.

## The US and European Default Cycles Are Diverging, and the Divergence Will Persist

One of the most important conclusions from the current data is that the US and European default cycles are on different trajectories. The US market, particularly the BSL segment, has passed its peak and is in a gradual normalization phase. The European market, by contrast, is still showing signs of trending higher, and the path ahead suggests further increases through year-end.

This divergence is driven by macroeconomic fundamentals, not market structure. The US economy has demonstrated remarkable resilience, with growth holding up better than expected, inflation moderating, and the Federal Reserve moving toward a more accommodative stance. The European economy faces a more challenging mix: weaker growth, persistent inflation driven by energy and commodity disruptions, and a monetary policy framework that is less flexible in responding to regional divergences.

The result is that European companies are facing a more prolonged period of earnings pressure and higher financing costs. This is particularly acute for floating-rate borrowers in the European BSL market, where the combination of higher base rates and tighter lending standards is squeezing cash flow. The European default rate is likely to rise modestly from current levels, even as the US rate continues to decline.

For global credit investors, this divergence creates both risk and opportunity. A portfolio that treats US and European credit as interchangeable is missing a critical dimension of differentiation. The relative value trade between US and European leveraged loans, in particular, deserves careful attention. The US market offers a more favorable default outlook, while the European market may offer higher spreads to compensate for the additional risk. The question is whether those spreads are adequate, and the data suggests they may not be.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Liquidity Cascade and the Sponsor Exit Problem

The analysis presented in the report is comprehensive, but it leaves several important questions unresolved. The most significant of these concerns the liquidity implications of the shift toward distressed exchanges. When a company executes a distressed exchange, it typically extends maturities, reduces cash interest payments, and pushes the problem into the future. This is rational for the company and its sponsors, but it creates a liquidity overhang for the market as a whole.

The bonds and loans that are restructured through distressed exchanges do not disappear. They remain in the market, often with lower coupons, longer maturities, and weaker covenants. They become "zombie" instruments that trade at distressed levels, absorbing capital that could otherwise be deployed into higher-quality credits. The cumulative effect of years of distressed exchanges is a growing pool of impaired assets that constrains market liquidity and amplifies price dislocations during periods of stress.

The report also does not fully address the sponsor exit problem. Private equity sponsors have been holding portfolio companies for longer than historical averages, and the traditional exit routes—IPOs, strategic sales, dividend recaps—have become more difficult. Distressed exchanges allow sponsors to defer the exit decision, but they do not eliminate it. At some point, these companies need to be sold or refinanced, and the market will need to absorb a wave of sponsor-led transactions. The timing and magnitude of this wave remain uncertain, and it represents a significant tail risk for the leveraged finance market.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the New Default Regime

The structural changes in the default landscape require a corresponding change in how investors analyze and manage credit risk. The following framework is designed to help investors translate the insights from this report into actionable portfolio decisions.

First, shift from a frequency-based to a structure-based default analysis. Stop relying solely on trailing default rates as a measure of portfolio health. Instead, analyze the composition of defaults in your portfolio: what percentage are distressed exchanges versus in-court restructurings? How many of the companies that have completed distressed exchanges are likely to default again? This analysis will reveal the true tail risk that trailing rates obscure.

Second, adjust recovery rate assumptions downward for loan-only structures. If your recovery model uses historical averages that include mixed capital structures, you are overestimating recoveries. Build a separate recovery assumption for loan-only issuers, and stress-test it under a scenario of iterative defaults. The difference between a 60% recovery assumption and a 40% recovery assumption is the difference between a manageable loss and a portfolio impairment event.

Third, align sector exposure with actual default risk, not narrative. Reduce exposure to sectors where the narrative is benign but the fundamentals are deteriorating. Increase exposure to sectors where the narrative is negative but the default data is supportive. This requires a discipline that most investors lack, but it is the most reliable way to generate alpha in a market characterized by dispersion.

Fourth, build a US-Europe relative value framework that incorporates default trajectory divergence. Do not treat global credit as a single asset class. Maintain separate risk budgets for US and European exposure, and adjust them based on the evolving macroeconomic outlook. The US market offers a more favorable default environment; the European market offers higher spreads. The optimal portfolio will overweight the US and underweight Europe until the European default trajectory stabilizes.

Fifth, monitor the liquidity cascade. Track the volume of outstanding bonds and loans that have been restructured through distressed exchanges. If this volume grows faster than the market's capacity to absorb it, liquidity risk will increase, and price dislocations will become more frequent. This is a leading indicator of systemic stress that most investors ignore.

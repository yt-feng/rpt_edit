# The Default Cycle Has Peaked, But the Structure of Default Has Fundamentally Changed

The most important conclusion for leveraged credit investors today is not about the direction of default rates. It is about the nature of defaults themselves. A global investment bank's latest research on the state of play for leveraged finance defaults makes a compelling case that while aggregate default activity has likely passed its peak in the syndicated credit market, the underlying mechanics of how companies restructure their liabilities have shifted in ways that will determine recovery outcomes for years to come. The headline numbers may look reassuringly median, but the composition of those numbers tells a far more troubling story.

For anyone managing a corporate credit portfolio, the distinction between a default rate and a recovery rate is the difference between a speedometer and a fuel gauge. The default rate tells you how many cars are breaking down. The recovery rate tells you how much of the wreckage can be salvaged. Right now, the first metric is stabilizing while the second is deteriorating. That divergence is the single most important strategic puzzle for credit investors to solve.

The report identifies five themes that deserve careful attention: the direction of defaults, the structural shift toward distressed exchanges, the erosion of ultimate recovery values, the sector composition of distress, and the outlook ahead. But beneath each of these lies a deeper question about whether the credit market's institutional machinery for handling distress is still functioning as it should. The rise of the "repeat defaulter" phenomenon, in particular, suggests that many companies are not actually being fixed by the current restructuring process. They are being kept alive, only to default again.

What follows is not a summary of the report. It is an interpretation of what these patterns mean for investment strategy, risk management, and the broader evolution of the leveraged finance market. The goal is to help readers think through the implications before they make their next allocation decision.

## The Peak in Aggregate Defaults Masks a Widening Dispersion Between Asset Classes and Regions

The report's first major finding is that the absolute level of defaults has been trending higher from the very low levels of 2022 and 2023 but remains near historical median levels. This is not a crisis. It is a normalization. However, the normalization is not uniform. The US broadly syndicated loan market saw its default rate peak in late 2024 at 7.4% and has since declined to 5.1%. US high-yield bonds, by contrast, have been oscillating in a narrow band between 3% and 4% for the past two years. Europe tells a different story entirely, with both HY bonds and leveraged loans showing signs of trending higher, driven by a more challenging mix of growth, inflation, and monetary policy.

So what does this dispersion mean for portfolio construction? It means that a single global default forecast is no longer sufficient. Investors need to differentiate not just by geography but by instrument. The floating-rate loan market has already repriced the risk of higher funding costs, and the default data reflects that. The fixed-rate bond market has been more resilient, but that resilience may be fragile if the growth backdrop deteriorates. In Europe, the direction of travel is still upward, which suggests that European credit portfolios need a different risk budget than US portfolios.

The report's data on 12-month trailing issuer-weighted default rates makes this divergence visible. The US loan market is decelerating from its funding-cost-driven spike, while European markets are accelerating. This is not a random divergence. It reflects fundamental differences in the macroeconomic environment. The US economy has absorbed higher rates with more resilience, partly because of its deeper capital markets and more flexible labor markets. Europe is still grappling with the aftereffects of commodity market disruptions and a tighter monetary policy transmission mechanism.

For investors, the implication is clear: a one-size-fits-all approach to default risk is no longer viable. The dispersion between markets is not a temporary anomaly. It is a structural feature of the current cycle.

## Distressed Exchanges Have Become the Default Restructuring Mechanism, But They Create a Repeat Defaulter Problem

The most striking structural change documented in the report is the dominance of distressed exchanges and liability management exercises as the vehicle of choice for reorganizing corporate balance sheets. In recent years, these out-of-court restructurings have accounted for 70% to 80% of all defaults, depending on the market. This is not a marginal shift. It is a fundamental change in how the credit market handles distress.

At first glance, this trend appears benign. Distressed exchanges preserve recovery values better than in-court restructurings, at least initially. The report notes that in 2025, US HY senior unsecured recovery rates for distressed exchanges averaged around 60%, compared to roughly 10% for the handful of "hard" defaults. For private equity sponsors, the logic is obvious: an LME keeps the company out of court, avoids the stigma of a formal bankruptcy, and gives the sponsor more control over the outcome.

But the report's data on repeat defaults reveals the hidden cost of this approach. Using a three-year window to define a repeat default, the analysis shows that distressed exchanges lead to a repeat default roughly 50% of the time. In-court restructurings, by contrast, are rarely followed by another default. This is not a small difference. It is a systematic failure of the LME process to deliver lasting balance sheet repair.

The reason is intuitive. A distressed exchange typically involves extending maturities, reducing coupon payments, or swapping debt for equity. These measures improve liquidity in the short term but often do not address the underlying business problem. The company remains overleveraged, its competitive position may still be weak, and the next shock—whether it is a downturn in revenue, a spike in input costs, or a refinancing wall—can push it back into distress.

For credit investors, this creates a painful dynamic. The initial recovery from an LME may look acceptable, but the cumulative recovery across multiple defaults is far worse. Each successive restructuring erodes recovery outcomes. The report's findings on this point are unambiguous: iterative defaults systematically destroy value.

The strategic implication is that investors cannot simply look at the default rate and assume that recovery rates will follow historical patterns. The composition of defaults matters enormously. A market dominated by distressed exchanges will have a different recovery profile than one dominated by in-court restructurings. And because LMEs are more common now, the tail risk of low recoveries is higher than the historical averages suggest.

## Recovery Rates Are Trending Lower, and the Reasons Are Structural, Not Cyclical

The report documents a longer-term pattern of declining recovery rates in the US market. This is not a new observation, but the report provides a useful framework for understanding the drivers. Three factors stand out.

First, the rise of repeat defaults systematically erodes recovery values. Each time a company restructures, the creditors who take equity or new debt in the exchange end up holding assets that are worth less than what they gave up. When the company defaults again, the recovery on those new instruments is even lower. This compounding effect is a direct consequence of the LME-dominated restructuring environment.

Second, the proliferation of "loan-only" capital structures has reduced the subordination cushion that high-yield bonds used to provide. The report estimates that over 70% of loans are now issued by loan-only capital structures, up from just 45% in 2010. In a mixed capital structure, the presence of subordinated bonds provides a buffer for senior lenders. When that buffer is absent, the entire recovery burden falls on the loan holders. This structural change means that recovery rates for loans are likely to be lower in the future than they were in the past, all else being equal.

Third, the US economy has structurally transitioned from asset-heavy industries like manufacturing to asset-light industries like technology and services. Asset-heavy companies have physical collateral that can be liquidated in a restructuring. Asset-light companies have intangible assets that are harder to value and harder to sell. This shift in the composition of the corporate sector has a direct impact on aggregate recovery rates.

The report does not fully answer the question of how much each of these factors contributes to the decline in recoveries. That is a meaningful open question. But the direction of the trend is clear, and it is not favorable for credit investors. If recovery rates are structurally lower, then the risk-adjusted return of leveraged credit is also lower, even if default rates remain moderate.

## The Sector Composition of Distress Contradicts the Prevailing Narrative About Software Disruption

One of the more counterintuitive findings in the report is about sector patterns. Despite the widespread market narrative about potential disruption of software-focused firms—driven by artificial intelligence, changing business models, or competitive dynamics—the sector composition of defaults still skews heavily toward capital industries and consumer-facing businesses.

This is a useful corrective to the media narrative. It is easy to assume that the next wave of credit losses will come from the most hyped or most disrupted sectors. But the data suggests otherwise. The companies that are actually defaulting are the ones that have always defaulted: capital-intensive firms that are sensitive to the economic cycle, and consumer-facing businesses that are exposed to shifts in disposable income.

For investors, this has two implications. First, it means that sector selection still matters more than thematic narratives. The default data is not telling a story about technology disruption. It is telling a story about cyclical exposure and capital structure. Second, it means that the risk of a sudden spike in defaults from an unexpected sector is relatively low. The distribution of distress is broadly consistent with historical patterns.

However, the report leaves an open question: will this pattern persist? The current data reflects defaults that originated from business conditions and capital structures that were put in place several years ago. The companies that are vulnerable to AI disruption or other technological shifts may not yet be showing up in the default data because their debt was issued more recently and their maturities are further out. The absence of software firms in today's default statistics does not guarantee their absence tomorrow.

## The Growth Backdrop Is the Key Signpost, and It Points to Divergent Paths for the US and Europe

The report's forward-looking section is appropriately cautious. The authors state clearly that the growth backdrop is the key signpost to watch. Their analysis suggests that none of the metrics they monitor signal a near-term uptick in expected defaults in the US. In Europe, they still envision a modest increase in defaults through year-end, but they expect defaults to remain below the local peak.

This is a reasonable forecast, but it relies on a critical assumption: that the growth backdrop does not deteriorate sharply. If growth weakens, the entire picture changes. The report acknowledges this explicitly, stating that the peak in aggregate default activity is likely behind us "absent a sharp deterioration in the growth backdrop."

The vulnerability is not evenly distributed. The report notes that the loan market is more vulnerable than high-yield bonds. This makes sense. Loans are floating-rate instruments, so their borrowers are more exposed to the level of interest rates. If growth slows and the economy enters a recession, loan borrowers will face both lower revenue and continued high debt service costs. High-yield bond borrowers, by contrast, have fixed-rate debt and are less sensitive to near-term rate changes.

For portfolio construction, this suggests that the relative attractiveness of loans versus bonds depends on the growth outlook. In a soft-landing scenario, loans may offer attractive yields with manageable default risk. In a recession scenario, the floating-rate feature becomes a liability, and bonds may offer better protection.

## What the Report Leaves Unanswered: The Interaction Between LME Dominance and the Next Downturn

The report does an excellent job of documenting the current state of play. But it leaves several meaningful open questions that investors need to think through for themselves.

The most important question is how the LME-dominated restructuring environment will perform in a genuine recession. The current cycle has been characterized by a relatively strong economy. Defaults have been driven by idiosyncratic factors and the lagged effect of higher rates, not by a broad economic contraction. The LME process has worked reasonably well in this environment because companies have had time to negotiate and because the underlying business fundamentals have been stable.

But what happens when the economy turns down? In a recession, revenue declines are sharp and broad. Companies that are already weakened by high leverage and thin margins will face a much more difficult negotiation with their creditors. The LME process, which relies on voluntary agreements between borrowers and lenders, may break down. The result could be a surge in in-court restructurings, which would push recovery rates even lower.

A second open question is about the role of private equity sponsors. The report notes that LMEs are often the preferred path for sponsors. But if the repeat defaulter phenomenon becomes more widespread, will sponsors change their behavior? Or will they continue to use LMEs as a way to extend the life of underperforming portfolio companies? The answer has significant implications for the supply of distressed debt and the timing of recovery.

A third question is about the loan-only capital structure trend. If over 70% of loans are now issued by loan-only structures, and if recovery rates for these loans are structurally lower, then the risk premium on loans should be higher. Is the market pricing this correctly? The report does not address this directly, but it is a critical question for relative value analysis.

## A Decision Framework for Credit Investors

Based on the report's analysis, investors can construct a simple decision framework for navigating the current environment.

The first dimension is geography. US and European markets are on different trajectories. US loan defaults are declining, European defaults are rising. This suggests that a US-focused loan portfolio may have a more favorable near-term outlook than a European-focused one. However, the US market also has the more pronounced trend of declining recovery rates, so the risk-adjusted return may not be as attractive as the default rate alone suggests.

The second dimension is instrument. Loans are more vulnerable than bonds in a downturn, but they offer higher yields. The choice between loans and bonds should depend on the investor's view of the growth outlook. If the outlook is benign, loans may be the better pick. If the outlook is uncertain, bonds offer more protection.

The third dimension is structure. The dominance of LMEs means that recovery rates are likely to be lower than historical averages, especially for loans. Investors should adjust their recovery assumptions downward when pricing risk. They should also be wary of companies that have already completed one LME, as the probability of a repeat default is high.

The fourth dimension is sector. The current default data points to capital industries and consumer-facing businesses as the areas of highest risk. Investors should not be distracted by narratives about technology disruption. The real risk is in cyclical exposure and capital structure.

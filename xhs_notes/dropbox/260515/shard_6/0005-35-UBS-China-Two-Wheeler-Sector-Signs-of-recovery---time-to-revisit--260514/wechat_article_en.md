# China’s Two-Wheeler Sector Has Passed the Inflection Point — But the Recovery Is Not What It Seems

The Chinese two-wheeler industry has been through a brutal correction. After a 20% decline in first-quarter 2026 volumes, the market has stabilised. But this is not merely a cyclical bounce. Beneath the surface, a structural reordering is underway — one that rewards companies with pricing power, export diversification, and technological differentiation, while punishing those that rely on volume alone.

A series of recent management calls with leading two-wheeler original equipment manufacturers reveals a sector that has bottomed, but whose recovery is uneven, conditional, and full of strategic nuance. The headline numbers are encouraging: volumes flat quarter-on-quarter after the steep drop, price increases of 3-5% being successfully passed through, and export demand surging on the back of elevated global energy costs. Yet the real story is about which companies are positioned to compound these gains, and which are simply surviving.

The argument of this article is straightforward: the recovery in China’s two-wheeler sector is real, but it is a recovery that favours incumbents with premium positioning, battery innovation, and overseas exposure. The next 12 months will separate the leaders from the laggards, and the data points available today already point to a clear hierarchy.

## The Volume Stabilisation Masks a Deeper Shift in Demand Structure

The most obvious signal from the recent data is that electric two-wheeler volumes in China have stopped falling. After a 20% year-on-year decline in the first quarter, the second quarter has seen volumes flat. This is not yet a recovery in the traditional sense — retail demand remains tepid — but it is a critical floor. The industry appears to have absorbed the demand overhang created by the previous cycle, and wholesale volumes are now showing an upward trend.

What matters more than the aggregate number is the composition of demand. The report indicates that e-motorcycles have risen to account for the majority of sales at one leading OEM, while e-bikes are expected to take over from mid-year. This is not a trivial detail. E-motorcycles carry higher average selling prices and better margins than basic e-bikes. The product mix is shifting upward, and that is where the margin story begins.

Furthermore, the emergence of e-moped pilot programs in Cities like Wuxi signals a potential regulatory opening. If e-mopeds — which have fewer restrictions on design and higher speed limits — are officially permitted on roads in more Cities, the addressable market expands meaningfully. The report notes that one OEM believes e-mopeds represent the true demand for more than 80% of end-consumers. This is a second-order implication that could redefine the competitive landscape, but it remains contingent on policy decisions that are not yet guaranteed.

## Pricing Power Has Returned, But It Is Not Universally Available

One of the most striking findings from the management calls is that OEMs have successfully raised prices by 3-5% in April and May. This is happening despite the recent volume weakness, and it is being driven by cost inflation in raw materials such as plastics, copper, and rare earths. The fact that price increases are sticking suggests that demand is not as elastic as feared, and that consumers are willing to pay more for quality and brand.

However, this pricing power is not evenly distributed. The report reveals that one leading OEM raised prices by up to RMB100 per vehicle, but simultaneously flagged that its gross margin for the electric two-wheeler business would decline slightly for the full year compared to 2025. This is a critical nuance: price increases are offsetting cost inflation, but they are not necessarily expanding margins. The company believes margins bottomed in the first quarter, stabilised in the second, and will improve in the second half. But the full-year outlook is still a step down.

Another OEM, by contrast, expects gross margins to be flat year-on-year, with net profit per vehicle rising from RMB179 to RMB180-200. This suggests a more favourable cost structure or a more effective mix upgrade. The divergence between these two outlooks is telling. Pricing power is a necessary condition for margin protection, but it is not sufficient. Companies also need cost discipline and product mix improvement to convert price increases into profit growth.

## Export Markets Are Reshaping the Competitive Dynamics

High oil and gas prices have created a tailwind for two-wheeler exports that is stronger than most analysts anticipated. One OEM’s e-scooter sales in Europe and another’s electric two-wheeler sales in ASEAN have beaten internal estimates. Export guidance for 2026 stands at 500,000 units for one company, up from 310,000 in 2025, with upside risk if energy prices remain elevated.

This export surge is not just about volume. It is about margin. Export markets, particularly in Southeast Asia and Europe, offer higher average selling prices and less price competition than the domestic Chinese market. The report notes that one OEM is conducting a thorough assessment of the Vietnam market, where the transition from internal combustion engine two-wheelers to electric is accelerating rapidly. The company believes it can replicate its success in China’s electric two-wheeler market in Vietnam — a bold claim that, if realised, would open a significant new growth frontier.

But there are headwinds. Tariffs, foreign exchange volatility, and the appreciation of the renminbi are all cited as near-term risks. One OEM notes that all overseas transactions are settled in US dollars, making renminbi appreciation a direct headwind to reported revenue and margins. The export story is real, but it is not without friction.

## Battery Innovation Is Creating a New Competitive Moat

The most technologically significant development in the sector is the adoption of sodium batteries in two-wheelers. One OEM expects to ship 1 million units of sodium batteries in 2026, representing approximately 6-7% penetration of its total two-wheeler sales. These batteries are being deployed in premium e-motorcycle products, and the company believes its sodium battery leadership could be a decisive edge when lithium carbonate prices remain elevated.

The strategic logic is clear. Sodium batteries have a cost advantage over lithium batteries when lithium carbonate prices are above RMB150,000 per ton. Given the volatility in lithium prices, this creates a hedge for the manufacturer and a potential pricing advantage in the market. More importantly, it differentiates the product in a way that is difficult for smaller competitors to replicate. Battery technology is capital-intensive and requires years of R&D. It is a moat, not a feature.

The second-order implication is that the sodium battery push could accelerate the premiumisation of the entire sector. If sodium batteries enable longer range, safer operation, or lower cost at scale, they could pull the entire product mix upward. This would benefit the leaders with R&D budgets and supply chain integration, while pressuring commoditised players who compete primarily on price.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Policy Wild Card and the Margin Trajectory

For all the detail in the management calls, several critical questions remain unresolved. The first is the regulatory trajectory. The e-moped pilot program in Wuxi is encouraging, but it is a single city. Whether other Cities follow, and how quickly, is unknown. The report also references the 2024 new national standards, which could shift transport preference back to buses and subways from electric two-wheelers. This is a downside risk that has not yet materialised, but it remains a latent threat.

The second unresolved question is the margin trajectory for the second half of 2026. The report suggests that margins will improve from the second quarter onward, but the guidance is cautious. One OEM expects full-year electric two-wheeler margins to decline slightly. Another expects margins to be flat. The difference between these two outcomes is significant for investors, and the report does not provide enough data to adjudicate between them. The key variable appears to be product mix — specifically, how quickly higher-margin e-motorcycles and e-bikes replace lower-margin models.

The third open question is the sustainability of export demand. High oil prices are driving the current surge, but oil prices are notoriously cyclical. If energy prices moderate, the export tailwind could fade. The report does not model this scenario, leaving it as an open risk.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating Two-Wheeler Investments

Given the complexity of the sector, a simple framework can help investors navigate the landscape. The three dimensions that matter most are pricing power, technological differentiation, and export exposure.

First, pricing power. Companies that can raise prices without losing market share are the ones with strong brands and premium positioning. The ability to pass through cost inflation is the single most important indicator of competitive strength in the current environment. Look for companies that have raised prices and maintained or improved margins.

Second, technological differentiation. Sodium batteries, smart features, and integrated electric vehicle platforms are creating barriers to entry. Companies that invest in R&D and have a clear technology roadmap are likely to outperform those that compete on price alone. The report highlights one company that expects a significant leap in product competitiveness in 2027 through intelligent integration. This is a bet on technology, and it is worth taking seriously.

Third, export exposure. The domestic market is recovering, but it is not growing rapidly. The real growth is in ASEAN and Europe. Companies with established distribution networks, local manufacturing, and brand recognition in these markets have a structural advantage. The report identifies Vietnam as a particularly attractive market, given the rapid transition from internal combustion to electric.

Applying this framework, the companies that score highest on all three dimensions are the ones with Buy ratings in the report. But the framework also reveals the risks: companies with high export exposure are vulnerable to tariffs and currency fluctuations, and companies with heavy R&D spending may see margins compressed in the near term even if their long-term position improves.

## The Recovery Is Real, But It Requires Active Selection

The Chinese two-wheeler sector has turned a corner. Volumes have stabilised, prices are rising, and exports are booming. But this is not a tide that lifts all boats. The recovery is favouring companies with pricing power, technological moats, and international reach. The next 12 months will test whether these advantages are durable or temporary.

The most important insight from the management calls is that the leaders are already positioning for the next phase of competition. They are investing in sodium batteries, expanding into Vietnam, upgrading their product mix, and building after-sales service networks. The laggards are still fighting the last war — trying to defend market share in a commoditised domestic market.

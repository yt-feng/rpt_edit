# The Fertilizer Cycle Is Peaking, but the Real Strategic Question Is Which Nutrient Exposure Will Deliver in 2027

The global fertilizer market has entered a phase of deceptive stability. Headline prices remain elevated, supply disruptions from the Middle East persist, and the seasonal demand peak is behind us. Yet beneath the surface, the three major nutrient markets—nitrogen, phosphates, and potash—are moving in fundamentally different directions, creating a divergence that will define relative performance for producers and investors over the next 18 months.

This is not a uniform cycle. The conventional framing of a "fertilizer rally" obscures a more nuanced reality: nitrogen is peaking now, phosphates are caught in a structural margin squeeze that may not resolve quickly, and potash faces a demand-versus-supply debate that will determine whether current pricing is sustainable. The critical insight from the latest sector analysis is that the second quarter of 2026 likely represents the high-water mark for nitrogen earnings, while phosphate producers are being punished by input cost inflation that has outpaced their output price increases. For potash, the story is more ambiguous—pricing has moved up, but much of that increase reflects freight costs rather than producer margin expansion.

The strategic question for anyone following this sector is not whether fertilizer prices will fall. It is which companies and which nutrient exposures are positioned to withstand the inevitable normalization, and which are carrying hidden risks that will emerge as the cycle turns. The answer depends on understanding three distinct market dynamics that are unfolding simultaneously, each with its own timeline, its own cost structure, and its own vulnerability to disruption.

## Nitrogen Pricing Has Peaked, and the Earnings Trajectory Is Now Declining from 2Q 2026

The clearest signal in the current data is that nitrogen markets have passed their cyclical apex. Ammonia, urea, and UAN benchmarks have all risen sharply since the onset of Middle East disruptions—by 25 to 60 percent depending on the product—but recent weeks have shown the first meaningful signs of softening. The Tampa ammonia contract settled below expectations. Urea spot prices in NOLA have fallen to their lowest level since early March. The market is beginning to price in a shorter duration for trade disruptions rather than a prolonged supply crisis.

This matters because the earnings trajectory for nitrogen-exposed producers is now starting to slope downward. The second quarter of 2026 will likely be recorded as the peak earnings period for this cycle, and from here, the outlook is for at least modest seasonal declines into the third quarter. The combination of post-seasonal demand softness, farmer affordability pressures, and the gradual restoration of supply from Iran and Qatar will outweigh the remaining supply constraints. Even if the Strait of Hormuz remains disrupted, the market has already absorbed much of the shock, and the marginal impact of additional disruptions is diminishing.

What makes this particularly challenging for investors is that the stock market has already priced in peak earnings for some names. For one major nitrogen producer, current estimates for 2026 and 2027 EBITDA are already 17 and 21 percent above consensus, respectively, and the stock is trading near levels that imply this outperformance is fully reflected. The upside from current levels is limited to roughly 30 percent under the most optimistic scenario, while the downside in a normalization scenario could be more severe. The asymmetry is unfavorable: the market is already paying for perfection, and perfection is precisely what the second half of the year is unlikely to deliver.

The wildcard here is natural gas. U.S. and Canadian gas prices have remained remarkably stable through the Middle East conflict, and the cost curve premium for nitrogen has widened significantly as a result. Ammonia pricing has risen roughly $100 per metric ton more than what the move in gas prices would normally justify. This premium is a direct function of supply constraints, and it will persist as long as those constraints remain. But the direction of travel is clear: as supply normalizes, the premium will compress, and nitrogen margins will revert toward historical levels. The question is not whether this happens, but how quickly.

## Phosphate Margins Are Being Compressed by Input Costs, Creating a Structural Squeeze That Pricing Alone Cannot Fix

If nitrogen is a story about peaking earnings, phosphates are a story about a structural margin crisis that no amount of output price increases can fully resolve. The fundamental problem is simple: the input costs for phosphate production—particularly sulfur and ammonia—have risen far more than phosphate prices themselves. Stripping margins, which measure the profitability of converting raw materials into finished phosphate products, are experiencing severe compression across global markets.

The data is stark. Sulfur pricing has reached between $900 and $1,150 per metric ton in key markets like India, China, and Brazil. This has compressed stripping margins by as much as $120 per metric ton in offshore regions. Producers are responding with supply curtailments: OCP has implemented production cuts of up to 30 percent in the second quarter, and one major North American producer has reduced operating rates at two facilities to 50 percent, representing roughly 1.7 million metric tons of capacity. These cuts are a rational response to negative margins, but they also create a paradox.

Theoretically, supply cuts should push phosphate prices higher, restoring margins. But the market may not be able to absorb those higher prices. Demand destruction is already evident in the United States and Brazil, where farmers are reducing phosphate applications due to affordability concerns. The price elasticity of demand is working against the producers: every dollar of price increase risks further demand erosion, which in turn limits the ability to pass through higher costs. This is a classic commodity squeeze where the cure for low prices is low prices—except that in this case, the input cost escalation is so extreme that the normal adjustment mechanism may not function effectively.

The implications for earnings are severe. For one major phosphate-exposed producer, combined phosphate and fertilizer EBITDA is expected to decline by more than 50 percent in 2026, dragging total EBITDA to roughly $1.6 billion—well below the mid-cycle estimate of $1.85 billion. Free cash flow generation is being depressed by both lower earnings and higher working capital requirements, and the stock is trading at levels that imply only a 0.2 to 2.0 percent free cash flow yield over the next two years. This is not a cyclical trough; it is a structural earnings reset driven by input cost dynamics that may not reverse quickly.

The bull case for phosphates depends on two simultaneous developments: higher phosphate prices driven by supply shortages, and a normalization of raw material costs. Both would need to occur together for a meaningful recovery. That is a narrow path, and the current evidence suggests it is not the most likely outcome.

## Potash Pricing Has Risen, but the Benefits Are Partially Illusory and the Medium-Term Supply Outlook Remains a Risk

Potash has been the most resilient of the three major nutrients during the current disruption. Pricing has moved up on the combination of peak Northern Hemisphere demand, increased Brazilian buying, and higher freight costs. Unlike nitrogen, potash has not yet seen a meaningful pullback, and the market expects only modest seasonal declines as the second quarter progresses. Demand destruction risks are also more limited—estimated at 5 to 15 percent—compared with the severe affordability pressures in phosphates.

But there is a critical nuance that is often overlooked. A significant portion of the potash price increase is attributable to higher freight costs rather than to improved producer margins. The headline price increases look supportive for earnings, but the actual benefit flowing through to producers is less than the gross number suggests. When freight costs account for a large share of the price movement, the netback to the producer is correspondingly smaller.

The medium-term outlook for potash is where the real debate lies. On one side, bulls argue that improved crop prices and farmer profitability will flow through to stronger potash demand into 2027. Corn and soybean futures have already moved higher since the Middle East conflict began, adding roughly $45 and $20 per acre, respectively, to potential revenue. If this trend continues as lower production expectations become apparent, farmer economics could support sustained potash pricing.

On the other side, the supply outlook is less favorable. Incremental potash supply is expected to exceed demand growth through the latter part of the decade, putting downward pressure on pricing. For one major potash producer, medium-term EBITDA estimates are already 3 to 8 percent below consensus, reflecting this bearish view. The key risk to this thesis is that potash pricing remains flat at 2025 levels, which would add $300 to $450 million to mid-cycle EBITDA estimates and support a stock price 10 to 15 percent above current levels.

This is a genuinely contested outlook. The near-term data supports the bulls: pricing is stable, demand is holding up, and supply from the Middle East has been largely uninterrupted. But the structural supply overhang is real, and it will become more apparent as we move through 2027 and into 2028. The potash market is pricing in a continuation of current conditions, but the medium-term fundamentals argue for moderation.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Crop Prices, Fertilizer Demand, and Farmer Behavior

The most important unresolved question in this analysis is how farmer behavior will evolve as crop prices and fertilizer costs interact. The report notes that corn and soybean futures have risen since the conflict began, and that this should improve farmer profitability. But it also acknowledges that fertilizer prices have risen even more sharply in some cases, and that demand destruction is already occurring, particularly in phosphates.

The missing piece is a rigorous framework for understanding the elasticity of fertilizer demand at current price levels. Farmers are not passive price takers; they make active decisions about application rates based on expected margins. When fertilizer costs rise faster than crop prices, the rational response is to reduce application rates, shift to lower-cost alternatives, or delay purchases. The report provides some evidence of this behavior—buyers shifting toward ammonium sulfate as a lower-cost nitrogen alternative, for example—but it does not fully quantify the magnitude of demand destruction that is already embedded in the current price deck.

This matters because the supply-side constraints that are driving current pricing may be self-correcting. If demand destruction accelerates, the market will rebalance at lower prices, and the premium to the cost curve that producers are currently enjoying will compress. The question is whether the supply constraints are severe enough to offset the demand response. The report suggests that phosphate margins are already so compressed that further supply cuts are likely, which would push prices higher. But it also acknowledges that the market may not be able to bear those higher prices. This tension is the central analytical challenge, and it is not fully resolved.

A second unresolved question is the duration of the Middle East disruption. The report models a range of outcomes, from a near-term reopening to a prolonged disruption that persists into 2027. The pricing implications are dramatically different under each scenario. But the report does not assign probabilities to these outcomes, nor does it provide a framework for updating those probabilities as new information emerges. This is a significant gap for anyone trying to position for the next 12 to 18 months.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Fertilizer Cycle

For readers trying to translate this analysis into actionable positioning, three questions should guide the decision-making process.

First, what is your view on the duration of the Middle East disruption? If you believe trade flows will normalize within the next three to six months, then nitrogen-exposed producers are likely to underperform as the premium to the cost curve compresses, while phosphate producers could benefit from a normalization of raw material costs. If you believe the disruption will persist into 2027, the opposite trade applies: nitrogen producers will continue to generate elevated earnings, while phosphate producers will remain trapped by input cost inflation.

Second, how do you assess the relative risk of demand destruction versus supply constraints? In phosphates, the evidence suggests that demand destruction is already significant and may cap the ability of producers to pass through higher costs. In potash, demand destruction is more limited, but the supply overhang is more concerning. The trade-off between these two risks should determine your relative exposure to each nutrient.

Third, what is your time horizon? The next two quarters are likely to be characterized by declining nitrogen earnings, persistent phosphate margin pressure, and relatively stable potash pricing. But by 2027, the picture could look very different. Crop prices are expected to continue rising as lower production becomes apparent, which could improve farmer economics and support fertilizer demand. The medium-term outlook for potash is the most contested, and this is where the opportunity for differentiated analysis lies.

The most interesting positioning is in the divergence between nitrogen and potash. If you believe the current disruption is short-lived, then the relative value is in phosphate-exposed names that are trading at distressed levels. If you believe the disruption is structural, then nitrogen producers with low-cost natural gas inputs remain attractive. The potash story is the hardest to call, because it depends on a supply-demand balance that will only become clear over the next 12 to 18 months.

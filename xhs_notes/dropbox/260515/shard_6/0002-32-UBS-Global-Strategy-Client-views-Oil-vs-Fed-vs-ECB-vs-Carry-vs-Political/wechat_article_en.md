# The Market Is Priced for Central Bank Dovishness, But the Supply Shock Has Not Yet Delivered Its Verdict

The dominant narrative across macro trading desks in May 2026 is a cautious return to risk-taking, but this confidence rests on a fragile foundation. After meetings with hedge funds, real money managers, and bank treasury clients, a clear picture emerges: appetite for large directional positions remains limited, yet the willingness to trade has improved since April’s geopolitical shock. The market is not complacent, but it is selectively optimistic. The problem is that this optimism is built on an assumption that central banks will eventually cut rates as growth slows, while ignoring the possibility that the ongoing supply shock—driven by energy disruptions and geopolitical fragmentation—may force them to keep rates higher for longer.

The core tension is between two incompatible worlds. In one world, disinflation resumes, growth falters, and central banks deliver the easing that markets have begun to price. In the other world, the supply shock proves persistent, inflation stays sticky above targets, and central banks are forced to maintain restrictive stances even as economies weaken. The May client feedback suggests that many investors are leaning toward the first world, but the data and structural dynamics point to a much higher probability of the second. This is not a time for blanket directional bets. It is a time for nuance, for relative value, and for understanding which markets are pricing which risks.

The most important insight from these client conversations is that the market is not yet fully pricing the second-order effects of a sustained energy disruption. Global oil inventories are declining, supply chains remain fragile, and the geopolitical risk premium has not been extinguished. Yet the realized range on oil prices has been less elevated than many feared, which has allowed some investors to take on risk again. This is a dangerous comfort. A range-bound oil price is not the same as a resolved oil crisis. The market may be mistaking a temporary lull for a permanent resolution.

## The US Rates Market Is Correctly Hawkish on the Front End, But the Curve Trade Is Still Unresolved

Short-duration positions in US rates have performed well, and the recent CPI data has validated the hawkish positioning that many clients have adopted. The April CPI print was relatively hot, reinforcing the view that inflationary pressures are not dissipating quickly. Clients are now split on what comes next. A significant group believes US rates will go higher from here, but there is far less conviction on the shape of the curve. This is the critical question: will the curve steepen or flatten from current levels?

The answer depends on whether the market believes the Federal Reserve will eventually need to cut rates to support growth, or whether it will be forced to keep rates high to contain inflation. If the supply shock is transitory, then the front end should eventually decline, and the curve should steepen as long-term inflation expectations remain anchored. But if the supply shock is persistent, then the front end may stay elevated, and the curve could flatten further as the market prices a prolonged period of restrictive policy.

Several real money and treasury clients see value in going long US 10-year at 4.50%, which is consistent with a baseline expectation that yields will not rise much further. But this view assumes that the energy disruption remains contained. If oil prices spike again or if supply chains face further disruptions, the 10-year yield could easily reach 4.75% or higher. The range of outcomes is wide, and the market is not yet pricing the tail risks. The most prudent approach is to avoid large directional bets on US rates and instead focus on curve trades that express a view on the relative pricing of short-term versus long-term risks.

## The ECB Is the Most Interesting Divergence Trade in Global Rates

The European Central Bank is at a fascinating inflection point. The central expectation among clients remains a total of 50 to 75 basis points of hikes in 2026, but sentiment has turned markedly more dovish in recent weeks. Investors are increasingly questioning whether more than one hike will be delivered, given subdued wage pressures and emerging growth concerns visible in the May PMI data. This shift in sentiment has created an opportunity for relative value trades that exploit the gap between market pricing and central bank communication.

Clients have shown strong interest in structures that position for backloaded ECB cuts, which suggests that many believe the hiking cycle will be short and shallow. The market is pricing about 20 basis points of hikes for the June meeting, but this pricing has already been challenged by comments from Austrian central bank governor Kocher, who said that a June hike cannot be considered the baseline. If the ECB delivers a hike in June but signals that further tightening is unlikely, the market could quickly reprice toward cuts. This is a classic scenario for receiving front-end rates, particularly in the July contract.

The key insight here is that the ECB is more vulnerable to a dovish repricing than the Fed, because the European economy is more exposed to the energy shock and has less fiscal capacity to absorb it. The European Commission is reviewing the escape clause for fiscal rules, but there is no appetite for the kind of large-scale fiscal support that was granted during the pandemic. This means that monetary policy will have to do more of the heavy lifting if growth deteriorates. The market is beginning to price this, but there is still room for further dovish repricing, especially if the data continues to soften.

## Relative Value in European Government Bonds Is a Test of the Carry Trade’s Staying Power

The debate over Italian versus French government bonds is a microcosm of the broader tension in European fixed income. Some clients argue that Italy is more vulnerable than France, and they have made a case to go short 10-year Italy versus France. The spread between Italian and French 10-year yields has risen from near zero in late 2025 to about 22 basis points in March, before settling around 11 basis points. The question is whether this spread will widen again or continue to compress.

The case for wider spreads is straightforward: Italy has higher debt levels, lower growth, and more exposure to energy prices. If the ECB is forced to keep rates higher for longer, Italy will suffer more than France. But the near-term dynamics are more complicated. A moderate search for carry has resumed in European government bonds, and this is compressing spreads across the board. Investors are reaching for yield in a low-return world, and this flow is supporting peripheral bonds.

The strategic question is whether this carry trade is sustainable. If the energy shock intensifies or if growth deteriorates sharply, the flight to quality could reverse the spread compression. But if the economy stabilizes and the ECB delivers a dovish outcome, the carry trade could continue to perform. The safest approach is to remain neutral on Italy, France, and Spain against Germany, but to avoid leaning against further tightening in spreads. The market is telling us that the carry trade has momentum, and fighting it without a clear catalyst is a losing proposition.

## The UK Gilt Market Reveals a Structural Vulnerability That Markets Have Not Yet Priced

The UK gilt market is experiencing a fascinating divergence between demand and performance. Demand for gilts has started to emerge at higher yields, but long positions taken ahead of local elections have performed poorly. The deeper issue, however, is structural. Bank of England MPC member Mann has warned about the implications of a change in the investor base for gilts, away from domestic pension funds toward highly price-sensitive foreign funds. This dynamic is now a concern for investors, and it has not yet been fully priced into the market.

The risk is that foreign investors are more likely to sell gilts in a stress scenario, amplifying any sell-off. This is not a hypothetical concern. The UK has a large current account deficit and relies on foreign capital to finance its fiscal deficit. If foreign investors become less willing to hold gilts, the government will have to offer higher yields to attract buyers. This could create a vicious cycle: higher yields increase the cost of debt service, which increases the fiscal deficit, which requires more issuance, which pushes yields even higher.

The market is not pricing this risk adequately. The 5s10s steepener that has been opened at 45 basis points with a target of 65 basis points is a reasonable trade, but it does not capture the full extent of the structural vulnerability. The real question is whether the UK will face a gilt crisis similar to the one that occurred in 2022. The answer depends on whether the government can convince foreign investors that the fiscal trajectory is sustainable. This is an open question, and it is one that the market should be paying more attention to.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Energy Shocks and Central Bank Credibility

The report provides a detailed snapshot of client positioning and central bank expectations, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. The most critical gap is the interaction between energy shocks and central bank credibility. If oil prices spike again, will central banks look through the temporary inflation, or will they feel compelled to tighten further to protect their credibility? The answer depends on whether the shock is seen as transitory or persistent, and that judgment is inherently uncertain.

A second unresolved question is the fiscal-monetary interaction. In the euro area, the European Commission is reviewing the escape clause for fiscal rules, but there is no clarity on what kind of fiscal support will be allowed. If growth deteriorates, will governments be able to provide stimulus, or will they be constrained by fiscal rules? The answer will have a significant impact on the outlook for growth and inflation, and it will determine whether the ECB can afford to be dovish.

A third question is the sustainability of the carry trade in peripheral European bonds. The search for yield has compressed spreads, but this flow is vulnerable to a sudden reversal if risk appetite deteriorates. The report notes that a moderate search for carry has resumed, but it does not provide a framework for assessing when this flow might reverse. This is a critical gap, because the carry trade is one of the most crowded trades in the market, and crowded trades tend to end badly.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Current Environment

For investors trying to navigate this complex environment, the key is to focus on relative value and to avoid large directional bets. The following framework can help structure the decision-making process.

First, assess the energy shock. The most important variable is the trajectory of oil prices and supply chains. If the energy disruption remains contained, then the dovish repricing in central bank expectations is justified. But if the disruption intensifies, then the market will have to reprice toward higher rates. The best way to express this view is through relative value trades that are sensitive to the energy shock, such as receiving ECB rates versus paying Fed rates.

Second, evaluate central bank credibility. The ECB is more vulnerable to a dovish repricing than the Fed, because the European economy is more exposed to the energy shock. The trade to receive July ECB rates is a good expression of this view. The Fed, by contrast, is more likely to maintain a hawkish stance, which means that short-duration positions in US rates remain attractive.

Third, monitor the fiscal backdrop. The UK is the most vulnerable to a fiscal crisis, and the structural change in the investor base for gilts is a risk that the market has not fully priced. The 5s10s steepener in gilts is a reasonable trade, but it should be sized carefully given the tail risks.

Fourth, avoid fighting the carry trade in peripheral European bonds, but be prepared for a reversal. The search for yield is a powerful force, but it is also fragile. If risk appetite deteriorates, the carry trade will unwind quickly. The best approach is to remain neutral on peripheral spreads and to wait for a clearer catalyst before taking a directional view.

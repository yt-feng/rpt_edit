# Japanese Banks Are Entering a Structural Growth Phase That the Market Has Not Yet Priced In

The conventional wisdom on Japanese banks has been that they are a play on Bank of Japan rate hikes and little else. That framing is increasingly misleading. Over the past month, bank stocks underperformed the TOPIX by 1.7 percentage points, seesawing on speculation about the timing and pace of BOJ monetary policy normalization. Yet beneath this surface-level volatility, a more consequential story is unfolding. The latest round of 26/3 financial results reveals that Japanese banks are not merely waiting for higher interest rates to lift their earnings. They are benefiting from a genuine, multi-year expansion in domestic lending that is structural in nature, driven by capital expenditure, digital transformation, green investment, supply chain reconfiguration, and corporate value enhancement activities. The market's fixation on the next BOJ decision is causing it to overlook a fundamental shift in the operating environment. The question for investors is not whether the BOJ will hike in April or July. The question is whether the current valuation adequately reflects the underlying earnings power of Japanese banks in a world where loan growth, not just net interest margin expansion, has become a durable driver of returns.

## The Market Is Mistaking Near-Term Policy Uncertainty for a Structural Problem

Over the past month, Japanese bank stocks have been whipsawed by shifting expectations around BOJ monetary policy. In mid-April, comments from the Minister of Finance suggesting a wait-and-see stance, combined with a Nikkei report that the BOJ was increasingly likely to leave its policy rate unchanged at 0.75%, caused bank stocks to fall sharply. Then, in early May, reports that three policy board members had opposed leaving rates unchanged, coupled with hawkish language in the Outlook Report, triggered a recovery. The net result was underperformance of 1.7 percentage points relative to the TOPIX.

This pattern is analytically dangerous because it encourages investors to treat Japanese banks as a binary option on the next rate decision. The reality is more nuanced. JGB yields have been on an essentially constant upward trajectory over the same period. The volatility in bank stocks has been driven not by changes in the actual interest rate environment, but by changes in expectations about the timing of future rate moves. The market is pricing in a sequence of policy decisions that may or may not materialize, while the underlying trend in yields is already supportive. The risk is that investors who wait for certainty on monetary policy will miss the window in which the structural improvement in bank fundamentals is being reflected in earnings, not just in sentiment.

## The 26/3 Results Show That Loan Growth Has Become a Durable Earnings Driver, Not a Cyclical Blip

The first impression from the 26/3 results at major and regional banks is that they have been slightly stronger than expected. This is not a one-off beat. The pattern across multiple banks suggests a genuine improvement in the domestic lending environment that goes beyond the familiar story of net interest margin expansion.

Loan-deposit operations have been performing better than expected, supported not only by an improvement in loan-deposit spreads but also by strong growth in loans outstanding, particularly in the corporate sector. This is the critical point. In previous cycles, Japanese bank earnings were heavily dependent on the direction of interest rates because loan volumes were stagnant. That assumption is no longer valid. The structural drivers of loan demand are multiple and reinforcing: solid capital expenditure at large companies backed by favorable economic sentiment, investment in digital transformation and green transformation, supply chain shifts driven by geopolitical factors, borrowings for corporate value improvement through LBOs, MBOs, and M&A, and investment in measures to cope with labor shortages.

Many bank executives expect these structural funding needs to remain in place for the foreseeable future. That is a significant departure from the past, when loan demand was viewed as a function of the economic cycle. The implication is that even if the pace of BOJ rate hikes slows, bank earnings can still grow through volume expansion. The market has not yet incorporated this into its valuation framework, which remains heavily weighted toward interest rate sensitivity.

## A Widening Dispersion in Securities Portfolio Management Skills Will Create Clear Winners and Losers

One of the less discussed findings from the 26/3 results is the widening disparity among banks in terms of securities portfolio management skills. This is a second-order implication that deserves close attention. In a rising interest rate environment, the ability to manage the duration and composition of securities holdings becomes a material differentiator of overall profitability and balance sheet health.

Banks that have actively managed their securities portfolios to minimize unrealized losses and optimize yield will emerge with stronger capital positions and more flexibility to return capital to shareholders. Banks that have been passive or reactive will face headwinds. This dispersion is not yet fully reflected in relative valuations. The market is still largely pricing Japanese banks as a homogeneous sector, but the earnings results suggest that the gap between the best and worst performers is widening.

For investors, this means that a simple beta approach to Japanese bank stocks is no longer sufficient. Stock selection based on securities portfolio management quality, balance sheet resilience, and loan growth trajectory will be far more important than it has been in the past. The banks that demonstrate superior execution in these areas will likely command a valuation premium that the market has not yet granted.

## Guidance for 27/3 Is More Bullish Than in the Past, But the Market Is Skeptical for the Wrong Reasons

The guidance provided by banks for the 27/3 fiscal year is more bullish than in the past, both in terms of profit growth expectations and divergence from market consensus forecasts. This is noteworthy because Japanese bank management teams have historically been conservative in their guidance. The fact that they are now projecting higher earnings suggests a genuine confidence in the underlying business momentum.

The market's skepticism toward this guidance appears to be rooted in the assumption that it is dependent on continued BOJ rate hikes. That assumption may be incorrect. The guidance is being supported by the same structural factors that drove the 26/3 results: strong loan growth, improving loan-deposit spreads, and a more favorable operating environment. Even if the BOJ pauses or slows the pace of rate increases, the volume-driven revenue growth should persist.

However, there is a nuance that the report does not fully address. The guidance assumes that the current geopolitical tensions in the Middle East do not escalate further. The report notes that cyclical factors have recently compounded the structural loan demand, with companies building up liquidity and increasing usage of commitment lines in response to the situation in the Middle East. If these tensions sUBSide, some of the working capital borrowings may unwind, creating a temporary drag on loan growth. The question is whether the structural drivers are strong enough to offset that cyclical reversal. The report suggests they are, but the evidence is still incomplete.

## The Unresolved Question: How Much of the Structural Loan Demand Is Rate-Sensitive, and How Much Is Genuinely Independent?

The most important analytical question that the report leaves open is the degree to which the structural loan demand is independent of the interest rate environment. The report attributes the strong loan growth to capex, DX, GX, supply chain shifts, corporate value improvement, and labor shortage measures. All of these are presented as structural factors that will persist regardless of the level of interest rates.

But this argument has not been fully stress-tested. If the BOJ were to raise rates more aggressively than currently expected, would some of this loan demand be deferred or cancelled? Conversely, if the BOJ were to hold rates steady for an extended period, would the loan growth momentum be sustained? The report provides anecdotal evidence from bank executives who expect the structural funding needs to remain in place, but it does not provide a quantitative framework for assessing the sensitivity of loan demand to interest rates.

This is a critical gap for investors. The market is currently pricing Japanese banks based on the assumption that earnings are highly sensitive to BOJ policy. If the structural loan demand is indeed independent of interest rates, then the market is undervaluing the sector's earnings power at current rate levels. If, on the other hand, the loan demand is partially rate-sensitive, then the market's caution may be justified. Resolving this question requires a deeper analysis of the composition of loan growth and the elasticity of each component to changes in funding costs.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Whether the Structural Thesis Is Real

For investors trying to assess whether the structural improvement in Japanese bank earnings is genuine, the following decision framework may be useful. It is based on three questions that go beyond the standard macro analysis of BOJ policy.

First, is the loan growth concentrated in cyclical or structural sectors? If the growth is primarily in working capital borrowings linked to geopolitical uncertainty, it will reverse when tensions ease. If it is in capex, DX, GX, and M&A-related lending, it is more likely to persist. The report suggests the latter is dominant, but investors should verify this by examining the loan book composition of individual banks.

Second, are the banks that are guiding for higher earnings also the ones with strong securities portfolio management? If guidance is coming from banks with weak securities portfolios, it may be overly optimistic. If it is coming from banks with strong balance sheets and active portfolio management, it is more credible. The report notes a widening dispersion in portfolio management skills, which implies that not all guidance should be treated equally.

Third, what is the sensitivity of bank earnings to a scenario in which the BOJ does not hike rates at all in the next 12 months? If the structural loan growth thesis is correct, earnings should still grow even in a no-hike scenario, albeit at a slower pace. If earnings would decline in that scenario, then the current guidance is effectively a bet on monetary policy tightening, not on structural improvement.

These three questions provide a framework for distinguishing between banks that are genuinely benefiting from structural change and those that are simply riding the rate hike narrative. The report provides strong evidence that the former group exists, but it does not fully identify which banks fall into which category. That is the work that individual investors must do.

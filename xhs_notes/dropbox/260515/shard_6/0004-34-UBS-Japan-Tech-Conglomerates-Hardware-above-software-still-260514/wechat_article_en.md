# The AI Infrastructure Build-Out Is Far From Over, But the Winners Are No Longer Obvious

The most critical insight from the latest research on Japan's technology conglomerates and semiconductor supply chain is not about which company has the best AI product. It is that the structure of AI demand itself remains fundamentally unchanged from the past two years, yet the market is beginning to price in a narrative shift that has not yet materialized. The expansion of coding AI has driven a sharp increase in demand for tokens and inference compute, and the fundamentals of AI data center-related businesses remain robust. However, the performance of coding AI is advancing across multiple fronts simultaneously, from Anthropic and OpenAI to China's Kimi, creating a landscape where hardware demand continues to grow while software businesses face persistent existential questions.

The conventional wisdom that AI winners will be found exclusively in software or exclusively in hardware is a false dichotomy. The real strategic question is which companies sit at the intersection of durable hardware demand and underappreciated software optionality. This is where the most compelling investment cases emerge, and it is precisely where the current market consensus is most vulnerable to being wrong.

The report makes a clear argument: growth in AI-related hardware demand is likely to continue across semiconductors, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, electric power, and high-performance packaging. Yet the market's enthusiasm for certain names has already pushed valuations to levels that leave little room for error. Meanwhile, other companies are being penalized for risks that may already be priced in or may never materialize. The divergence between price and fundamental trajectory creates the kind of opportunity that disciplined investors live for.

What follows is a strategic dissection of the report's core arguments, extended into second-order implications that should inform how serious allocators think about Japan's technology landscape over the next twelve to eighteen months.

## The Hardware Cycle Is Still Accelerating, But Valuation Discipline Will Separate Winners from Losers

The most actionable insight from the report is that the AI hardware demand cycle is not peaking; it is still in an expansion phase that has room to run. The report identifies semiconductors, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, electric power, and high-performance packaging as the four sub-sectors where growth is likely to persist. This is not a speculative call. It is grounded in observable demand: the expansion of coding AI has created an insatiable need for tokens and inference compute, and this demand is only growing as more products come to market.

What makes this phase different from the initial AI capex wave is that the market has become more discriminating. Not all hardware companies are created equal, and the report's recommendations reflect this nuance. Tokyo Electron is highlighted as a standout, not merely because it benefits from AI demand, but because it did something unexpected during this results season: it raised prices. A price hike in semiconductor equipment is a signal of pricing power, and pricing power in a capital-intensive industry is the single most reliable indicator of sustainable competitive advantage. The report expects Tokyo Electron's market share and profit margins to expand going forward, and this is a call that deserves serious attention.

Renesas presents a different but equally compelling case. The company appears likely to post a sixth consecutive quarter of sequential sales growth, which would reconfirm an upcycle. More importantly, overseas competitors have implemented further price hikes following those in March and April, and the report expects Renesas to follow with profit margin improvement. This is a cyclical upswing that is being reinforced by structural factors, including AI demand and supply constraints among competitors.

The counterpoint to these positive cases is found in Advantest and Disco. Both companies are seeing solid trends and benefit from AI tailwinds, but the report explicitly states that their multiples are excessively high, leaving little appeal in their share prices. This is a critical distinction. A good business is not necessarily a good investment at any price. The market has already priced in the AI narrative for these names, and the report is effectively saying that the risk-reward no longer favors the buyer.

The implication for investors is clear: the hardware cycle is real, but the entry point matters enormously. The companies that are executing well and have room for multiple expansion are Tokyo Electron and Renesas. The companies that are riding the same wave but are already priced for perfection are Advantest and Disco. The strategic decision is not whether to be in hardware; it is which hardware names to own.

## SoftBank and Sony Offer Asymmetric Bets on AI Optionality That the Market Is Misunderstanding

Among the technology conglomerates, the report focuses on SoftBank and Sony, and the logic behind both recommendations reveals a pattern that is worth understanding in depth.

For SoftBank, the driver is ARM. The report argues that SoftBank's share price growth is likely to be driven by ARM's ability to capture AI inference demand, combined with the growing business value of OpenAI due to accelerated monetization from stronger coding AI. This is a compound thesis: ARM benefits from the proliferation of AI inference workloads, and OpenAI benefits from the improvement in coding AI capabilities that is driving demand for tokens and compute. SoftBank sits at the intersection of both trends through its ownership stakes.

The market may be underestimating this dynamic for several reasons. First, SoftBank's historical volatility and its reputation as a "Vision Fund story" create a perception of risk that may not fully account for the structural transformation of its core holdings. Second, the ARM narrative is often framed narrowly around smartphone licensing, when the real opportunity lies in AI inference at the edge and in data centers. If ARM captures even a modest share of the inference market, the upside for SoftBank could be sUBStantial.

Sony presents a different kind of asymmetric opportunity. The report notes that Sony has managed to offset the negative impact of DRAM price hikes through its own price increases, which was the most concerning issue before its full-year results. Furthermore, the share price is currently at a low level that, in the report's view, excessively reflects concerns about content value erosion due to AI.

This is a fascinating argument. The prevailing narrative is that AI will commoditize content creation, reducing the value of Sony's entertainment and gaming assets. But the report is suggesting that this concern is overdone. Sony's ability to pass through cost increases suggests pricing power, and its content library may be more resilient to AI disruption than the market assumes. If the AI-driven content erosion thesis proves to be exaggerated, Sony's current valuation represents a significant mispricing.

The contrast between these two names is instructive. SoftBank offers upside through AI adoption and monetization. Sony offers upside through the repricing of AI risk. Both are bets on the market being wrong, but in opposite directions.

## The "Death of SaaS" Narrative Is Real, But It Is Not the Whole Story

One of the most provocative sections of the report addresses the persistent concerns about software-related businesses, specifically the "death of SaaS" narrative. The report states that these concerns are likely to persist, and that in the domestic IT services market, the greatest risk factor appears to be the shift by customers toward in-house development.

This is a nuanced observation. The narrative around software disruption often focuses on the idea that AI will replace software companies entirely. But the report's framing suggests something more specific: the risk is not that AI kills software, but that customers move development in-house, bypassing traditional IT services providers. This is a different threat, and it has different implications for different types of companies.

For large IT services firms like Fujitsu, this trend is particularly dangerous. The report explicitly warns that a profit decline for hardware is likely hard to avoid in the first quarter, and that the outlook will remain challenging for a while. Fujitsu is being squeezed from two directions: its hardware business faces cyclical headwinds, and its services business faces structural disruption from in-house development.

But the "death of SaaS" narrative should not be applied uniformly. Some software companies have pricing power, recurring revenue, and network effects that make them resistant to disruption. The report's caution is directed at specific segments, not the entire software universe. The strategic implication is that investors need to differentiate between software companies that are exposed to commoditization risk and those that are not.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Pricing Power and the Timing of the Cycle

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several meaningful open questions that deserve further exploration. The first is the sustainability of pricing power. Tokyo Electron's price hike is cited as a positive development, but the report does not address how long this pricing power can persist. If the semiconductor equipment cycle turns, pricing power could evaporate quickly. The same question applies to Renesas and its competitors. Are the price hikes a structural shift, or are they a cyclical phenomenon driven by temporary supply constraints?

The second open question is the timing of the cycle. The report identifies an upcycle for Renesas, but it does not specify how long this upcycle is expected to last. Semiconductor cycles are notoriously difficult to time, and the risk of a downturn is always present. Investors who buy into the cycle late may find themselves holding positions that peak sooner than expected.

The third open question is the trajectory of AI software monetization. The report assumes that coding AI improvements will drive continued demand for inference compute, which benefits ARM and other hardware companies. But if software monetization disappoints, the demand for inference compute could plateau. This is a risk that is difficult to quantify but cannot be ignored.

These questions are not weaknesses in the report; they are invitations for further analysis. Serious investors should use them as starting points for their own research.

## A Decision Framework for Allocating Capital Across Japan's Technology Landscape

Based on the report's logic, a decision framework emerges that can help investors navigate the current environment. The framework has three dimensions: cyclical positioning, structural tailwinds, and valuation.

First, assess cyclical positioning. Companies that are in the early or middle stages of an upcycle, like Renesas, offer better risk-reward than companies that are late in the cycle or facing headwinds, like Fujitsu. The report's emphasis on Renesas's six consecutive quarters of growth is a signal that the cycle has room to run.

Second, evaluate structural tailwinds. Companies that benefit from AI demand, like Tokyo Electron and SoftBank, have a tailwind that is independent of the broader economic cycle. These are the names that can outperform even if the macro environment deteriorates.

Third, apply valuation discipline. Companies with solid fundamentals but excessive multiples, like Advantest and Disco, should be avoided or underweighted. Companies with depressed valuations that overstate risks, like Sony, should be considered for overweight positions.

This framework leads to a clear portfolio construction: overweight Tokyo Electron, Renesas, SoftBank, and Sony. Neutral on Hitachi and NEC. Underweight or avoid Advantest, Disco, Fujitsu, and Panasonic. The report's recommendations align with this framework, but the framework itself is a tool that can be applied to other names and sectors as well.

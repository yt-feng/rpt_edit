ZH_A: 最近关于数据中心的新闻特别多，什么电力短缺、社区反对、供应链问题，听得人眼花缭乱。但到底哪些市场真正值得关注？

ZH_B: 这个问题的答案其实就一句话：取决于你在看哪个市场。但整体上，我们对所谓的“主要都市”市场以及在那里建数据中心的公司，感觉相当不错。

ZH_A: 主要都市市场？这个定义很关键。报告里是怎么划分的？

ZH_B: 报告把数据中心市场分成了三类。第一类是主要都市，也就是北美排名前八到十的核心城市。第二类是次要都市，你可以理解为二三线城市或者主要都市的远郊。第三类是乡村市场。

ZH_A: 那像Digital Realty和Equinix这样的上市公司，它们主要布局在哪里？

ZH_B: 它们的资产绝大多数都集中在主要都市和次要都市。这意味着它们受市场噪音的影响相对较小，是比较稳健的选择。

ZH_A: 所以，主要都市市场到底有什么特别的？

ZH_B: 它们是对延迟最敏感的应用场景的核心，比如人工智能推理，还有企业托管服务。这些市场非常成熟，但想新建数据中心也极其困难。

ZH_A: 困难在哪里？是审批慢吗？

ZH_B: 不止是慢。电力排队时间长达4到7年，建设成本极高，供应链也非常紧张。所以，现有的产能和已经拿到许可的在建项目，就变得非常有价值。

ZH_A: 这听起来是个好生意，但风险呢？

ZH_B: 风险也在增加。报告特别提到了“邻避效应”在增强，尤其是在北弗吉尼亚和凤凰城。因为数据中心耗电量大，推高了当地电价，引发了社区反弹。不过报告认为，那些经验丰富的开发商有能力应对。

ZH_A: 那次要都市市场呢？它们扮演什么角色？

ZH_B: 它们是主要都市的溢出承接地。当客户对延迟不那么敏感，或者对价格更敏感时，就会流向这里。过去五年，这类市场增长非常快。

ZH_A: 那乡村市场就是纯粹为了训练大模型了？

ZH_B: 对。乡村市场主要服务训练任务，对延迟完全不敏感，但对成本极度敏感。那里的设施更大，建设成本更低，电力获取也快得多，只要1到3年。

ZH_A: 听起来乡村市场全是优点？

ZH_B: 也不全是。最大的问题是，长期需求的可见性不那么清晰。很多建设带有投机性质，而且开发商的实力和经验也参差不齐。

ZH_A: 那我们来聊聊具体城市。报告重点分析了哪几个？

ZH_B: 三个最有代表性的：北弗吉尼亚、凤凰城和亚特兰大。

ZH_A: 北弗吉尼亚，也就是NoVa，可以说是数据中心行业的首都了。

ZH_B: 没错。它现在的供应量就有大约8吉瓦，还有5吉瓦在建。电力排队据说已经排到了七年之后。但即便如此，它的定价还在涨，目前每千瓦每月大概217美元，空置率稳定在1%以下。

ZH_A: 这简直是印钞机。那凤凰城呢？

ZH_B: 凤凰城是一个拥有电力优势的成长型市场。土地更便宜，电力也更容易获取，正在吸引大量超大规模云厂商的部署。但它的网络生态没有NoVa那么深厚。

ZH_A: 亚特兰大听起来像是一个平衡点。

ZH_B: 对。报告对亚特兰大的定位是，它正在快速成长为一个次级枢纽。既靠近企业需求，电力条件也在改善。目前定价是每千瓦每月175美元，空置率大约2%。

ZH_A: 我注意到，Digital Realty和Equinix都在亚特兰大有大动作。

ZH_B: 是的，它们都在亚特兰大周边建设面向超大规模客户的大型园区。报告很看好这个策略，因为市场动态有利，而且这两家公司都是经验丰富的建设者，执行风险相对有限。

ZH_A: 报告里有一个数据让我很震惊，就是新建设施的规模比现有设施大得多。

ZH_B: 对。在排名前八的市场里，现有设施的平均规模是27兆瓦，而规划中新建的平均是112兆瓦，大了四倍多。这说明需求密度在急剧提升。

ZH_A: 那谁是这场扩张的最大玩家？

ZH_B: 从数据看，亚马逊和微软的扩张计划最激进，各自都有5到6吉瓦的容量在规划中。谷歌和Meta也很庞大，但Meta的未来规划相对保守。

ZH_A: 所以，这个报告的核心结论是什么？投资者应该怎么理解？

ZH_B: 核心结论是，数据中心市场的增长正在从受限的城市核心，向成本更低、电力更丰富的地区迁移。但最优质、最稀缺的资产，仍然集中在主要都市市场。

ZH_A: 那报告里有没有哪些问题，是它提出来了，但并没有完全解答的？

ZH_B: 有两个问题很值得继续深挖。第一，邻避效应到底会发展到多严重？会不会真的限制主要都市的增长？报告指出了风险，但没有给出明确的量化判断。第二，乡村市场的长期需求到底能不能兑现？如果AI训练的需求放缓，那些投机性建设会不会变成过剩产能？

ZH_A: 这两个问题确实很关键，看来完整报告里会有更深入的分析。

ZH_B: 没错。如果你想了解更详细的估值模型，或者想知道Digital Realty和Equinix在具体市场里的项目细节，那完整报告是必读的。

EN_A: We keep hearing headlines about data centers facing power shortages and community pushback. But is every market actually facing the same problems?

EN_B: Not at all. The short answer is, it really depends on which market you are looking at. But broadly speaking, we feel pretty good about what the report calls Major Metro markets, and the companies building there.

EN_A: Major Metro. That is a specific term. How does the report define it?

EN_B: It breaks the U.S. data center market into three tiers. Major Metros are the top 8 to 10 Cities in North America. Then you have Minor Metros, which are smaller Cities or the far outskirts of Major Metros. And then Rural markets.

EN_A: And where do the big public companies, like Digital Realty and Equinix, sit in this picture?

EN_B: Their footprints are overwhelmingly in Major and Minor Metros. That makes them among the most insulated from the market noise and the uncertainty.

EN_A: So what makes a Major Metro so special?

EN_B: These are the most important markets for latency-sensitive workloads, things like AI inferencing, and also for enterprise colocation. They are well established, but incredibly hard to build in.

EN_A: Hard to build in meaning what exactly?

EN_B: Power queues can take four to seven years. Construction costs are the highest. Supply chains are tight. So the existing capacity, and the projects that are already permitted and under construction, become extremely valuable.

EN_A: Sounds like a great business. But there must be a catch.

EN_B: There is an emerging risk. The report highlights growing NIMBYism, especially in Northern Virginia and Phoenix. The rising power costs from data centers are triggering local pushback. The established builders will likely manage it, but it is a risk to watch.

EN_A: And what about the Minor Metro markets? What role do they play?

EN_B: They act as overflow for the Major Metros. When customers are a little less sensitive to latency, or a little more sensitive to price, they go to these markets. There has been significant growth here over the last five years.

EN_A: Then Rural markets are purely for AI training?

EN_B: Exactly. Rural markets are optimized for training workloads. They are latency-insensitive and very price-sensitive. You get bigger facilities, lower build costs, and much faster power availability, typically one to three years.

EN_A: So Rural sounds like the best of all worlds.

EN_B: Not quite. The downside is that there is not as clear visibility into sustained demand. A lot of the builds there are speculative, and the developers can be less experienced.

EN_A: The report does a deep dive on three specific U.S. markets. Which ones?

EN_B: Northern Virginia, or NoVa, Phoenix, and Atlanta. They represent three very different archetypes.

EN_A: NoVa is the king of data centers.

EN_B: It is the dominant global hub. It has about 8 gigawatts of supply today, with another 5 gigawatts under construction. Power queues are reportedly seven years long. But pricing has only gone up, currently around 217 dollars per kilowatt per month, with vacancy below one percent.

EN_A: That is an incredible market. What about Phoenix?

EN_B: Phoenix is a power-advantaged growth market. It attracts huge hyperscale deployments because of lower costs and available land. But it has less network density and ecosystem depth compared to NoVa.

EN_A: And Atlanta sounds like it sits somewhere in between.

EN_B: It does. The report calls it a rapidly scaling secondary hub. It combines proximity to enterprise demand with improving power access. Pricing is around 175 dollars per kilowatt per month, with vacancy around two percent.

EN_A: I noticed both Digital Realty and Equinix are making big moves in Atlanta.

EN_B: Yes. They are both building large hyperscale-focused campuses outside of Atlanta. The report likes this strategy. Market dynamics are favorable, and both companies are savvy builders with strong teams.

EN_A: One chart in the report really stood out to me. The new facilities are much larger than the old ones.

EN_B: That is a key insight. Across the top eight markets, the average existing facility is 27 megawatts. The average planned facility is 112 megawatts. That is more than four times larger. It shows how demand density is increasing.

EN_A: And who are the biggest players in this expansion?

EN_B: Amazon and Microsoft have the most ambitious capacity expansion plans, each with 5 to 6 gigawatts slotted. Google and Meta are also huge, but Meta's future plans are more modest.

EN_A: So what is the big takeaway for investors?

EN_B: The big takeaway is that growth is migrating from constrained urban hUBS to lower-cost, power-rich regions. But the most valuable and scarce assets remain in the Major Metros.

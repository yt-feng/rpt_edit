EN_B: It means credit demand from the private sector remains very weak. Household loans dropped by 341 billion, and corporate loans fell by 410 billion. Both are down significantly year over year.

EN_B: Bill financing did pick up, reaching 1.2 trillion in April, up 409 billion year over year. But that's likely driven by policy push, and it wasn't enough to offset weakness elsewhere.

EN_B: Correct. Loan growth decelerated further to 5.6 percent year over year, down from 5.7 percent in March and 6.4 percent at the end of last year.

EN_B: Yes. New TSF came in at 625 billion in April, 535 billion below last year and far below the market expectation of 1.25 trillion. Government bond issuance was also slightly lower than last year.
EN_A: The credit impulse indicator the report mentions turned more negative. What does that tell us?
EN_B: It turned from minus 0.4 percent of GDP in March to minus 1.9 percent in April. That means credit's contribution to economic growth is weakening.

EN_B: Actually, the opposite. Interbank liquidity has been very loose. The DR007 rate has been hovering around 1.3 percent, below the policy rate of 1.4 percent. The PBC withdrew some liquidity in April through open market operations, MLF and PSL, but overall conditions remain ample.

EN_B: The key issue is not a lack of money, but a lack of demand. The PBC is keeping conditions accommodative to try to boost lending, but it's not working well.

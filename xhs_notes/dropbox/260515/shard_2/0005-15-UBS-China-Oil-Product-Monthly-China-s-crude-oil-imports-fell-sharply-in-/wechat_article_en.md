# China’s Refining Crunch Is Not a Demand Story — It Is a Structural Supply Shock That Will Reshape Global Product Flows

The conventional narrative around China’s April crude oil import collapse is that weak domestic demand is to blame. That reading is dangerously incomplete. China’s crude imports fell 20% year-on-year to 38.47 million tonnes in April, the lowest since July 2022. Major refinery utilization dropped to 70.8%, far below the 2025 average of 80%. But the real story is not about Chinese consumers buying less gasoline. It is about a synchronized global refining supply crisis that has trapped Chinese refineries between soaring input costs and policy-constrained output channels. The result is a structural compression of margins that will force strategic realignments across the entire Asia-Pacific product market.

The evidence is clear: China’s crude inventory has remained remarkably stable at roughly 1.3 billion barrels since March, equivalent to 76 days of crude demand and over 100 days of crude imports. This is not a market that is running out of oil. It is a market where refineries cannot economically process the crude they have. The bottleneck is not upstream supply but midstream processing economics, compounded by a deliberate policy choice to restrict refined product exports. Understanding this distinction is essential for any investor, trader, or strategist positioning for the next 12 to 18 months in global energy markets.

## The Collapse in Chinese Refinery Utilization Reflects Not Just Weak Demand But a Policy-Induced Margin Squeeze That Creates a Binding Constraint on Output

When major refinery utilization falls to 70.8%, the instinct is to attribute it to soft demand. Demand is indeed weak — gasoline and diesel retail prices fell by nearly 1,000 renminbi per tonne and 300 renminbi per tonne, respectively, in April. But the more important factor is the cost side. The official selling price for crude imports has risen sharply, and freight and insurance premiums have climbed alongside it. Chinese refineries are paying more for their key input while being unable to pass through those costs because domestic product demand is insufficient to absorb higher prices.

This is where the export policy becomes decisive. China suspended refined oil product exports in March to prioritize domestic energy security amid Middle East tensions. That decision, while prudent from a security standpoint, removed the safety valve that would have allowed refineries to offload excess product into international markets. With domestic storage already full — gasoline and diesel inventories remain high — refineries face a choice: run at lower utilization or produce into inventory that generates no revenue. They are choosing the former. The result is that China’s refining system is operating well below its structural capacity, not because crude is unavailable, but because the economic and policy framework makes running unprofitable.

The approval of 0.5 million tonnes of exports to non-Hong Kong destinations in May, double the April level, confirms that policymakers recognize the pressure. But this volume is still only about 30% of pre-conflict export levels. The question is whether this represents a gradual reopening or a one-time adjustment. The answer will determine whether Chinese refineries can return to normal utilization rates or remain constrained for an extended period.

## The Middle East Supply Disruption Is Far Larger Than the Market Has Priced, and Its Impact on Refined Products Will Be More Severe Than on Crude

The market’s focus on crude supply disruptions from the Middle East is understandable but myopic. The far more consequential impact is on refined product availability. According to estimates from a global investment bank report, even assuming strategic petroleum reserve releases at maximum pace, the outright shortfall of product exports from the Middle East would be approximately 3.5 million barrels per day. Actual SPR releases are likely to be slower and more challenging than theoretical maximums, meaning the real shortfall may be higher.

The reason is structural: over 3.5 million barrels per day of refining capacity in the Gulf Cooperation Council has halted operations due to strikes and attacks. More than 2 million barrels per day of that capacity may be damaged and require repairs. To put this in perspective, at the peak of drone attacks on Russian refineries in fall 2025, less than 1 million barrels per day of capacity was affected. The current scale is multiple times larger. GCC refineries are estimated to be running at roughly 50% capacity on average.

The downstream implications are stark. When crude supply is disrupted, the market can draw on inventories and adjust production. When refining capacity is disrupted, the entire product supply chain is severed. A crude shortfall of roughly 5.3 million barrels per day translates into a product shortfall of approximately 3.5 million barrels per day because the crude that cannot be processed becomes unavailable product. The market has not fully priced the second-order effects of this dynamic, particularly for middle distillates like diesel that are critical for industrial activity and transportation.

## Global Refining Capacity Has Taken an Unprecedented Hit, Creating a Window of Opportunity for Export-Capable Refiners While Crushing Those Without Access to Product Markets

The Middle East disruption is not happening in isolation. March and April are typically the peak of scheduled maintenance for global refineries. This year, that seasonal maintenance has been compounded by intensified drone attacks on Russian assets, a fire at the 120,000 barrel-per-day Geelong refinery in Australia, and a workers’ strike at the Whiting refinery in the United States. Based on data from Kpler and industry estimates, offline capacity globally may have exceeded 12 million barrels per day in April.

This is an extraordinary number. It means that at a time when Chinese refineries are being forced to cut runs due to domestic policy constraints, the rest of the world is losing capacity at an even faster rate. The net effect is a global product market that is simultaneously undersupplied from the Middle East, Russia, and parts of the OECD, while Chinese refineries sit with idle capacity and high inventories.

The strategic implication is that refiners with the ability to export products into international markets will capture extraordinary margins. European refining margins, which had been range-bound, are likely to spike as Middle East product exports disappear and Russian capacity remains degraded. Chinese refineries that receive export quotas will benefit disproportionately. Those that do not will continue to suffer from weak domestic margins and high inventory costs. The divergence between export-capable and domestic-only refiners will widen sharply.

## The Report Leaves an Open Question That Will Define the Next Phase: Will China’s Export Policy Shift Become Permanent, and What Would That Mean for Global Product Balances?

The report provides extensive data on the current state of Chinese refining but leaves one critical question unresolved: is the May export approval a tactical adjustment or the beginning of a strategic pivot? The answer depends on how Beijing weighs two competing objectives: domestic energy security versus the economic health of its refining sector.

If the government views the current export restrictions as temporary emergency measures, then a return to pre-conflict export levels would release significant product volumes into a global market that desperately needs them. This would benefit Chinese refiners and help alleviate global product tightness. But it would also mean that China is willing to accept the security risk of exporting refined products while Middle East tensions remain elevated.

If instead the government decides that maintaining higher domestic inventories is a permanent priority, then Chinese refineries will remain constrained, global product markets will stay tighter for longer, and the margin advantage will accrue to refiners in other regions who can serve the product deficit. The report provides the data to frame the question but does not — and cannot — resolve it. That uncertainty is itself a strategic input for anyone positioning in energy markets.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Positioning in the Refining and Product Trade

The analytical challenge posed by this report is that the variables are interconnected but not perfectly correlated. Investors need a framework to cut through the complexity. The following three questions provide a structured approach to assessing the opportunity set.

First, does your portfolio have exposure to Chinese refineries that have export quotas or the potential to receive them? The divergence between export-capable and domestic-only refiners will be the single largest determinant of relative performance in the Chinese energy sector. If export policy loosens, the beneficiaries are clear. If it tightens, the pain will be concentrated among independents.

Second, are you positioned for the product dislocation rather than the crude dislocation? The market has focused heavily on crude supply from the Middle East, but the refining capacity damage is the more persistent and impactful story. Products, particularly diesel and jet fuel, will see more sustained pricing dislocations than crude. Investors who are long crude but short products may be missing the more important trade.

Third, what is your view on the duration of the Middle East refining disruption? If damaged capacity requires months of repairs, the product shortfall becomes a multi-quarter event that will reshape trade flows. If capacity returns quickly, the disruption is a trading event. The report suggests that the scale of damage is significant, but the exact repair timeline remains uncertain. This is the variable that will determine whether the current situation is a spike or a new equilibrium.

# China’s Refining Sector Is Trapped Between a Crude Supply Shock and Weak Domestic Demand — and the Policy Response Will Determine the Winners

The narrative that China’s oil market is simply suffering from a temporary geopolitical disruption misses the deeper structural tension now unfolding. In April, China’s crude oil imports collapsed 20% year-on-year to 38.47 million tonnes, the lowest monthly volume since July 2022. Major domestic refineries slashed utilization rates to 70.8%, far below the 2025 average of 80%. These are not merely cyclical adjustments. They are the visible symptoms of a sector caught between two powerful forces: a severe supply-side shock from the Middle East and persistently weak domestic demand for refined products. The critical question for investors is not whether these pressures will ease, but how the Chinese government’s policy levers — particularly on refined oil product exports — will reshape the competitive landscape for refiners, traders, and downstream players.

The conventional wisdom holds that high crude oil prices are the primary culprit. That is only half the story. The data show that Chinese crude inventories have remained remarkably stable since March, hovering around 1.3 billion barrels, equivalent to 76 days of crude oil demand and over 100 days of crude imports. Gasoline and diesel inventories are also high. This suggests that the import decline is not driven by a lack of supply availability, but by a deliberate demand-side response: high prices are suppressing domestic consumption, and refiners are unable to offload excess product because export channels remain restricted. The result is a system under internal pressure — ample crude in storage, weak end-user demand, and limited outlets for finished products.

This is not a normal inventory cycle. It is a policy-induced bottleneck that is amplifying the impact of external supply disruptions. The next several months will test whether Beijing is willing to use export policy as a strategic release valve, or whether it will prioritize domestic price stability at the cost of refinery profitability and market efficiency.

## The Collapse in Refinery Utilization Reflects a Mismatch Between Crude Cost and Product Demand, Not Just a Supply Disruption

The headline figure — major refinery utilization falling to 70.8% — demands deeper interpretation. This is not a uniform decline across the sector. Shandong independent refineries, often considered the marginal players in China’s refining system, actually saw utilization rise slightly month-on-month to 55.2%. The divergence is instructive. Major state-owned refineries have greater exposure to term crude contracts with higher official selling prices and are more constrained by policy obligations to maintain domestic supply. Independents, by contrast, can adjust feedstock sourcing more flexibly and operate closer to spot market conditions.

The real story lies in the margin compression. Domestic retail price ceilings for gasoline and diesel saw cumulative cuts of 135 yuan per tonne and 130 yuan per tonne respectively in April, yet market average prices fell nearly 1,000 yuan per tonne for gasoline and 300 yuan for diesel. That gap between official ceilings and realized market prices is a direct measure of demand weakness. It signals that refiners are discounting aggressively to move product, compressing their margins even as crude input costs rise due to higher import premiums and freight insurance.

The implication is straightforward: the refining margin environment in China has deteriorated to a point where even high utilization rates would not translate into profitability. The utilization decline is therefore not just a supply-side story — it is a rational response to a demand-deficient market. Investors should watch for further capacity idling if the current conditions persist into the third quarter.

## China’s Tentative Reopening of Product Exports Is a Strategic Signal, Not a Volume Solution

In March, China suspended refined oil product exports entirely to prioritize domestic energy security. Now, according to a Reuters report, Beijing has approved approximately 0.5 million tonnes of exports to non-Hong Kong destinations for May — double the April level but still only about 30% of pre-conflict export volumes. This is a modest reopening, but its significance lies in the rationale behind it.

The report analysis suggests this decision is driven by persistently weak domestic demand and high inventory pressure for gasoline and diesel. In other words, the government is acknowledging that the domestic market cannot absorb current production levels, and that continued export restrictions would only deepen the inventory overhang and further depress refinery margins. This is a pragmatic shift, but it is not yet a policy pivot.

The open question is whether this export authorization will expand further. If domestic demand remains weak through the summer driving season — a period that typically sees higher gasoline consumption in China — the case for larger export quotas will become compelling. However, the government faces a trade-off. Increasing exports would relieve domestic inventory pressure and support refinery margins, but it would also reduce the volume of products available for domestic consumption at a time when global refined product markets are already tight due to Middle East supply disruptions. The risk is that higher exports could feed domestic price inflation, which would run counter to Beijing’s broader economic stability objectives.

Investors should monitor not just the volume of export approvals, but the geographic composition. Exports to non-Hong Kong destinations suggest a broader market access strategy, potentially targeting Asia-Pacific markets where product shortages are most acute. If export flows shift toward higher-margin destinations, that would be a bullish signal for China’s integrated refiners.

## The Middle East Supply Shock Is Larger Than the Market Prices In, and Its Second-Order Effects on Global Refining Are Just Beginning

The scale of the Middle East disruption is difficult to overstate. According to UBS estimates, even assuming strategic petroleum reserve releases at maximum pace, the outright shortfall of product exports from the Middle East would be approximately 3.5 million barrels per day. The actual shortfall could be higher, because SPR releases on both crude and product sides may prove slower and more challenging than theoretical models assume.

Critically, the crude shortfall is likely to translate into equivalent cuts in refinery throughput, meaning roughly 9 million barrels per day of crude disruption could become visible on the product side. To put that in context, UBS estimates that more than 3.5 million barrels per day of refining capacity in the Gulf Cooperation Council has halted operations, with over 2 million barrels per day potentially damaged and requiring repairs. At the peak of drone attacks on Russian refineries in fall 2025, less than 1 million barrels per day of capacity was affected. The current scale is qualitatively different.

Beyond the Middle East, the global refining system is under synchronized stress. March and April are typically the peak of scheduled maintenance for global refineries. This year, that seasonal maintenance coincides with intensified drone attacks on Russian assets, a fire at the 120,000 barrel-per-day Geelong refinery in Australia, and a workers’ strike at the Whiting refinery in the United States. Based on Kpler data and UBS estimates, offline capacity globally may have exceeded 12 million barrels per day in April.

The second-order implication is that the global refining margin environment is likely to remain elevated for longer than many expect. However, this does not benefit all refiners equally. Chinese refiners face a unique disadvantage: they are paying higher crude import costs due to elevated OSPs and freight premiums, while their domestic product market is too weak to pass through those costs. The result is a margin squeeze that is specific to China, even as global refining margins improve. For Chinese refiners, the question is not whether global margins are rising, but whether they can access those margins through exports.

## What the Report Does Not Yet Fully Answer: The Speed and Shape of China’s Policy Adjustment

The report provides a detailed snapshot of current conditions, but it leaves several critical analytical gaps that investors must fill themselves. First, the elasticity of China’s domestic demand to price changes remains poorly quantified. The data show that high oil prices have dampened gasoline and diesel consumption, but we do not know the precise demand response function. If demand proves more inelastic than assumed, the inventory overhang could clear faster than expected, reducing the need for exports and supporting domestic margins.

Second, the report does not model the interaction between China’s export policy and global product markets. If China significantly increases exports, it could depress product prices in Asia-Pacific markets, compressing margins for other regional refiners. That would create a feedback loop: lower Asian product prices would reduce the incentive for Chinese exports, potentially re-trapping domestic refiners. The equilibrium point is unknown.

Third, the impact on China’s independent refineries — the so-called teapots — is underexplored. The report notes that independent utilization was relatively stable, but their margin position is precarious. Independents typically have less access to term crude contracts and are more exposed to spot market volatility. If the current environment persists, a wave of capacity closures among independents could accelerate industry consolidation, benefiting state-owned majors. The timing and magnitude of such consolidation remain uncertain.

These are not gaps in the report’s quality; they are inherent uncertainties in a rapidly evolving situation. Investors should treat the report’s data as a baseline and stress-test their own assumptions about demand elasticity, policy responsiveness, and competitive dynamics.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the China Refining Dislocation

For portfolio managers and analysts covering the Chinese energy sector, the current environment demands a structured decision framework rather than a directional bet. The following three-factor model can help evaluate opportunities and risks across the value chain.

Factor One: Export Policy Trajectory. The single most important variable is whether Beijing will expand product export quotas beyond the current May authorization. A bullish scenario for integrated refiners would involve a sustained increase in export volumes, allowing them to capture higher global margins. A bearish scenario would see export restrictions maintained or tightened, trapping excess supply domestically and compressing margins further. The key leading indicator is not the export volume itself, but the government’s commentary on domestic price stability and energy security. If official statements emphasize the need to support refinery operations, expect export expansion. If they emphasize guarding against domestic price inflation, expect continued restrictions.

Factor Two: Domestic Demand Recovery Path. The second variable is the trajectory of Chinese gasoline and diesel demand. The current weakness may reflect not just high prices but also broader economic softness, including slower industrial activity and reduced transportation demand. Investors should monitor high-frequency data such as highway traffic volumes, industrial electricity consumption, and PMI indices for transport and logistics sectors. A meaningful demand recovery would reduce the need for exports and support domestic margins directly. A prolonged demand slump would increase the pressure for export liberalization.

Factor Three: Global Refining Margin Persistence. The third variable is the duration of elevated global refining margins. If Middle East disruptions prove short-lived and global offline capacity returns quickly, the window for Chinese refiners to export profitably may close rapidly. Conversely, if disruptions persist into the second half of the year, the margin environment could support a longer export cycle. The key data points to monitor are Middle East refinery restart timelines, SPR release rates, and global product inventory levels.

Combining these three factors yields four scenarios. The most favorable for Chinese refiners is a combination of export liberalization, domestic demand recovery, and persistent global margins. The least favorable is continued export restrictions, prolonged domestic demand weakness, and a rapid normalization of global margins. Investors should position accordingly, with a bias toward integrated state-owned majors that have the balance sheet and policy access to weather the dislocation.

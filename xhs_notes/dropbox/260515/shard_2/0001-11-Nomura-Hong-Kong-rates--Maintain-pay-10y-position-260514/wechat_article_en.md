# The Hong Kong Long-End Rate Trade Is No Longer a Structural Anomaly: It Is a Cyclical Opportunity

The prevailing wisdom among many fixed-income investors has been that long-end Hong Kong dollar interest rates are structurally depressed—a consequence of chronic bond supply shortages and persistently weak loan demand. For much of the past two years, that narrative has been correct. But it is now becoming a dangerous consensus to hold. A subtle but powerful shift is underway in the Hong Kong rates market, one that argues for a decisive repositioning. The core argument is straightforward: the long-end Hong Kong rates market is entering a phase of normalization, driven by a material pickup in bond supply, a cyclical recovery in the property market, and a spread versus US rates that remains historically cheap. The window to position for this move is open now, and the evidence suggests it is not a fleeting tactical trade but the beginning of a more sustained repricing.

Why now matters is a question of both timing and conviction. For months, the 10-year Hong Kong interest rate swap (IRS) has traded at a significant discount to its US equivalent, a gap that has at times exceeded 80 basis points. That gap, while wide, was rational when the only source of long-duration demand came from bank treasury desks starved of alternatives. But the supply-demand calculus has changed. Since April, a string of high-profile local issuers—including the Airport Authority, MTR, and the Hong Kong government itself—have tapped the long-end HKD bond market. This is not a one-off. It signals a structural shift in issuance patterns tied to major infrastructure projects, most notably the Northern Metropolis development. The market is being forced to absorb duration it has not had to price in for years. The trade, therefore, is not a bet against Hong Kong; it is a bet that the market will finally price the cost of that duration correctly.

This article is structured around four key arguments. First, the supply-demand imbalance in HKD bonds has decisively turned. Second, Hong Kong's macro recovery is broadening in ways that directly support higher long-end rates. Third, the long-end HK-US spread offers a compelling risk-reward entry point. Finally, we will examine what the report does not fully answer—the behavior of loan growth and the role of the aggregate balance—and offer a decision framework for investors considering this trade.

## A Structural Shift in Bond Supply Has Broken the Long-End Duration Drought

The most important driver of the long-end HK rates trade is not a macro forecast; it is a balance-sheet reality. For the better part of two years, the Hong Kong dollar bond market has suffered from a pronounced lack of long-duration issuance. Non-financial entities, which typically provide the bulk of 10-year and longer paper, were largely absent. This created a vacuum that bank treasury desks were forced to fill by receiving long-tenor IRS—essentially buying synthetic duration because they could not buy it in the cash bond market. The result was a persistent downward bias in long-end swap rates, even as US rates moved higher.

That dynamic has now reversed. Since April, a cluster of issuers has come to market with bonds of 10 years or longer. The Hong Kong Airport Authority, MTR Corporation, the West Kowloon Cultural District Authority, and the Hong Kong government itself have all issued long-dated HKD paper. The significance here is not the absolute size of any single deal, but the signal it sends about the future supply pipeline. The Northern Metropolis project alone represents a multi-decade infrastructure program that will require regular, predictable long-term funding. The government has already indicated its expectation that the project will be completed in the mid-2030s. That is a timeline that demands a steady stream of long-dated issuance.

The "so what" for investors is direct: the days of structurally cheap long-end duration are ending. The supply-demand imbalance that suppressed HK rates is being unwound. Each new deal forces the market to reprice the cost of carrying duration. For those positioned to pay fixed in the 10-year HK IRS, this is the fundamental catalyst. The trade is no longer reliant on a bearish macro view; it is anchored in a measurable shift in market structure. The question is not whether supply will increase, but how quickly the market will adjust to the new equilibrium.

## Hong Kong's Macro Stabilization Is Broadening, and Property Recovery Is the Key Transmission Mechanism

The supply-side argument is powerful, but it is not sufficient on its own. For long-end rates to move decisively higher, demand-side dynamics must also shift. Specifically, the loan-to-deposit (LTD) ratio, which has been stuck near cyclical lows, needs to recover. The latest data from March shows the LTD ratio at 72.3 percent, still close to its trough. This has been a persistent drag on long-end rates because banks, flush with deposits and facing weak loan demand, have been forced to deploy excess liquidity into duration. But there are now clear signs that this headwind is beginning to fade.

Hong Kong's first-quarter GDP growth came in at 5.9 percent year-on-year, well above expectations. More importantly, residential property prices have rebounded sharply, returning to their highest level since October 2023. This is not just a wealth effect story; it is a credit cycle story. Higher property prices feed directly into mortgage demand, which is the largest single component of Hong Kong's loan book. As transaction volumes pick up and prices stabilize, the pipeline for new mortgage origination expands. The lag between price recovery and loan growth is typically three to six months, which means the effects of the recent property rebound should begin to show in loan data over the coming quarters.

The implication for the rates trade is clear: if loan growth recovers, banks will have less need to receive long-dated swaps to generate yield. The natural hedging behavior that has suppressed long-end rates will reverse. This is not a speculative forecast; it is a logical consequence of the macro cycle. The report's analysts are optimistic that this spillover will occur, and the data supports that view. The risk is that the recovery in loan growth is slower than anticipated, but even in that scenario, the supply-side argument provides a floor for the trade. The combination of more supply and eventually higher loan demand creates a powerful two-sided argument for higher long-end HK rates.

## The HK-US Spread Remains Historically Wide, Offering a Compelling Asymmetric Bet

The third pillar of the trade is valuation. The spread between 10-year HK and US IRS currently stands at approximately minus 73 basis points. This is well below the historical average, even after a modest recovery from the March low of minus 88 basis points. To put that in perspective, the spread has already narrowed by 15 basis points from its trough, but it remains at levels that have historically been associated with extreme stress or structural imbalance. As the supply-demand and macro arguments play out, there is no reason the spread cannot narrow to minus 50 basis points or even tighter.

The asymmetry of this bet is what makes it attractive. The report's analysts argue that if loan growth rebounds, the spread could easily move to minus 50 basis points or narrower. If loan growth remains stagnant, the spread may oscillate around minus 80 basis points. In other words, the downside is limited to roughly 7 basis points from current levels, while the upside is at least 23 basis points and potentially much more. This is a favorable risk-reward profile for a conviction level of 4 out of 5, meaning the analysts are already positioned at two-thirds of their desired size.

It is also worth noting what is happening on the front end. Three-month HIBOR has been bid up since the start of April, driven by a hot IPO pipeline, outright USD/HKD buying, and dividend payments by Chinese companies to H-share investors. These factors are well-understood and already priced into the front end. The real opportunity, as the report emphasizes, is in the long end. The front-end moves are a tailwind for the trade because they steepen the curve, but the primary driver remains the structural repricing of long-duration risk. The spread trade against US rates simply amplifies the return potential while providing a relative-value anchor.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Behavior of the Aggregate Balance and the Path of Loan Growth

No analysis is complete without identifying its blind spots. This report is rigorous in its logic, but it leaves several open questions that should give any serious investor pause. The first is the role of the aggregate balance. During the third quarter of 2025, long-end HK rates moved sharply lower when the aggregate balance dropped after USD/HKD reached the weak-side convertibility limit of 7.85. The report acknowledges this episode but does not fully model the probability of a repeat. If capital outflows intensify and the aggregate balance declines again, the liquidity drain could compress long-end rates in the short term, even as the structural supply story plays out. The trade's timeline to end-May may be too short to fully capture the supply-driven repricing if a liquidity shock intervenes.

The second open question is the pace of loan growth. The report is optimistic that the property recovery will spill over into mortgage demand, but the transmission mechanism is not automatic. Hong Kong's loan market has been subdued for reasons that go beyond the property cycle: corporate deleveraging, cautious bank lending standards, and a shift in corporate funding toward capital markets. If these structural factors persist, loan growth may remain tepid even as property prices rise. In that scenario, the LTD ratio stays low, banks continue to receive swaps, and the spread remains wide. The trade would not lose money—the downside is limited—but it would not generate the 40-basis-point gain the analysts target.

Finally, the report does not address the potential for US rates to move in a way that undermines the relative-value argument. The trade is structured as paying 10-year HK IRS versus two-thirds US, which implies a view that HK rates will underperform US rates. If US rates rally sharply—for example, on a dovish Fed surprise—the spread could widen even if HK rates are rising in absolute terms. The trade would still profit if HK rates rise more than US rates, but the margin for error narrows. These are not reasons to avoid the trade, but they are reasons to size it carefully and monitor the macro backdrop actively.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Considering the Pay 10-Year HK IRS Trade

For investors evaluating this trade, a structured decision framework is essential. The first step is to assess the conviction level. The report assigns a conviction of 4 out of 5, meaning the analysts are positioned at two-thirds of their desired size. This suggests that while the trade is well-supported, there is enough uncertainty to warrant leaving room for error. Investors should calibrate their position size accordingly, perhaps starting at a smaller allocation and adding on any pullback in the spread.

The second step is to define the catalyst timeline. The trade targets a 40-basis-point gain by end-May, which is a relatively short horizon. The primary catalysts are new bond issuance and monthly loan data. Investors should monitor the primary market calendar for any additional long-dated HKD bonds from government-related entities. Each new deal is a data point that validates the supply thesis. Similarly, monthly LTD ratio data and property transaction volumes will provide real-time confirmation of whether the macro recovery is translating into loan growth.

The third step is to manage the tail risks. The most significant tail risk is a liquidity shock that drives the aggregate balance lower and compresses long-end rates. Investors should have a stop-loss or a hedging strategy in place for this scenario. One approach is to layer in a short-duration hedge, such as receiving 2-year HK IRS, to protect against a steepening move driven by front-end weakness. Another is to use options to cap downside while retaining upside exposure. The trade's favorable risk-reward profile makes it worth executing, but no trade is risk-free.

The fourth and final step is to decide on the exit strategy. The report targets 40 basis points of gains, which implies a spread of approximately minus 33 basis points, or a significant narrowing from current levels. Investors should predefine their exit criteria: if the spread reaches the target, take partial profits. If the macro data disappoints, reduce the position. The key is to avoid the trap of holding a winning trade too long, waiting for the absolute top. The structural thesis is strong, but the market can always deliver surprises.

# China’s Data Center Buildout Is Not Just a Capacity Story—It’s a Structural Shift in How AI Infrastructure Gets Built and Monetized

The global investment bank report on China’s data center operators makes one thing unmistakably clear: the AI infrastructure wave in China is real, it is accelerating, and it is fundamentally different from what the US market has experienced. While much of the public discussion has focused on whether Chinese hyperscalers are spending enough relative to their American counterparts, the more consequential question is what kind of infrastructure they are building—and who will own it.

The report’s data shows that China’s top five hyperscalers—ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, and Kingsoft—will collectively spend an estimated $722 billion on capex by 2028, up from just $56 billion in 2025. That is a staggering ramp. But the headline number obscures a more important pattern: the ratio of capex to cash flow for these companies is projected to rise from 15% in 2022 to 89% by 2026 before settling back to 77% by 2028. This is not a cyclical uptick. It is a multiyear commitment to building out AI-grade infrastructure at a scale that China has never attempted.

The opportunity for data center operators is not simply about filling more racks. It is about repositioning themselves as essential partners in a capital-intensive, technology-driven transformation. The operators that succeed will be those that can offer high-density power, access to advanced GPUs, and new business models like GPU-as-a-Service. The ones that cannot will be left with commoditized colocation space and shrinking margins.

This article unpacks the strategic logic behind the report’s findings, identifies the key fault lines that will separate winners from laggards, and offers a framework for investors trying to navigate a market that is moving faster than the data can keep up.

## The AI Infrastructure Spending Gap Between China and the US Is Closing, But the Composition of That Spending Matters More Than the Total

The report’s opening chart comparing hyperscaler capex between China and the US is instructive, but not for the reason most readers might assume. Yes, US hyperscalers have outspent their Chinese counterparts by a wide margin in absolute terms. But the trajectory is what matters. By 2027, the capex-to-cash-flow ratio for Chinese hyperscalers is projected to reach 85%, nearly identical to the US level of 89%. The spending gap is narrowing rapidly.

What the chart does not show directly—but what the report’s analysis implies—is that the nature of this spending is also shifting. Chinese hyperscalers are not simply buying more servers. They are investing in higher-density data center infrastructure that can support the power and cooling requirements of advanced AI workloads. This is not a marginal upgrade. It is a fundamental redesign of the data center stack, from power distribution to thermal management to network architecture.

For data center operators, this means that the value of a megawatt of capacity is no longer uniform. A legacy colocation facility running at 5 kW per rack is not competing in the same market as a new AI-optimized facility delivering 30 kW or more per rack. The operators that can build and lease high-density capacity will command premium pricing and enjoy stronger tenant retention. Those stuck with older, lower-density assets will face relentless pricing pressure as hyperscalers consolidate their demand into fewer, more capable facilities.

This is the core strategic insight that the report’s capacity and utilization data supports. The market is not growing uniformly. It is bifurcating between AI-grade and general-purpose infrastructure, and the gap between the two will widen over the next three years.

## The Rise of GPU-as-a-Service Is Rewriting the Economics of Data Center Ownership

One of the most striking sections of the report focuses on the emergence of GPU-as-a-Service as a new business model for data center operators. The pricing data shows that US GPUaaS pricing surged more than 30% from December 2025 to March 2026, reversing a prolonged decline. In China, H100 and H200 rental contract prices rose even more sharply over the same period, with H200 pricing jumping from 63 to 83 RMB per hour between February and April 2026 alone.

This is not a temporary spike. It reflects a structural shift in how AI compute is being consumed. As training workloads become more complex and inference demand grows, the need for dedicated, high-performance GPU clusters is outstripping the capacity that hyperscalers can build internally. Data center operators that can offer GPUaaS are effectively becoming compute brokers, not just real estate landlords.

The unit economics presented in the report are compelling. For every 100 units of GPUaaS revenue, EBITDA comes in at 80 units, with depreciation consuming 72 units, leaving an EBIT margin of 8%. That is a thin net margin, but the EBITDA yield is extraordinary. The key variable is utilization. If GPU clusters run at high utilization rates—which the report suggests is happening given the pricing environment—the returns on invested capital can be attractive even after accounting for the rapid depreciation of GPU hardware.

The risk, however, is that GPUaaS is a capital-intensive business with a short technology cycle. The H100 and H200 chips that command premium pricing today will be obsolete within two to three years. Operators must constantly refresh their hardware to remain competitive, which means they are effectively running a treadmill of capital expenditure. The winners will be those that can secure favorable financing terms, maintain high utilization rates, and lock in long-term contracts with hyperscalers that insulate them from price declines.

The report does not fully answer the question of whether GPUaaS economics are sustainable in a downturn, but it does provide enough data to suggest that for the next 12 to 18 months, the environment remains strongly favorable.

## Western China HUBS Are Not a Second-Best Option—They Are the Future of AI Training Infrastructure

The report’s analysis of China’s data center hUBS reveals a geographic shift that is often misunderstood by outside investors. The conventional wisdom is that Western China hUBS like Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, and Gansu are secondary locations, chosen only because land and power are cheaper than in the eastern hUBS. The data tells a different story.

These western hUBS are home to 79% of China’s intelligent computing power as of mid-2025, and they accounted for 70% of new computing power supply between 2021 and 2025. The reason is not just cost. It is latency tolerance. AI training workloads, unlike real-time inference, do not require ultra-low latency to end users. They require massive amounts of power, low electricity costs, and cool ambient temperatures to reduce cooling overhead. The western hUBS deliver on all three fronts.

Take the Horinger cluster in Inner Mongolia. It offers electricity prices as low as 0.36 RMB per kWh and an average temperature of just 5.4 degrees Celsius. Compare that to the Yangtze River Delta hub, where electricity prices range from 0.35 to 0.45 RMB per kWh and the average temperature is 15 degrees. The cost advantage for a large-scale training facility in Inner Mongolia is material, and it compounds over the multiyear life of the facility.

The strategic implication for data center operators is clear: the race for capacity is not just about how many megawatts you can build, but where you build them. Operators with land and power reservations in the western hUBS have a structural cost advantage that will be difficult for competitors in the eastern hUBS to replicate. The report’s capacity data shows that Range Intelligent has 2,500 MW of capacity under construction or in reserve in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei hub, but it also has 150 MW in Gansu and 900 MW in other regions. The geographic diversification is not accidental. It is a deliberate strategy to capture the cost advantages of the western clusters while maintaining proximity to the eastern demand centers.

## The Report’s Stock Ratings Reveal a Market That Is Pricing Growth Unevenly—And That Creates Both Opportunity and Risk

The report initiates coverage on seven data center and cloud infrastructure stocks, with ratings that range from Buy to Sell. The dispersion in valuations is striking. Range Intelligent trades at 33x 2026E EV/EBITDA, while VNET trades at 12x. Both are rated Buy. The difference is not a mistake. It reflects fundamentally different growth profiles, business models, and risk exposures.

Range Intelligent is the fastest-growing operator in the coverage universe, with projected revenue and EBITDA CAGRs of 40% and 47% respectively from 2025 to 2028. Its total capacity pipeline of 6 GW, including 5.25 GW under construction or in reserve, gives it a multiyear runway for growth that few competitors can match. The premium valuation is justified by the growth trajectory, but it also leaves little room for execution missteps. If Range fails to convert its capacity pipeline into utilized revenue at the expected pace, the multiple compression could be severe.

VNET, by contrast, is undergoing a transformation from a traditional retail IDC operator to a wholesale-focused player. Its growth is less explosive than Range’s, but its valuation is also less demanding. The report sees wholesale IDC contributing 35% of VNET’s revenue in 2025 and growing at 37-38% CAGRs through 2028. If VNET can execute on this transition, the upward re-rating potential is significant. But the near-term overhang from shareholder selling and financing needs introduces uncertainty that the current valuation may not fully discount.

The Sell rating on Sinnet Tech and the Neutral rating on Athub are equally instructive. Sinnet’s revenue is projected to grow at just 7% CAGR through 2028, with no capacity expansion to speak of. Athub has zero capacity under construction or in the pipeline, making it a static asset in a rapidly growing market. The market is already pricing in this stagnation, but the report suggests that the downside risk remains, particularly if pricing pressure accelerates.

What the report does not fully address is how these companies will finance their growth. The capex requirements for building AI-grade data centers are enormous, and not all operators have equal access to capital. Range Intelligent’s diverse, low-cost financing capabilities are cited as a competitive advantage, but the details of its financing strategy are not fully disclosed. Investors need to ask: how much equity dilution is baked into these growth projections, and what happens if debt markets tighten?

## An Investor Decision Framework: Three Questions to Separate the Leaders from the Laggards

Based on the report’s analysis, investors evaluating China’s data center operators should focus on three strategic questions that cut across all the financial metrics.

First, what is the operator’s capacity pipeline in the western AI training hUBS? The cost advantages of Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, and Gansu are not marginal; they are structural. Operators with significant capacity in these regions will have lower power costs, better cooling efficiency, and stronger relationships with hyperscalers who are building their training clusters there. Operators that are concentrated in the eastern hUBS will face higher costs and increasing competition for scarce power allocations.

Second, does the operator have a credible GPUaaS strategy? The economics of GPUaaS are attractive today, but they require deep technical expertise, strong supply chain relationships for GPU procurement, and the ability to manage rapid technology obsolescence. Operators that treat GPUaaS as a side business rather than a core competency will struggle to compete with hyperscalers’ internal offerings. Those that can differentiate their GPUaaS on service quality, security, and contract flexibility will capture a disproportionate share of the market.

Third, how does the operator plan to finance its growth without destroying shareholder value? The capital intensity of AI infrastructure means that even high-ROI projects require significant upfront investment. Operators that rely on equity issuance to fund growth will dilute existing shareholders, while those that can access low-cost debt or structured financing will preserve per-share value. The report’s data on leverage ratios and EBITDA growth provides a starting point, but investors need to dig deeper into the financing structures and covenant terms.

These three questions do not yield a simple scoring system, but they do provide a framework for distinguishing between operators that are building durable competitive advantages and those that are simply riding the AI wave.

## The Data Points to a Clear Thesis, But Key Unanswered Questions Remain

The report makes a compelling case that China’s data center market is entering a multiyear growth phase driven by AI infrastructure spending. The capacity projections, pricing trends, and geographic shifts all point in the same direction. But the report also leaves several important questions unanswered.

The most significant is the sustainability of GPUaaS pricing. The 30% price increase in the US and the sharp rises in China are encouraging, but they are also a function of supply constraints. As more GPU capacity comes online, pricing could normalize or even decline. The report’s unit economics analysis assumes a favorable pricing environment, but it does not stress-test what happens if GPUaaS pricing falls by 20% or 30% from current levels. The EBITDA margins would compress significantly, and the return on invested capital for new GPU deployments would become marginal.

A second unanswered question is the impact of technology shifts on demand. The report assumes that AI training workloads will continue to grow at a rapid pace, but the emergence of more efficient model architectures, specialized AI chips, or new training paradigms could reduce the demand for generic GPU clusters. The report’s demand projections are based on current trends, but the AI landscape is evolving quickly, and the risk of technological disruption is real.

Finally, the report does not fully address the regulatory and geopolitical risks that are unique to China’s data center market. Access to advanced GPUs is already constrained by US export controls, and further restrictions could limit the ability of Chinese operators to build competitive GPUaaS offerings. The report mentions this risk in the context of individual stock price targets, but it does not provide a systematic assessment of how the market would adjust if chip availability deteriorates further.

These unanswered questions do not invalidate the report’s thesis, but they do mean that investors need to monitor the market with a high degree of vigilance. The opportunity is real, but so are the risks.

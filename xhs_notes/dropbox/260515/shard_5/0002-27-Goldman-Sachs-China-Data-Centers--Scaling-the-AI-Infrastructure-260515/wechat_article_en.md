# AI Infrastructure in China Is Entering a Capacity Race That Will Separate Winners from Survivors

The conventional narrative about China's data center industry—that it is a steady, utility-like business driven by cloud migration—is no longer sufficient. A structural break is underway. China's hyperscalers are pivoting from cost optimization to AI infrastructure buildout at a pace that has no precedent in the domestic market. The data is stark: the five largest Chinese hyperscalers—ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, and Kingsoft—collectively spent approximately US$37 billion on capital expenditures in 2024, and that figure is projected to nearly triple to US$94 billion by 2028. This is not incremental growth; it is a step-change in capital allocation intensity. For investors and operators in the data center space, the implications are profound. The question is no longer whether demand will materialize, but which operators have the capacity, the capital, and the strategic positioning to capture it.

The report from a global investment bank makes a compelling case that the Chinese data center market is at an inflection point. Demand is forecast to grow at a 20% compound annual rate from 2025 through 2028, driven almost entirely by AI workloads. Yet the live capacity supply is expected to grow at a slower pace, pushing utilization rates from roughly 40% today toward 45% by 2028. This supply-demand dynamic should, in theory, support pricing power. But the reality is more nuanced. The market is fragmenting into two distinct tiers: operators that can build high-density AI infrastructure at scale, and those that cannot. The former will command premium pricing and long-term contracts; the latter will face margin compression and obsolescence. This is the central strategic tension that every participant in this ecosystem must navigate.

The report identifies Range Intelligent as the standout operator, with a Buy rating and a 12-month price target implying 15% upside. But the thesis is not simply about picking a winner. It is about understanding a structural shift in how data center value is created. The old model—leasing space, power, and cooling to enterprise tenants—is being supplanted by a new model: GPU-as-a-Service (GPUaaS), where operators package compute capacity with managed services and charge by the hour. This shift has already driven a 36% price increase for GPU rental in the US since December 2025, and similar trends are emerging in China. The operators that can execute on GPUaaS will not just be landlords; they will be infrastructure partners to the AI economy. Those that cannot will be left with stranded assets.

## The Capacity Pipeline Is the Single Most Important Leading Indicator of Future Market Share

In a market where demand is growing at 20% CAGR, the operator with the largest construction pipeline wins. This is not a speculative claim; it is a structural reality. The report shows that Range Intelligent has 0.7 GW of live capacity and 5.5 GW under construction or in pipeline—a ratio of nearly 8:1. GDS Holdings, the incumbent leader, has 1.5 GW live and 3.9 GW in pipeline. VNET Group has 1.1 GW live and 1.3 GW in pipeline. The gap between Range and its peers is not just about scale; it is about strategic intent. Range is building for a future where AI workloads dominate, and it is doing so in the geographic clusters that matter most.

The geographic dimension is critical. The report maps China's major data center hUBS and computing clusters, and the pattern is clear: the majority of intelligent computing power—79% as of mid-2025—is concentrated in just eight western and northern clusters. These regions offer lower electricity prices (as low as RMB 0.35 per kWh in Guizhou and Inner Mongolia), cooler average temperatures, and proximity to renewable energy sources. Operators that have secured land, power quotas, and construction permits in these clusters have a multi-year advantage. Range Intelligent's pipeline is heavily weighted toward the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei hub, the Yangtze River Delta, and the Greater Bay Area, but it also has exposure to emerging clusters in Chengdu-Chongqing and Gansu. This geographic diversification reduces single-region risk and positions the company to serve both latency-sensitive and cost-sensitive workloads.

The so-what for investors is straightforward: in a capacity-constrained market, the operator with the largest and most strategically located pipeline will capture disproportionate market share. The report's market share projections confirm this. Range's share of utilized capacity is expected to rise from 3.5% in 2024 to 4.6% by 2028. GDS, VNET, and Range are forecast to be the three biggest gainers over the next three years. Operators like Athub and Sinnet, which have limited or no pipeline growth, are likely to see their market share erode. This is not a judgment on their operational quality; it is a reflection of the structural reality that AI infrastructure buildout requires massive upfront capital and a multi-year development horizon.

## GPUaaS Is Not Just a Revenue Diversifier; It Is a Margin Multiplier That Redefines the Business Model

The traditional data center business model is a low-margin, capital-intensive utility. EBITDA margins for colocation services typically range from 40% to 50%, with depreciation consuming a significant portion of revenue. GPUaaS changes this equation fundamentally. The report provides a unit economics breakdown that is instructive: for every 100 units of GPUaaS revenue, power costs account for 10, labor and maintenance for 3, and colocation for 7, leaving an EBITDA margin of 80%. Depreciation eats 72% of revenue, resulting in an EBIT margin of 8%. On the surface, this looks like a thin profit. But the key insight is that GPUaaS revenue is growing rapidly, and the incremental margin on each additional GPU deployed is extremely high once the infrastructure is in place.

The report notes that GPUaaS pricing in the US has rebounded sharply, rising 36% from December 2025 to March 2026. In China, H100 and H200 rental contract prices have also increased, with H100 rising from RMB 57 per hour in February 2026 to RMB 70 in April 2026. This pricing power is driven by supply constraints—access to advanced GPUs remains limited due to export controls and domestic production bottlenecks. Operators that can secure GPU supply and package it as a service are capturing scarcity rents. The report identifies four key drivers of GPUaaS adoption: access to advanced GPUs, capex and cost efficiency for customers, time-to-market and scalability, and the competitive dynamics of the AI ecosystem.

The strategic implication is that GPUaaS creates a virtuous cycle. Operators that offer GPUaaS attract higher-value customers, which drives utilization rates up, which improves unit economics, which generates cash flow for further capacity expansion. This is exactly the dynamic that Range Intelligent is positioned to exploit. The report forecasts that Range's revenue will grow at a 40% CAGR from 2025 to 2028, with EBITDA growing at 47% and net profit at 26%. These are not typical data center growth rates; they are characteristic of a platform business that is capturing value from the AI supply chain. The risk, of course, is that GPU pricing eventually normalizes as supply catches up with demand. But for the next two to three years, the window of opportunity is open.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered the Question of How Chinese Operators Will Fund This Buildout Without Stretching Balance Sheets

The report is bullish on capacity expansion, but it is notably less explicit about the financing mechanics. Range Intelligent's pipeline of 5.5 GW under construction or in reserve implies a capital requirement that runs into the tens of billions of renminbi. The report mentions that Range has "diverse, low-cost financing capabilities," but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of debt versus equity funding, nor does it model the impact of rising interest rates or tightening credit conditions. This is a meaningful gap for investors.

The comparison with US hyperscalers is instructive. US-based operators like Amazon, Microsoft, and Google are funding their AI infrastructure buildout through operating cash flow and balance sheet capacity. Their capex-to-cash-flow ratios are high—reaching 73% in 2025—but they have the revenue scale and credit ratings to support it. Chinese operators, by contrast, operate in a higher-cost-of-capital environment with less predictable cash flows. The report shows that Range's EV/EBITDA multiple for 2026E is 29x, compared to 12x for GDS and 11x for VNET. This premium reflects growth expectations, but it also implies that Range is priced for perfection. Any delay in capacity delivery, regulatory setback, or financing disruption could trigger a sharp re-rating.

The report also does not fully address the risk of overcapacity. If every operator builds aggressively, the utilization rate improvement from 40% to 45% by 2028 could prove optimistic. The report's own data shows that live capacity is growing at a 15% CAGR, while demand is growing at 20%. That gap should support utilization, but it assumes that demand growth does not decelerate. If AI model training efficiency improves faster than expected, or if export controls tighten further, demand could soften. The report acknowledges these risks in its price target methodology but does not stress-test them in the base case. For a serious business audience, this is the analytical frontier: understanding the sensitivity of operator valuations to changes in capacity utilization, pricing, and financing costs.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Filters to Separate Tier-One Operators from the Rest

The report provides a wealth of data, but its real value is in enabling a structured decision framework. Based on the analysis, investors should evaluate Chinese data center operators through three lenses: capacity pipeline quality, business model evolution, and balance sheet resilience.

First, capacity pipeline quality is not just about total gigawatts under construction. It is about location, power availability, and regulatory approvals. Operators with capacity in the eight western and northern computing clusters—where electricity costs are low and renewable energy is abundant—have a structural cost advantage. Operators with capacity in the eastern hUBS—Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, Yangtze River Delta, Greater Bay Area—have a latency advantage for inference workloads. The optimal operator has both. Range Intelligent and GDS score highly on this dimension. Athub and Sinnet do not.

Second, business model evolution matters because it determines revenue quality. Operators that are transitioning from pure colocation to GPUaaS will see higher revenue per cabinet, longer contract durations, and stronger customer stickiness. The report's unit economics for GPUaaS show that it is a high-margin, high-growth business. Operators that offer a full-stack AI infrastructure solution—including managed GPU services, networking, and storage—will capture more value than those that simply lease space. Range Intelligent is explicitly positioned as a full-stack operator. VNET is moving in that direction with its wholesale IDC pivot. Sinnet and Athub remain largely traditional.

Third, balance sheet resilience is the binding constraint. The buildout of 5.5 GW of capacity requires financing that is both available and affordable. Operators with low leverage, strong cash flow generation, and access to diverse funding sources—including equity, debt, and strategic partnerships—are better positioned to execute. The report's scorecard shows that Athub ranks best on current leverage, but its capacity pipeline is zero. Range Intelligent has strong profitability and leverage scores but is carrying a high valuation multiple that leaves little room for error. GDS and VNET have more moderate multiples but face higher leverage and financing risk. Investors need to weigh growth potential against financial risk, and the optimal trade-off will vary by risk appetite.

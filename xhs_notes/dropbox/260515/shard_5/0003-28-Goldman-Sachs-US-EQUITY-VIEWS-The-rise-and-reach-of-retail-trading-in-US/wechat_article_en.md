# Retail Trading Has Permanently Reshaped US Equity Markets, and Most Institutional Investors Are Still Underestimating Its Structural Impact

The conventional view that retail traders are a marginal, cyclical force in US equities no longer holds. A global investment bank's latest research reveals that retail trading activity has not only surged alongside the recent equity market rally—rising 28 percent since mid-April—but now accounts for roughly 20 percent of total US equity trading volumes, up from 15 percent a decade ago. This is not a pandemic-era anomaly re-emerging. It is a structural shift driven by regulatory changes, technological infrastructure, and the sheer scale of household wealth now held in self-directed brokerage accounts. Retail investors currently hold an estimated $12 trillion in equity assets within self-directed accounts, representing approximately 10 percent of total US corporate equity market value. The replacement of pattern day trader rules with less stringent margin requirements further locks in this higher baseline of activity. For institutional investors, the strategic implication is unambiguous: ignoring the footprint of retail flow is no longer a viable approach to portfolio construction, risk management, or trade execution.

The narrative that retail traders are unsophisticated noise traders who disappear during market stress is contradicted by the data. Retail trading activity has consistently increased during recent equity market drawdowns, including the selloffs in 2020, 2022, and 2025. During the most recent selloff, volumes remained light but accelerated sharply as the market rebounded. This pattern suggests that retail investors are not merely momentum chasers; they are increasingly acting as liquidity providers during periods of institutional withdrawal, whether by design or by default. The structural question is no longer whether retail matters, but how to measure its impact and adapt to its presence.

## Retail Investors Now Hold $12 Trillion in Self-Directed Accounts, Yet Their Trading Behavior Is Far More Active Than Their Asset Share Suggests

The scale of retail equity ownership is often underestimated because it is dispersed across multiple account types and brokerages. The report estimates that equity assets in self-directed brokerage accounts total $12 trillion, which is 18 percent of all household equity assets and roughly 10 percent of the total US corporate equity market. However, households collectively own the vast majority of the $111 trillion US equity market when including retirement accounts, mutual funds, and ETFs. The top 10 percent of households own 87 percent of household equity assets, while the bottom 50 percent own just 1 percent.

What makes retail traders strategically significant is not their asset share but their activity share. Retail accounts for roughly 20 percent of trading volumes, which is double their proportional asset ownership. This high turnover creates a structural footprint that institutional investors cannot ignore. The bottom 50 percent of households, despite owning minimal equity assets, have historically made portfolio adjustments amounting to 3 percent of their assets per quarter—nearly three times the adjustment rate of the top 10 percent. This means that even small shifts in retail sentiment can generate disproportionate trading volume, particularly in stocks with high retail concentration.

The concentration of assets among a few large brokers further amplifies this effect. Charles Schwab, Fidelity, and Vanguard account for roughly 80 percent of retail self-directed assets, while Robinhood holds just 2 percent. Yet Robinhood's daily average trades per account are among the highest in the industry, and Interactive Brokers exhibits roughly four times the activity per account compared to other brokers. The infrastructure of retail trading is centralized, but the behavior is fragmented and volatile. This creates both opportunities and risks for institutional investors who can monitor aggregate retail flow signals.

## The Institutional Blind Spot: Most Retail Order Flow Is Internalized by Wholesalers, Making It Invisible to Traditional Exchanges

The most critical structural feature of retail trading is not its size but its plumbing. Over 87 percent of retail order flow is routed to specialized market makers known as wholesalers. A recent academic analysis cited in the report shows that wholesalers execute 84 percent of retail orders against their own inventories through a practice called internalization. Only a small fraction of retail orders reaches traditional exchanges, dark pools, or other market makers. This means that institutional investors typically interact with only a fraction of retail trading activity under normal market conditions.

The strategic implication is profound. Retail flow is largely invisible to institutional order books, which means it does not directly affect the price discovery process in the same way as institutional flow. However, this invisibility is not absolute. During periods of high volatility, when retail order flow becomes large and correlated, wholesalers face greater difficulty hedging their internalized positions. A 2021 SEC report noted that on-exchange volumes spike during volatility events partly because wholesalers offload risk to exchanges. This means that retail flow can suddenly become visible and impactful precisely when markets are most fragile.

For institutional investors, this creates a two-regime world. In calm markets, retail flow is absorbed by wholesalers and has minimal direct impact on institutional execution quality. In volatile markets, retail flow can flood exchanges, amplifying price moves and creating dislocations that institutional algorithms are not designed to handle. The report's data showing that retail volumes in index-linked ETFs rose during the selloffs in 2020, 2022, and 2025 confirms that retail participation is counter-cyclical in terms of volume, even if not in terms of direction. Institutional investors who do not account for this regime shift risk being caught on the wrong side of retail-driven volatility.

## Retail Traders Are Using Leverage at Record Levels, but the Composition of That Leverage Has Shifted Away from Traditional Margin Debt

Margin debt across all FINRA member firms has risen to $1.3 trillion, representing 52 percent of gross customer balances—the highest level on record and 6 percentage points above the previous peak in 1998. However, the report makes an important distinction: much of this increase is likely driven by institutional investors, not retail. Margin balances at Interactive Brokers, Robinhood, and Charles Schwab totaled $230 billion at the end of March, equating to 1.8 percent of customer assets. This share is slightly above the 2021 record but has not meaningfully surpassed it, in contrast to the broader FINRA data.

The more significant retail leverage story is the use of leveraged ETFs. The report finds that the retail share of trading volumes in leveraged S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 ETFs has been roughly double the share in unleveraged ETFs tracking the same indices. This suggests that retail investors are increasingly using structured products to amplify exposure rather than traditional margin borrowing. The strategic implication is that retail leverage is now embedded in ETF flows, which means it affects the underlying index futures and cash markets through the hedging activities of ETF issuers.

For institutional investors, this creates a new layer of complexity. Retail-driven leveraged ETF flows can amplify intraday momentum and increase the risk of sharp reversals during deleveraging events. The report's finding that retail trading activity has recently tilted toward stocks with small market caps, volatile returns, high valuations, and high short interest suggests that retail leverage is concentrated in the most fragile corners of the market. This is not merely a risk for retail traders themselves; it is a systemic risk for any institution that holds positions in the same names or sectors.

## Stocks with High Retail Participation Exhibit Distinct Price Behavior, Including Greater Downside Sensitivity to Earnings Misses

The report provides evidence that retail trading activity affects how stock prices incorporate fundamental information. Stocks with elevated retail trading activity carry elevated valuations and exhibit above-average volatility, even after controlling for fundamentals like sales growth. More importantly, these stocks have exhibited greater underperformance following earnings misses. This suggests that retail participation amplifies the price impact of negative news, either because retail investors are more prone to panic selling or because the market structure around these stocks is less resilient.

This finding has direct implications for institutional portfolio managers. If a stock with high retail ownership misses earnings, the expected downside is larger than a fundamental model would predict. Conversely, positive earnings surprises may also be amplified, but the asymmetry matters: the downside risk is more pronounced. For hedge funds running long-short strategies, this creates a potential source of alpha if they can identify stocks where retail positioning is stretched and fundamentals are deteriorating.

The report also notes that the retail share of trading volumes in Russell 3000 stocks with the highest short interest was higher in 2025 than in 2021. This is a striking data point because it suggests that retail traders are increasingly willing to trade against institutional short positions, even after the 2021 meme stock episode. The pattern is not a one-off anomaly; it is a persistent feature of the market. For institutional investors, this means that short-selling in high-retail names carries additional risk of retail-driven squeezes, and that the cost of borrowing these stocks may remain elevated.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Feedback Loop Between Retail Sentiment, Social Media, and Market Structure

The report provides rigorous data on retail trading volumes, asset ownership, and leverage, but it leaves several critical questions unresolved. First, the report does not fully address the role of social media and retail communication platforms in coordinating trading behavior. The data shows that retail activity is concentrated in certain stocks and increases during market selloffs, but it does not explain how information and sentiment propagate among retail traders. If retail trading is becoming more correlated due to social media amplification, then the risk of sudden, coordinated moves is higher than the historical data suggests.

Second, the report does not model the feedback loop between retail flow and wholesaler behavior. If wholesalers are increasingly using retail order flow to inform their own proprietary trading, then retail flow may have a more indirect but pervasive impact on market pricing than the internalization statistics suggest. The report notes that wholesalers internalize 84 percent of retail orders, but it does not explore what wholesalers do with the information embedded in those orders. This is a strategic blind spot for institutional investors who treat retail flow as irrelevant to their trading decisions.

Third, the report does not address the potential for regulatory changes that could alter the retail trading infrastructure. The replacement of pattern day trader rules is cited as a factor that increases retail activity, but the report does not speculate on what further regulatory changes might occur, such as changes to payment for order flow or to the wholesaler exemption from exchange registration. These are open questions that could significantly alter the competitive dynamics of retail trading.

## A Decision Framework for Institutional Investors: How to Integrate Retail Flow into Portfolio Construction and Risk Management

Given the structural importance of retail trading, institutional investors need a systematic approach to incorporating retail flow data into their investment process. The following framework is derived from the report's findings and is designed to be actionable.

First, monitor aggregate retail flow as a regime indicator. The report shows that retail volumes rise during market drawdowns and accelerate during rebounds. A sustained increase in retail volumes relative to institutional volumes is a signal that market dynamics may be shifting toward a retail-dominated regime. In such regimes, momentum strategies may perform differently, and the risk of sharp reversals increases. Investors should adjust their position sizing and stop-loss levels accordingly.

Second, identify stocks with high retail ownership and high short interest as a distinct risk category. The report's data shows that these stocks have elevated volatility and greater downside sensitivity to earnings misses. For long-only portfolios, these stocks require tighter risk controls. For hedge funds, they represent both opportunity and danger: shorting them carries squeeze risk, but going long may offer asymmetric upside if retail sentiment turns positive.

Third, incorporate leveraged ETF flows into volatility forecasting. The report finds that retail share of leveraged ETF trading is double that of unleveraged ETFs. This means that leveraged ETF flows are a more concentrated source of retail-driven momentum and reversal risk. Investors should monitor daily flows into leveraged ETFs as a leading indicator of intraday volatility, particularly in the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100.

Fourth, recognize that retail flow is not monolithic. The report shows that retail activity varies significantly across brokerages. Charles Schwab has the highest total daily average trades, while Interactive Brokers has the highest activity per account. Robinhood users may behave differently from Schwab users. Investors who can disaggregate retail flow by broker may gain a more nuanced understanding of retail sentiment.

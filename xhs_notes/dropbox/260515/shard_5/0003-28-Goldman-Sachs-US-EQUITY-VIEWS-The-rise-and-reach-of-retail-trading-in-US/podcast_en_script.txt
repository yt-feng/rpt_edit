EN_A: So the market has been on a roll lately, but there is one group of traders making a lot more noise than others. Who are we talking about?
EN_B: Retail traders. A recent report from a global investment bank shows that retail trading volumes have jumped 28% since mid-April, and their favorite stocks are up 29% in the same period.
EN_A: That is a big move. So what is the big question this report tries to answer?
EN_B: It is really about how big retail traders are in the U.S. equity market. Not just in trading activity, but in terms of assets, leverage, and behavior. It gives you the full picture.
EN_A: So how much money do they actually control?
EN_B: The report estimates retail traders hold about 12 trillion dollars in self-directed brokerage accounts. That is roughly 10% of total U.S. corporate equity market value. But here is the interesting part: they account for about 20% of all trading volume.
EN_A: So they trade way more than their asset base suggests. But how does that actually work? Do they trade directly on exchanges?
EN_B: Not really. Most retail orders never hit the public exchanges. Over 87% of retail order flow goes to specialized market makers called wholesalers, and about 84% of those orders are internalized, meaning the wholesaler fills them from their own inventory.
EN_A: So when do retail traders actually move the market?
EN_B: During periods of high volatility. The report notes that retail trading in index-linked ETFs spiked during the selloffs in 2020, 2022, and 2025. And when individual stocks drop sharply, retail tends to pile in.
EN_A: What about leverage? Are they using borrowed money?
EN_B: Yes, margin debt and leveraged ETFs. Margin balances at major brokers are now at 1.8% of customer assets, similar to the 2021 peak. And retail traders are twice as active in leveraged S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 ETFs compared to their non-leveraged versions.
EN_A: That sounds risky. Does the report fully explain the risk?
EN_B: Not entirely. And that is one of the things it leaves open. For example, it shows that stocks with high retail activity tend to have high valuations and high volatility, and they underperform after earnings misses. But the report does not fully explain how retail behavior changes price discovery. Also, while total margin debt is at a record, most of that growth may come from institutions, not retail. So the real risk from retail leverage is still unclear.

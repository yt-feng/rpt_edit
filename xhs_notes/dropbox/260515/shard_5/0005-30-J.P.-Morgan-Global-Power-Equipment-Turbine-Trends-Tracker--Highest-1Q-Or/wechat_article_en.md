# The Gas Turbine Supercycle Is Not a Mirage — It Is a Structural Shift That Investors Are Still Underpricing

The first quarter of 2026 delivered 29 gigawatts of global gas turbine orders, a 39 percent year-over-year increase and the highest first-quarter total on record. This is not a cyclical bump driven by catch-up spending or one-off megaprojects. It is the fourth-largest quarter of any type in history, and it follows a fourth quarter of 2025 that itself was a record. The pattern is now unmistakable: the global gas turbine market has entered a structural supercycle, and the forces behind it — data center electrification, reshoring of manufacturing, and the limitations of intermittent renewables — are still in their early innings.

Investors who treat this as a transient boom risk missing the most consequential re-rating of power generation equipment in decades. The data from a major global investment bank's latest quarterly tracker shows that order volumes, pricing power, and capacity constraints are all moving in directions that reward strategic positioning and punish hesitation. The question is not whether the cycle is real. The question is which companies, which technologies, and which geographies will capture the disproportionate share of value as the market tightens further.

The report's findings on OEM capacity expansion, project cost inflation, and the emergence of new competitors like Doosan all point to a market that is fundamentally repricing risk and opportunity. But beneath the headline numbers lie second-order implications that most investors have not yet fully incorporated into their models. This article unpacks the key insights, draws out the strategic logic, and identifies the open questions that will define the next phase of the cycle.

## The United States Has Become the Indispensable Market for Gas Turbines, and That Concentration Creates Both Opportunity and Vulnerability

North America accounted for approximately 18.3 gigawatts of orders in the first quarter, with the United States alone contributing roughly 17.2 gigawatts. That is nearly 60 percent of global orders concentrated in a single country. To put this in perspective, the next largest national markets were Oman at roughly 2.0 gigawatts and the UAE at 1.5 gigawatts. The US market is not just leading; it is dominating to a degree that has no modern precedent in the gas turbine industry.

The driving force behind this concentration is the data center buildout. Hyperscalers and colocation providers are signing power purchase agreements at a pace that strains the grid interconnection queue, and gas turbines offer the only scalable, dispatchable, and relatively quick-to-deploy solution for baseload power that can match the 24/7 demand profile of AI training and cloud computing. The report notes a record 3.8-gigawatt order booked by Doosan, including a 2.7-gigawatt data center order in the US. That single order is larger than the entire quarterly order volume of many established OEMs.

This geographic concentration carries a strategic implication that is easy to overlook: the gas turbine supply chain, which historically served a more diversified global market, is now increasingly optimized for US demand. OEMs that have strong US market share — particularly GE Vernova, which has captured roughly 45 percent of the North American market since 2023 — are building capacity and service networks that are tailored to this demand profile. Conversely, OEMs that are heavily exposed to other regions may find themselves at a structural disadvantage if US demand continues to outpace the rest of the world.

The vulnerability is equally important. A policy shift in the United States — whether related to tax incentives, grid interconnection rules, or environmental permitting — could disproportionately impact the global order book. Investors need to assess whether the current concentration is a source of momentum or a concentration of risk. The answer likely depends on the durability of data center demand, which itself hinges on the pace of AI infrastructure investment. That question is not settled.

## OEM Capacity Expansions Are Real but Insufficient, and the Gap Between Orders and Deliverables Is Widening

The report estimates that global gas turbine OEM capacity will reach roughly 85 gigawatts by 2028, up from current levels. GE Vernova has announced expansions targeting approximately 26 gigawatts of total capacity by 2028, including aeroderivative units. Siemens Energy has guided toward more than 30 gigawatts in combined-cycle terms by fiscal 2028-2030. Mitsubishi Heavy Industries has signaled potential capacity increases of 50 percent or more from its baseline.

These numbers sound impressive, but they must be measured against the order trajectory. If first-quarter 2026 orders annualize to roughly 116 gigawatts — and that is a conservative extrapolation given that the fourth quarter of 2025 alone was 34 gigawatts — then even 85 gigawatts of capacity by 2028 would be insufficient to meet demand. The gap is not hypothetical. It is already visible in the shipment data, where orders placed today are being scheduled for delivery in 2028, 2029, and even 2030.

The strategic implication is that OEMs are not merely competing on technology or price. They are competing on delivery slots. Customers who want gas turbines in the near term are facing allocation constraints and rising premiums. This dynamic is already feeding into project costs, which the report tracks in detail. Median combined-cycle capital expenditure costs for 2031 commercial operation dates have risen roughly 200 percent compared to 2025. For simple-cycle plants, 2028 costs are up about 50 percent. The report's channel checks suggest that some combined-cycle projects are now pushing toward $2,800 to $3,000 per kilowatt, with certain regions exceeding $3,000.

This cost inflation is not a temporary phenomenon. It reflects genuine scarcity in manufacturing capacity, skilled labor, and critical components. It also creates a feedback loop: higher project costs make it harder for developers to achieve acceptable returns, which could eventually slow order growth. But for now, the demand pull from data centers and electrification is strong enough to absorb higher costs. The question is how long that elasticity lasts.

## The Competitive Landscape Is Shifting in Ways That Favor Specialists and Disruptors Over Incumbents

The Big Three OEMs — GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries — accounted for roughly 77 percent of first-quarter orders. But the remaining 23 percent, while smaller in aggregate, tells a more interesting story. Baker Hughes booked a 1.1-gigawatt data center order using its Nova LT small industrial turbines, with Quanta Services as the named customer and EPC contractor. Doosan, a relative newcomer to the US market, booked a record 3.8 gigawatts, including the largest data center order in its history.

These developments suggest that the market is fragmenting in ways that benefit technology specialists and regional players. Baker Hughes is not competing head-on with GE Vernova in the heavy-duty frame market; it is winning orders for smaller, aeroderivative-style turbines that are well-suited to data center applications requiring modularity and speed. Doosan is leveraging its price competitiveness and willingness to commit to delivery timelines that incumbents may be unable to match given their own capacity constraints.

The report's market share data since 2023 shows that GE Vernova leads with 32 percent, followed by Siemens Energy at 29 percent and Mitsubishi at 25 percent. But these shares are not static. Siemens Energy has been particularly strong in the Middle East and Europe, while Mitsubishi dominates Asia. The emergence of Doosan and the growth of Baker Hughes suggest that the oligopoly is becoming less stable, especially in the data center segment where procurement decisions are driven by speed and reliability rather than long-standing OEM relationships.

For investors, the key question is whether the disruptors can scale. Doosan's record quarter is impressive, but it follows a cumulative 5.2 gigawatts since 2020. That is a small fraction of the incumbents' installed base. The risk is that these new entrants become capacity-constrained themselves, or that they struggle with service and maintenance at scale. The opportunity is that they capture a disproportionate share of the incremental demand growth, particularly if incumbents are forced to ration their delivery slots.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Data Center Demand and the Risk of a Two-Tier Market

The report provides granular data on orders, capacity, and pricing, but it does not — and cannot — resolve the most critical uncertainty facing the gas turbine market: how long will data center demand sustain its current growth trajectory? The report's data shows that orders are being placed for commercial operation dates as far out as 2030 and 2031. Those decisions are being made today based on assumptions about AI adoption, electricity prices, and regulatory frameworks that may prove optimistic.

The report also does not fully address the risk of a two-tier market emerging, where customers with the strongest credit profiles and most urgent needs secure delivery slots at premium prices, while smaller developers and less creditworthy off-takers are pushed to the back of the queue or priced out entirely. This dynamic could concentrate orders among a narrower set of end-users, reducing the breadth of demand even as total volumes remain high.

Furthermore, the report's capacity estimates are based on announced expansion plans, but those plans carry execution risk. Factory expansions require permits, equipment, and skilled labor that are themselves in short supply. If OEMs fail to deliver on their capacity targets, the supply-demand imbalance could become even more acute, driving further price increases and potentially triggering project cancellations.

These open questions do not invalidate the supercycle thesis. They simply define the range of outcomes that investors need to stress-test. The bull case assumes that data center demand remains robust, that OEMs execute on capacity expansions, and that higher costs are absorbed by end-users. The bear case assumes that AI investment peaks, that capacity additions fall short, and that project economics deteriorate to the point of cancellation. The truth is likely somewhere in between, but the report's data suggests that the market is currently pricing in a scenario closer to the bull case than the bear case.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Map Your Exposure Along Three Axes

The report's findings can be synthesized into a decision framework that helps investors identify where value is being created and where it is at risk. The framework has three axes: technology tier, geographic exposure, and project timeline.

The first axis is technology tier. Advanced class turbines, particularly H-class and J-class, represented roughly 12.4 gigawatts of first-quarter orders, or about 42 percent of the total. These are the high-margin, high-complexity machines that drive OEM profitability. Investors should favor companies with strong positions in this tier, as it is less susceptible to commoditization and more likely to benefit from pricing power. Conversely, exposure to smaller aeroderivative turbines is growing rapidly due to data center demand, but this segment may face more competition from new entrants like Baker Hughes and Doosan.

The second axis is geographic exposure. The US market is the primary growth engine, but it also carries regulatory and policy risk. Companies with diversified geographic footprints — particularly in the Middle East, where Siemens Energy has a dominant position, and Asia, where Mitsubishi leads — may offer a hedge against US-specific shocks. However, the report's data shows that non-US markets are growing more slowly, so diversification comes at the cost of near-term growth.

The third axis is project timeline. Orders with commercial operation dates in 2028 and beyond carry more execution risk and more uncertainty about end-user demand. Companies that are booking orders with near-term delivery dates — 2026 and 2027 — have greater visibility into revenue and earnings. The report's shipment data shows that 2026 and 2027 are heavily booked for all major OEMs, suggesting that near-term earnings are relatively secure. The risk lies in the outer years, where capacity constraints and demand sustainability are less certain.

Investors should use this framework to assess their current positions and identify gaps. For example, a portfolio that is overweight US-exposed advanced turbine OEMs may be well-positioned for near-term growth but vulnerable to a policy shift or a demand slowdown. Adding exposure to a Middle East-focused player or a small-turbine specialist could improve the risk-return profile.

## The Supercycle Is Real, But Its Full Implications Are Still Unfolding

The first-quarter 2026 gas turbine order data is a powerful confirmation that the market is in the early stages of a multi-year upcycle. The forces driving demand — data center electrification, reshoring, and the limits of renewable intermittency — are structural, not cyclical. The supply side is tightening, pricing is rising, and new entrants are challenging the established order.

But the report also leaves important questions unanswered. Will data center demand sustain its current trajectory? Can OEMs deliver on their capacity expansion plans? How much cost inflation can the market absorb before project economics break? These are not rhetorical questions. They are the analytical frontier for investors who want to move beyond the headline numbers and position for the next phase of the cycle.

# Hong Kong Long-End Rates Are Poised for a Regime Shift: The Supply-Demand Imbalance That Suppressed Yields Is Finally Breaking

For over a year, the long end of the Hong Kong dollar interest rate curve has been an anomaly. While global rates experienced significant volatility, the 10-year Hong Kong swap rate has remained stubbornly low relative to its US counterpart, trading at spreads as wide as -88 basis points in March. Many market participants attributed this to a structural imbalance: a chronic shortage of long-duration HKD bond supply forced bank treasury desks to receive long-tenor swaps to manufacture the duration exposure they could not buy in the cash market. This dynamic, however, is now showing clear signs of reversal. A combination of accelerating government and quasi-government bond issuance, a recovering property market, and a macroeconomic rebound that is beginning to stir loan demand is creating the conditions for a sustained repricing of long-end HK rates. The strategic implication is straightforward but underappreciated: the structural bid that has compressed long-end HK rates is being withdrawn, and investors should be positioned for this shift.

The trade recommendation from a recent global investment bank report is to maintain a pay position in the June 2026 10-year Hong Kong dollar interest rate swap, with a conviction level of 4 out of 5 and a target of 40 basis points in gains from the entry level. This is not a tactical, short-term call. It is a conviction-driven view that a multi-quarter regime change is underway. The report identifies three reinforcing drivers: a pickup in non-financial HKD bond supply, a macro stabilization that is beginning to feed into loan growth, and a valuation gap that remains historically wide. Each of these factors, individually, would be noteworthy. Taken together, they form a powerful and mutually reinforcing case for higher long-end HK rates.

The timing matters as much as the direction. The report was published in mid-May 2026, a moment when the aggregate balance had already experienced some volatility, and the HIBOR fixing had moved higher on the front end due to a hot IPO pipeline and dividend-related flows. But the real opportunity, the report argues, is not in the front end, where these transient factors are well understood and priced. It is in the long end, where a structural shift in supply-demand dynamics is only just beginning to be recognized by the market. The key question for investors is not whether HK rates will rise, but how far and how fast the repricing will occur, and which triggers will accelerate it.

## The Long-Awaited Supply of Long-Duration HKD Bonds Has Finally Arrived, and It Is Changing the Structural Dynamics of the Market

The most critical driver of the long-end HK rate regime shift is the sudden and significant increase in non-financial HKD bond supply with tenors of 10 years or longer. For much of the period since early 2024, long-end HK rates outperformed their US counterparts largely because there was simply not enough long-duration paper available. Bank treasury desks, which require duration exposure for asset-liability management and regulatory purposes, were forced to receive long-tenor interest rate swaps to manufacture the synthetic duration they could not obtain in the cash bond market. This created a persistent structural bid that compressed HK swap rates relative to US swaps, even as global rates moved higher.

That supply-demand imbalance has now materially improved. Since April 2026, a number of prominent local ex-financial entities have issued HKD bonds with tenors of 10 years or longer. The Hong Kong Airport Authority, MTR Corporation, the West Kowloon Cultural District Authority, and the Hong Kong government itself have all come to market with long-dated issuance. This is not a one-off event. The Hong Kong government has signaled that these issuances are part of a broader, more regular funding program for major infrastructure projects, most notably the Northern Metropolis development, which is expected to continue into the mid-2030s. The implication is clear: the supply of long-duration HKD bonds is not a temporary phenomenon but the beginning of a structural shift.

The "so what" for investors is that the synthetic demand for duration via swaps is likely to decline as the cash market finally provides the paper that bank treasuries need. As these institutions are able to purchase actual bonds instead of receiving swaps, the structural bid on long-end IRS will fade. This is a classic example of a supply-driven regime change that can persist for quarters or even years. The report's conviction level of 4 out of 5 reflects the analysts' view that this supply dynamic is not yet fully priced into the market. The question that remains unanswered is whether the pace of future issuance will be sufficient to keep the pressure on swap rates, or whether there will be periods of supply digestion that create tactical opportunities to add to pay positions.

## Hong Kong's Macroeconomic Rebound and Residential Property Recovery Are Beginning to Stir Loan Growth, Which Will Further Reduce the Need for Synthetic Duration

The second driver of the long-end HK rate shift is the macroeconomic environment. Hong Kong's Q1 2026 GDP growth came in well above expectations at 5.9% year-on-year, a number that signals a robust post-pandemic recovery. More importantly, residential property prices have rebounded strongly, returning to their highest level since October 2023. This combination of strong growth and rising asset prices is beginning to have the predictable effect on credit demand that the market has been waiting for.

For the past several quarters, subdued loan growth has been a persistent drag on the loan-to-deposit (LTD) ratio, which stood at 72.3% in March, still near its lows. A low LTD ratio means that banks have excess deposits relative to loans, which in turn reduces their need to issue new debt or to manage duration aggressively. When loan growth is weak, bank treasury desks have fewer natural buyers of duration, and the synthetic demand via swaps becomes more dominant. The recovery in property prices and economic activity, however, is expected to gradually spill over into higher mortgage lending and overall loan growth. As the LTD ratio begins to normalize, the structural need for banks to receive swaps will diminish.

The report is careful to note that this spillover has not yet materialized in the data. The March LTD ratio remains low, and there is a lag between macro recovery and loan growth. This uncertainty is precisely what creates the opportunity. If loan growth does rebound as expected, the 10-year HK-US spread, currently at -73 basis points, could narrow significantly toward the -50 basis point level or even tighter. If loan growth remains subdued, the spread may oscillate around -80 basis points. The asymmetry of outcomes favors the pay side: the potential for spread narrowing is larger than the risk of further widening.

This section of the argument also raises an important second-order question. If loan growth does rebound strongly, it will not only reduce the need for synthetic duration but also increase the natural demand for funding from banks. This could create a self-reinforcing cycle where higher rates attract more issuance, which in turn pushes rates higher. The report does not fully explore this feedback loop, but it is a logical extension of the thesis. Investors should consider whether the current positioning adequately accounts for the possibility of a more aggressive repricing than the 40-basis-point target implies.

## The Valuation Gap Between Long-End HK and US Rates Remains Historically Wide, Offering a Favorable Risk-Reward for Paying HK Rates

The third driver is valuation. The current spread between 10-year HK and US IRS stands at -73 basis points, which is well below the historical average. Even though the spread has already rebounded from the -88 basis point low reached in March, it remains at a level that, in the context of the improving supply-demand dynamics, appears unsustainable. The report argues that the spread can easily narrow to -50 basis points or narrower if loan growth rebounds, and that even in a stagnant loan growth scenario, the downside from current levels is limited.

This is a classic relative value argument, but it is grounded in a structural analysis rather than simple mean reversion. The historical average spread reflects a period when the supply-demand balance was different. The question is whether the new equilibrium spread should be narrower than the historical average, given the structural changes underway. The report suggests that it should be, because the factors that suppressed HK rates are now reversing. The 40-basis-point target for the trade is therefore not an arbitrary number but a reasonable estimate of the potential repricing based on the convergence toward a new equilibrium.

One critical nuance that the report touches on but does not fully resolve is the relationship between front-end and long-end HK rates. The front end has already been bid up by transient factors: a hot IPO pipeline, outright USD/HKD buying, and dividend payments by Chinese companies to H-share investors. These factors have pushed 3-month HIBOR higher since the start of April. The report argues that these front-end dynamics are better understood and priced, and that the real opportunity lies in the long end. This is a defensible position, but it also implies that if front-end rates were to reverse sharply, the entire curve could shift, potentially affecting the long-end trade.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Global Rate Dynamics and Hong Kong's Idiosyncratic Factors

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several important questions unresolved. The most significant is the interaction between global rate dynamics and Hong Kong's idiosyncratic factors. The report focuses almost exclusively on Hong Kong-specific supply-demand dynamics, but the HK-US spread does not exist in a vacuum. If US long-end rates were to decline sharply due to a global risk-off event or a shift in Federal Reserve policy, the absolute level of HK rates might not rise as much as the spread narrowing would imply. The trade is structured as a pay position in HK IRS versus a partial hedge in US IRS, but the effectiveness of this hedge depends on the correlation between the two markets, which can change during periods of stress.

Another unresolved question is the pace of future bond issuance. The report cites recent issuances by the Airport Authority, MTR, and the government, but it does not provide a detailed forecast of the issuance calendar. If the pace of issuance slows or if the tenors shorten, the supply-demand dynamic could shift back in favor of lower rates. The report's conviction level of 4 out of 5 suggests a high degree of confidence, but investors should be aware that the timing of the repricing is uncertain and that the trade may require patience.

Finally, the report does not fully explore the implications of the property market recovery for credit risk. If property prices continue to rise, it could lead to a virtuous cycle of higher collateral values, more lending, and stronger economic growth. But it could also lead to concerns about overheating and potential regulatory tightening. The report's optimistic view on loan growth is based on a benign scenario, but the risks are worth monitoring.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Assess the Long-End HK Rate Trade

For investors considering this trade, a structured decision framework can help clarify the key variables and scenarios. The framework should address three dimensions: conviction, timing, and sizing.

First, conviction depends on the trajectory of bond supply. If the Hong Kong government and quasi-government entities continue to issue long-dated bonds at the current pace, the structural bid on swaps will continue to fade. Investors should monitor the issuance calendar closely and increase conviction if the pace accelerates. Conversely, if issuance slows or if the market shows signs of supply indigestion, conviction should be reduced.

Second, timing depends on the path of loan growth. The report identifies the LTD ratio as the key metric to watch. If the ratio begins to rise from the current 72.3% level, it will confirm that the macro recovery is feeding through to credit demand. This would likely accelerate the repricing of long-end rates. Investors should set specific triggers for adding to or reducing positions based on monthly loan data.

Third, sizing should reflect the asymmetric risk-reward. The report's target of 40 basis points of gains from entry is achievable under the base case, but the downside risk is limited if loan growth remains subdued. The trade is therefore well-suited for a core position with a conviction level of 4 out of 5, meaning that investors should be positioned at two-thirds of their desired size. This allows room to add if the thesis is confirmed by incoming data.

The framework also suggests that investors should consider hedging tail risks. A sharp global risk-off event that drives US rates lower could offset the benefits of spread narrowing. A partial hedge in US rates, as the report suggests at a 2/3 ratio, is prudent but not sufficient. Investors should also consider the possibility of a liquidity shock in HKD markets, which could cause spreads to widen temporarily before narrowing again.

## The Full Picture: Why This Trade Deserves a Place in a Strategic Portfolio

The long-end HK rate trade is not a speculative punt. It is a structurally grounded position that benefits from a confluence of factors that are all moving in the same direction. The supply of long-duration bonds is increasing, the macro environment is improving, loan growth is poised to rebound, and valuations remain historically attractive. Each of these factors, on its own, would be a reason to pay HK rates. Together, they form a powerful thesis that is likely to play out over the coming months.

The report's conviction level of 4 out of 5 is appropriate for a position that is well-researched and supported by multiple independent drivers. The 40-basis-point target is achievable, and the risk-reward is favorable. The key risk is not that the thesis is wrong, but that the timing is delayed. Investors with a medium-term horizon should be comfortable holding this position through periods of short-term noise.

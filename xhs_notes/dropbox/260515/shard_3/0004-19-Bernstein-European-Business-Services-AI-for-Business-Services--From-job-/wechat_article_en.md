# AI Will Not Destroy Business Services — It Will Reshape the Margin Structure, And That Is Where the Investment Opportunity Lies

The dominant narrative surrounding artificial intelligence and business services has been one of existential threat. Stock prices for staffing firms, data processors, and catering operators have been punished as investors price in a future where automation eliminates jobs, compresses fees, and destroys the economic moats of incumbent service providers. This fear, while understandable, is analytically incomplete. A careful reading of the evidence suggests that the market is conflating disruption with destruction, and in doing so, is mispricing a significant sUBSet of the sector.

The core argument of this analysis is straightforward: AI is a productivity shock, not a demand shock for business services. The distinction matters profoundly for portfolio construction. A demand shock reduces the total addressable market. A productivity shock changes how value is created and captured within that market. History shows that productivity shocks tend to reallocate labor and expand output rather than collapse employment. The critical question for investors is not whether jobs will be lost — some will — but whether the companies that survive will be more profitable, more resilient, and more valuable on a per-share basis.

The market has already made its initial judgment. Over the past sixteen months, business services stocks have broadly underperformed. Teleperformance has lost roughly a quarter of its value. Experian and Adecco have each declined by approximately one-fifth. Even companies with limited direct AI exposure, such as Sodexo, have been caught in the downdraft. This broad-based selloff reflects a sentiment that AI represents a structural headwind for the entire category. Yet the underlying exposures are far from uniform. Some business services firms are genuinely at risk of commoditization. Others are positioned to benefit from the very efficiency gains that the market fears.

The analytical task is to separate these outcomes. This requires a framework that assesses AI not as a single force but as a set of distinct mechanisms — automation, augmentation, and market creation — each of which affects different business models in different ways. The report from which this analysis draws provides the raw material for such a framework. What follows is an interpretation of that material, extended into strategic implications and open questions that the market has yet to fully resolve.

## The Market Is Correct to Worry About Automation, But Wrong to Assume It Is a Zero-Sum Game

The most immediate concern for business services investors is that generative AI will automate cognitive tasks that form the core of many service offerings. Customer service agents, data processors, back-office administrators, and even some categories of inspectors face the prospect that their functions can be performed more cheaply and more accurately by software. McKinsey's midpoint scenario projects that roughly 30% of current work hours in Europe and the US could be automated by 2030. For a sector that sells labor, this appears catastrophic.

Yet the historical record offers a powerful counter-narrative. The electrification of factories did not eliminate manufacturing employment; it transformed it. The introduction of IT did not destroy office work; it redefined it. The internet did not kill retail; it created e-commerce. In each case, the initial phase of technological adoption was accompanied by widespread fear of job destruction, followed by a longer period of job reallocation and, ultimately, higher employment. The correlation between productivity growth and employment growth in the United States over ten-year rolling periods is essentially 100%. This is not a coincidence. Higher productivity lowers costs, expands output, and creates new categories of demand that did not previously exist.

The same dynamic is likely to play out with AI, but with an important twist. Unlike previous technological shocks that primarily affected blue-collar or routine white-collar work, generative AI targets cognitive tasks that have historically been the preserve of educated professionals. This means the disruption will be felt higher up the income and skill ladder, which is precisely why the market is reacting so negatively. The companies most exposed — staffing firms that place white-collar temps, data companies that process information, and testing and certification firms that rely on human judgment — are facing a challenge that feels more personal to the analysts and portfolio managers who are doing the selling.

But the data from within the sector itself suggests that the fear is outsized relative to the immediate impact. According to the global staffing leader Adecco, only 1.4% of layoffs in its career transition business were attributed directly to AI. A broader 12% of layoffs had some AI component, but the company notes that Citing AI as a reason for termination often serves as a convenient narrative for other stakeholders rather than a genuine driver of the decision. Randstad, another major staffing firm, estimates that AI will drive job losses of 6-7% over the next five to ten years, concentrated in clerical, customer service, and marketing roles. That is not trivial, but it is also not the apocalypse that the stock market is pricing.

The key insight is that AI will not eliminate the need for human intermediation in business services; it will change the nature of that intermediation. Tasks will be automated, but roles will be augmented. The companies that understand this distinction will be the ones that thrive.

## The Most Exposed Sub-Sectors Are Also the Most Likely to See Margin Expansion Through Efficiency Gains

The conventional wisdom holds that companies with high labor intensity and low barriers to entry are the most vulnerable to AI disruption. On the surface, this applies to staffing firms, customer service outsourcers, and data processors. Yet this analysis misses a critical point: these same companies are also the most likely to benefit from AI-driven efficiency gains within their own operations.

Consider the staffing industry. Firms like Adecco and Randstad place hundreds of thousands of temporary workers globally. Their profitability depends on the spread between the wage they pay the worker and the fee they charge the client. If AI can reduce the cost of matching workers to roles, managing compliance, processing payroll, and handling administrative tasks, the margin on each placement can increase. The revenue per placement may decline if automation compresses pricing, but the cost per placement can decline even more. The net effect is not necessarily margin compression; it is margin transformation.

The same logic applies to customer service outsourcing. Teleperformance, the largest player in this space, employs over 400,000 people. The market has punished the stock severely, assuming that AI chatbots will render human agents obsolete. But the reality is more nuanced. Large enterprises with complex customer service needs cannot simply hand over their most sensitive interactions to an off-the-shelf AI model. They need integration, customization, escalation protocols, and human judgment for edge cases. What AI can do is handle the first tier of routine inquiries, reducing the cost per call and allowing human agents to focus on higher-value interactions. Teleperformance can offer this hybrid model to its clients, charging a premium for the AI layer while reducing its own labor costs. The net result could be higher margins on a smaller revenue base.

For data companies like Experian, the threat is different. AI can potentially replicate some of the data aggregation and scoring functions that have historically been the company's core value proposition. If a client can generate its own credit scores using publicly available data and a large language model, why pay Experian? The answer is that data quality, accuracy, and regulatory compliance are not easily replicated. Experian's moat is not just its data; it is the trust that its data is accurate, the legal framework that governs its use, and the integration of that data into client workflows. AI may compress pricing for commoditized data products, but it may also expand the market for premium, AI-augmented analytics. The risk is to low-margin commoditized revenue, not to the franchise as a whole.

The common thread across these sub-sectors is that AI will force a reallocation of revenue from low-value tasks to high-value solutions. Companies that manage this transition successfully will emerge with higher margins and stronger competitive positions. Companies that fail will see their revenue base erode without commensurate cost savings. The dispersion of outcomes is wide, and the market is currently pricing an average outcome that is too pessimistic.

## The Physical Services Sub-Sector — Catering, Pest Control, and Equipment Rental — Offers a Natural Hedge That the Market Is Overlooking

Not all business services are equally exposed to AI. A significant portion of the sector derives its revenue from activities that require physical presence, manual labor, or site-specific operations. These companies — catering operators like Compass and Sodexo, pest control and hygiene firms like Rentokil, and equipment rental companies like Sunbelt — have a natural immunity to the cognitive automation threat.

This is not to say they are unaffected. AI can optimize route planning for pest control technicians, reduce food waste in canteens through demand forecasting, and improve utilization rates for rental equipment. These are real efficiency gains that can improve margins. But the fundamental demand driver for these services is not cognitive labor; it is physical presence. A catering company cannot serve a meal through a chatbot. A pest control company cannot spray a warehouse through a software update. An equipment rental company cannot deliver a generator through the cloud.

The risk for these companies is not that AI replaces their workers, but that AI reduces the number of workers in their clients' offices, which in turn reduces demand for on-site catering and facility services. This is a second-order effect, and it is worth quantifying. The analysis in the underlying report estimates that for catering operators, the maximum negative impact on revenue from AI-driven headcount reduction is around 1-2%. This is partially offset by cost efficiencies from AI implementation within the catering companies themselves. The net impact is negligible.

The market has not fully absorbed this distinction. When investors sell catering stocks on AI fears, they are extrapolating from the cognitive automation narrative to a sector where the primary value driver is physical service delivery. This creates an opportunity for investors who can separate the noise from the signal. Compass, for example, has a global scale, a diversified client base, and a track record of margin improvement. The AI narrative adds a modest tailwind from operational efficiency and a negligible headwind from demand reduction. The risk-reward is asymmetric in favor of the upside.

## The Report Leaves an Unresolved Question: Will AI Compress Pricing in Data and Testing Markets Faster Than Volume Growth Can Compensate?

One of the most analytically difficult questions in the business services AI debate is the impact on pricing. The report acknowledges this tension but does not fully resolve it. On one hand, AI can increase the volume of data that can be processed, the number of tests that can be analyzed, and the speed at which services can be delivered. This volume growth is a positive for revenue. On the other hand, if AI makes these services cheaper to produce, clients will demand lower prices, and the commoditization of formerly high-value activities could compress margins.

The testing, inspection, and certification (TIC) sector is a particularly interesting case. Companies like Bureau Veritas, SGS, and Intertek employ tens of thousands of inspectors and technicians who physically examine products, facilities, and processes. AI can augment this work by analyzing images, detecting anomalies, and flagging potential issues before a human inspector needs to intervene. This can increase the throughput per inspector and reduce the cost per inspection. But it also raises the question of whether clients will pay the same price for an AI-augmented inspection as they did for a purely human one.

The report suggests that the impact on TIC companies is likely to be modest, with inspection activities somewhat more exposed than testing. But this is an area where the data is thin and the scenarios are wide. If AI enables a 20% reduction in inspection costs, and clients capture half of that saving through lower prices, the revenue impact is roughly 10%. Volume growth from new markets and new services could offset this, but the timing and magnitude are uncertain.

This unresolved question is precisely why the full report is worth reading. The authors provide a framework for thinking about the trade-off, but the ultimate answer will depend on competitive dynamics, client behavior, and the pace of technological adoption. ## A Decision Framework for Investors: Assess Your Portfolio Along Three Dimensions — Physicality, Pricing Power, and Productivity Capture

The analysis above can be distilled into a practical framework for portfolio construction. Investors evaluating business services stocks in the age of AI should assess each company along three dimensions.

First, physicality. How much of the company's revenue depends on a human being physically present at a specific location? High physicality companies — caterers, pest control, equipment rental — have a natural moat against pure software sUBStitution. Low physicality companies — data processors, customer service outsourcers, some staffing segments — are more exposed to direct automation risk.

Second, pricing power. Can the company maintain or increase its price per unit of service as AI reduces the cost of production? Companies with strong brands, regulatory moats, or entrenched client integrations are better positioned to capture the efficiency gains rather than pass them through to clients. Companies in commoditized markets will see their pricing compressed.

Third, productivity capture. Is the company using AI to reduce its own cost base, and if so, can it retain the savings? A company that can automate 20% of its labor costs while maintaining its revenue base will see significant margin expansion. A company that automates 20% of its labor costs but loses 20% of its revenue to competitors offering lower prices will see no net benefit.

Applying this framework to the coverage universe yields a clear pattern. Compass and Rentokil score highly on all three dimensions: high physicality, strong pricing power through long-term contracts and scale, and clear opportunities for internal productivity gains. Staffing firms score moderately on physicality and pricing power, but have significant potential for productivity capture. Data and TIC companies score lower on physicality and face more uncertainty on pricing, but have the highest potential for productivity capture if they can maintain their margins.

The market is currently pricing an average outcome that is too pessimistic for the high-physicality names and too optimistic for some of the low-physicality names. The dispersion of outcomes is wider than the dispersion of valuations, which creates opportunity for active investors.

# Europe’s Gas Storage Rebuild Is Falling Behind Schedule, and the Market Has Not Yet Priced the Risk

The global natural gas market is entering the summer injection season with a structural problem that the current forward curve does not fully reflect. European storage is filling at a pace that is materially below both last year’s trajectory and the five-year seasonal average. As of mid-May, EU gas storage stood at only 36% full, compared with 43% a year ago and 48% for the five-year norm. Net injections over the past week reached 1.5 billion cubic meters, trailing last year’s 1.8 bcm and the seasonal standard of 2.0 bcm. If the current injection rate were sustained through October, storage would reach approximately 86% full before next winter. But that is a generous assumption, and the analysis underlying this report suggests the actual outcome is likely to be closer to 80% — a level that leaves little room for error in the event of a cold winter or supply disruption.

The gap between the current injection pace and the required trajectory is not a temporary data point. It reflects a deeper imbalance in the global LNG market that is being driven by three simultaneous forces: Asian demand resilience, a structural shift in US export flows, and the diminishing role of Chinese LNG resales as a balancing mechanism. Each of these forces is pulling cargoes away from Europe at precisely the moment when the continent needs to accelerate its storage build. The result is a market that is tighter than the headline numbers suggest, and one where the risk premium embedded in forward prices may still be too low.

The front-month TTF contract is grinding higher toward EUR 50 per MWh, but the curve remains prompt-led, with the seasonal spread hovering near flat to slightly negative. That flatness is itself a signal. It tells us that the market does not yet believe the summer tightness will persist into winter. Yet the data on LNG flows, storage trajectories, and geopolitical risk all point in the opposite direction. This article unpacks the evidence, identifies the analytical gaps that the report leaves open, and offers a decision framework for positioning ahead of what could be a volatile second half of the year.

## The Injection Deficit Is Not a Weather Story; It Is a Structural Supply Problem

It would be easy to attribute the slow pace of European storage injections to a late spring or mild weather delaying demand. But the data does not support that explanation. European gas consumption on a four-week rolling average basis is broadly in line with recent norms. The issue is on the supply side. LNG arrivals into Europe were down 6% week-over-week and 11% year-over-year for the most recent week. That decline is not a one-off; it is part of a pattern that has persisted for several weeks and is likely to continue.

The reason is straightforward: the global LNG market is not producing enough surplus cargoes to satisfy both Asian and European demand simultaneously, and Asia is winning the competition for marginal volumes. The JKM-TTF spread has been holding at USD 1-2 per mmBtu, a level that keeps Asia competitive for US cargoes that are not locked into long-term contracts. When that spread is positive, the marginal cargo goes east, not west. Europe can only attract those cargoes if it pays a premium, and so far, the prompt TTF has not risen enough to force a reallocation.

The structural shift in US export flows reinforces this point. US LNG exports to Europe still dominate at nearly 60% of total outflows, but the share of flows to Asia has increased by more than 10 percentage points to reach 33% compared with pre-conflict periods. That is a meaningful rebalancing. US exports to Asia, while down 21% in the most recent week, remain more than 60% above last year’s level on a four-week average basis. The trend is clear: Asia is absorbing a growing share of US LNG, and Europe is left to compete for what remains.

This is not a temporary phenomenon driven by a single cold snap in Northeast Asia. It reflects a structural increase in Asian gas demand, particularly from price-sensitive buyers in India and Southeast Asia, who are willing to pay the spot price to secure supply. The report’s data on LNG cargo trackers shows that Asian imports fell by 5% week-over-week, but that is a noisy short-term move. The four-week average tells a more important story: arrivals are down 9% year-over-year, but that decline is concentrated in contract volumes, not spot purchases. The spot market remains competitive, and Europe is losing.

## The Diminishing Chinese Resale Buffer Adds a Second Layer of Tightness

One of the most underappreciated mechanisms that helped balance the European gas market last year was the willingness of Chinese buyers to resell LNG cargoes into Europe. When Chinese demand was soft, Chinese importers would divert or resell cargoes, effectively acting as a swing supplier to the Atlantic basin. That buffer is now shrinking.

The report notes that Chinese LNG resales surged to 10 in March, then collapsed to just 1 in April, and activity has remained subdued in May. The reasons are twofold. First, Chinese domestic gas demand has recovered, reducing the volume of surplus cargoes available for resale. Second, China is poised to resume direct imports of US LNG, with three cargoes expected to arrive between mid- and late June. This development, which the report flags as a potential signal of improving energy relations between the two countries, has a direct market implication: if China is taking direct delivery of US LNG, those cargoes are not being resold into Europe.

The loss of the Chinese resale buffer matters because it removes a critical source of marginal supply that the European market had come to rely on. Last year, when European storage was filling rapidly, Chinese resales provided a flexible, just-in-time source of LNG that could be redirected to Europe on short notice. That flexibility is now gone. The European market must compete for every incremental cargo on the open market, and as we have seen, the current price structure does not favor Europe.

This is a second-order effect that the report’s base case storage forecast of 80% full may not fully capture. If Chinese resales remain subdued through the summer, and if direct US LNG imports into China reduce the pool of available spot cargoes, the injection deficit could widen further. The report’s estimate that storage would reach 86% full at the current injection rate is already optimistic; the actual outcome could be lower, and the risk is skewed to the downside.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: What Happens If the US Gulf Supply Chain Disruptions Persist?

The report mentions that several LNG tankers have recently exited the Gulf, but it does not fully explore the implications of persistent supply chain disruptions in the US Gulf Coast. The Gulf is the single most important source of marginal LNG supply for the global market, and any disruption to loading, liquefaction, or shipping schedules has an outsized impact on the European balance.

The question that the report leaves open is whether these disruptions are transitory or structural. If they are transitory — the result of a few weeks of maintenance or weather-related delays — then the market can absorb them through inventory draws and price adjustments. But if they reflect a deeper issue, such as a shortage of available liquefaction capacity, a regulatory bottleneck, or a shift in US domestic gas allocation, then the summer market could tighten significantly more than the base case assumes.

The evidence is mixed. US gas storage injections are broadly in line with consensus, suggesting that the upstream domestic market is functioning normally. But the downstream export chain is showing signs of strain. The report’s data on US exports to Europe and Asia shows a decline in total outflows from the Gulf, even as the share going to Asia increases. That could be a sign that the total volume of LNG available for export is constrained, not just that cargoes are being redirected.

If that is the case, then the European storage deficit is not a pricing problem that can be solved by a higher TTF. It is a physical supply problem that will require demand destruction or a draw on strategic reserves. The report does not model that scenario, but the data points it presents point in that direction.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in a Tightening Market

For readers who need to translate this analysis into actionable positioning, the following framework outlines the key variables to monitor and the thresholds that would signal a shift in the market regime.

First, monitor the JKM-TTF spread. As long as this spread remains at USD 1-2 per mmBtu, Asia will continue to attract marginal US cargoes. A narrowing of the spread to near zero or negative would signal that Europe is becoming competitive again. That would be a bullish signal for European storage injections and a bearish signal for Asian spot prices.

Second, track the weekly EU injection rate relative to the seasonal norm. The current deficit of approximately 0.5 bcm per week is manageable if it is temporary. But if the deficit persists for four consecutive weeks or widens to 1.0 bcm per week, the market will need to reprice the risk of a winter shortage. That repricing would likely push the TTF curve into backwardation, with front-month prices rising relative to forward months.

Third, watch the Chinese LNG resale data. If resales remain below 5 per month through June, the European market loses its most flexible source of marginal supply. That would increase the probability of the report’s lower-end storage forecast of 80% full, and it would raise the risk premium embedded in winter contracts.

Fourth, monitor US Gulf export volumes on a weekly basis. A sustained decline in total US LNG outflows of more than 10% from the four-week average would indicate a structural supply constraint, not a temporary disruption. That would be the most bearish signal for European storage and the strongest argument for a higher TTF.

Finally, consider the geopolitical dimension. The report notes that three US LNG cargoes are expected to arrive in China between mid- and late June. If this marks the beginning of a sustained increase in direct US-China LNG trade, it would reduce the pool of available spot cargoes for Europe and increase the competition for Atlantic Basin supply. That is a slow-burn risk, but one that could become material by the third quarter.

## The Analytical Gaps That Demand Further Investigation

No report can answer every question, and this one leaves several important analytical gaps that readers should investigate further. The most significant is the lack of a detailed scenario analysis for the US Gulf supply chain. The report mentions tankers exiting the Gulf but does not quantify the impact of a prolonged disruption on European storage trajectories. A Monte Carlo simulation that varies the duration and severity of Gulf disruptions would provide a more robust estimate of the probability distribution for winter storage levels.

A second gap is the absence of a demand-side elasticity analysis. The report assumes that European gas demand remains broadly stable, but it does not model the price level at which industrial demand would begin to curtail. If TTF rises above EUR 55 per MWh, European industrial gas demand could drop sharply, providing a balancing mechanism that the current analysis does not capture. Understanding that price threshold is critical for assessing the upper bound of the TTF.

Third, the report does not explore the interaction between European gas storage and power sector demand. As renewable generation fluctuates, gas-fired power plants act as the swing fuel. A summer with low wind and low hydro output would increase gas demand for power generation, putting additional pressure on storage injections. The report’s storage model appears to assume normal weather conditions, but a scenario analysis that incorporates weather variability would be valuable.

Finally, the report touches on but does not fully analyze the implications of US-China energy relations for the global LNG market. The resumption of direct US LNG imports into China is a significant development, but it is not clear whether it represents a one-time adjustment or a structural shift. If it is structural, it would reduce the volume of US LNG available for the spot market and increase the premium that European buyers must pay to secure cargoes.

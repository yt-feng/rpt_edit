# Gilts Are Not Always the Problem: Why the UK Bond Market's Underperformance Is a Structural Symptom, Not a Contagion

The 30-year Gilt yield recently breached 5.8%, a level not seen since 1998. For investors conditioned to view UK debt as a perennial source of bond-market drama, this milestone invites a familiar narrative: fiscal recklessness, pension fund fragility, and a central bank perpetually behind the curve. That narrative is incomplete and increasingly misleading.

The core argument of this analysis is that Gilt underperformance is primarily a function of the UK's uniquely unfavorable macro configuration — weak growth, high inflation, and extreme policy uncertainty — amplified by global repricing of term premium, not a standalone crisis of confidence in British fiscal credibility. While supply-demand mechanics have deteriorated, the evidence suggests that the recent surge in yields owes more to repricing of the Bank of England's policy path than to a rising risk premium specific to Gilts. Investors who treat UK bonds as a structural outlier risk misallocating capital and missing the convergence signals already visible in swap spreads and curve shapes.

This distinction matters because it changes the investment question. The relevant inquiry is not "Why are Gilts so bad?" but rather "When does the macro regime shift?" The answer to that question determines whether current yields represent a buying opportunity or a trap.

## The UK's Macro Trifecta — Weak Growth, Sticky Inflation, and Record Uncertainty — Justifies an Elevated Term Premium, but the Recent Spike Is Driven by Policy Path Repricing, Not Panic

The first-order explanation for Gilt underperformance is macroeconomic. Since 2022, the UK has experienced the worst combination among major developed economies: growth has been weak relative to peers, while inflation has been the highest. This configuration fundamentally alters the hedging properties of long-dated government bonds. When growth is weak and inflation is high, the correlation between bonds and risk assets attenuates. Gilts become less effective as portfolio insurance, and investors demand a higher term premium to hold them.

The data on uncertainty is striking. Survey-based measures of macro uncertainty in the UK are the highest among the G4 economies, both for growth and inflation forecasts. This is not a transient phenomenon. It reflects structural features of the UK economy: a high dependence on natural gas in the energy mix, which amplifies external price shocks, and a relatively high degree of inflation indexation across wages and prices, which makes second-round effects more persistent. The result is a bond market that must price in a wider range of outcomes than its peers.

Yet the most important insight from the recent data is that the marginal driver of Gilt yields in the last several months has not been a further expansion of risk premium. On various measures of long-end premium, Gilts have actually shown relative improvement versus peer bond markets. The underperformance is instead attributable to the repricing of the BoE policy path — the market pricing in higher terminal rates and a later cutting cycle. This is a fundamentally different source of weakness. It suggests that if the macro data evolves in line with the consensus expectation of the BoE remaining on hold through 2026 and cutting in 2027, Gilts could experience significant relief. The risk premium is not the problem; the policy outlook is.

## Global Term Premium Repricing Is the Tide Lifting All Boats, and Gilts Are Not the Only Ones Taking on Water

It is easy to focus on the UK's idiosyncratic problems and miss the broader context. Thirty-year JGB yields are at multi-decade highs. Thirty-year Bund yields are at post-sovereign-crisis highs. Thirty-year UST yields are near cyclical highs. The global repricing of long-end yields is a systemic phenomenon driven by the post-pandemic hangover: a significant repricing of inflation risk, tighter monetary policy via both higher rates and smaller balance sheets, and loose fiscal policy generating elevated bond supply across jurisdictions.

When we examine relative measures of bond pricing, the Gilt story becomes more nuanced. Swap spreads — the difference between gilt yields and swap rates — show that Gilts have cheapened versus swaps, but so have all G4 bonds. The 10s30s curve in the UK is steep, but it is not an outlier relative to the US, Germany, or Japan. On these metrics, the UK is not uniquely dysfunctional. It is participating in a global phenomenon with local amplification.

The analytical challenge is to separate the global from the local. The decomposition of UK term premium into its drivers reveals that supply factors — including BoE quantitative tightening and the broader increase in bond supply — alongside the repricing in G3 term premium explain the bulk of the movement. Policy uncertainty, while elevated, has played a modest and diminishing role. This suggests that as global term premium stabilizes or compresses, the UK will benefit disproportionately, precisely because so much of its current premium is imported rather than homegrown.

## The Structural Shift in Gilt Supply-Demand Dynamics Is Real but Not Worsening, and the Market Has Already Begun to Price a New Equilibrium

The post-2022 period has seen a profound transformation in the Gilt market's plumbing. The BoE has swung from being a buyer of duration to a seller through quantitative tightening. UK pension funds, historically the bedrock of demand for long-dated Gilts, have reduced their holdings structurally in the wake of the 2022 LDI crisis. The duration-adjusted bond free-float — the stock of bonds available to the private sector, weighted by duration — has increased sUBStantially. The ownership base has shifted toward more price-sensitive holders.

These are not trivial changes. They have likely affected the level and behavior of Gilt risk premium. A market that loses its most captive buyers and gains a seller in the form of its own central bank will naturally require a higher yield to clear. But the critical question for investors is whether these factors are worsening or stabilizing.

The evidence points toward stabilization. The BoE's balance sheet is shrinking, but the pace of quantitative tightening will eventually slow. Fiscal deficits under current policy settings are due to narrow gradually. Pension fund repositioning, while painful, has largely been completed. The free-float is no longer expanding at the pace it was in 2022-2024. The market has had time to adjust to the new equilibrium. This does not mean Gilt risk premium will collapse, but it does mean the structural headwinds are no longer intensifying. The marginal driver going forward will be macro, not mechanical.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer — The Fiscal Feedback Loop and the Threshold for a Self-Fulfilling Crisis

The analysis provides a robust framework for understanding the past and present, but it leaves several critical questions open. The most important is the nature of the fiscal feedback loop. The report estimates that a 1 percentage point increase in the medium-term deficit is worth approximately 30-40 basis points of higher Gilt yields. This is a useful calibration, but it does not address the endogenous dynamics: higher yields themselves worsen the fiscal position, which in turn pressures yields further. At what point does this loop become self-reinforcing?

The UK's debt maturity structure matters here. With a relatively long average maturity, the immediate impact of higher yields on debt service costs is muted. But the refinancing needs over the next several years are sUBStantial. If the market begins to price in a sustained deterioration in fiscal fundamentals — for example, from higher defense spending commitments that are not offset by tax increases or spending cuts — the risk premium could re-emerge as a dominant driver. The report acknowledges this possibility but does not provide a threshold analysis.

A second open question concerns the behavior of the new marginal buyers. As pension funds retreat and the BoE sells, who steps in? Hedge funds and other price-sensitive investors have increased their presence. These investors are more likely to demand a premium for providing liquidity and are more prone to rapid position unwinding in stress scenarios. The market may be more resilient in normal times but more fragile in tail events. The report does not fully explore the implications of this compositional shift for market functioning.

## A Decision Framework for Gilt Investors: Distinguishing Between Macro-Driven and Premium-Driven Regimes

For investors, the key analytical task is to determine which regime the market is in. The distinction between macro-driven and premium-driven yield moves has profound implications for positioning.

In a macro-driven regime, the primary driver of Gilt yields is the expected path of the BoE policy rate. Yields move because the market is repricing the timing and magnitude of rate cuts or hikes. In this regime, the term premium is relatively stable, and the curve moves in a direction consistent with the policy outlook. The recent period has been macro-driven: yields rose because the market pushed out the expected timing of BoE cuts, not because investors demanded a higher premium for bearing Gilt risk.

In a premium-driven regime, yields move because investors demand a higher compensation for holding duration, independent of the policy path. This is typically associated with fiscal concerns, supply surprises, or a loss of confidence in the institutional framework. The 2022 LDI crisis was a premium-driven event. In this regime, the curve can steepen sharply, and swap spreads can widen. The risk of contagion is higher.

The framework for investors is straightforward: in a macro-driven regime, the correct response is to assess the probability of the macro scenario materializing. If the BoE is indeed on hold through 2026 and cutting in 2027, current yields offer significant upside. In a premium-driven regime, the correct response is to reduce duration and wait for stabilization.

The current evidence suggests we are in a macro-driven regime with elevated but stable premium. The risk is that a fiscal shock — for example, a surprise loosening to accommodate defense spending — flips the regime. Investors should monitor fiscal announcements, gilt issuance schedules, and the behavior of swap spreads as real-time indicators of regime change. A widening of swap spreads beyond recent ranges would be the clearest signal that premium is becoming the dominant driver.

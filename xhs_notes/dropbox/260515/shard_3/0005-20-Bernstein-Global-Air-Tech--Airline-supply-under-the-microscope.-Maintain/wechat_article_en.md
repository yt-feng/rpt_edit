# Air Tech Profits Depend on Volume, But Airlines Are Already Cutting Capacity — And Winter Will Be Worse

The single most important shift in air travel technology investing this year is not about artificial intelligence, disintermediation, or new product cycles. It is about a brutal, structural divergence of incentives between airlines and the technology providers that serve them. Airlines can raise fares to offset fuel costs. Air tech companies cannot. Airlines can cut unprofitable winter routes. Air tech companies simply lose the revenue. This divergence is not a temporary disruption from the Middle East conflict. It is a recurring feature of the industry that investors systematically underestimate, and it is about to assert itself with force in the second half of this year.

First-quarter results from the two dominant air technology platforms were solid by most measures. Amadeus delivered strong numbers, with its broad IT portfolio offsetting the direct impact of geopolitical disruptions on booking volumes. Sabre posted its strongest air booking growth in over two years, despite cancellations in the Middle East. Both companies maintained full-year guidance. On the surface, the story appears resilient. Under the surface, the foundation is shifting.

The problem is that air tech revenues are almost entirely volume-driven, while airline profitability depends on a combination of fares and capacity. When fuel prices spike — and jet fuel has risen roughly 70 percent above pre-conflict levels — airlines have two levers: raise prices or reduce supply. They are doing both. But the capacity reductions are only beginning, and the cuts that matter most for air tech have not yet been announced. Winter schedules, which carry lower contribution margins and have far less booking visibility, are the real risk. If fuel remains elevated through the autumn, the capacity cuts we have seen so far will look modest by comparison.

This report examines the mechanics of that divergence, why current guidance likely overstates volume recovery, and what it means for the two dominant platforms. The conclusion is not symmetrical: one company has the product breadth and balance sheet to absorb the shock; the other does not.

## Airlines Are Reducing Schedules Now, But the Real Risk Is That Winter Capacity Cuts Will Be Deeper and Broader Than the Market Expects

The immediate impact of the Middle East conflict on airline schedules is visible and measurable. Global seat capacity in the second quarter is roughly flat year on year, a mid-single-digit decline from pre-conflict expectations. Third-quarter schedules show 3.9 percent growth, which is still a low-single-digit shortfall versus earlier plans. Middle Eastern carriers have been hit hardest: Emirates is operating at roughly 82 percent of normal capacity, Etihad at 73 percent, and Qatar at just 57 percent. These are direct, conflict-driven reductions that will persist as long as regional instability continues.

But the indirect effects are spreading far beyond the region. Airlines in Europe, North America, and Asia are trimming schedules not because of security concerns, but because the math of flying at current fuel prices no longer works. The typical airline operates on single-digit EBIT margins and spends roughly a quarter of its revenue on fuel. A 70 percent increase in jet fuel prices, even partially hedged, pushes marginal routes deep into negative territory. The only reliable way to restore unit revenue is to reduce capacity, and that is precisely what carriers are doing.

United Airlines has cut capacity by 5 percent through September. Delta reduced second-quarter growth by 3.5 percentage points. Lufthansa, Air France-KLM, and IAG have all trimmed full-year guidance by low-single-digit percentages. These cuts are modest relative to the scale of the cost shock, and they are concentrated in the summer months when demand is strongest and contribution margins are highest. That is the rational response for airlines: protect peak-season revenue while preserving flexibility to cut deeper later.

Winter is the problem. Booking visibility for the fourth quarter is roughly 20 to 30 percent, compared with 80 percent for the second quarter and 50 percent for the third. Airlines have not yet committed to winter schedules, and they will not until they see how demand holds up through the summer. If fuel prices remain elevated — and there is no reason to assume they will fall sharply — the incentive to cut winter capacity will be overwhelming. Contribution margins are seasonally lower in winter, meaning each flight generates less profit to absorb higher fuel costs. Routes with shorter stage lengths, which have a higher share of variable costs, are particularly vulnerable. The result is that seat reductions may be larger than the reduction in available seat kilometers, because airlines will cut shorter, less profitable routes first.

For air tech companies, this is not a theoretical risk. It is a direct revenue headwind that has not yet been fully reflected in guidance.

## Amadeus and Sabre Face the Same Volume Headwind, But Their Ability to Absorb It Is Fundamentally Different

Both Amadeus and Sabre are forecasting roughly 3 percent volume growth for the full year, supported by a recovery in the second half. That forecast now looks optimistic. If winter capacity cuts materialize in the 5 to 10 percent range — which is plausible if fuel stays high — volume growth for the full year could be flat or negative. The question is not whether this will affect both companies, but how differently they will weather the storm.

Amadeus has three structural advantages that Sabre lacks. First, its revenue mix is far more diversified. More than 60 percent of Amadeus's EBITDA now comes from Air IT and Hospitality, leaving less than 40 percent from the slower-growth global distribution system business. Air IT revenues are less sensitive to short-term booking volatility because they are tied to passengers boarded and long-term contracts, not transactional booking fees. Amadeus also has a growing professional services and rebooking revenue stream that directly benefits from the operational complexity created by schedule changes and cancellations. In the first quarter, these ancillary IT revenues helped offset the booking volume decline from the Middle East.

Second, Amadeus has a strong product cycle in Nevio, its next-generation airline IT platform. The recent contract wins with British Airways, Air France-KLM, and Lufthansa Group validate the product's competitiveness and create a multi-year revenue tailwind that is independent of short-term capacity trends. Nevio revenues are contracted and implementation-driven, not volume-driven. They provide a layer of earnings stability that distribution revenues cannot match.

Third, Amadeus has a healthy balance sheet with low leverage and consistent cash generation. It can return capital to shareholders through dividends and buybacks while still pursuing accretive acquisitions. The IDEMIA acquisition is already being incorporated into forecasts, and further bolt-on deals are plausible. In a downturn, financial strength is a competitive advantage that allows Amadeus to invest when weaker competitors must retrench.

Sabre faces the opposite profile. Roughly 80 percent of its revenues come from the GDS business, which is ex-growth and highly sensitive to booking volumes. Its smaller IT Solutions segment is concentrated on a few large customers, with American Airlines alone accounting for roughly 35 percent of volumes. Sabre is overweight North America, which means its performance is tied to the health of a single region and a single large airline relationship. The balance sheet is the most pressing concern: net debt to EBITDA is expected to remain above 5 times through the end of the decade. That leaves no cushion for a downturn. If airline capacity cuts accelerate, Sabre's earnings will fall, leverage will rise further, and the company will have limited ability to invest, acquire, or return capital.

The divergence is not about which company has better management or more market share. It is about business model resilience. Amadeus has a portfolio that can absorb volume shocks. Sabre does not.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question It Does Not Fully Answer: How Much of the Winter Capacity Risk Is Already Priced In?

The report is clear about the mechanics of capacity risk and the divergence in airline and air tech incentives. It quantifies the schedule reductions announced so far, the regional breakdown of capacity changes, and the vulnerability of winter schedules. It provides a framework for thinking about how fuel prices, demand trends, and airline hedging positions interact to determine capacity outcomes.

What the report does not fully answer is how much of this risk is already reflected in the share prices of Amadeus and Sabre. Amadeus trades at roughly 15.8 times forward earnings, which is below its historical average but not obviously distressed. Sabre trades at a negative earnings multiple and is priced for a highly uncertain recovery path. The market is clearly discounting some risk, but it is difficult to tell whether the discount is sufficient.

Several open questions remain. First, how quickly would airlines cut winter capacity if fuel prices remain at current levels? The report suggests 5 to 10 percent cuts are plausible, but the timing and magnitude depend on demand trends through the summer, which are inherently uncertain. Second, how much of the current strong fare environment reflects pricing power versus the fact that only half of third-quarter capacity is booked? If demand softens, airlines may not be able to sustain higher fares, which would force deeper capacity cuts. Third, what is the probability of involuntary capacity reductions from weaker airlines ceasing to exist? The report mentions this possibility but does not model it explicitly.

For investors, these questions are not academic. They determine whether the current valuation of air tech stocks represents a buying opportunity or a value trap. The report provides the analytical tools to think about these questions, but it leaves the final judgment to the reader. That is appropriate, because the answer depends on assumptions about fuel prices, demand elasticity, and airline behavior that no single report can resolve.

## A Decision Framework for Air Tech Investors in a Capacity-Constrained Environment

For investors trying to navigate the current environment, the report points to a clear but uncomfortable framework. The key variable is not market share, product quality, or management execution. It is airline capacity, which is outside the control of any air tech company. The question is not whether capacity will be cut, but how much and how fast.

The framework has three layers. The first is exposure. Investors need to assess how sensitive each company's revenues are to a given percentage change in global seat capacity. For Amadeus, the sensitivity is lower because of the IT and hospitality revenue mix. For Sabre, the sensitivity is higher because of the GDS concentration. A 5 percent decline in global capacity would reduce Amadeus's distribution revenues by roughly that amount, but total revenues would fall less because IT revenues are more stable. For Sabre, a 5 percent capacity decline would hit the core business directly, with limited offsets.

The second layer is leverage. The impact of a revenue decline on equity value depends on the company's cost structure and balance sheet. Amadeus has operating leverage, meaning fixed costs are high, but it also has financial flexibility because debt is low and cash flow is strong. Sabre has both operating and financial leverage, which creates a double hit: lower revenues flow through to lower EBITDA, and higher debt means a greater share of that EBITDA goes to interest payments. In a downturn, Sabre's equity could be severely impaired.

The third layer is optionality. Companies with strong product cycles, diversified revenue streams, and healthy balance sheets have the ability to invest through the cycle and emerge stronger. Amadeus has that optionality. Sabre does not. The report's rating reflects this asymmetry: Outperform on Amadeus, Market-Perform on Sabre.

This framework does not tell investors exactly when to buy or sell. But it provides a structured way to think about the risks and opportunities, and it highlights why the two companies are not interchangeable despite operating in the same industry.

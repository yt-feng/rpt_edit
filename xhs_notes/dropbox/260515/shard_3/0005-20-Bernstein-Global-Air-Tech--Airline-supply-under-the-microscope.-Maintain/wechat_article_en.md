# The Air Tech Volume Trap: Why Capacity Cuts, Not AI, Will Define Airline Technology Returns Through 2027

The most dangerous assumption in air tech investing right now is that volume growth will return to normal in the second half of the year. It will not. The Middle East conflict has created a structural headwind that the market is underestimating, and the divergence between airline interests and air tech provider interests means that even if fares remain strong, booking volumes face a material downside that current guidance does not fully capture. Amadeus and Sabre both delivered solid first-quarter results, but the real story is not about execution—it is about the winter capacity cliff that neither company can control.

Investor attention has shifted dramatically from artificial intelligence disruption to near-term volume trends, and for good reason. AI remains a theoretical long-term threat to the global distribution system model, but capacity cuts are immediate, measurable, and already visible in the data. Global schedules are flat year-over-year in the second quarter and only 3.9% higher in the third quarter, representing a mid-single-digit decline versus pre-conflict expectations for Q2 and a low-single-digit shortfall for Q3. These are not minor adjustments. They are the beginning of a broader recalibration that will intensify as winter approaches and fuel costs remain elevated.

The first-quarter results from both Amadeus and Sabre confirm that the operating model works when volumes are stable. Amadeus demonstrated the power of its diversified IT portfolio, offsetting geopolitical headwinds through upselling, Nevio revenues, professional services, and rebooking. Sabre reported its strongest air booking growth in over two years, despite cancellations. But these results are backward-looking. The question investors must answer is whether the current trajectory can persist through a period when airlines are actively reducing capacity and the macro environment is deteriorating.

The answer depends on understanding a fundamental structural tension: air tech companies generate profit from volume, while airlines generate profit from a combination of volume and pricing. When fuel prices spike and margins compress, airlines have every incentive to cut capacity to support yields. Air tech providers have no such lever. They are pure volume plays in an environment where volumes are under threat. This divergence of interests creates a risk profile that the current consensus does not fully price.

## Airlines Are Already Cutting Capacity, and the Winter Season Will Accelerate the Trend

The capacity reductions announced so far are modest relative to the scale of the fuel cost shock, but they are strategically significant because they reveal the direction of travel. United Airlines has cut capacity by 5% through September. Delta has trimmed 3.5% in the second quarter. Lufthansa Group, IAG, and Air France-KLM have all announced low-single-digit percentage reductions versus prior plans. These cuts are not yet devastating, but they are happening at a time when airlines are still reporting strong demand and fare environments, particularly on long-haul routes.

The critical insight is that airlines currently have roughly 80% of Q2 capacity booked and about 50% for Q3, but visibility into Q4 is likely around 20-30%. This means the strong fare environment that executives are Citing reflects less than half of the flights on sale. As airlines gain visibility into the winter schedule, the incentive to cut further will intensify for three reasons. First, contribution margins are seasonally lower in winter, making each flight less profitable and therefore more vulnerable to cancellation. Second, fuel hedges are rolling off for many European carriers, exposing them to spot prices that remain roughly 70% above pre-conflict levels. Third, the macro environment is deteriorating, with consumer confidence weakening and business travel still below pre-pandemic levels.

The most vulnerable routes are shorter stage-length segments, which have a higher share of variable costs and therefore lower contribution margins. If airlines start pulling back on schedules, the reduction in seats may fall more than the reduction in available seat kilometers, meaning the volume impact on air tech providers could be disproportionately large relative to the headline capacity numbers. This is a nuance that most investors overlook, but it has direct implications for booking volumes and passengers boarded.

For Amadeus, the Middle East represents a high-single-digit share of both bookings and passengers boarded, giving it direct exposure to the conflict zone. Emirates is operating at 82% of normal capacity, Etihad at 73%, and Qatar at 57%. These are not temporary disruptions; they reflect a structural reduction in connectivity that will persist as long as the security situation remains uncertain. Sabre has lower direct exposure to the Middle East, but it is overweight North America, where capacity growth has already flattened to zero year-over-year.

## The Air Tech Business Model Creates a Structural Disadvantage When Volumes Decline

This is the point that requires careful unpacking, because it is the analytical foundation for the entire investment thesis. Air tech providers earn revenue primarily from transactions—bookings and passengers boarded. Their cost base is largely fixed, meaning that volume declines translate directly into margin compression. Airlines, by contrast, have a cost base that is 60-80% variable, giving them the flexibility to adjust capacity and mitigate losses. When fuel prices spike, airlines can cut flights, reduce frequencies, or consolidate routes. Air tech providers cannot.

The divergence of interests becomes most acute during periods of capacity reduction. Airlines benefit from higher yields per passenger, even if total passenger numbers decline. Air tech providers benefit only from volume. This means that the optimal strategy for an airline during a fuel cost crisis—cut capacity to support pricing—is directly harmful to air tech companies. There is no offsetting mechanism. Higher fares do not generate more revenue for Amadeus or Sabre. They simply mean fewer bookings at higher prices, with the net effect being lower transaction volumes.

This structural dynamic is not fully reflected in current guidance. Amadeus maintained its full-year volume expectations despite the geopolitical impact, while Sabre modestly reduced its booking volume guidance from mid-single-digit growth to low-to-mid-single-digit growth. Both companies are assuming around 3% volume growth for the full year, supported by a recovery in the second half. That assumption looks increasingly optimistic given the capacity reductions already announced and the risk of further cuts in winter.

The second-order implication is that air tech stocks may be more vulnerable to a macro downturn than the market currently prices. If the global economy weakens and business travel contracts further, the volume headwind will intensify. Airlines will cut capacity more aggressively, and air tech providers will have no way to compensate. The current valuation of Amadeus at roughly 16 times 2026 earnings and Sabre at negative earnings with leverage above 5x net debt to EBITDA does not seem to fully discount this scenario.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Magnitude of the Winter Capacity Cliff

The report identifies winter as the period of greatest risk, but it does not quantify the potential downside with precision. This is not a criticism of the analysis—it is a reflection of genuine uncertainty. The outcome depends on multiple variables that are inherently unpredictable: the trajectory of fuel prices, the duration of the Middle East conflict, the willingness of airlines to absorb margin compression, and the resilience of consumer demand.

What the report does establish is a framework for thinking about the problem. Airlines have already demonstrated a willingness to cut capacity by low-single-digit percentages. The question is whether these cuts will accelerate to mid-single-digit or high-single-digit levels if fuel prices remain elevated. The report suggests that additional cuts in the 5-10% range are plausible, but it does not specify the conditions under which this scenario becomes likely.

The missing piece is a probabilistic assessment of different outcomes. Under what fuel price scenarios do cuts remain at current levels? At what point do they become involuntary, as weaker airlines cease to exist? How do different hedging profiles across carriers affect the timing of capacity decisions? These are the questions that investors need to answer for themselves, and the report provides the building blocks without offering a definitive conclusion.

For Amadeus, the key unknown is the extent to which its diversified IT portfolio can offset volume weakness. The first quarter demonstrated that upselling, Nevio revenues, professional services, and rebooking can mitigate the impact of cancellations. But there is a limit to this buffer. If volumes decline by more than low-single digits, the IT portfolio alone will not be sufficient to maintain earnings momentum. The company's low leverage and strong balance sheet provide a cushion, but they do not prevent earnings from declining.

For Sabre, the uncertainty is more existential. With leverage expected to remain above 5x net debt to EBITDA through the end of the decade, the company has no margin for error. A volume decline that pushes EBITDA below current expectations could trigger covenant concerns or force dilutive refinancing. The report maintains a Market-Perform rating, but the risk-reward is asymmetric: the upside is limited by the company's capital structure, while the downside could be severe if volumes deteriorate.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating Air Tech Exposure in the Current Environment

Investors should approach air tech exposure through a three-part framework that separates what is knowable from what is uncertain and then positions accordingly.

First, assess the volume trajectory using leading indicators rather than lagging ones. Airline schedule data is the most reliable forward-looking signal, and it is already showing deceleration. The current Q3 capacity of plus 3.9% year-over-year is likely to be revised downward as airlines gain visibility into the winter schedule. Investors should monitor monthly schedule updates and pay particular attention to short-haul and medium-haul routes, which are more vulnerable to cuts than long-haul international routes. If Q3 capacity growth falls below 2% and Q4 turns negative, the volume assumptions embedded in current guidance will need to be revised.

Second, evaluate the balance sheet resilience of each company independently of the operating story. Amadeus has the financial flexibility to withstand a prolonged volume downturn. Its net debt to EBITDA is low, it generates strong free cash flow, and it has the capacity to continue returning cash to shareholders even in a weaker environment. Sabre does not. The company's leverage is a structural constraint that limits its strategic options and increases its vulnerability to any negative surprise. Investors should ask whether the potential upside from a volume recovery is sufficient to compensate for the balance sheet risk, and the answer for most institutional portfolios is likely no.

Third, distinguish between temporary volume disruptions and structural changes in the competitive landscape. The current capacity cuts are a cyclical phenomenon driven by fuel prices and geopolitical events. They will reverse when conditions normalize. The AI threat to the GDS model is a structural phenomenon that will play out over years, not quarters. The report's analysis suggests that AI disruption is overdone in the near term, but the long-term risk remains real. Investors should not conflate the two. The appropriate response to a cyclical volume downturn is to maintain exposure to the strongest operator and avoid the weakest. The appropriate response to a structural threat is to reassess the entire thesis.

This framework leads to a clear conclusion for most investors: Amadeus remains the preferred exposure in the air tech space, not because it is immune to volume risk, but because its balance sheet, product portfolio, and market position give it the ability to compound returns through the cycle. Sabre remains a show-me story that requires either a significant improvement in earnings or a reduction in leverage before it becomes investable for long-only portfolios.

# FOCUS TOPIC: MIDDLE EAST CONFLICT AND AIRLINE CAPACITY FOR 2026 AND BEYOND

# AIRLINE CAPACITY RISKS REMAIN AHEAD

The Middle East conflict has created both direct and indirect headwinds for air travel. There was initially a sharp reduction in flying in the region on security concerns, with significant cancellations impacting booking volumes. At the same time, higher fuel costs are putting pressure on an already low-margin industry, forcing airlines into a capacity response. Q2 schedules now appear largely flat YoY, while Q3 is more resilient (+3.9% globally). For now, airlines continue to highlight a strong demand and fare environment — particularly on long-haul routes — which is helping to offset cost pressures. Booking visibility diminishes into winter, where we see capacity as being at greater risk, on seasonally lower contribution margins and lower hedging (for those that hedge). For Air Tech, revenues are primarily volume-driven, while airlines rely on a mix of pricing and capacity adjustments, creating a divergence in interests. With airlines still retaining flexibility to cut capacity any further demand weakness could translate into additional cuts, ultimately weighing on bookings and passengers boarded.

- Middle East conflict creates volume headwind, with effects both direct... The first two months of the year were broadly stable. However, the war in the Middle East has since changed the outlook, with the most immediate impact being a reduction in flying across the region. Many airlines from other regions canceled flights to affected countries, while airlines based in the region are operating well below pre-war activity levels. For the two largest carriers, Emirates is down around 20% and Qatar around 40%. Booking volumes have been impacted even more, as figures are reported net of cancellations. For Amadeus, the Middle East represents a high-single-digit share of both bookings and passengers boarded.   
- ...And indirect, with capacity cuts beyond the Middle East coming. The typical airline runs at a single digit EBIT margin and spends \~25% of its revenue on fuel. Jet fuel prices have doubled: all else equal, and before the impact of hedging (for those that do hedge), margins fall well into the negative double digits. That is clearly unsustainable, and prices need to rise. Airlines largely lack pricing power, and the only reliable way to get unit revenue up is to reduce capacity. Into summer, these cuts have been modest, but if conditions persist, additional cuts in the 5-10% range become plausible. Several carriers, particularly in Europe, are still supported by fuel hedges, but they will become increasingly exposed to spot prices over time. If the industry resists voluntary capacity cuts, these could become involuntary, as weaker airlines cease to exist.   
- Current schedule: cuts in Q2, Q3 in wait-and-see mode. Airline schedules have declined compared with pre-conflict levels, with global capacity currently at +0.4% YoY in Q2 and +3.9% in Q3. This is a mid-single-digit percentage point cut vs pre-conflict expectations in Q2, and slightly below pre-conflict levels in Q3. Regionally, the Middle East has seen the largest cuts, with Emirates operating at 82% of normal capacity as of May 5, Etihad at 73%, and Qatar at 57%, highlighting the disruption to travel in the region. By contrast, Asia-Europe routes are adding capacity, benefiting from the reduction in Gulf connectivity. Elsewhere, capacity cuts have been more limited, with Latin America, Europe, and China remaining among the most resilient regions, still delivering low to mid-single-digit growth, while North America and Asia ex-China are now flat YoY. The Q3 peak season appears, for now, relatively less affected. For the full year, Air Tech providers are assuming c.3% growth, supported by a recovery in the second half of the year: an expectation we see as optimistic.   
- From airlines: fewer flights, but fares remain very strong. Europe's and North America's largest legacy airlines have now reported their Q1 results, with most reducing capacity guidance for the year: United by $5\%$ through September, Delta by $3.5\%$ in Q2, and Air France-KLM, Lufthansa, and IAG by low-single-digit percentages versus prior plans. Meanwhile, airlines point to a strong demand and fare environment, particularly on long-haul routes, while trends in medium-haul and domestic markets remain more mixed depending on hedging levels and competitive intensity. Notably, Lufthansa expects to offset $>100\%$ of fuel cost increases in H2, primarily through higher fares adjustments in 2026.   
- Airline supply: from summer strength to winter strain? Air Tech stocks generate revenue from volumes — i.e. bookings and passengers boarded — while airlines rely on a combination of yields (fares) and volumes, meaning their interests can diverge, especially during periods requiring schedule cuts. Airlines currently have around 80% of Q2 capacity booked and roughly 50% for Q3, with visibility likely around 20-30% for Q4. As a consequence, the currently strong fare environment discussed is likely to reflect less than half of the flights on sale. Airlines benefit from a cost base that is 60-80% variable, allowing them to adjust capacity and mitigate losses if needed. Q2 schedules now appear largely finalized, with global capacity at around +0.4% YoY, while Q3 capacity of +3.9% YoY still carry downside risk if current conditions persist. Elevated jet fuel prices — around 70% above pre-conflict levels — are putting pressure on an industry that typically operates on

single-digit margins, reinforcing capacity discipline. Looking further ahead, winter is in our view even more exposed to potential cuts given weaker seasonality, increasing macro headwinds and lower visibility, weighing on Air Tech volumes.

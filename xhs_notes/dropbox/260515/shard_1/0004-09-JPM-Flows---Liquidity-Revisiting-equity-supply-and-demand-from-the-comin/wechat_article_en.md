## The Historical Pattern of Free Float Expansion After Lock-Ups Is a Reclassification, Not a Selling Event

The typical narrative surrounding IPO lock-up expirations is that they unleash a wave of insider selling that depresses stock prices. The data from the ten largest IPOs since 2010 tells a different story. The average free float share increases slowly until lock-ups expire, typically around six months after the IPO, and then jumps. But this jump is not primarily driven by insiders selling their stakes. The average insider share declines only modestly over the twelve months following the IPO, from around 6.9 percent to 5.3 percent. At the individual company level, the variation is enormous. Some companies, like Palantir and Coinbase, saw virtually no reduction in insider stakes. Others, like Airbnb and Snap, experienced more significant declines. The common factor is the mechanical reclassification by index providers. Once lock-up periods expire, holdings of non-insiders, including non-strategic investors with stakes below 10 percent and employees not classified as insiders, are counted toward the free float regardless of whether they sell or retain their positions. This reclassification inflates the free float share without any actual transaction occurring.

The implication for investors is straightforward. The post-lock-up jump in free float should not be interpreted as a signal of imminent selling pressure. It is an accounting artifact. The real source of market impact will come from the passive demand generated by index inclusion, not from insider liquidation. This distinction matters because it shifts the analytical focus from supply-side fears to demand-side mechanics. The question is not whether insiders will flood the market with shares, but whether passive funds can absorb the new supply that index rebalancing will require.

## Index Inclusion Will Generate $410 Billion in Passive Demand, But the Absorption Mechanism Depends on Sustained Inflows

The scale of passive demand from the three anticipated IPOs is unprecedented. Assuming a combined market cap of $3.5 trillion and an initial free float of around 4 percent at listing, the initial passive rebalancing demand would be roughly $40 billion. This figure is manageable. The more consequential phase comes after the six-month lock-up period, when the free float share is expected to increase to around 40 percent, the midpoint of the 30 to 50 percent range estimated by the research. This would generate an additional $370 billion in passive rebalancing demand, bringing the cumulative total to $410 billion. This $410 billion must be accommodated by a combination of companies releasing previously repurchased shares and non-strategic investors selling to passive funds.

The critical insight is that this demand does not have to come at the expense of existing index constituents if passive fund inflows remain strong. The research notes that the increased weights of the newly included companies will come at the expense of the companies they replace and the largest tech names like the Mag7. However, if the historical pattern of strong and steady inflows into passive equity funds continues, a disproportionate amount of these inflows can be deployed into the newly listed companies to reach their increased weights without requiring actual selling of Mag7 stocks. The passive demand is additive, not zero-sum, as long as the overall pool of passive capital is growing.

This creates a strategic bifurcation. For investors who believe passive inflows will remain robust, the IPOs represent a tailwind for the broader market, not a drag. For those who expect a slowdown in passive flows, the rebalancing could force actual selling of incumbent stocks, creating relative underperformance for the Mag7. The research does not take a firm position on which scenario will prevail, but it provides the framework for investors to make their own assessment based on flow trends.

## The Free Float Ratio Decline at the Index Level Is a Temporary Structural Adjustment, Not a Bearish Signal

The inclusion of the three large AI companies will cause a temporary decline in the free float to total market cap ratio for both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq composite. This pattern has historical precedent. From 2020 to late 2021, the free float share of the Nasdaq declined significantly during the tech IPO and SPAC boom, as newly public companies entered indices with large stakes held by founders, private equity, and venture capital investors. From 2022 to 2024, this trend reversed as lock-up periods expired, founders sold to institutional investors, and strategic investor holdings were reclassified or sold. The inclusion of the three anticipated IPOs at an expedited timeline could produce a similar pattern of temporary decline followed by gradual normalization.

The strategic takeaway is that the free float ratio decline is a mechanical consequence of index methodology, not a reflection of deteriorating corporate governance or insider pessimism. Index providers have strict criteria for what counts as free float, and the initial inclusion of companies with concentrated ownership structures will mechanically lower the ratio. Over time, as lock-ups expire and ownership disperses, the ratio will recover. Investors who interpret the initial decline as a bearish signal risk misreading the underlying dynamics. The more relevant question is how quickly the free float ratio normalizes, which depends on the pace at which insiders and strategic investors choose to reduce their stakes.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered Questions About the Timing and Magnitude of Insider Monetization

The research provides a robust framework for understanding passive demand, but it deliberately leaves several critical questions open. The most important is the behavior of insiders and strategic investors after lock-up periods expire. The historical data shows considerable heterogeneity. Some companies see significant insider selling, while others see virtually none. The research notes that the capacity of secondary markets, including structured tender offers, may be limited for the three large AI companies. It also acknowledges that the companies themselves may have repurchased shares ahead of listing to facilitate passive demand. But it does not provide a definitive answer on how much insider selling will actually occur.

A second open question is the pace of index inclusion. The research outlines timelines from index providers, but the actual inclusion process depends on factors such as the size of the free float at listing, the speed at which index providers update their methodologies, and the potential for accelerated inclusion due to the sheer size of the companies. The research assumes lock-up expiration in the first half of 2027, but this timeline could shift based on company decisions and market conditions.

A third question is the impact on active managers. The $410 billion in passive demand is a direct flow into index funds. Active managers who are underweight the new companies will face performance drag if the stocks appreciate on index inclusion. But the research does not model active manager behavior, which could amplify or dampen the price impact depending on whether active managers choose to front-run the passive flows or remain underweight.

These open questions are not weaknesses in the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of what can be known before the IPOs occur. Investors who want to make informed decisions will need to monitor insider filings, index provider announcements, and flow data in real time. The research provides the structural framework, but the tactical execution requires ongoing vigilance.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios for Positioning Around the AI IPO Wave

The report's analysis can be translated into a practical decision framework with three scenarios. The first scenario is the base case: passive inflows remain strong, the $410 billion in demand is absorbed without significant disruption, and the new companies gradually take weight from the Mag7 without causing a selloff. In this scenario, the optimal strategy is to be overweight the new companies relative to their index weight in the period leading up to lock-up expiration, as passive demand will drive prices higher. The risk is that the initial listing price already reflects this demand, leaving limited upside.

The second scenario is the disruption case: passive inflows slow or reverse, the $410 billion in demand cannot be fully absorbed by new inflows, and index rebalancing forces actual selling of incumbent stocks. In this scenario, the Mag7 and other large-cap tech names could underperform as passive funds are forced to trim positions to accommodate the new weights. The optimal strategy would be to reduce exposure to the Mag7 in advance of the rebalancing and to focus on sectors that are less affected by the weight redistribution.

The third scenario is the timing mismatch case: the IPOs occur earlier or later than expected, or the lock-up period is structured differently, creating a gap between the initial listing and the passive demand wave. In this scenario, the stocks could trade at a discount to their eventual index-inclusion value, creating an opportunity for active investors to accumulate positions before the passive flows arrive. The risk is that the timing uncertainty creates volatility that makes it difficult to execute the strategy without incurring significant tracking error.

Each scenario has different implications for portfolio construction, risk management, and sector allocation. The research does not prescribe which scenario is most likely, but it provides the analytical tools for investors to make their own assessment based on their view on passive flow trends, index inclusion timelines, and insider behavior.

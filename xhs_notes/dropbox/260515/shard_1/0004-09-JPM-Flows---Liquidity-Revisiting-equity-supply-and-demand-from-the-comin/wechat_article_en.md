# The $410 Billion Index Inclusion Event Is Not a Supply Shock—It Is a Demand Catalyst Disguised as One

The coming initial public offerings of the three largest private artificial intelligence companies will not flood the equity market with unwanted supply. They will instead trigger the largest single passive-demand event in equity market history, one that reshapes index composition without requiring the forced selling of existing megacap holdings. The critical analytical error is to conflate the mechanical expansion of free float after lock-up expiry with actual insider selling. The two are not the same, and the difference matters enormously for portfolio positioning.

A global investment bank report estimates that once lock-up periods expire, likely during the first half of 2027, total passive buying from index inclusion and rebalancing could reach $410 billion. This figure assumes a combined market capitalization of roughly $3.5 trillion for the three companies, an initial free float of around 4 percent at listing, and a post-lock-up free float rising to approximately 40 percent. The initial inclusion generates roughly $40 billion in passive demand. The lock-up expiry generates the remaining $370 billion.

This is not a supply overhang. It is a structural demand pipeline that passive funds must fulfill, and the mechanism by which it is accommodated is far more orderly than the narrative of a "dilution event" suggests.

## The Jump in Free Float After Lock-Up Expiry Is a Mechanical Reclassification, Not a Wave of Insider Selling

The most common misreading of IPO dynamics is to look at the free float chart and assume that the sharp rise after six months represents insiders dumping shares. The historical evidence from ten of the largest IPOs since 2010 tells a different story. The average free float share rises from roughly 45 percent at listing to around 50 percent at day 100, then jumps to approximately 75 percent at day 150—coinciding with the typical lock-up expiry. Yet the average insider share declines only modestly, from about 6.9 percent at listing to roughly 5.3 percent twelve months later.

The reason for the disconnect is straightforward. Once lock-up periods expire, the holdings of non-insiders—non-strategic investors with stakes below 10 percent, employees who are not classified as officers or directors—are counted toward the free float regardless of whether they sell or hold. Index providers reclassify these shares as freely tradable by definition. The jump is a statistical artifact of index methodology, not a signal of selling pressure.

The heterogeneity across individual companies reinforces this point. At one extreme, Lyft and Uber had free float shares of roughly 10 percent at listing, reflecting very concentrated insider and strategic ownership. At the other extreme, Palantir and Coinbase debuted with free float shares above 80 percent. The post-lock-up pattern is consistent—a step-change in free float—but the level is entirely company-specific. This means that generalizing from historical averages to the three anticipated AI IPOs is inherently uncertain. Their capital structures remain opaque, and the eventual free float could settle anywhere within a wide range.

## The $410 Billion Passive Demand Pipeline Will Be Absorbed Without Forced Selling of the Magnificent Seven

The index inclusion process creates a mechanical buyer of last resort. When the three companies enter major equity indices after lock-up expiry, index funds and ETFs must increase their holdings to match the new weights. The report estimates that this generates $410 billion in cumulative passive buying.

The critical question is whether this buying forces passive funds to sell existing holdings, particularly the Magnificent Seven, to free up capacity. The answer depends on the trajectory of inflows into passive equity funds. If the pattern of strong and steady inflows that has characterized the past several years continues, a disproportionate share of these new flows can be deployed directly into the newly included companies. The index rebalancing reduces the relative weights of the Mag7, but it does not require absolute selling if total assets under management are growing.

This is a subtle but crucial distinction. A passive fund with $100 billion in assets that receives $10 billion in net inflows can rebalance by directing the new money toward the stocks whose index weights are rising. The existing Mag7 holdings remain untouched. The reduction in weight is a denominator effect, not a portfolio action.

The implication is that the $410 billion event is less about redistribution of existing capital and more about the absorption of new capital. The companies themselves can facilitate this process by releasing previously repurchased shares, and non-strategic investors can sell into the passive demand. The market does not need to find a buyer for $410 billion of new stock; it needs to accommodate a structural shift in ownership from private hands to passive index funds.

## The Index-Level Free Float Ratio Will Decline Temporarily, Repeating the 2020-2021 Pattern

The historical data on free float ratios for the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq composite reveals a striking pattern. The Nasdaq free float ratio has been converging toward the S&P 500 level over time, but the convergence has not been linear. From 2020 to late 2021, the Nasdaq free float ratio declined significantly, driven by a wave of tech IPOs and SPAC listings that brought companies with concentrated insider and venture capital ownership into the index. From 2022 to 2024, the ratio recovered as lock-ups expired, founders sold to institutional investors, and IPO activity dried up.

The inclusion of the three large AI companies is likely to produce a similar temporary decline. These companies will enter the indices with free float ratios well below the current index average. The S&P 500 free float ratio currently sits near 94 percent, and the Nasdaq composite is close to 94 percent as well. If the three companies enter with free float ratios in the 30 to 50 percent range, the aggregate index ratio will dip.

This is not a sign of deteriorating market quality. It is a mechanical consequence of how index construction works. Over time, as lock-ups fully expire and secondary offerings occur, the free float ratio will recover. Investors who interpret the temporary decline as a signal of insider bearishness are misreading the data.

## What the Report Does Not Yet Answer: The Timing and Magnitude of Insider Monetization

The report is admirably clear about what it can and cannot measure. It cannot predict the level of insider selling after lock-up expiry for the specific three companies, because the heterogeneity across historical IPOs is too wide. It cannot determine whether the companies themselves will use share repurchases to facilitate the transition, or whether non-strategic investors will prefer to hold rather than sell.

The most important unresolved question is the behavior of the founders and early investors. The historical data shows that insider shares decline only modestly on average in the year after an IPO, but the range is wide. Some companies see insiders reduce their stakes by half. Others see virtually no change. For the three AI companies, the stakes are enormous, and the motivations of founders—who may want to retain control, diversify wealth, or fund new ventures—are unknowable from public data.

A second open question is the role of structured tender offers and secondary market transactions before the listing. The report notes that non-insiders may have already had opportunities to sell through these channels, but the capacity of secondary markets is limited. Whether the pre-IPO secondary market has already absorbed a significant portion of the eventual selling pressure is a critical unknown.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios for the Next 18 Months

The report provides the raw material for a structured scenario analysis. Investors should prepare for three distinct outcomes.

In the base case, the pattern of steady passive inflows continues, the three companies achieve free float ratios near 40 percent after lock-up expiry, and the $410 billion of passive demand is absorbed without significant disruption to existing megacap holdings. The Mag7 experience relative weight reduction but no forced selling. The main portfolio implication is that active managers should consider underweighting the Mag7 relative to passive benchmarks, because the passive flows into the new companies will mechanically reduce Mag7 weights.

In the bear case, passive inflows slow or reverse, forcing index funds to sell existing holdings to fund the new allocations. This would create genuine selling pressure on the Mag7 and other large-cap tech stocks. The trigger for this scenario would be a broader market downturn that reduces risk appetite and triggers outflow from equity funds. Investors should monitor weekly flow data for signs of deceleration in passive equity inflows.

In the bull case, the three companies achieve free float ratios above 50 percent, either because insiders sell more aggressively or because the companies themselves facilitate the transition through buybacks. The passive demand event becomes even larger, and the new companies quickly become dominant index constituents. The portfolio implication is that early positioning in the three companies—either through direct investment or through derivative exposure—could generate significant relative returns.

The report does not assign probabilities to these scenarios, and it should not. The data does not support that level of precision. But the framework allows investors to monitor the key variables: passive flow trends, free float announcements, insider filing data, and secondary market activity.

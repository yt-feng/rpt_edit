# China's Credit Contraction Is Not a Liquidity Problem—It Is a Demand Crisis That Policy Rate Cuts Cannot Fix

China's aggregate financing growth fell to 7.8% year-on-year in April, the weakest reading on record, and new RMB loans turned negative for the first time since July 2025. This is not a temporary soft patch. It is a structural signal that the transmission mechanism between monetary policy and real economy credit demand has broken down. The headline numbers are bad, but the composition is worse: surging bill financing masks a collapse in genuine corporate borrowing, while household sector loan contraction deepens to a level that suggests a lasting shift in behavior. Against this backdrop, a global investment bank report has now removed all expectations for policy rate cuts or reserve requirement ratio reductions in 2026. The logic is not that the economy is fine—it is that monetary easing would be ineffective against the current headwinds and potentially dangerous given rising imported inflation from the Strait of Hormuz blockade. The critical question for investors is not whether Beijing will loosen policy, but whether the current strategy of fiscal-led credit support can work when the private sector is actively deleveraging.

The April data forces a reassessment of the entire China credit narrative. For years, the playbook was straightforward: when growth slowed, the central bank cut rates, banks lent more, and the economy reaccelerated. That playbook is now broken. New RMB loans to the corporate sector fell to RMB390 billion from RMB610 billion a year earlier, but within that, medium- to long-term loans collapsed to negative RMB410 billion from positive RMB250 billion. The only reason the headline corporate loan number did not look catastrophic was a record surge in bill financing to RMB1.24 trillion. Bill financing is short-term, low-quality credit that banks use to meet regulatory quotas without actually lending to productive enterprises. Strip it out, and the underlying corporate loan picture is among the weakest in modern Chinese history.

The household sector is even more concerning. New household loans fell to negative RMB787 billion, deepening from negative RMB522 billion a year earlier. Both short-term and mortgage-related medium- to long-term components worsened. This is not a seasonal pattern. Households have been persistently reluctant to borrow since early 2025, and the trend is accelerating. When households stop borrowing, it signals a collapse in consumption confidence and property market expectations that no amount of bank liquidity can reverse. The deposit data confirms the story: new household deposits fell sharply to negative RMB1.9 trillion, while deposits shifted toward non-bank financial institutions, suggesting households are moving money out of banks in search of higher yields rather than spending or investing in real assets.

## The central bank's policy stance has shifted from proactive easing to defensive patience, and the Strait of Hormuz blockade is the reason why

The global investment bank report has completely removed its forecasts for any RRR or policy rate cut in 2026, reversing its prior expectation of a 50-basis-point RRR cut in the second quarter and a 10-basis-point policy rate cut in the fourth quarter. This is a significant revision, and the reasoning is worth unpacking carefully. The April Politburo meeting showed no sense of urgency for monetary easing. The wording on monetary policy was consistent with recent People's Bank of China communications—cautious, measured, and focused on transmission rather than stimulus.

But the real game-changer is inflation. The Strait of Hormuz double blockade has triggered a global energy shock, and China is feeling the effects. Domestic inflation picked up notably in April, and imported inflation is now a tangible concern. Import growth remained highly elevated at 25.3% year-on-year, with semiconductor imports—particularly memory chips—contributing meaningfully to price pressures. The PBOC's first-quarter Monetary Policy Report explicitly warned about monitoring imported inflationary pressures from the energy shock. This is a direct constraint on rate-cutting ability. If the central bank cuts rates while energy-driven inflation is rising, it risks fueling broader price increases and undermining the currency.

The implication is profound: China's monetary authorities are now boxed in by supply-side inflation that they cannot control. They cannot ease to support growth because the inflation they face is not demand-driven—it is imported and structural. This is a very different environment from the post-COVID period, when deflationary pressures allowed aggressive easing. The PBOC is choosing to hold its fire, and the market should take that signal seriously.

## The collapse in medium- to long-term corporate loans reveals a structural shift in corporate behavior that fiscal policy alone cannot reverse

The April breakdown of corporate lending is alarming, but the most important detail is the collapse in medium- to long-term loans. New medium- to long-term corporate loans fell to negative RMB410 billion, compared with positive RMB250 billion a year earlier. Short-term loans were also deeply negative. The only bright spot was bill financing, which surged to a record high.

This pattern tells a clear story: corporations are not willing to commit to long-term investment. They are using bill financing—which is essentially very short-term working capital management—to meet immediate cash flow needs, but they are not borrowing to build factories, buy equipment, or expand capacity. This is consistent with a business environment where demand expectations are weak, profit margins are compressed, and the policy outlook is uncertain. Companies are in survival mode, not expansion mode.

The corporate bond market offers a revealing contrast. New corporate bond financing under aggregate financing rose to RMB454 billion from RMB234 billion a year earlier. This suggests that some companies are shifting their funding mix from bank loans to bonds. Why? Because bond markets may offer more favorable terms or because banks are tightening lending standards. But the net effect is the same: the banking system is not functioning as an effective conduit for long-term investment capital.

For investors, this means that even if the government accelerates bond issuance—as the report expects—the impact on real economic activity may be limited. Fiscal spending can support infrastructure and public investment, but it cannot force private companies to borrow and invest. The private sector's reluctance to take on long-term debt is a structural headwind that will persist regardless of how many government bonds are issued.

## The household sector is in a deleveraging cycle that is self-reinforcing and likely to persist through 2026

The household loan data is the most worrying element of the April report. New household loans fell to negative RMB787 billion, the worst April reading on record. Both short-term loans (negative RMB446 billion) and medium- to long-term loans (negative RMB341 billion) deteriorated year-on-year. This is not a one-month anomaly. Household loans have been persistently soft since early 2025, and the trend is accelerating downward.

The deposit data provides the other side of the coin. New household deposits fell markedly to negative RMB1.9 trillion from negative RMB1.4 trillion a year earlier. But overall deposits increased, driven by the government sector and non-bank financial institutions. The report suggests this may reflect a shift from household deposits to NBFIs, as trillions of yuan in household time deposits mature and households seek better returns from investment products.

This is a critical insight. Households are not saving more—they are reallocating savings away from bank deposits toward higher-yielding alternatives. But they are not borrowing either. The combination of declining loans and declining deposits suggests households are compressing both sides of their balance sheets. They are paying down debt, reducing consumption, and moving liquid assets to non-bank channels. This is classic deleveraging behavior.

Why does this matter for the macro outlook? Because household deleveraging is self-reinforcing. As households reduce spending, corporate revenues fall, which leads to weaker employment and income growth, which further reduces household willingness to borrow and spend. Breaking this cycle requires either a large positive income shock (unlikely in the current environment) or a fundamental shift in confidence about future income and asset prices. Monetary policy cannot engineer that shift. Lower rates might make borrowing cheaper, but if households do not want to borrow, the transmission channel is dead.

## What the report does not fully answer: whether fiscal acceleration can overcome private sector deleveraging, and at what cost

The report expects government bond issuance to accelerate in coming months, owing to Beijing's need to support still-weak fixed asset investment. This is a reasonable forecast, but it leaves a crucial question unanswered: can fiscal expansion alone stabilize credit growth when both households and corporations are actively reducing leverage?

Historical experience suggests that fiscal stimulus works best when the private sector is willing to co-invest. When the government builds infrastructure, it creates demand for private sector inputs, which encourages corporate borrowing and investment. But if corporations are so risk-averse that they will not borrow even when demand is present, the multiplier effect of fiscal spending is dramatically reduced. The money goes to contractors and suppliers, but it does not generate a second round of private investment.

There is also a cost question. Government bond issuance at scale, especially when the private sector is not borrowing, can crowd out private credit by absorbing bank lending capacity and pushing up long-term interest rates. The report notes that the 10-year government bond yield declined to 1.78% in April, but that is partly because the PBOC was withdrawing liquidity from the banking system. If bond issuance accelerates while the central bank is not easing, yields could rise, which would further discourage private borrowing.

A second unanswered question is the interaction between the energy shock and fiscal policy. If imported inflation continues to rise, the PBOC may be forced to tighten monetary conditions even as the fiscal authority tries to stimulate. This would create a policy mix that is both contractionary for the private sector and expansionary for the public sector—a recipe for volatile asset prices and uncertain growth outcomes.

## A decision framework for investors: how to interpret China credit data going forward

For institutional investors trying to make sense of these numbers, the following framework may be useful. First, stop looking at headline aggregate financing growth as a reliable signal. The composition matters far more than the total. Track the ratio of bill financing to total corporate loans: when this ratio is rising, it indicates that banks are meeting quotas without genuine lending, and the real credit impulse is negative.

Second, watch household loan data as a leading indicator of consumption and property market health. If household loans remain negative for another quarter, it will confirm that the deleveraging cycle is entrenched. A recovery in household medium- to long-term loans would be the first signal that the property market is stabilizing.

Third, monitor the spread between the DR007 interbank rate and the PBOC's seven-day reverse repo rate. In April, DR007 fell below the policy rate for the first time since August 2023. This suggests that liquidity is ample and the banking system is not constrained. The problem is not liquidity supply—it is credit demand. If DR007 stays below the policy rate while credit growth remains weak, it confirms that monetary policy is pushing on a string.

Fourth, track imported inflation data, particularly semiconductor and energy prices. If these continue to rise, the PBOC will remain on hold regardless of domestic growth weakness. The central bank's tolerance for easing is inversely correlated with import price pressures.

Finally, watch the fiscal-monetary policy coordination. If the government accelerates bond issuance but the PBOC does not ease, long-term yields will rise and the fiscal impulse will be partially offset by tighter financial conditions. The ideal scenario for credit stabilization would be simultaneous fiscal expansion and monetary easing. The report suggests that monetary easing is unlikely. That means the burden falls entirely on fiscal policy, and the risk of disappointment is high.

# Asia’s LNG Demand Recovery Is Reshaping the Risk Calculus for European Gas Prices

The dominant narrative in European natural gas markets over the past eighteen months has been that weak Asian demand acts as a safety valve, diverting LNG cargoes toward Europe and capping TTF price spikes. That narrative is now under pressure. Preliminary data for May 2026 shows that Asia LNG imports have begun to rebound, with the four-week moving average running four million tonnes per annum above expectations. This shift, while still in its early stages, has profound implications for the second half of 2026. The cushion that Europe has relied upon is thinning, and the risks to TTF prices are moving decisively to the upside.

This is not a forecast of immediate crisis. It is a structural reassessment of how the global LNG market will balance through the rest of the year. The key variable is no longer just the duration of the Hormuz disruption, but the pace at which Asian buyers re-enter the spot market. If the current trajectory holds, the combination of summer cooling demand, storage rebuilding, and delayed normalization of Hormuz flows could push TTF prices well above current consensus forecasts. A global investment bank report now sees a plausible scenario where third-quarter TTF prices reach 65 EUR/MWh, a 48% increase from its base case of 44 EUR/MWh.

The strategic question for gas buyers, traders, and policymakers is whether they are prepared for a market where Asian demand is no longer a passive buffer but an active source of price tension. The data from May offers the first real test of that thesis.

## The Recovery in Asian LNG Imports Is Not a Blip; It Is the Beginning of a Structural Rebalancing

The most important data point in the recent analysis is the trajectory of Chinese LNG imports. The four-week moving average for China has risen to 48 mtpa, a sharp recovery from the 36 mtpa average in March. This is not yet a return to year-on-year growth, but the gap is narrowing rapidly. The report projects further upside, with third-quarter imports reaching 67 mtpa as China builds gas inventories to levels slightly above last year ahead of the next winter.

South Korea shows a similar pattern, albeit at a smaller scale. The four-week moving average for Korean LNG imports now stands at 42 mtpa, above both the April average of 39 mtpa and the report’s May expectation of 40 mtpa. These two markets alone account for a significant share of global LNG demand, and their synchronized recovery signals a broader shift away from the shoulder-season lull.

What makes this recovery strategically important is not the absolute level of imports, but the direction of travel. The market had priced in a continuation of weak Asian demand through the summer. The May data contradicts that assumption. If the current trend persists, the volume of LNG available for diversion to Europe will shrink precisely when European buyers need it most.

## The JKM-TTF Spread Is Sending a Clear Signal That Asia Is Willing to Pay for Cargoes

The strengthening of the JKM premium relative to TTF provides independent confirmation that Asian demand is firming. In May, the JKM-TTF spread widened to 1.87 USD/mmBtu from 1.59 USD/mmBtu in April, and this holds even after adjusting for changes in shipping costs. This is not a passive reflection of higher freight rates; it is an active signal that Asian buyers are bidding more aggressively for Atlantic-basin cargoes.

The implication for European buyers is straightforward: the days of cheap diversion are ending. When Asian demand is weak, European buyers can secure LNG at a modest premium over shipping costs. When Asian demand recovers, they must compete directly with buyers who are willing to pay a higher price. The spread data suggests that competition is already intensifying.

This dynamic is especially relevant for traders who have been positioning for a soft TTF market. The widening spread indicates that the marginal buyer of LNG is shifting from Europe back to Asia. If this trend continues, European storage injections will become more expensive, and the risk premium embedded in TTF will rise.

## Japan’s Weakness Is Temporary and Should Not Be Mistaken for a Structural Demand Deficit

Japan presents a more complex picture. The four-week moving average for Japanese LNG imports has fallen to 44 mtpa, well below the report’s May expectation of 48 mtpa. On the surface, this appears to contradict the broader Asian recovery narrative. However, the report identifies several temporary factors driving this weakness: warm April weather, increased coal utilization, low operating rates at chemical companies due to reduced feedstock availability, and comfortable LNG inventories that allowed for temporary destocking.

None of these factors are structural. The key insight is that Japan still needs to rebuild LNG inventories ahead of peak summer and the following winter. As cooling demand rises seasonally, the report expects higher gas usage and inventory builds to limit any further year-on-year decline in imports. The temporary destocking is exactly that—temporary. Once the weather normalizes and industrial activity picks up, Japan will re-enter the market as a significant buyer.

The risk for Europe is that Japan’s return to the market will coincide with the peak demand period for both regions. If Japanese buyers begin restocking in June and July, they will be competing directly with European buyers who are also trying to fill storage ahead of winter. The net effect will be upward pressure on both JKM and TTF prices.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Storage Levels and Price Elasticity

The report provides a clear framework for understanding the supply-side risks, but it leaves several important questions unresolved. The most critical is the relationship between European storage levels and the price elasticity of Asian demand.

European gas storage is currently at comfortable levels, but the trajectory of injections will depend on how much LNG Europe can attract relative to Asia. If Asian demand continues to recover, European buyers will need to pay a higher price to secure cargoes. The question is how high prices need to go before Asian demand becomes price-elastic and begins to ration itself.

The report does not model this threshold explicitly. It assumes that Asian demand will continue to recover through the summer, but it does not specify at what price level Chinese or Korean buyers would start to reduce imports. This is a critical gap for anyone trying to assess the tail risks to TTF prices.

A related question is the impact of the Hormuz disruption on global LNG supply chains. The report’s base case assumes normalization by late June, with a delayed scenario pushing that to late July. However, the report does not explore the possibility of a prolonged disruption that extends into August or September. In such a scenario, the combination of reduced Middle Eastern supply and recovering Asian demand could create a genuine supply crisis for Europe.

## A Decision Framework for Assessing TTF Price Risks in the Second Half of 2026

For readers who need to translate this analysis into actionable decisions, the following framework can help structure the assessment.

First, monitor the four-week moving average of Chinese and South Korean LNG imports. If these continue to trend above the report’s base-case expectations, the upside risk to TTF prices increases. The key threshold is whether China’s imports reach the projected 67 mtpa in the third quarter. If they exceed that level, the market is tighter than the base case assumes.

Second, track the JKM-TTF spread net of shipping costs. A widening spread indicates that Asian buyers are outbidding European buyers for Atlantic-basin cargoes. If the spread exceeds 2.00 USD/mmBtu, it signals that the competition for LNG is intensifying and that European storage injections will become more expensive.

Third, assess the duration of the Hormuz disruption on a weekly basis. Each week that flows remain disrupted increases the probability that the delayed scenario becomes the base case. If the disruption extends into August, the report’s upside scenario of 65 EUR/MWh for third-quarter TTF becomes increasingly plausible.

Fourth, evaluate Japan’s import trajectory. If Japan’s imports begin to recover from the current 44 mtpa level, it confirms that the temporary destocking is over and that Asian demand is broad-based. A recovery above 48 mtpa would be a strong bullish signal for both JKM and TTF.

Finally, consider the storage position of European buyers. If European storage reaches 90% of capacity by October, the market may have enough buffer to absorb higher prices. If storage is only 80% full, the risk of winter price spikes increases significantly.

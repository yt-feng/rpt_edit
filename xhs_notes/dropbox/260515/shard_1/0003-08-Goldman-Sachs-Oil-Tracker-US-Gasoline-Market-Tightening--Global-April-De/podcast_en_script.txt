EN_A: Oil prices jumped again this week. Brent crude up 5 percent. What is driving this move?
EN_B: The main factors are very low flows through the Strait of Hormuz and a rapidly tightening gasoline market in the United States.
EN_A: The US gasoline market? That is not the first thing people think of. Tell me more.
EN_B: Since April 1, US gasoline inventories have been drawing at a rapid pace of 700,000 barrels per day. They are now 5 percent below the historical seasonal median.
EN_A: Five percent below median. That sounds significant. So what does that mean in practice?
EN_B: It means wholesale gasoline prices in the US are about 15 percent higher than in Asia and Europe. That is roughly 21 dollars per barrel more. And US retail prices are just half a dollar below their all-time high.
EN_A: So what is causing this rapid drawdown?
EN_B: Three things. First, US gasoline net exports have surged. They are up 340,000 barrels per day year on year. Second, domestic demand is resilient, only 200,000 barrels per day below last year, and we are entering the summer driving season.
EN_A: So strong demand plus strong exports. What is the third factor?
EN_B: Refineries have an incentive to shift production. Strong margins for jet fuel and diesel are pushing them to produce more of those products instead of gasoline.
EN_A: With retail prices so high, does the report see any policy response?
EN_B: That is one of the more interesting observations. It is not their base case, but the probability of US oil export restrictions likely rises if retail gasoline prices keep climbing.
EN_A: That is a big risk to watch. What about the global picture? The report says the April deficit may be less large than expected.
EN_B: Correct. The IEA estimates an April deficit of 5.3 million barrels per day. That is smaller than what we estimated last month. The main reason is that the IEA has significantly downgraded its demand estimate.
EN_A: Demand was downgraded? Which products saw the biggest cuts?
EN_B: LPG and ethane were cut 11 percent. Naphtha was cut 13 percent. Jet and kerosene were cut 7 percent. Interestingly, US demand was actually upgraded, driven by resilient diesel and gasoline demand.
EN_B: There is also a big disagreement on supply. The IEA estimates Gulf crude supply in April at 15 million barrels per day. That is 4 million barrels per day higher than our balance estimate.
EN_A: Four million barrels per day difference. That is huge. What explains it?
EN_B: The report suggests it is mostly driven by Iran and the UAE. The storage constraints may be less binding than expected because there is untrackable storage capacity.
EN_A: What about strategic petroleum reserve releases? How much of an offset are they?
EN_B: IEA countries released an average of 2.1 million barrels per day in April. That matches our tracking. But 90 percent of that was crude oil, not refined products.
EN_A: So crude markets are feeling more pressure than product markets. What questions does the report leave open?
EN_B: A few key ones. How accurate is the IEA's April deficit estimate? How will the gap between their data and our balance converge? And will US gasoline prices trigger actual export restrictions? Those are all open questions.

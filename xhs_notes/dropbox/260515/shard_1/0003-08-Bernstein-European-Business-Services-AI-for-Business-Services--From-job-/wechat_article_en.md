# AI Will Reshape European Business Services, Not Destroy Them — But Investors Must Distinguish Between the Winners and the Losers

The dominant narrative surrounding artificial intelligence in European business services has become one of fear: white-collar job destruction, structural margin compression, and the erosion of competitive moats. This narrative has driven a significant sell-off in stocks ranging from Teleperformance to Experian to Adecco, with some names losing nearly half their value over the past sixteen months. Yet a careful examination of the evidence suggests that the market is conflating disruption with destruction. The real story is more nuanced — and more strategically important.

The central argument of this analysis is that AI represents a productivity shock that will reallocate labor and reshape market structures, but it will not eliminate the fundamental demand drivers for business services. The companies that will emerge stronger are those that can harness AI to lower costs, accelerate service delivery, and create new markets — not those that simply hope to avoid disruption. The critical question for investors is not whether AI will impact these sectors, but which companies are positioned to benefit from the reallocation and which are structurally vulnerable to the commoditization that AI enables.

The market's current pessimism reflects a misunderstanding of how technological shocks historically play out. Past disruptions — electrification, IT, the internet — consistently resulted in net job creation over the long term, not collapse. The mechanism is straightforward: productivity gains lower costs, which expands addressable markets, which in turn drives demand for new types of labor. The telephone operator jobs that disappeared in 1920s France were replaced by demand for similar-quality roles that grew to offset the loss. The same dynamic is likely to unfold with generative AI, though the transition will be painful for specific cohorts of workers and for companies that fail to adapt.

What makes the current moment different is the speed and breadth of the potential automation. McKinsey's midpoint scenario projects that roughly 30% of current work hours in Europe and the US could be automated by 2030, with generative AI accelerating the timeline significantly. This is not a slow, marginal shift. It is a structural transformation that will compress the demand for certain roles — particularly in back-office functions, customer service, and clerical positions — while simultaneously creating demand for new capabilities in AI system development, retraining, integration management, and compliance. The net effect on employment is likely to be neutral to positive, but the composition of that employment will shift dramatically.

For European business services companies, this creates a strategic imperative that most have not yet fully internalized. The firms that treat AI primarily as a cost-cutting tool will find themselves competing in increasingly commoditized markets. The firms that treat AI as a platform for service innovation will capture disproportionate value. The distinction between these two approaches will determine which stocks recover from current depressed valuations and which continue to underperform.

## The Market Has Priced in a Catastrophe That Historical Evidence Does Not Support

The scale of the sell-off in business services stocks is striking. Teleperformance has lost 25% of its value, Adecco and Experian are down 20%, and the broader sector has underperformed significantly over the past sixteen months. These declines reflect a market that has priced in a worst-case scenario: that AI will structurally destroy demand for the services these companies provide, that barriers to entry will collapse, and that pricing power will evaporate.

Yet the evidence from Adecco's own career transition business — the global leader in outplacement services — tells a different story. Only 1.4% of layoffs were attributed primarily to AI, while 12% had some AI-related component. Even within that 12%, there is reason to believe that companies may be Citing AI as a rationale for layoffs that would have occurred anyway, using the technology narrative to enhance their positioning with stakeholders. This suggests that the direct labor-displacement effects of AI are, at this stage, far more modest than the market's pricing implies.

The historical correlation between productivity growth and employment growth is also instructive. In the United States, the correlation between increased labor productivity and increased employment is approximately 80% on an annual basis and reaches 100% on a ten-year view. Germany shows similar patterns, albeit with slightly lower short-term correlations. This is not a coincidence. Higher productivity drives economic growth, which expands the total demand for labor even as it changes the composition of that demand. The companies that provide business services — from temporary staffing to catering to testing and inspection — are directly exposed to this dynamic. If AI boosts productivity and growth, the addressable market for these services should expand, not contract.

Randstad's observation that 60-70% of the jobs they currently fill did not exist when the company was founded 65 years ago is a powerful reminder of the creative destruction that characterizes labor markets. The jobs that disappear are visible and painful; the jobs that emerge are often invisible until they become ubiquitous. The market's current pessimism reflects a cognitive bias toward the visible losses rather than the invisible gains.

## The Impact of AI Varies Dramatically Across Business Services Sub-Sectors, and Investors Must Differentiate

Not all business services are created equal when it comes to AI exposure. The sector encompasses everything from high-touch catering operations to data-intensive credit reporting to physical inspection services. The magnitude of AI's impact depends on the extent to which a company's core value proposition can be automated, the physicality of its service delivery, and the degree to which AI creates new addressable markets versus simply compressing pricing in existing ones.

The companies most at risk are those whose value proposition is built on processing information or performing routine cognitive tasks. Teleperformance, the customer service outsourcing giant, faces the most direct threat. AI-powered chatbots and voice agents are already capable of handling a significant share of standard customer interactions, and the technology is improving rapidly. The company's medium-term growth framework is likely to prove optimistic as AI enables clients to automate precisely the types of interactions that have historically driven Teleperformance's revenue. The stock's 25% decline may not fully reflect the structural headwinds the company faces.

Experian faces a different but equally consequential challenge. The company's credit data and analytics business benefits from high barriers to entry and strong network effects. However, AI could lower the cost of credit decisioning and enable new entrants to compete more effectively, potentially compressing the total addressable market and reducing pricing power. The impact is not catastrophic — Experian's data assets remain valuable — but the growth trajectory that the market has priced in may prove too optimistic as AI democratizes access to credit analytics.

At the other end of the spectrum are companies whose services are inherently physical and difficult to automate. Rentokil's pest control operations, Elis's textile rental business, and Sunbelt's equipment rental division all require on-site physical intervention that AI cannot easily replicate. These companies benefit from AI indirectly — through improved routing, predictive maintenance, and back-office efficiency — but their core value propositions are largely insulated from direct automation risk. The market's relative underreaction to these stocks represents an opportunity for investors who understand the physicality of service delivery.

The staffing companies — Adecco and Randstad — occupy an interesting middle ground. Their core business of placing temporary workers is exposed to AI-driven job displacement, but the nature of that exposure is nuanced. Both companies have significant exposure to blue-collar temporary placements, which are less vulnerable to AI automation than white-collar roles. Moreover, the complexity of managing large, distributed workforces in a rapidly changing labor market creates demand for the very services these companies provide. The market's 49% decline in Adecco and Randstad appears to price in a worst-case scenario that ignores the structural demand for workforce intermediation in an AI-disrupted economy.

## The Catering Sector Will Be Disrupted Only Marginally, and Operational Gains Will Offset Most of the Revenue Risk

The catering sector — dominated by Compass, Sodexo, and Elior — has been caught in the crossfire of AI fears, despite the fact that its core operations are fundamentally physical. The concern is that AI will reduce white-collar employment in the offices and facilities these companies serve, thereby reducing demand for workplace catering. This logic is not entirely wrong, but it significantly overstates the magnitude of the impact.

A careful analysis suggests that the maximum negative impact on catering revenue from AI-driven employment reduction is approximately 1-2%. This is because catering revenue is driven not just by headcount but by the frequency and quality of service, the expansion of offerings into adjacent categories, and the pricing power that comes from long-term contracts. Moreover, the operational gains that AI enables — optimized kitchen workflows, predictive demand forecasting, automated inventory management — can offset most of the revenue risk through cost efficiencies.

Compass, in particular, is well-positioned to navigate this transition. The company's scale, its focus on operational excellence, and its ability to leverage data across thousands of client sites give it a structural advantage in deploying AI tools effectively. Compass's exposure to sectors like healthcare and education, where employment is expected to grow rather than shrink, further insulates it from the white-collar contraction that AI may drive in corporate offices.

Sodexo faces a slightly more challenging position due to its greater exposure to corporate client sites, but the magnitude of the risk remains manageable. Elior, the smallest of the three, has less operational leverage to invest in AI capabilities, but its focus on specific geographic and vertical niches provides some insulation.

The key insight for investors is that the catering sector's demand drivers are more resilient than the market assumes. People will continue to eat, and workplaces will continue to require food service, even as the composition of the workforce shifts. The 1-2% revenue headwind from AI is more than offset by the operational efficiencies that AI enables, making the sector's current valuation discount an opportunity rather than a value trap.

## The Full Report Raises Critical Questions That the Market Has Not Yet Answered

While the analysis above provides a framework for understanding AI's impact on European business services, several critical questions remain unresolved. These are the questions that the full report explores in greater depth, and they represent the most important analytical challenges for investors.

First, how quickly will AI adoption actually occur in the business services sector? The McKinsey scenarios range from aggressive to conservative, with adoption timelines spanning decades. The speed of adoption depends not just on technical capability but on regulatory frameworks, labor market dynamics, and corporate willingness to disrupt existing operations. The market appears to be pricing in a faster adoption scenario than the evidence supports, but the direction of error is uncertain.

Second, how will pricing dynamics evolve in AI-disrupted sub-sectors? The conventional wisdom is that AI will compress pricing by lowering barriers to entry and enabling new competitors. But this assumes that the incumbents will not respond by using AI to enhance their own value propositions. The companies that can use AI to offer faster, more accurate, or more customized services may actually increase their pricing power relative to competitors who simply automate existing processes.

Third, what is the second-order impact of AI on the demand for business services? If AI boosts overall productivity and economic growth, the total addressable market for business services should expand. But the magnitude of this expansion is difficult to quantify, and it depends on assumptions about macroeconomic multipliers and the elasticity of demand for outsourced services.

Fourth, which companies have the organizational capability to execute an AI strategy effectively? The technology itself is increasingly commoditized; the competitive advantage comes from the ability to integrate AI into existing operations, retrain workforces, and capture value from new service offerings. This is a question of management quality and organizational culture, not just technological investment.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Which Business Services Stocks Will Win

For investors trying to navigate the uncertainty, three questions can serve as a decision framework for evaluating individual business services stocks.

First, can the company's core service be automated? This is the most important question, and the answer determines the baseline level of risk. Companies whose services are primarily cognitive and routine — customer service, data processing, basic analytics — face the highest automation risk. Companies whose services require physical presence, specialized expertise, or complex judgment face lower risk. The distinction is not binary, but it provides a useful starting point for analysis.

Second, does the company have pricing power that can withstand commoditization? Even if a company's services can be partially automated, it may retain pricing power if it owns proprietary data, has long-term contracts, or provides services that are deeply integrated into client operations. Companies that compete primarily on price in commoditized markets are most vulnerable to AI-driven compression.

Third, can the company use AI to create new revenue streams or expand its addressable market? The winners in the AI transition will not be the companies that simply defend their existing positions. They will be the companies that use AI to offer new services, enter new verticals, or serve clients that were previously uneconomical to reach. This requires not just technological capability but strategic imagination and execution discipline.

Applying this framework to the current landscape suggests that the market has oversold some stocks and undervalued others. The staffing companies, despite their recent declines, have the potential to benefit from the very disruption that investors fear, as companies increasingly need help managing a complex, rapidly changing workforce. The testing, inspection, and certification companies, which have been relatively resilient, may face more disruption than the market assumes as AI enables automated inspection and remote certification. The catering companies, which have been caught in the crossfire of AI fears, are likely to emerge largely unscathed, with operational gains offsetting modest revenue headwinds.

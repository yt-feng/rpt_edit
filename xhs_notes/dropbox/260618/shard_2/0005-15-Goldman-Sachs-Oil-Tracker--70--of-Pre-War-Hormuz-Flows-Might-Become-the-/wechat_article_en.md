# The Strait of Hormuz Will Not Return to Normal: 70% of Pre-War Flows Is the New 100%

The recent interim agreement between the United States and Iran, signed electronically and reportedly including a waiver for Iranian oil and petrochemical exports, has sent spot Brent futures below $80 per barrel. Markets are pricing a full normalization of Persian Gulf flows. This is a mistake.

The controlling insight from the latest oil tracker data is straightforward but counterintuitive: the normalization of Persian Gulf exports to pre-war levels may require only a 12.7 million barrels per day increase in Strait of Hormuz flows from current levels, bringing them to approximately 70% of pre-war volumes. But that 70% figure will become the new operational baseline, not a temporary waypoint on a path back to 100%. The structural constraints on flows—shipper risk aversion, unresolved geopolitical negotiations, and the physical limits of alternative routing infrastructure—will prevent a complete recovery for the foreseeable future.

This matters now because the market is mispricing both the speed and the completeness of the recovery. The IEA's latest Oil Market Report assumes Hormuz flows recovery begins in the third quarter of 2026 and takes several months to complete. The interim deal suggests a faster timeline for the political agreement, but the operational reality on the water will lag the diplomatic calendar by weeks or months. Investors who assume that a signed Memorandum of Understanding translates immediately to 20 million barrels per day flowing through the Strait are overlooking the gap between what is politically possible and what is logistically feasible.

The second-order implications are significant. If 70% of pre-war Hormuz flows becomes the new normal, the global oil balance shifts permanently. The physical oil market will need to adapt to a world where Persian Gulf exports are structurally lower, alternative supply routes are permanently more active, and the premium on non-Hormuz crude increases. The next several months will determine whether this adjustment is orderly or disruptive.

## The Interim Deal Unlocks Supply Faster Than the Market Expects, but the Physical Recovery Will Be Slower Than the Political One

The reported Memorandum of Understanding includes a waiver for Iranian oil and petrochemical exports, along with related financial and transportation services. This is not a minor concession. It potentially unlocks over 50 million barrels of Iranian oil currently sitting on the water, available for immediate delivery. The market's immediate reaction—a decline in spot Brent below $80—reflects the correct directional view that supply is coming back.

But the speed of the physical recovery is constrained by factors that no diplomatic agreement can address overnight. The report estimates that the normalization of Gulf exports to pre-war levels would require a 12.7 million barrels per day increase in Hormuz flows. That is a massive logistical operation. It requires not just political permission but also the physical movement of tankers, the reactivation of loading schedules, and the resolution of insurance and financing questions that have been disrupted for months.

The critical variable is not the political will but the operational readiness of the shipping ecosystem. The report notes that empty tanker capacity within the Strait or within five days of navigation is estimated at 861 million barrels. Ship availability is not the binding constraint. The binding constraint is shipper risk aversion. Many shipowners reportedly remain cautious about clear guidelines for transit. They will not send vessels through the Strait until they have unambiguous legal and insurance coverage, which may take weeks to materialize even after the deal is signed.

This creates a predictable sequence. The political agreement comes first. The financial and insurance infrastructure adjusts second. The physical movement of oil follows third. The market is pricing all three steps as if they happen simultaneously. They do not.

## The IEA's Demand Destruction Estimates Are Larger Than Goldman's, and the Gap Reveals a Critical Divergence in Assumptions About China

The IEA estimates a second-quarter deficit of 3.1 million barrels per day, significantly below the report's estimate of 5.0 million barrels per day. The gap is driven by the IEA's assumption of larger demand destruction: 5.5 million barrels per day versus the report's 4.9 million, peaking in May at 6.1 million.

This is not a minor methodological disagreement. It reflects fundamentally different views about the persistence of demand weakness, particularly in China. The IEA downgraded China's total oil demand by 2.2 million barrels per day, or 13% below February expectations. The report's own data shows that China's crude imports dropped by 4.2 million barrels per day year-over-year, far more than the demand destruction figures would suggest.

The reconciliation of these numbers is revealing. The report estimates that China's implied crude stock draws were 1.6 million barrels per day in May, while visible stocks showed draws of only 0.3 million barrels per day. The gap of 1.3 million barrels per day suggests either meaningful invisible crude destocking—possibly from underground strategic petroleum reserves—or even lower crude demand than official data implies. Either explanation points to the same conclusion: China's oil demand is weaker than headline numbers suggest, and the weakness is concentrated in areas that are difficult to measure.

The product-level breakdown reinforces this view. The IEA's demand loss estimates are concentrated in petrochemical feedstocks, with LPG and ethane demand down 1.7 million barrels per day and naphtha down 1.1 million barrels per day. This is consistent with the report's analysis that the demand destruction is structural, not cyclical. When petrochemical feedstocks are the primary driver of demand weakness, the recovery is unlikely to be V-shaped.

## The Physical Oil Market Is Already Adapting Through Redirections, but These Routes Have Hard Capacity Limits

The report documents a complex system of alternative flows that are partially offsetting the Hormuz disruption. Visible Hormuz flows are estimated at 1.3 million barrels per day over the past seven days. Gulf of Oman flows, which may represent "dark" Hormuz crossings, are at 1.6 million barrels per day. Redirections via Yanbu, Fujairah, and Ceyhan total 7.5 million barrels per day.

This system is working, but it is operating at or near its limits. The Yanbu pipeline on Saudi Arabia's west coast, the Fujairah pipeline outside the Strait, and the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline through Turkey are all running at elevated utilization. The report estimates that 28% of the hit to Persian Gulf crude and condensate exports is currently being offset, with contributions from higher US exports, Americas ex-US exports, and Russian exports.

The key question is whether these alternative routes can scale further. The answer is likely no, at least not without significant capital investment and time. Pipeline capacity is fixed in the short term. Tanker availability is not the constraint, but port infrastructure and loading capacity at alternative export points are. The system has already absorbed the maximum practical volume of redirections. Any additional recovery must come through the Strait itself.

This has a direct implication for the supply outlook. If Hormuz flows recover to 70% of pre-war levels, as the report estimates, the remaining 30% gap must be filled by either further demand destruction or higher production from non-Persian Gulf sources. Neither option is straightforward. Demand destruction is already concentrated in China and petrochemical feedstocks, and further declines would require a deeper economic slowdown. Non-Persian Gulf production increases are possible but face their own constraints, including OPEC+ discipline and the physical limits of US shale.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer Whether Shipper Risk Aversion Will Persist Beyond the Initial Recovery Phase

The report identifies shipper risk aversion as a potential constraint on flows, alongside Iran's geopolitical goals over the upcoming 60-day nuclear deal negotiations. But it does not fully resolve the question of whether this risk aversion is a temporary friction or a permanent shift in the shipping industry's operating model.

The distinction matters. If shipper risk aversion is temporary, then the recovery of Hormuz flows to pre-war levels is merely a matter of time—weeks, not months. The insurance industry will update its terms, shipowners will receive clear guidelines, and the tankers will move. The 861 million barrels of empty tanker capacity will be deployed, and the physical market will normalize.

If shipper risk aversion is permanent, the implications are more profound. The experience of the past several months may have fundamentally changed how shipowners assess the risk of transiting the Strait of Hormuz. Even with a signed agreement, the geopolitical environment remains volatile. The 60-day negotiation period introduces uncertainty. Shipowners may demand a risk premium that makes Hormuz transit economically unattractive at current oil prices.

The report's data on tanker positioning is suggestive but not conclusive. The fact that empty tanker capacity is abundant within five days of navigation suggests that shipowners are keeping vessels nearby, ready to move if conditions improve. But the fact that they have not moved yet, even as the political environment has shifted, suggests that the threshold for action is higher than the market assumes.

This is the unresolved analytical question that will determine the trajectory of oil prices over the next quarter. If shipper risk aversion dissipates quickly, the supply recovery will be faster than the IEA assumes. If it persists, the market will remain tighter than current prices reflect.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios for Hormuz Flow Recovery

The report's data supports a structured approach to scenario analysis. Rather than forecasting a single outcome, investors should evaluate the probability and implications of three distinct paths.

Scenario One: Rapid Normalization. In this scenario, the political agreement is followed by swift operational normalization. Shipper risk aversion dissipates within two to three weeks. Hormuz flows recover to 70% of pre-war levels by late July and approach 90% by October. The IEA's assumption of a 2026 recovery start is too conservative. The global oil balance shifts from deficit to surplus by the fourth quarter. Brent prices decline toward $70 per barrel.

Scenario Two: Stalled Recovery. In this scenario, the political agreement holds but operational normalization is slow. Shipowners remain cautious. The 60-day nuclear deal negotiations introduce periodic disruptions. Hormuz flows recover to 50% of pre-war levels by October but do not reach 70% until 2026. The global oil balance remains in deficit through the end of the year. Brent prices stabilize in the $75 to $85 range.

Scenario Three: Partial Reversal. In this scenario, the political agreement unravels or the 60-day negotiations fail. Sanctions snap back. Hormuz flows decline again from current levels. The global oil balance moves into a deeper deficit. Brent prices spike above $100.

The report's data suggests that Scenario Two is the most likely outcome, with a non-trivial probability of Scenario Three. The key indicators to watch are tanker movements through the Strait, insurance premiums for Hormuz transit, and the tone of the nuclear deal negotiations. Investors who position for Scenario Two, with hedges for Scenario Three, are best aligned with the underlying data.

## The Market Is Pricing a Smooth Recovery, but the Data Points to a Bumpy One

The decline in Brent below $80 following the interim deal announcement suggests that the market is pricing a relatively smooth recovery. This is inconsistent with the operational data in the report. The gap between visible and invisible Chinese stock draws, the concentration of demand destruction in petrochemical feedstocks, the capacity limits on alternative routes, and the unresolved question of shipper risk aversion all point to a recovery that will be slower and more incomplete than the market expects.

The most important number in the report is not the 70% figure for Hormuz flows. It is the 1.3 million barrels per day gap between implied and visible Chinese stock draws. That gap tells you that the official data is missing something significant. Whether it is underground strategic reserves or lower refinery runs, the direction is the same: Chinese demand is weaker than it appears, and the weakness is structural.

For investors, the implication is clear. The oil market is tighter than headline prices suggest, and the recovery of Persian Gulf supply will be slower than the political calendar implies. The risk is not that supply comes back too fast, but that it does not come back fast enough to prevent a renewed tightening in the second half of the year.

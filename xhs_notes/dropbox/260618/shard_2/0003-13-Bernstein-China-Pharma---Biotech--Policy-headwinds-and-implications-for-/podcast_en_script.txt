EN_A: The pharma and biotech sector has been under a lot of pressure lately. Are we looking at a genuine structural risk, or is the market just overreacting to headlines?
EN_B: That is the exact question a recent global investment bank report tries to answer. And its core takeaway is that while policy risks are real, the market may already be pricing in a scenario far worse than reality.
EN_A: So you are saying the sell-off is overdone?
EN_B: Exactly. The report runs a scenario analysis with two main cases. The first one is called indirect access. In that scenario, the US market remains open to China-origin assets, but through intermediaries like European or Japanese partners.
EN_A: What does that mean for the numbers?
EN_B: The report estimates that under indirect access, business development income for Chinese biotechs would drop by about 50 percent, and global peak sales would fall by roughly 20 percent. But the market currently seems to be pricing in something much more severe.
EN_A: What is the more severe scenario?
EN_B: That is the second case, called complete block. If the US market is effectively off-limits, BD income would plunge 80 percent, and global sales would drop 60 percent. But the report is very clear that this is a low-probability event.
EN_A: So the fear is mostly emotional?
EN_B: That is the argument. The report believes the more likely path is a middle ground, where cross-border collaboration continues but with added friction. And current valuations seem to reflect something much worse than that base case.
EN_A: Let us break down the policies. What are we actually looking at?
EN_B: There are two main buckets. On the US side, you have legislation like BINSA and the Biosecure Act. BINSA would mandate government review of cross-border licensing deals. The Biosecure Act restricts federal funding from being used to buy services from designated Chinese CDMOs.
EN_A: Where do these bills stand now?
EN_B: BINSA is expected to be attached to the FY27 NDAA by the end of this year. For the Biosecure Act, the OMB is set to publish an official list of companies of concern by year end. WuXi AppTec is very likely to be on it.
EN_A: What about China’s own policies? Are they supportive or restrictive?
EN_B: On the domestic front, China’s policies are generally positive for innovative drugs. There is a new pricing rule that allows highly innovative drugs to launch at a high price. There is also seven-year market exclusivity for orphan drugs and two years for pediatric drugs.
EN_A: That sounds good. So why is the market not reacting?
EN_B: The report points to a structural issue. Domestic commercialization is far less profitable than the US market, and direct government subsidies are limited. So positive signals often fail to sustain equity performance, while negative news gets amplified.
EN_A: How does China fit into the global innovation chain?
EN_B: The report paints a fascinating picture. China now accounts for 30 to 40 percent of global preclinical programs and about 50 percent of early clinical pipelines. But when you look at late-stage presence, the numbers are tiny.
EN_A: How tiny?
EN_B: China-origin assets represent less than 10 percent of US Phase 3 trials, less than 5 percent of FDA approvals, and only about 1 percent of US commercial sales. So the bottleneck is not generating assets, it is converting them into global value.
EN_A: So it is a supply of innovation, but with very limited capture of downstream value.
EN_B: Exactly. The report describes China biopharma as an increasingly important source of innovation supply, but with limited capture of downstream economics.
EN_A: Which companies are most exposed? And which ones are more resilient?
EN_B: In the complete block scenario, globally exposed names like Innovent, Hengrui, and Hansoh could see roughly 50 percent downside to valuation. Kelun-Biotech looks the most resilient, because its flagship product sac-TMT has 17 ongoing US trials and is relatively derisked.
EN_A: So Kelun is a safe bet?
EN_B: The report rates it outperform. But note that it is not yet profitable, and its valuation is based on EV to sales. The report also rates BeOne Medicines and Innovent as outperform.
EN_A: What about the deals that have been called out? I read that some recent collaborations were labeled dangerous.
EN_B: Yes. The report mentions that the Innovent-Pfizer and Hengrui-BMS deals were explicitly called dangerous by a US congressman. That highlights the increasing regulatory sensitivity around cross-border partnerships.
EN_A: So what should investors do now?
EN_B: The report’s key advice is not to be driven by emotion. The market may have overreacted. But there is one big open question that the report does not fully answer.
EN_A: What is that?
EN_B: It is about China’s own potential restrictions on out-licensing. The report notes that China is expanding curbs on foreign deals and tech transfer. But so far, the latest Innovent-Pfizer deal was not affected. CSPC management also said product-level IP transfer remains flexible.
EN_A: So the big unknown is whether China will tighten controls on biopharma out-licensing.
EN_B: Exactly. That is the key variable to watch. The report leaves that question open, which is why reading the full document is worthwhile.

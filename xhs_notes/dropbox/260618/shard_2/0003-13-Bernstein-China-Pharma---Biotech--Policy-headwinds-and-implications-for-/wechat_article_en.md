# China Biopharma’s Value Bridge Is Under Construction — and Policy Is Tightening the Toll Gates

The global investment thesis for Chinese biopharma has long rested on a simple promise: the country has become an engine of early-stage innovation, and that innovation will eventually translate into global commercial value. That promise is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The translation from preclinical pipeline to US market revenue is not automatic. It depends on a fragile value bridge — a chain of out-licensing deals, cross-border collaborations, and regulatory approvals that convert Chinese-origin assets into global product sales. That bridge is now under direct policy pressure from both Washington and Beijing, and the implications for equity valuation are far larger than the market currently prices in.

The healthcare sell-off in 2026 has been triggered by a cascade of US legislative measures — the Biosecure Act, the COINS framework, and the newly introduced BINSA — that collectively target capital flows, data access, licensing structures, and supply chains. The direction is unambiguous: the US is reducing its willingness to serve as the primary commercial destination for China-origin drugs. At the same time, China’s domestic policies remain broadly supportive of innovation, but new scrutiny on technology outflows introduces an additional layer of uncertainty. The result is a sector whose fundamental innovation engine continues to grow, but whose path to monetization is narrowing.

The core strategic question for investors is not whether Chinese biopharma can generate novel assets. It clearly can. The question is whether those assets can still reach the US market — which represents roughly 50 to 60 percent of global pharmaceutical value — and at what cost. The answer will determine which companies survive, which thrive, and which are structurally impaired.

## China’s Biopharma Innovation Engine Is Real, but It Is Front-Loaded in the Development Funnel

The numbers are striking. China now accounts for an estimated 30 to 40 percent of global preclinical programs and roughly 50 percent of early clinical-stage pipelines. In 2026 year-to-date, approximately 55 percent of first clinical trial registrations globally originated from China. These figures reflect genuine improvements in research capability, development speed, and the scale of the domestic patient base for efficient trial recruitment. The country has become a significant node in global drug discovery.

Yet this early-stage dominance has not translated proportionally into late-stage presence. China-origin assets still represent less than 10 percent of US Phase 3 trials, fewer than 5 percent of FDA approvals, and only about 1 percent of US commercial sales. The gap is not a failure of science. It is a structural feature of the current ecosystem. The bottleneck is not asset generation — it is conversion into global value.

Two factors explain this. First, there is a time lag. The recent acceleration in outbound licensing deals has only begun to feed into US late-stage pipelines. Second, there is a competitiveness gap. FDA regulatory standards and the intensity of global competition mean that assets which succeed in China’s domestic market face higher attrition rates when subjected to US scrutiny. The innovation supply is real, but the downstream economics are disproportionately captured by US and European partners.

This front-loaded profile means that Chinese biopharma companies are currently valued more for their pipeline potential than for their commercial execution. That makes them acutely sensitive to any policy that disrupts the licensing and collaboration mechanisms through which that potential is realized.

## US Policy Is Not a Single Blow — It Is a Multi-Layered Architecture of Restriction

The recent US legislative efforts are often discussed as isolated measures, but they function as a coordinated system. The Biosecure Act restricts US federal agencies and federally funded entities — including Medicare and Medicaid — from sourcing from designated biotechnology companies of concern. This directly pressures the CDMO model and creates structural decoupling between Chinese manufacturers and Western pharma. The COINS Act and its component BINSA introduce mandatory government review of cross-border licensing transactions, tighter scrutiny on joint ventures, and limits on equity investments. Even if implementation remains staged, the direction is clear: the US is building a wall around its pharmaceutical market.

A global investment bank report highlights that recent high-profile collaborations — including those involving Innovent and Hengrui — have been explicitly called out by US legislators as problematic. This is not a hypothetical risk. It is a live regulatory environment in which the approval of a licensing deal could become contingent on national security review. The report notes that the next key catalyst is the national security assessment due by August 2026, which will determine whether the Pentagon formally links US capital flows into Chinese drugs to direct military vulnerabilities. If BINSA is attached to the FY27 NDAA later this year, it becomes permanent statutory law.

The implication is that the current market sell-off may be pricing in a worst-case scenario, but it may also be underestimating the structural persistence of these restrictions. Even if a complete block is unlikely, the cumulative friction from multiple layers of regulation will raise the cost and uncertainty of every cross-border transaction.

## China’s Domestic Policy Is Supportive but Adds Its Own Layer of Uncertainty

On the domestic front, the Chinese government has maintained a consistent pro-innovation stance. Recent policies include higher pricing flexibility for differentiated drugs at launch, multi-year market exclusivity for orphan and pediatric drugs, and expanded commercial health insurance coverage. These are genuinely positive signals. However, they operate in a market that is significantly less profitable than the US, and direct financial subsidies remain limited.

The more important development is the growing scrutiny on technology outflows. Following the Meta-Manus block, the Chinese government has signaled an intent to expand curbs on foreign deals and technology transfers. For biopharma, this raises a specific concern: could out-licensing of Chinese drug assets be restricted? So far, the evidence suggests that product-level IP transfers remain acceptable. Companies like CSPC have indicated continued flexibility from regulators in transferring product-level assets rather than core platform technologies. But the uncertainty itself is a headwind. It adds a second layer of approval risk on top of US restrictions, and it creates the possibility that future deals could be delayed or restructured.

The net effect is that Chinese biopharma companies now face a dual regulatory squeeze: one from the US restricting market access, and one from China potentially restricting deal structures. The innovation engine remains intact, but the channels through which it generates value are narrowing.

## Scenario Analysis Reveals How Much Valuation Depends on the Value Bridge

The report constructs two scenarios that illustrate the sensitivity of equity value to policy outcomes. Under an “indirect access” scenario, the US market remains open to China-origin assets through collaborators in the EU, Japan, and other regions, but the route becomes more difficult. In this case, business development income declines by roughly 50 percent and global peak sales by about 20 percent. Under a “complete block” scenario, where the US market is effectively off-limits, BD income falls by approximately 80 percent and global sales by 60 percent.

The equity implications are stark. In a full block, globally exposed names such as Innovent, Hengrui, and Hansoh could see roughly 50 percent downside to valuation. The one outlier is Kelun-Biotech, whose flagship product sac-TMT serves as the main valuation anchor and is relatively derisked with competitive data and 17 ongoing US trials. But even for Kelun, the scenario matters.

The report argues that a complete block is a low-probability outcome. The more likely path is a middle ground in which cross-border collaboration persists but with added friction. However, the current market pricing appears to reflect a more severe scenario than this base case. That creates a potential opportunity, but it also means that any further negative policy developments could trigger additional downside. The market is not pricing in a smooth path — it is pricing in a significant discount for uncertainty.

## The Report Leaves Unanswered Questions That Are Critical for Investors

The report is thorough in its policy analysis and scenario construction, but it leaves several open questions that are essential for forming a complete investment view.

First, how will US partners respond to the new regulatory environment? The report notes that recent transactions have been called out by legislators, but it does not fully explore how MNCs are adjusting their deal structures, legal terms, or partner selection criteria. If US companies begin to demand different contractual protections or shift toward non-Chinese partners for early-stage assets, the impact on Chinese biopharma could be more severe than the friction-based scenario implies.

Second, what is the actual probability of a complete block? The report treats it as low-probability, but the legislative trajectory suggests that the political incentives are asymmetric. Restricting Chinese biopharma is a low-cost, high-visibility action for US lawmakers. If BINSA passes and is enforced, the definition of what constitutes a national security risk could expand over time.

Third, how will China’s domestic capital markets respond if the US path is constrained? If out-licensing becomes more difficult, the domestic return on investment for innovative drugs will need to improve to sustain the current level of early-stage investment. The Chinese government has shown willingness to support innovation through pricing and reimbursement, but it has not yet demonstrated the ability to create a commercial environment that matches US profitability.

These are not gaps in the report — they are the natural boundaries of any analysis that focuses on policy and valuation. But they are the questions that investors need to answer for themselves before making portfolio decisions.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Dual Squeeze

For investors assessing Chinese biopharma exposure, the key is to distinguish between companies based on their dependency on the US market and their ability to generate value through alternative pathways.

The first dimension is US exposure. Companies whose valuation depends heavily on out-licensing to US partners and eventual US commercial sales are the most vulnerable. Those with assets that are already derisked through ongoing US trials or that have established partnerships with non-US collaborators have more resilience.

The second dimension is asset specificity. Companies that are transferring product-level IP — specific drug candidates with clear data — face lower regulatory risk from Chinese restrictions than those whose value depends on platform technologies or core research capabilities. The former can be structured as straightforward licensing deals; the latter may trigger deeper scrutiny.

The third dimension is domestic commercial strength. Companies that can generate meaningful revenue within China are less dependent on the US path. The Chinese market is less profitable, but it is large and growing. A company with a strong domestic commercial engine can afford to wait for the policy environment to clarify.

Applying this framework, the most resilient names are those with derisked US assets, product-level licensing structures, and growing domestic revenue. The most vulnerable are those with early-stage pipelines that depend entirely on future US licensing deals for their valuation.

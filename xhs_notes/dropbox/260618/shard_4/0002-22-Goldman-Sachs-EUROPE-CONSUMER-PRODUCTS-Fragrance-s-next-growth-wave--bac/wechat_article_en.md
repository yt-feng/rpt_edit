# The Fragrance Industry’s Next Growth Phase Will Be Won by Blockbuster Creators, Not Category Riders

The European fragrance market is entering a period of strategic separation. For three years following the pandemic, nearly every player benefited from an expanding consumer base, increased usage frequency, and a tailwind that lifted all boats. That era is ending. As category growth normalises to approximately 3.5% and the number of annual launches surges past 6,000 — more than double the 2,500 seen in 2019 — the industry is shifting from a demand-driven expansion to a supply-driven contest. In this new phase, growth will no longer be a function of riding the category wave. It will be determined by a company’s ability to create new blockbuster pillars that capture consumer attention, drive trial, and sustain commercial momentum over multiple years.

This is not a cyclical shift. It is a structural change in how value is created in the fragrance industry. The companies that understand this — and have the organisational capability to execute on it — will separate from the pack. Those that rely on past successes or broad category exposure will find themselves compressed in valuation and relevance.

The evidence from the last decade is clear. L’Oréal’s fragrance sales grew 8% in 2019, a year when the category was not in a supercycle, driven by three launches including Libre — which six years later became the world’s leading women’s fragrance, overtaking Chanel. Puig’s Good Girl surpassed 3% market share within six years of launch. Interparfums built Jimmy Choo into a franchise generating over €200 million in sales through a steady rollout of new pillars. These are not anomalies. They are proof that blockbuster creation is the most durable growth engine in this industry, and it is about to become the only one that matters.

The market, however, is not fully pricing this reality. Puig and Interparfums trade at 13x and 16x forward P/E respectively, levels that do not appear to reflect the launch pipeline visible over the next three years. L’Oréal’s premium at 26x is partly justified by its execution track record and diversified category footprint, but even that gap versus history suggests room for reassessment. The question is not whether blockbusters matter. It is which companies can deliver them consistently, and what that means for investors who position ahead of the evidence.

## The Post-Covid Tailwind Is Fading, and Launch Intensity Is the New Competitive Moat

The post-Covid period was exceptional for fragrance, but it was also deceptive. The consumer base expanded as younger cohorts entered the category earlier — the average entry age for fragrance in the US dropped from 13 in the 2000s to 11.5 for today’s teens, according to Boston Consulting Group data cited in the report. Usage frequency increased as remote work gave way to social re-engagement. These forces lifted growth across the board, masking differences in execution quality.

That is no longer the case. Category growth is normalising, and the number of new launches has more than doubled. In an offer-driven category where demand is disproportionately shaped by new formats, scents, and activations that drive trial, the competitive landscape is becoming brutally efficient. A brand that does not have a compelling new launch in any given year risks losing shelf space, consumer mindshare, and distributor support. The cost of standing still has never been higher.

This is where the concept of the blockbuster becomes strategic, not just descriptive. A blockbuster is not simply a successful launch. It is a fragrance that achieves sufficient scale and longevity to become a brand anchor — a pillar that generates consistent revenue, supports flanker extensions, and attracts new consumers to the franchise. Libre, La Vie Est Belle, Good Girl, and Jimmy Choo are examples. Each took years to build, but each continues to generate returns long after the initial launch window.

The implication is clear. In a market with 6,000 annual launches, most will fail to achieve critical mass. The winners will be those that concentrate their R&D, marketing, and distribution resources behind a small number of high-conviction bets. This requires discipline, brand equity, and a willingness to invest through the noise. It also requires a portfolio structure that can absorb the risk of a miss. Not every company has that luxury.

## Three Companies Have Visible Blockbuster Pipelines, but the Risk Profiles Are Distinct

The report identifies L’Oréal, Puig, and Interparfums as the companies best positioned to benefit from the next wave of blockbuster launches. Each has a credible pipeline, but the risk-reward profile differs meaningfully across the three.

L’Oréal is the execution benchmark. Its track record is unmatched, and its portfolio breadth provides natural diversification. The report points to the potential for a new Armani pillar — the brand has not had a major launch since My Way in 2020 — and continued development of the Kering Beauty franchise, with Gucci expected by FY28 at the latest. Valentino and Lancôme also screen as credible candidates for new blockbusters. The risk, however, is that L’Oréal’s size and complexity mean that even a successful launch has a smaller marginal impact on overall growth. The premium valuation already embeds a high level of execution confidence.

Puig presents a more asymmetric opportunity. The company is set to launch a women’s blockbuster at Jean Paul Gaultier in H2 2026, a move that could narrow the gap between its feminine and stronger masculine positions. Rabanne is expected to introduce a new pillar in FY27 to reinvigorate the brand following no major men’s launch since Phantom in FY21. Carolina Herrera’s La Bomba, recently launched, is tracking ahead of comparable past launches in search interest, suggesting it is resonating with consumers. If Puig executes on this pipeline, the re-rating potential is significant. The risk is that the company remains heavily reliant on a handful of large franchises, and any misstep in these high-stakes launches would be disproportionately damaging.

Interparfums offers a different model. The company is exclusively exposed to fragrance, which means its fortunes rise and fall with the category. But its disciplined approach to building pillars across Jimmy Choo, Coach, Montblanc, and Lacoste — alongside the debut of Longchamp — provides a diversified launch cadence that reduces single-point failure risk. The valuation at 16x forward P/E suggests the market is pricing in steady execution but not upside surprise. If Interparfums can accelerate growth through its new launches, the upside could be meaningful.

## The Report Raises a Critical Question That It Does Not Fully Answer: Cannibalisation vs. Expansion

One of the most important strategic questions in fragrance is whether a new blockbuster brings new consumers into the brand or simply cannibalises existing pillars. The report acknowledges this uncertainty but does not resolve it. Puig has suggested that La Bomba is attracting a different consumer from the traditional Good Girl audience, which it attributes to a distinct scent profile. That is plausible, but it is not yet proven at scale.

The distinction matters for valuation. If new blockbusters primarily expand the total addressable market for a brand, then the growth runway is long and the return on investment is high. If they mainly shift share within the existing portfolio, then the marginal benefit diminishes, and the case for premium valuation weakens.

This is not a question that can be answered with aggregate category data. It requires brand-level analysis of consumer acquisition costs, repeat purchase rates, and cross-purchase behaviour. The report provides useful frameworks but leaves this as an open analytical question. For investors, this is the kind of uncertainty that creates opportunity — but only for those who do the work to resolve it.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Filters to Evaluate Fragrance Companies

For investors looking to apply the insights from this report, three filters can help separate the companies that will thrive in the next phase from those that will struggle.

First, assess launch pipeline density. A company’s growth in a normalising category will be a function of the number and quality of its upcoming blockbuster candidates. This is not about counting launches; it is about identifying which launches have the brand equity, distribution support, and consumer resonance to become pillars. The report provides a useful starting point by mapping the expected launch cadence for L’Oréal, Puig, and Interparfums, but investors should extend this analysis to other companies in their coverage.

Second, evaluate the diversification of launch risk. A company with a single high-conviction launch is more vulnerable than one with a portfolio of credible candidates across multiple brands. Interparfums’ model of building pillars across four major brands provides natural diversification. Puig’s concentration in a few mega-franchises creates higher upside but also higher downside risk. L’Oréal’s breadth provides the most diversification, but also the lowest marginal impact from any single launch.

Third, monitor the cannibalisation vs. expansion dynamic. This is the hardest filter to apply, but it is also the most important. Investors should look for evidence that new launches are attracting different consumer segments — through scent profiles, price points, distribution channels, or marketing campaigns. The mists category, which the report identifies as a recruitment opportunity for younger consumers, is one area where expansion rather than cannibalisation seems more likely. Companies that can demonstrate they are growing the pie, not just slicing it differently, will deserve premium valuations.

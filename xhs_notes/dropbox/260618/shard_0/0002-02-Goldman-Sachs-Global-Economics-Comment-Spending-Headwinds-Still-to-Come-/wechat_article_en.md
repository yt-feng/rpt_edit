# The Consumer Spending Slowdown Has Not Arrived Yet—But the Data Shows It Is Already Baked In

The most dangerous moment in a macro cycle is when the data looks fine but the mechanics are already broken. Right now, consumer spending on both sides of the Atlantic has held up reasonably well despite the sharpest energy price shock in decades. Headline numbers show resilience. Confidence surveys, while cautious, have not collapsed. And an interim peace deal between the US and Iran has reduced the probability of even higher oil prices, leading major forecasters to revise down their Brent crude outlook to $80 per barrel by late 2026. These are genuinely positive developments.

But they are also misleading. The argument of this article is straightforward: the consumer spending headwinds have not been avoided—they have merely been delayed. Three structural forces are converging to produce a drag on real cash flow and spending that will materialize over the next two to three quarters, even under the most benign energy price assumptions. The passthrough from wholesale energy to consumer prices is slower than markets assume. Seasonal demand patterns will amplify the income hit this winter, especially in Europe. And a large, one-time fiscal boost in the United States has already reversed.

Investors who extrapolate recent spending resilience into the second half of the year are making a timing error with material portfolio consequences. The data that matters is not the spending data we have seen, but the cash flow mechanics that are still working their way through the system.

## The Energy Passthrough Is Not Complete, and the Lag Is Longer Than Markets Assume

The first and most underappreciated force is the slow and incomplete passthrough from wholesale energy prices to the consumer prices that actually drive household budgets. Gasoline prices rose quickly after the conflict began, and consumers felt that immediately at the pump. But natural gas and electricity prices—which matter far more for household energy bills, particularly in Europe—have risen far less so far. This is not because the wholesale price shock has been contained. It is because of structural lags built into the system.

Fixed-price contracts, government regulated tariffs, and utility hedging programs all slow the transmission of wholesale price increases to end consumers. In Europe, where natural gas and electricity account for a much larger share of household energy consumption than in the US, this lag is especially pronounced. A global investment bank report notes that its European economists have consistently highlighted that regulatory frameworks and contract structures lead to more gradual price increases relative to wholesale movements. The implication is clear: a large share of the consumer price increases from the wholesale energy shock has yet to be realized.

This is not a forecast of higher energy prices. Even if wholesale prices stay flat from here, the consumer price level for household energy will continue to rise mechanically as older, cheaper contracts roll off and are replaced by contracts priced at current wholesale levels. The peak impact on real household cash flow from energy costs alone will arrive months after the wholesale price shock itself has peaked. Markets that look at headline inflation and assume the consumer impact has passed are missing the lagged transmission mechanism entirely.

## Winter Demand Will Transform a Cash Flow Drag into a Cash Flow Crunch, Particularly in Europe

The second force is seasonal, but its magnitude is systematically underestimated in standard macroeconomic models. Most analyses of energy price impacts on consumer spending use seasonally adjusted income measures or assume fixed consumption shares across the year. This approach smooths out the very real cash flow pressure that households experience when energy demand is highest.

In winter, households consume dramatically more natural gas and electricity for heating. This means that a given percentage increase in energy prices translates into a much larger absolute hit to disposable cash flow during the cold months. The seasonal effect is not trivial. In Europe, where winter heating demand is highest and energy intensity per household is greater than in the US, the incremental drag from seasonal demand alone is estimated to lower real incomes by an additional 14 basis points in the first quarter of 2027. That is more than 20 percent of the total combined energy price impulse.

The timing matters enormously. Under a fixed-consumption-share assumption, the trough of the cash flow hit in Europe would arrive in mid-2026. But once seasonal demand is accounted for, the trough shifts to early 2027. This means that the worst of the consumer spending drag in Europe has not yet arrived and will not arrive for several more quarters. The pattern is similar, if less extreme, in the United States, where the seasonal effect adds roughly 5 basis points to the winter quarter drag.

For portfolio construction, this has a clear implication: the energy price shock is not a one-time event to be priced in and moved past. It is a recurring quarterly drag that builds through the winter, peaks later than consensus expects, and then fades slowly. The path of consumer spending in the second half of this year and into early next year will look very different from the path in the first half.

## The US Fiscal Boost Has Already Reversed, Creating a Sharp Swing in Real Cash Flow

The third force is specific to the United States and is the most immediately impactful. In the first half of this year, larger-than-usual tax refunds provided a temporary and significant boost to real household cash flow. The numbers are striking: the boost was estimated at 0.8 percent in the first quarter and 1.2 percent in the second quarter. These are large, one-time fiscal transfers that temporarily masked the underlying pressure from energy prices.

That boost is now gone. Tax refunds have been fully paid out, and the year-over-year comparison is about to turn sharply negative. Combining the reversal of the fiscal boost with the ongoing energy price drag implies a swing from a 0.7 percent positive contribution to real cash flows in the second quarter to a 0.6 percent drag in the third quarter. That is a swing of 1.3 percentage points in a single quarter—a magnitude that is almost certain to show up in spending data, even if the precise timing and transmission lag are uncertain.

The statistical relationship between real cash flow and consumer spending is well-established. Based on historical relationships, a 1 percent decline in real cash flow from an energy shock leads to approximately a 0.6 percent decline in the level of consumer spending after two quarters. Applying this framework to the combined cash flow headwinds from energy prices and fiscal reversal implies peak spending drags of 0.5 percent in Canada, 0.4 percent in the euro area, and 0.3 percent in both the United States and the United Kingdom.

These numbers may not sound catastrophic, but they represent a meaningful deceleration from recent trends. More importantly, the model suggests that the spending drag in the United States has not yet begun to materialize in the data. In the euro area, United Kingdom, and Canada, only about half of the peak spending hit is estimated to have been realized by the end of the second quarter. The rest is still in the pipeline.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Asymmetry of Consumer Response and the Role of Savings Buffers

Any rigorous analysis must acknowledge its own limitations, and this one has two important open questions that the underlying report does not fully resolve.

The first is whether the historical relationship between real cash flow and consumer spending holds in an environment where households have accumulated significant excess savings. The pandemic-era savings buffers in most developed economies are still above pre-crisis levels, particularly in the United States and parts of Europe. If households are willing to draw down savings to maintain consumption, the spending response to a cash flow shock could be smaller than historical relationships suggest. The report's model does not explicitly account for this asymmetry, and it is a reasonable question whether the 0.6 percent spending response per 1 percent cash flow decline is too high or too low in the current context.

The second open question is about the composition of the spending response. The historical relationship is an aggregate estimate. But the evidence from consumer surveys, which the report cites, suggests that households are planning to reduce large discretionary purchases specifically. If the spending pullback is concentrated in durable goods and big-ticket items, the impact on GDP could be larger than the aggregate spending drag implies, because durable goods have higher multiplier effects and are more sensitive to credit conditions. If, on the other hand, households smooth the adjustment by reducing saving rather than cutting spending, the GDP impact could be smaller. The report does not provide a framework for distinguishing between these two scenarios, and the answer will depend on household confidence, credit availability, and the trajectory of real wages.

These are not criticisms of the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of any model-based forecast. But they are precisely the questions that a serious investor should be asking when deciding how to position for the second half of the year.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: What to Watch and When to Act

The analytical framework in this report translates naturally into a decision framework for portfolio construction. The key insight is that the timing of the spending drag is both predictable and delayed, which means there is a window for preparation but also a risk of complacency.

The first decision point is geographic. The spending drag is most backloaded in Europe, where the seasonal demand effect pushes the trough into early 2027. This implies that European consumer-exposed equities and currencies may face headwinds that are not yet priced in, particularly as we move through the fourth quarter and into the first quarter of next year. The United States faces a sharper but earlier swing, driven by the fiscal reversal in the third quarter, which means the US consumer data will likely deteriorate before the European data does.

The second decision point is sectoral. If the spending pullback is concentrated in discretionary durable goods, as the survey evidence suggests, then consumer discretionary sectors—particularly autos, home furnishings, and electronics—are most exposed. Staples, healthcare, and services that are less discretionary may be relatively insulated. The report does not provide sector-level estimates, but the logic is straightforward: the cash flow shock is largest for households with higher energy intensity in their consumption baskets, and these households are also the ones most likely to cut back on large purchases first.

The third decision point is about the energy price assumption itself. The report's forecasts assume Brent crude at $80 per barrel by late 2026, which is a significant downgrade from earlier estimates. If energy prices surprise to the upside—due to supply disruptions, geopolitical escalation, or stronger-than-expected demand—then the spending drag estimates in this report would be too low. If energy prices surprise to the downside, the drag would be smaller. The base case is already conservative relative to pre-deal expectations, but the risk asymmetry is worth noting: an upside energy surprise would amplify an already negative spending dynamic.

The final decision point is about the savings buffer. The report does not model it, but investors must form their own view. If households are willing to run down savings, the spending drag will be smaller and shorter. If they are not—because of uncertainty about the labor market, higher interest rates, or a desire to rebuild precautionary buffers—the drag will be larger and more persistent. This is the most important judgment call that the data alone cannot resolve.

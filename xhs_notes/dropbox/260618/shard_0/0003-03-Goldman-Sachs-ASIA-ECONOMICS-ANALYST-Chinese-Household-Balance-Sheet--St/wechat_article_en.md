# The Great Rotation in Chinese Household Wealth Has Begun, But the Consumption Payoff Will Be Delayed

For more than two decades, the Chinese household balance sheet told a simple story: buy property, watch it appreciate, borrow against it, and consume. That narrative has now fractured. The aggregate household balance sheet is no longer expanding. It peaked in early 2023, declined for roughly six quarters, and has since stabilized at approximately RMB 730 trillion. The composition of that wealth is undergoing a structural shift that will reshape consumption patterns, capital market flows, and the transmission mechanism of Chinese monetary policy for years to come.

The controlling insight is this: Chinese households are simultaneously reallocating assets away from property toward financial instruments while continuing to deleverage. These two forces pull in opposite directions for consumption. Asset reallocation supports financial markets but does not directly boost spending, because property still dominates household wealth and its wealth effect is far more powerful than that of equities. Deleveraging constrains spending directly by reducing disposable income available for consumption. The net effect is that the consumer recovery will lag the financial market recovery, and the transmission from policy stimulus to household spending will remain weak until property prices stabilize more broadly.

This matters now because the September 2024 policy pivot marked a genuine inflection point in financial asset performance, but the property market has not yet followed. Investors who assume that equity gains will translate quickly into stronger consumption are likely to be disappointed. The data from a quarterly household balance sheet tracker, constructed to address the long lags in official data, makes this disconnection visible for the first time in a timely manner.

## The Composition of Household Wealth Has Shifted Dramatically, and the Trend Will Accelerate

The most important single data point in the tracker is the change in asset composition between mid-2021, when the property market peaked, and early 2026. In that period, property's share of household total assets fell from 67 percent to 52 percent. Cash and deposits rose from 16 percent to 25 percent. Other financial assets, including equities and bonds, increased from 15 percent to 20 percent. Directly held equities within that category moved from 5 percent to 6 percent.

This is not a cyclical adjustment. It is the early stage of a structural shift driven by three irreversible forces. First, the property market's role as a reliable store of wealth has been broken by a roughly 30 percent decline in nominal home prices from the peak. The psychological scar from that loss will take years to heal, and it will permanently alter the risk appetite of a generation of Chinese households. Second, deposit rates have been driven to historically low levels as the PBOC eases monetary policy, making cash holdings increasingly unattractive as a store of value. Third, the financial system is gradually developing alternative investment channels, including insurance products, wealth management products, and equity market participation mechanisms that were underdeveloped during the property boom.

The implication for investors is straightforward but profound. The marginal buyer of Chinese financial assets over the next decade will increasingly be the Chinese household sector, not foreign institutional investors or domestic institutions. This creates a structural tailwind for equities, insurance, and certain bond products, but it also means that the household sector's risk preferences and liquidity needs will become a more dominant factor in determining asset prices. The old regime, in which property market conditions drove household sentiment and therefore consumption, is being replaced by a more complex regime in which financial asset returns and property prices jointly determine household behavior.

## Deleveraging Continues, and the Debt Service Burden Remains the Binding Constraint on Consumption

On the liability side, the tracker reveals a household sector that is still in repair mode. Outstanding mortgage balances have been declining as households make early repayments and new home purchase demand remains subdued. Short-term consumer loans have also softened. The headline household debt-to-GDP ratio, which peaked at 61 percent in early 2024 and has since moderated to approximately 59 percent, looks moderate compared to developed markets. The United States is around 70 percent, Japan around 62 percent.

But the debt-to-income ratio tells a different and more troubling story. Chinese household debt is high relative to income flows, which means the debt service burden is heavy even if the stock of debt is not extreme by international standards. This is the binding constraint on consumption. Households cannot spend freely when a large portion of their income must go to servicing existing debt, particularly when income growth expectations have deteriorated.

The policy response has been to lower mortgage rates and introduce interest subsidies for consumer loans. These measures reduce the cost of debt service but do not address the stock of debt itself. For households that are already deleveraging voluntarily, lower rates may simply accelerate the repayment process rather than freeing up income for consumption. The effectiveness of monetary easing in stimulating household spending is therefore muted in an environment where households prefer to reduce debt rather than take on new debt.

This creates a strategic dilemma for policymakers. If they want to boost consumption, they need either to engineer a sustained recovery in household income growth or to find a way to reduce the debt stock directly. The former requires labor market improvement, which is slow. The latter would require some form of debt restructuring or forgiveness, which carries moral hazard and financial stability risks. The tracker data suggests that the deleveraging phase has further to run, and consumption will remain suppressed until household balance sheets are more fully repaired.

## The Housing Wealth Effect Dominates, and Financial Asset Gains Are Concentrated Among the Wealthy

The tracker allows us to quantify why the equity market recovery has not translated into a consumption boom. Property accounts for roughly 60 percent of household assets, while equities account for less than 10 percent. More than 90 percent of households own property, while only about 25 percent of Chinese adults participate in the equity market. A 10 percent gain in equity prices therefore has a much smaller impact on aggregate household wealth than a 10 percent gain in property prices, and the gains from equity are concentrated among higher-income households who have a lower marginal propensity to consume.

This distributional effect is critical for understanding the macro outlook. The wealthy households that benefit from equity gains are likely to save a larger portion of those gains or invest them in additional financial assets rather than spending them. The mass of middle-income and lower-income households, whose wealth is concentrated in housing, are still experiencing negative wealth effects from falling property prices even if the rate of decline has slowed. Their consumption behavior will not improve until they feel that their housing wealth is stabilizing or recovering.

There are early signs that the property drag may be easing. The latest high-frequency data show stable sales in both new and existing homes. Official indices suggest that sequential price declines have moderated, and prices in Tier-1 cities have risen for both new and existing homes. If Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities achieve local home price stabilization within the next year or two, the impact on confidence and spending could be meaningful. But this is a conditional forecast, not a certainty. The tracker provides the framework to monitor this process in real time, but it cannot predict the timing or magnitude of the recovery.

## What the Tracker Cannot Yet Tell Us: Three Open Questions

The quarterly household balance sheet tracker is a significant improvement over the lagged official data, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. First, the tracker does not capture household financial investments held through less transparent channels, such as unlisted equity holdings. This means the official estimate of financial asset holdings is lower than the true level, and the shift toward financial assets may be even more pronounced than the data suggest. But it also means that we cannot fully assess the risk exposure of households to private assets that may be illiquid or overvalued.

Second, the tracker cannot distinguish between voluntary and involuntary deleveraging. Some households are repaying mortgages early because they have the financial capacity to do so and prefer to reduce debt. Others are repaying because they are forced to sell assets or because their income has fallen. The economic implications of these two scenarios are very different. Voluntary deleveraging is a sign of balance sheet strength; involuntary deleveraging is a sign of distress. The aggregate data cannot tell us which is dominant.

Third, the tracker provides no information about the distribution of assets and liabilities within the household sector. If the wealth losses from property are concentrated among highly leveraged households while the financial asset gains are concentrated among wealthy households with low leverage, the aggregate balance sheet may appear stable while the underlying financial health of the median household continues to deteriorate. Understanding this distribution is essential for forecasting consumption, but the data to assess it is not available on a quarterly basis.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Monitoring the Household Balance Sheet

For investors who want to use this tracker to inform their positioning, three metrics should be monitored on a quarterly basis. The first is the ratio of property assets to total assets. A continued decline in this ratio below 50 percent would confirm that the structural shift is accelerating and would strengthen the case for a sustained allocation to Chinese equities and insurance products. A stabilization or reversal of this ratio would suggest that property is regaining its role as the primary store of wealth, which would have negative implications for financial assets.

The second metric is the household debt-to-income ratio. As long as this ratio remains elevated, consumption will be constrained regardless of what happens to asset prices. A sustained decline in this ratio would be the most reliable leading indicator of a consumption recovery. The tracker can be used to estimate this ratio on a quarterly basis by combining the liability data with estimates of household disposable income derived from national accounts data.

The third metric is the contribution of equity gains to total financial asset growth. If equities are contributing more than 20 percent of financial asset growth on a sustained basis, it would signal that the wealth effect from financial assets is becoming large enough to potentially offset some of the drag from property. Below that threshold, the property wealth effect will continue to dominate household behavior.

These three metrics together provide a framework for assessing whether the household balance sheet is healing or deteriorating, and whether the consumption recovery is imminent or still distant. The tracker makes this assessment possible in real time for the first time, but the interpretation requires judgment about the distributional dynamics that the aggregate data cannot capture.

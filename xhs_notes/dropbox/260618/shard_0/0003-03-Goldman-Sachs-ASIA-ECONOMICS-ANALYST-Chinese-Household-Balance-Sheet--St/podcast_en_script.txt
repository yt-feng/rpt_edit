EN_A: So there has been a lot of talk about whether Chinese household wealth is recovering or still shrinking. And this report really caught my eye because it actually builds a quarterly tracker for that. B, what is the big picture takeaway?
EN_B: The big takeaway from this global investment bank report is that total household assets peaked in early 2023, then declined for about six quarters, and have now stabilized at around 730 trillion RMB. But the real story is not just the number. It is the structural shift underneath. The engine of household wealth growth is rotating away from property and toward financial assets.
EN_A: So what does that actually mean for the average person? Is the old idea that housing is the ultimate store of wealth breaking down?
EN_B: Exactly. The report puts numbers on this shift. Back in mid-2021, when the property market peaked, housing made up 67 percent of household assets. Cash and deposits were 16 percent. Other financial assets like stocks and bonds were 15 percent. Fast forward to early 2025, housing dropped to 52 percent, cash and deposits rose to 25 percent, and other financial assets climbed to 20 percent. And this trend is still ongoing.
EN_A: So if housing is shrinking, does that mean the stock market is now the main driver of household wealth recovery?
EN_B: That is the nuance here. The report points out that even though financial assets are growing, housing still has a much bigger wealth effect on consumption. More than 90 percent of households own property, while only about 25 percent of adults participate in the stock market. So falling home prices hit mass consumption harder, while financial asset gains mostly benefit a narrower, higher-income group.
EN_A: What about the debt side? A lot of people are worried about mortgage pressure.
EN_B: On the liability side, there is clear deleveraging. Household leverage has been declining since mid-2024, mainly because mortgage balances are shrinking. The report shows that the household debt-to-GDP ratio peaked at 61 percent in early 2024 and has since fallen to about 59 percent by the third quarter of 2025. But here is the catch: the debt-to-income ratio remains high. So debt servicing is still a heavy burden relative to household cash flow.
EN_A: Has the government done anything to help?
EN_B: Yes, policymakers have cut mortgage rates and introduced interest subsidies for consumer loans. But the impact has been limited so far. The labor market is still weak and household confidence is fragile. So the household sector remains in a deleveraging phase.
EN_A: So what is the forward-looking view from this report? Where might savings flow next?
EN_B: This is the most intriguing part. The report argues that Chinese household asset allocation is at the early stage of a structural shift. As housing loses its role as a wealth accumulator and deposit rates stay low, savings are likely to gradually migrate toward broader financial assets. Equities and insurance are seen as the key medium-term beneficiaries. But the report leaves a big question open: how fast can this shift happen? It really depends on whether home prices can truly stabilize.
EN_A: And what does the report say about home price stabilization?
EN_B: It sees some early positive signals in Tier-1 cities. For example, prices in Shenzhen and Shanghai have seen month-on-month increases for both new and existing homes. The report expects Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities to achieve local price stabilization within the next year or two. But it also adds a caution: if there is a major negative shock, like significant AI-driven job displacement, that outlook would need to be reassessed.

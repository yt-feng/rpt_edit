# Data Center Pricing Power Has Entered a New Regime, and the Enterprise AI Demand Story Is Still Being Written

The narrative around AI infrastructure has bifurcated. On one side, hyperscaler demand for data center capacity remains unrelenting, driving pricing power to levels that were unthinkable just three years ago. On the other side, enterprise adoption of AI — the second wave that many investors are counting on to sustain the cycle — is still in its earliest, most tentative stages. A recent investor trip to Denver, which included a tour of a Flexential data center and management meetings with both Flexential and Lumen Technologies, provides a rare ground-level view of where the industry actually stands. The conclusions are simultaneously bullish and sobering.

The core argument emerging from this fieldwork is straightforward: the data center supply-demand imbalance is structural, not cyclical, and it is creating a new pricing regime that will persist for years. However, the enterprise AI demand that would justify the next leg of infrastructure buildout remains largely a promise, not a reality. Investors who conflate hyperscaler momentum with broad-based enterprise adoption are likely misreading the opportunity set. The winners will be those who understand which parts of the infrastructure stack have genuine pricing power and which are still waiting for demand to arrive.

This article synthesizes the key insights from the trip and extends them into a strategic framework for investors. It does not summarize the report. It argues a thesis.

## Data Center Pricing Has More Than Doubled in Three Years, and Structural Constraints Will Keep It There

The most striking data point from the Denver trip is the trajectory of data center pricing. Flexential, a private data center operator, reports that pricing per kilowatt for new leases is approaching $200 and is expected to trend toward $250 later this year. To put that in perspective, the comparable figure was roughly $70 in 2021. That is a more than 250% increase in pricing power over a four-year period, a magnitude of change that is rare in any asset class, let alone one as capital-intensive and long-cycle as data centers.

This is not a temporary spike. The pricing power is being driven by a confluence of structural constraints that show no signs of easing. On the supply side, lead times for critical infrastructure components have stretched dramatically. Substations now require four years to deliver. Power generators require two years. The result is that new data center builds are taking approximately three years to complete, nearly double the timeline in 2021. Even when a site is acquired, leasable capacity takes about 18 months to become available. These are not bottlenecks that can be solved by throwing more capital at them; they are fundamental constraints in the global supply chain for electrical equipment and construction labor.

The demand side, by contrast, is being driven by hyperscalers whose capital expenditure plans are measured in tens of billions of dollars per quarter. These customers are not price-sensitive in the traditional sense. They need capacity, and they need it now. Flexential's strategy of avoiding pre-selling new capacity more than nine months in advance — even as its Atlanta Douglasville 2 site is 85% pre-sold with final completion in 4Q27 — is a deliberate choice to maximize pricing power. The company is effectively betting that waiting will yield higher rents, and the market is validating that bet.

The implication for investors is that data center real estate investment trusts and private operators with existing capacity are sitting on an asset that is appreciating faster than most financial models can capture. The risk is not oversupply; it is that lease renegotiations may not fully capture the new pricing environment. Flexential is negotiating 3-4% annual escalators on wholesale deals and 5-6% on multi-tenancy deals. Those are attractive, but they lag the spot market. The real value creation will come from new leases, not renewals.

## The Enterprise AI Demand Story Is Real, but It Is Not Yet a Demand Curve

The report makes an important distinction that many market narratives gloss over: hyperscaler AI demand and enterprise AI demand are fundamentally different phenomena, and they are on very different trajectories. Flexential's Englewood data center, which the tour visited, supports a 60/40 split of AI to non-AI workloads. But the vast majority of that AI workload is coming from large customers — hyperscalers and large enterprises — not from the broad base of mid-market and small enterprises that would represent the next wave of demand.

Lumen's management was candid on this point. They described enterprise AI use as "relatively nascent" and acknowledged that the company's Networking-as-a-Service (NaaS) platform and cloud on-ramps are primarily serving enterprise customers who are still in the early stages of AI deployment. The positive sign is that more than 20% of NaaS net adds in the first quarter of 2026 came from new customers, and more than 60% were expanding their footprint. That suggests early adopters are scaling, which is a necessary condition for a broader adoption cycle.

But scaling from early adopters to mainstream adoption is never linear. The enterprise AI infrastructure demand that would justify a second wave of data center buildout — the kind that would sustain pricing power beyond the current hyperscaler cycle — requires that companies across industries move from experimentation to production deployment. That transition is happening, but the data from the field suggests it is happening more slowly than the market expects. The report does not provide a timeline for when enterprise AI demand will reach an inflection point, and that is one of its most important open questions.

The strategic implication is that investors should be cautious about extrapolating hyperscaler demand trends to the broader infrastructure ecosystem. Companies that are dependent on enterprise AI adoption for their growth — particularly in areas like fiber connectivity, edge computing, and colocation for mid-market customers — may face a longer wait than current valuations imply.

## Fiber Networks Are Becoming a Strategic Moat, but Only for Those Who Own the Physical Layer

Lumen's presentation offered a compelling case for why fiber infrastructure is becoming more valuable, not less, in the AI era. The company's competitive advantage rests on two pillars: its vast physical network of fiber, and its ability to offer private, direct connections to hyperscaler cloud platforms through its multi-cloud gateway offering. The argument is that as enterprises deploy AI workloads, they will need low-latency, secure, private connections to the cloud — and that Lumen's fiber network, combined with its cloud on-ramps, provides exactly that.

The key insight is that Lumen's recent private connectivity fabric (PCF) deals with hyperscalers are not just revenue events. They are strategic infrastructure investments. Because the hyperscalers are effectively pre-paying for fiber capacity, Lumen can expand its physical network at a drastically reduced build-out cost. The proximity of Lumen's fiber to hyperscaler data centers then becomes a self-reinforcing advantage: it enables Lumen's multi-cloud gateways, which in turn attract enterprise customers, which in turn justifies further fiber investment.

This is a classic network effect, but it is one that is only available to companies that already own significant physical infrastructure. The report estimates the total addressable market for East-West connectivity — the type of traffic that flows between data centers and cloud regions — at $58 billion, growing at a 13% compound annual rate. Lumen's proposed acquisition of Alkira, a cloud networking platform, is designed to accelerate its ability to capture that market by adding a software-defined networking layer on top of its physical network.

The question that the report does not fully answer is whether Lumen's fiber advantage is durable or will be competed away. The company acknowledges that enterprise adoption is nascent and that competition for incremental private connectivity fabric deals is intensifying. The moat is real, but it is not unbreachable. Investors need to assess whether Lumen can convert its first-mover advantage into a sustainable market share position before larger competitors — or alternative technologies — erode its lead.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Timing and Shape of Enterprise AI Adoption

For all its valuable field evidence, the report leaves several critical questions open. The most important is the timing of enterprise AI adoption. The data from Flexential and Lumen suggests that enterprise AI workloads are growing, but the growth is from a very small base and is concentrated among the largest, most technologically sophisticated companies. When will the mid-market arrive? What will trigger that adoption? And what will it look like in terms of infrastructure demand?

A second open question is the impact of technological change on infrastructure requirements. The report notes that Flexential's Englewood site is an 18-megawatt air-cooled facility supporting a mix of AI and non-AI workloads. But the industry is rapidly moving toward liquid cooling and higher-density configurations for AI workloads. How quickly will existing air-cooled capacity become obsolete? Will the pricing power that operators are enjoying today survive a wave of technological obsolescence, or will it require continuous capital expenditure to maintain?

A third question relates to the competitive dynamics in fiber. Lumen's strategy depends on the idea that enterprises will want private, direct connections to hyperscaler clouds. But the hyperscalers themselves are building their own fiber networks and offering their own connectivity solutions. Will they disintermediate companies like Lumen, or will they continue to rely on third-party fiber providers? The answer has profound implications for the fiber industry's long-term economics.

These are not criticisms of the report. They are the natural analytical questions that emerge from good fieldwork. The report provides the data and the context; the investment community must do the work of forming views on the unresolved questions.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the AI Infrastructure Landscape

Based on the evidence from the Denver trip, investors should evaluate AI infrastructure opportunities using a three-dimensional framework: pricing power durability, demand visibility, and competitive moat.

First, pricing power durability. The data center market has entered a new pricing regime, but not all operators will benefit equally. The key variable is the mix of new leases versus renewals. Operators with significant new capacity coming online in the next 12-24 months are best positioned to capture spot pricing. Operators with long-dated leases negotiated at pre-2021 pricing will see slower earnings growth, even if their assets are appreciating. The report's evidence suggests that the pricing power is real and structural, but its translation into shareholder returns depends on the operator's lease maturity profile.

Second, demand visibility. Hyperscaler demand is visible, contractually committed, and growing. Enterprise demand is real but nascent and unpredictable. Investors should apply a discount to revenue streams that depend on enterprise AI adoption, and a premium to those that are already backed by hyperscaler contracts. The distinction matters because the two demand sources have very different risk profiles. Hyperscaler demand is cyclical in the sense that it depends on their capital expenditure budgets, but it is not discretionary in the same way that enterprise IT spending is.

Third, competitive moat. In data centers, the moat is increasingly about power access and construction capability, not just location. In fiber, the moat is about physical network scale and the ability to offer multi-cloud connectivity. In both cases, the moat is becoming more expensive to replicate, which favors incumbents. But the report also highlights a risk: the same supply chain constraints that create pricing power for incumbents also create opportunities for new entrants who can secure power and equipment. The moat is real, but it is not absolute.

## The Questions That Will Define the Next Phase of the Cycle

The Denver trip provides a rare window into the operational realities of AI infrastructure. The message is clear: the supply-demand imbalance is real and structural, and pricing power has entered a new regime. But the enterprise AI demand that would sustain that regime beyond the current hyperscaler buildout is still in its infancy.

The most important analytical question for investors is not whether AI infrastructure is a growth market — it clearly is. The question is which parts of the value chain have pricing power that is sustainable, and which parts are dependent on a demand inflection that has not yet arrived. The evidence from the field suggests that data center operators with existing capacity are in the former category, while fiber and connectivity providers serving enterprise customers are in the latter.

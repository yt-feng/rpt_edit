# China's Auto Market Is Not Weakening Uniformly: The Divergence Within the Slowdown Reveals the Real Winners

The headline numbers from May and early June suggest a Chinese auto market under pressure. Overall passenger vehicle sales missed expectations. Internal combustion engine sales collapsed, and new energy vehicle sales were flat month-over-month. The natural assumption is that the entire sector is slowing. That assumption is wrong. The data from a recent expert call with auto journalist Mr. Sun Shaojun reveals a market that is not simply weak but structurally divergent. The real story is not about a uniform slowdown. It is about a market splitting along price points, powertrain types, and brand positions. Understanding this divergence is the only way to make strategic sense of the current environment.

The critical insight is that weakness is concentrated in the premium segment, while the mass market below RMB 200,000 remains resilient. This is not a demand collapse. It is a demand shift. Customers are still buying cars, but they are voting with their wallets against traditional luxury and for value-oriented new energy vehicles. The collapse in ICE sales did not benefit NEV overall because the ICE buyer is not switching brands; they are leaving the market entirely or deferring purchases. The NEV buyer, however, is still active but increasingly price-sensitive. The result is a market where volume is holding up for the right products at the right price points, while the high end is bleeding.

This divergence has immediate implications for which companies are positioned to weather the current soft patch and which are facing structural erosion. It also sets the stage for a potential policy catalyst later this year. If the market remains sluggish, stimulus is likely to arrive after July, following historical patterns. But stimulus will not rescue every player equally. The companies that are already winning the battle for the resilient mass market will be the primary beneficiaries. The luxury incumbents and the premium NEV brands that have lost their differentiation face a much harder road.

## The Premium Segment Is Collapsing While the Mass Market Holds, and That Changes the Competitive Calculus

The most striking data point from the call is the collapse in weekly orders for traditional German luxury brands. BBA weekly orders fell to below 7,000 units last week, compared to 11,000 to 12,000 units in the same period last year. That is a decline of roughly 40 percent year-over-year. BMW China was at 6,500 units, Audi at roughly 6,000, and Mercedes-Benz at under 7,000. This is not a cyclical dip. It is a structural share loss to domestic premium NEV brands that is accelerating.

The expert noted that the luxury segment, including both traditional BBA and the flagship SUVs of NEV brands, has seen a sharp order decline in June. This is a critical nuance. It is not just the old guard that is suffering. Even the high-end models from domestic NEV makers are seeing softening demand. The premium segment as a whole is under pressure, not just the incumbents. This suggests that the issue is not simply brand preference but a broader hesitation among higher-income consumers to commit to large-ticket purchases in the current economic environment.

The mass market below RMB 200,000 tells a different story. BYD and Leapmotor are maintaining resilient weekly orders of roughly 50,000 and 13,000 units respectively. These are not just holding steady; they are demonstrating that demand exists when the price-value equation is right. The implication is clear: the current market weakness is not a demand problem for the industry as a whole. It is a demand problem for the premium segment. Companies that compete in the mass market with strong value propositions are largely insulated from the broader slowdown.

For investors and strategists, this means that the competitive landscape is being reshaped in real time. The traditional luxury brands are losing their customer base not because those customers are disappearing but because they are migrating to domestic NEV brands. However, even some domestic premium models are now facing headwinds, suggesting that the migration has limits. The mass market winners are the ones that have successfully captured the price-sensitive buyer who is still active. The strategic question is whether these mass market leaders can maintain their momentum or whether the premium weakness will eventually pull the entire market down.

## BYD and Leapmotor Are Winning Through Structural Advantages That Are Difficult to Replicate

The resilience of BYD and Leapmotor is not accidental. It is the result of specific structural advantages that are proving decisive in the current environment. For BYD, the key factors are overseas demand and battery technology. The expert noted that BYD has repurchased small battery PHEV models from dealers for overseas sales, indicating that international demand is robust enough to absorb domestic surplus. This creates a buffer that most competitors lack. If domestic demand softens further, BYD can redirect volume to export markets.

The second-generation blade battery is another advantage. The expert indicated that monthly production capacity for the new battery is ramping up at 20,000 to 30,000 units each month. Customer feedback has been decent. The launch of the Great Tang on June 17 is expected to further boost weekly orders. BYD is not just maintaining volume; it is upgrading its product cycle at a time when competitors are struggling to hold their ground.

Leapmotor presents a different but equally compelling story. Its weekly orders of 12,500 to 13,000 units are remarkable for a brand that is still building its reputation. The A10 model is contributing 4,000 to 4,500 units, and the B series is adding 2,000 to 3,000. The upgraded C series launched on June 16 is expected to provide a further boost. Leapmotor is proving that there is room for a second strong mass-market player alongside BYD, particularly if it can maintain its pricing discipline.

The contrast with other brands is stark. Li Auto is at 4,500 units per week, with the L series contributing only roughly 2,000 units. The L9 is below 1,000 units. The expert attributed this to pressure from the ES9 and M9. Li Auto, which was once seen as a premium NEV leader, is now being squeezed from both sides: by mass-market alternatives and by other premium competitors. Xiaomi is at 5,000 units, down from 6,000 two weeks ago. Tesla China is at over 10,000 units, but that is driven by the upcoming FSD launch, which is a one-time catalyst rather than a sustainable trend.

The so-what is clear: the market is rewarding companies that have either a cost advantage or a technology advantage that translates into a compelling price-value proposition. BYD has both. Leapmotor has the cost advantage. The others are struggling to differentiate themselves in a market that is increasingly commoditized at the premium end.

## The EREV Thesis Is Fading, and the Implications for Product Strategy Are Significant

One of the most thought-provoking observations from the expert call is the outlook for extended-range electric vehicles. The expert anticipates that the EREV penetration decline trend will be sustained going forward. The reasoning is that EREV battery capacity has hit its ceiling with electric vehicle mileage at roughly 400 kilometers. Meanwhile, entry-level BEVs at around RMB 100,000 can already achieve 600 to 700 kilometers of electric range.

This is a significant strategic insight. The EREV segment has been one of the fastest-growing areas of the NEV market, with Li Auto and others building their entire product strategy around it. If EREV penetration is indeed peaking, then companies that have bet heavily on this technology face a fundamental product cycle risk. The value proposition of an EREV was that it eliminated range anxiety while offering an electric driving experience. As BEV range continues to improve and prices continue to fall, that value proposition erodes.

The expert also noted that entry-level BEVs are now achieving ranges that were previously only available in premium models. This is a direct threat to the EREV segment. It also has implications for the premium BEV segment, which has been relying on range as a differentiator. If a RMB 100,000 BEV can do 600 kilometers, what is the premium for a RMB 300,000 BEV that does 700 kilometers? The answer is increasingly less.

For product planners and investors, this suggests that the window for EREV growth is closing. Companies that are heavily exposed to EREV need to accelerate their BEV transitions or risk being left with a technology that is no longer competitive. The expert's view also implies that the next wave of NEV growth will come from BEV penetration in the mass market, not from EREV. This favors companies like BYD and Leapmotor that already have strong BEV offerings.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How the Joint Venture Crisis Will Unfold or What It Means for Supply Chains

While the expert call provides granular data on NEV brands, it is notably sparse on the joint venture automakers. The expert anticipates that some JV automakers may face production halts. This year, JV brands may need to make a choice: either fully localize for the Chinese market or leave the Chinese market altogether. This is a dramatic statement, but the report does not provide the data to assess which JVs are most at risk or what the timeline might be.

This is a meaningful gap. The JV segment still accounts for a significant share of total vehicle production in China, and its decline has supply chain implications that extend well beyond the automakers themselves. If a major JV halts production, it would disrupt parts suppliers, dealership networks, and even the used car market. The report does not address which JVs have the balance sheet to survive a prolonged downturn or which are likely to exit first.

There is also no analysis of how a JV exit would affect the domestic NEV players. Would it free up market share for BYD and Leapmotor? Or would it create a demand vacuum that depresses pricing across the board? The answer depends on whether the JV customers migrate to NEV brands or simply stop buying cars. The expert's earlier observation that ICE sales collapsed without benefiting NEV suggests that the migration is not automatic. A JV exit could simply mean fewer cars sold, which would be negative for the entire industry.

Another open question is the impact of raw material cost inflation. The expert noted that some models could see price hikes in the second half of 2026. The launch prices for the Leapmotor A10 and the new BYD model are already higher than expected because rising raw material costs have been factored in. This is a double-edged sword. It supports margins for companies that can pass through costs, but it also risks further dampening demand in a market that is already tepid. The report does not quantify how much pricing power the various players have or how demand would respond to further price increases.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Current Market

For readers trying to make strategic sense of this environment, the data from the expert call can be distilled into a three-part decision framework. First, assess price-point exposure. Companies that compete primarily above RMB 200,000 are facing the most acute demand risk. The premium segment is collapsing, and even domestic NEV premium models are seeing order declines. Companies below that threshold are in a much stronger position, provided they have the right product mix.

Second, evaluate powertrain strategy. The EREV thesis is fading. Companies that are heavily dependent on EREV need to accelerate their BEV transitions or accept that their growth trajectory will flatten. The BEV penetration story is strongest in the mass market, where range improvements are making BEVs viable for a much broader customer base. Companies that can offer compelling BEVs at competitive prices are best positioned.

Third, consider the catalyst timeline. The expert anticipates stimulus policy could be introduced after July if the market remains sluggish. Historical patterns support this view. The weaker the market performance, the higher the likelihood of stimulus. This creates a potential second-half catalyst for the entire sector. However, stimulus will not rescue every player equally. The companies that are already winning in the resilient mass market will be the primary beneficiaries. The premium players and the JV incumbents will see less benefit because their problems are structural, not cyclical.

The framework suggests that the current market is not a time to bet on a broad recovery. It is a time to be selective. The mass market NEV leaders with strong cost positions and expanding BEV lineups are the most attractive. The premium segment, whether traditional luxury or domestic NEV, is the most vulnerable. The JV segment is the most uncertain.

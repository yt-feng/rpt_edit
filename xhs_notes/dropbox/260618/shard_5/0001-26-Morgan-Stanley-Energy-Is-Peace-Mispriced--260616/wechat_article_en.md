# Peace Is Mispriced: Why the Oil Market Has Already Discounted a Deal That Hasn't Been Signed Yet

The market has moved 29% lower since early April, wiping roughly $30 per barrel off WTI, all on the expectation that a US-Iran memorandum of understanding will be signed this Friday in Switzerland. But the speed and magnitude of this repricing raise a fundamental strategic question: has the market already priced in not just the peace deal, but an idealized version of what comes after it? The evidence suggests it has. Oil producers are now trading at valuations that imply a WTI price of roughly $66 per barrel, a full 13% below the 12-month strip of approximately $75. This disconnect creates a rare analytical wedge between what the market fears and what the fundamentals support.

The argument here is not that a peace deal is unimportant. It is that the market's reaction has been both too fast and too linear, ignoring the structural constraints that will govern the pace of Iranian supply restoration. Even under the most optimistic scenario, production will not snap back overnight. Tanker flows take weeks to re-establish. Inventory replenishment will take months. And the summer demand window remains structurally tight, with a projected 3.4 million barrels per day average deficit in the third quarter. The market has priced a ceasefire as though it were a finished outcome, when in reality it is the beginning of a complex, multi-quarter adjustment process.

This article examines why the current valuation dislocations represent a strategic opportunity for investors who can separate the signal of a political agreement from the noise of a commodity selloff. It draws on proprietary sensitivity analysis from a major investment bank's North America energy research team to show exactly what is and is not priced in, and to identify where the analytical gaps remain.

## The Market Has Already Discounted a Full Restoration of Iranian Supply, Even Though Production Cannot Return That Quickly

The most striking feature of the current price action is the sheer speed of the decline. WTI has fallen by nearly a third in roughly two months. This is not a gradual adjustment to shifting probabilities. It is a market that has moved to price in a binary outcome: full peace, full restoration of Iranian exports, and a return to normalcy. The problem is that the physical realities of oil production and logistics do not operate on the same timeline as diplomatic negotiations.

According to the bank's oil strategist, the restoration of Iranian production will occur in phases. Assuming a near-term re-opening of the Strait of Hormuz, it will take several weeks just to restore tanker flow. The base case calls for 50% of pre-sanction production back by September and 80% by December. That means the third quarter will still see a significant supply deficit. The bank estimates a 3.4 million barrel per day average deficit in the third quarter, and even when narrowing the lens to OECD countries alone, the deficit remains at 1.1 million barrels per day.

The inventory picture reinforces this view. Global observable oil inventories have been drawing rapidly since the start of the conflict, and they remain well below five-year averages. OECD crude oil inventories have fallen further below those averages, driven by a large draw in the United States. Refined product stocks have continued to grind lower as well. Even if Iranian production ramps up as fast as possible, the world will still need to replenish the substantial inventory draws that have occurred since the conflict began. That process does not happen in weeks. It happens over quarters.

The implication is clear. The market has priced a supply restoration that the physical system cannot deliver in the same timeframe. The bank expects Brent to be supported around $90 in the third quarter and at or above $80 into next year. If that forecast is even close to accurate, then the current valuation of oil producers implies a discount that is not justified by the near-term supply-demand balance. The market is pricing in a reality that does not yet exist and may not exist for some time.

## Oil Producers Are Pricing in a $66 WTI World That Is 13% Below the Forward Strip, Creating a Structural Valuation Mismatch

The most powerful analytical tool in this report is the implied price sensitivity embedded in current equity valuations. The bank calculates that oil producers are currently discounting an average WTI price of approximately $66 per barrel. That is 13% below the current 12-month strip of roughly $75 per barrel. This is not a marginal mispricing. It is a structural gap that suggests the equity market is pricing in a more pessimistic outcome than the futures market.

To understand the magnitude of this gap, consider the free cash flow sensitivity. At strip pricing of approximately $72 WTI, the median 2027 free cash flow yield for the bank's oil coverage is 11%. For US oil E&Ps specifically, it is 13%. For US majors, it is 8%. For Canadian producers, it is 9%. These are attractive yields in any environment, but they become even more compelling when you stress-test them against different price scenarios. At $55 WTI, the median free cash flow yield falls to 5%. At $105 WTI, it rises to 19%. The sensitivity is roughly 3 percentage points for every $10 move in WTI.

This means that the current valuation is not just slightly below strip. It is pricing in a scenario that is closer to a $65-$66 world than a $75 world. The gap is $9 per barrel. For a sector where every $10 per barrel moves free cash flow yields by 300 basis points, that $9 gap represents a meaningful divergence between what the equity market expects and what the futures market expects.

The natural question is which market is right. The futures market, which includes physical hedgers, commercial traders, and financial speculators, is pricing in a $75 strip. The equity market, which reflects investor sentiment about the sustainability of those prices, is pricing in $66. One of these is wrong. The bank's analysis suggests that the equity market has overcorrected, creating an opportunity to add exposure to majors and high-quality E&Ps at discounted valuations.

## The Natural Gas Market Is Correctly Priced, Which Makes the Oil Discrepancy Even More Striking

One of the most revealing aspects of the analysis is the contrast between oil and natural gas. For gas E&Ps, the implied pricing is much closer to the forward strip. The bank estimates that gas producers are reflecting a median Henry Hub price of approximately $3.38 per mmbtu, which is essentially in line with the forward strip. The free cash flow sensitivity for gas is also meaningful, with each $0.50 move in Henry Hub changing the average free cash flow yield by roughly 3 percentage points in 2027. At strip pricing of $3.50, the median free cash flow yield is 9%. At $4.50, it rises to 14%. At $2.50, it falls to 4%.

The fact that gas is priced in line with the strip while oil is priced at a 13% discount to the strip tells you something important about the nature of the selloff. The gas market has not been disrupted by the same geopolitical shock. There is no peace deal for natural gas. The gas market is responding to its own supply-demand fundamentals, and the equity market is pricing those fundamentals accurately.

The oil selloff, by contrast, is driven almost entirely by the peace narrative. The equity market has taken the prospect of a deal and extrapolated it into a full normalization of oil prices, ignoring the physical constraints that will govern the pace of that normalization. The result is a valuation gap that does not exist in gas. If you believe that the oil market's supply-demand fundamentals are at least as robust as the gas market's, then the oil valuation gap represents a genuine opportunity.

This is not to say that oil prices will not eventually adjust to a lower equilibrium if Iranian supply returns in full. But the timing matters. The bank's analysis suggests that the summer remains tight, and that even with a phased restoration of Iranian production, the market will still need to replenish inventories well into next year. The equity market is pricing in the end state without pricing in the transition. That is the mispricing.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Demand Side and the Nuclear Negotiation Timeline

Any serious analysis must also identify what is not known. The report is thorough on the supply side, but it leaves two critical questions unanswered. The first is demand. The report shows that implied US product demand has held up well so far, with gasoline consumption still within normal ranges. But the global demand picture is more uncertain. China's seaborne net imports of total petroleum have maintained a downward trend, and based on known tanker movements, net seaborne oil imports into China are expected to be down even further in June versus May. If Chinese demand continues to weaken, that could offset some of the supply tightness that the bank expects in the third quarter.

The second unanswered question is the nuclear negotiation timeline. The memorandum of understanding calls for discussions surrounding Iran's nuclear capabilities to take place over the next 60 days. The full text of the agreement has not been released. If those negotiations break down or extend beyond the 60-day window, the entire premise of the current price move could be undermined. The market is pricing in a clean resolution, but the most difficult part of the negotiation is yet to come.

These open questions do not invalidate the analysis. They simply mean that the opportunity comes with a risk premium. The bank's sensitivities are based on a phased restoration of supply, not an immediate one. If the nuclear talks stall, the supply restoration timeline could stretch even further, which would support higher prices for longer. If the talks accelerate, the timeline could compress, which would put downward pressure on prices. The valuation gap is real, but its resolution depends on variables that are inherently uncertain.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Position Across the Oil Producer Spectrum

For investors looking to act on this analysis, the decision framework should be structured around three variables: geography, quality, and time horizon.

First, geography matters because the valuation discrepancies are not uniform. US oil E&Ps trade at a 13% median free cash flow yield at strip, while US majors trade at 8% and Canadian producers trade at 9%. The E&P segment offers the highest yield, but it also carries the highest single-commodity risk. The majors offer lower yield but greater diversification, including downstream and chemical exposure that can provide a buffer if crude prices soften. Canadian producers sit in between, with a yield that is attractive but not as high as US E&Ps.

Second, quality matters because not all producers are created equal. The bank's coverage includes ratings that range from overweight to underweight across the sector. Investors should focus on producers with strong balance sheets, low breakeven costs, and the operational flexibility to adjust production in response to changing price environments. High-quality producers will generate free cash flow even at $66 WTI, which is the price the equity market is currently discounting. Lower-quality producers may struggle at that price level.

Third, time horizon matters because the mispricing is most acute in the near term. The bank expects Brent to be supported around $90 in the third quarter and at or above $80 into next year. If that forecast is correct, then the current equity valuations represent a compelling entry point for investors with a six- to twelve-month horizon. The risk is that the market is right to be pessimistic, and that prices will drift lower as Iranian supply comes back online. But the bank's analysis suggests that the pace of that supply restoration will be slow enough to keep prices elevated through the end of the year.

The framework can be summarized as follows: overweight high-quality US E&Ps and majors for their free cash flow yield and valuation discount, with a six- to twelve-month time horizon. Use the gas segment as a hedge or a neutral position, since it is already correctly priced. Monitor the nuclear negotiations closely, because any delay in those talks would support the bull case, while any acceleration would challenge it.

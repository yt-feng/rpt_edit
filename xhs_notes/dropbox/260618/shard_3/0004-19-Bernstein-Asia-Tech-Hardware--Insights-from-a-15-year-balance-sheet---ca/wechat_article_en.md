# AI Is Reshaping Asia Tech Hardware: The ROE Divergence That Demands a New Investment Framework

The single most important insight from a 15-year balance sheet and cash flow analysis of Asia’s tech hardware universe is this: the artificial intelligence boom is not lifting all boats equally. It is creating a structural divergence in return on equity that separates companies into two distinct regimes—those that compound capital at accelerating rates and those that will struggle to escape the gravitational pull of legacy end-markets.

This divergence is not merely a matter of cyclical tailwinds. It reflects fundamental differences in business model architecture, capital intensity, and the ability to monetize AI demand. The companies that can sustain ROE above 30 percent are those that operate in testing, power management, and advanced substrates—where AI creates proprietary, high-margin revenue streams. The consumer electronics names, by contrast, face a slower and more uncertain path to mid-teens or low-20 percent ROE, as their AI contributions remain nascent and their legacy businesses face margin compression.

The strategic question for investors is no longer whether AI demand is real. It is which companies have the balance sheet structure, the operational efficiency, and the capital allocation discipline to convert that demand into durable shareholder value. The report’s 15-year framework provides the data to answer that question—but it also raises new ones that demand further analysis.

## The AI-Driven ROE Upcycle Is Concentrated in a Few Business Models, Not the Entire Supply Chain

The most striking pattern in the data is the sharp upward inflection in ROE for a small cluster of companies. Chroma, Delta Electronics, and Quanta are all projected to reach ROE levels above 30 percent by 2026. Chroma, in particular, shows a trajectory from roughly 25 percent ROE in 2024 to an estimated 45 percent by 2026 and beyond. This is not a cyclical bounce. It is the result of a structural shift in revenue composition toward AI datacenter power testing, semiconductor test equipment, and photonics.

What makes Chroma’s case instructive is the DuPont decomposition. Its ROE is driven by a net profit margin of approximately 30 percent over the past three years, supported by gross margins near 60 percent. This is a business that sells customized, niche equipment into markets where technology leadership commands pricing power. The AI demand surge for SLT and photonics testers, combined with power testing for AI datacenter infrastructure, is amplifying a model that was already profitable. The operating leverage is enormous: the report models a 46 percent revenue CAGR and a 65 percent operating profit CAGR through 2028.

Delta Electronics tells a similar story, though from a different starting point. Its ROE had fluctuated in the mid-teens to low-20s for years, weighed down by pricing wars in PC power electronics and slower-than-expected acquisition synergies. The inflection began when AI datacenter revenue reached 40 percent of total revenue. This is the key threshold: once a company crosses into meaningful AI exposure, the margin structure changes fundamentally. Delta’s ROE is projected to rise from 24 percent to over 40 percent by 2026.

The lesson is clear. AI does not improve ROE across the board. It rewards companies that already had strong business models and simply needed a demand catalyst. The companies that lack this structural advantage will see only modest improvement.

## Consumer Electronics Names Face a Structural Ceiling on ROE, Even With AI Upside

The contrast with consumer-facing hardware companies is stark. Luxshare, Largan, and Sunny Optical are all projected to see ROE improve, but only to mid-teens or low-20 percent levels. This is not a failure of execution. It reflects the inherent characteristics of their business models: lower gross margins, higher capital intensity, and a greater reliance on volume-driven revenue growth.

Largan is the most instructive case. Over the past five years, it has achieved an average net profit margin of roughly 39 percent—the highest in the coverage universe. Yet its ROE averaged only about 12 percent. The DuPont analysis reveals why: Largan’s asset turnover is extremely low, at 27 percent, and its equity leverage is also low, at 117 percent. The company holds substantial cash reserves and invests heavily in high-end lens manufacturing equipment to meet Apple’s stringent requirements. This is a capital-intensive, cash-heavy model that depresses ROE despite exceptional margins.

Sunny Optical faces a different challenge. Its revenue is roughly 70 percent camera modules, a business with lower technological barriers and higher margin volatility. The report notes that Sunny’s earnings volatility is significantly higher than Largan’s, reflecting the cyclicality of Android smartphone demand and intense competition in modules. Even as Sunny gains iPhone camera module share, the margin structure of the module business limits the upside to ROE.

The implication is uncomfortable but necessary: for consumer electronics companies, AI exposure is a supplement, not a transformation. They will need to build AI-related revenue streams over years, not quarters, and the margin contribution will be diluted by their legacy businesses.

## Balance Sheet Strength Is Universal, but Capital Allocation Discipline Is Not

One of the report’s most reassuring findings is that balance sheets across the coverage universe remain strong. Most companies maintain net cash positions, with stable or declining debt-to-equity ratios. Cash conversion cycles are generally under 120 days, reflecting solid operational efficiency and bargaining power with customers and suppliers.

But strength in solvency does not automatically translate into strength in shareholder returns. The report reveals a wide dispersion in payout ratios and capital allocation strategies. Quanta and Chroma lead with payout ratios of 75 to 85 percent. Others have not established regular share buyback programs. This is a critical distinction for investors focused on total return.

The report also highlights an important nuance in Quanta’s case. While its ROE has been impressive—reaching low-30 percent levels last year—its leverage is rising with AI server expansion. The buy-sell business model for AI servers inflates reported revenue and introduces working capital demands that are not present in traditional ODM models. The report rates Quanta Underperform, suggesting that consensus estimates may be too optimistic about the sustainability of its profitability.

This raises a broader question: as AI server assembly becomes a larger share of revenue for ODMs, will the capital intensity of the business model increase? If so, the high ROE of today may come with higher balance sheet risk tomorrow.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of ROE at 40 Percent

The report’s projections for Chroma and Delta imply ROE levels above 40 percent for multiple years. Historically, such high returns on equity attract competition, regulatory scrutiny, or customer pressure to lower prices. The report does not fully address how these companies will defend their margins as AI testing and power infrastructure markets mature.

For Chroma, the key question is whether its total addressable market in CPO—estimated at over NT$5 billion by 2027—is large enough to sustain the growth trajectory. The report models CPO contributing roughly 7 percent of company revenue by 2027. That is meaningful but not dominant. If the CPO market fails to materialize as expected, or if competitors enter the space, the ROE trajectory could flatten.

For Delta, the question is whether its 40 percent AI revenue mix can expand further without margin dilution. As AI datacenter power solutions become more standardized, pricing pressure may emerge. The report does not model this scenario.

These open questions are not criticisms of the analysis. They are the natural consequence of a framework that identifies inflection points but cannot predict competitive dynamics five years out. Investors who rely on the report’s projections should stress-test the assumptions around market share, pricing, and technology cycles.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Filters for Selecting AI-Exposed Hardware Names

Based on the report’s 15-year analysis, investors can apply a three-filter framework to evaluate any Asia tech hardware company claiming AI exposure.

First, assess the business model’s inherent margin structure. Companies with gross margins above 50 percent and net margins above 20 percent have the operating leverage to convert AI demand into ROE acceleration. Companies with gross margins below 30 percent will see AI improve revenue but not fundamentally transform profitability.

Second, evaluate capital intensity and asset turnover. High-ROE companies in this universe combine strong margins with efficient use of assets. Chroma’s asset turnover of 58 percent, combined with its 30 percent net margin, produces a powerful ROE engine. By contrast, Largan’s 27 percent asset turnover cancels out its 39 percent net margin. Investors should look for companies where AI demand improves asset utilization, not just revenue.

Third, examine capital allocation discipline. The report shows that payout ratios vary widely, and share buybacks are not common. Companies that return capital to shareholders while maintaining strong balance sheets are better positioned to compound returns over time. Companies that reinvest aggressively into capacity expansion may create value, but they also introduce execution risk.

This framework does not guarantee correct investment decisions. But it provides a systematic way to separate genuine AI beneficiaries from companies that are merely riding the thematic wave.

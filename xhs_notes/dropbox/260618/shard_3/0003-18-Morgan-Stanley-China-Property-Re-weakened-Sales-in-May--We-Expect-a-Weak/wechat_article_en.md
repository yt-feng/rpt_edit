# China’s Property Recovery Has Already Peaked: The Second Half of 2026 Will Be Worse Than the First

The narrative that China’s property market was stabilizing has officially broken. After a brief improvement in April, national sales reversed course in May, falling 9.3% year-on-year in value and 13.2% in volume, widening from April’s already negative prints of 7.6% and 9.5% respectively. This is not a temporary wobble. The data tells a clear story: whatever policy-driven momentum existed in the first quarter has fully dissipated, and the structural forces weighing on the market are reasserting themselves with greater force. For investors, the critical question is no longer whether a recovery is coming, but how much further the downside extends and which pockets of the market can still generate returns.

The May numbers are not an isolated data point; they are the leading edge of a broader deterioration that will define the second half of 2026. The report we are analyzing makes this case with clarity, but the implications go deeper than the headline figures suggest. The re-widening of sales declines is occurring alongside accelerating weakness in construction activity, rapidly decelerating secondary home sales, and a growing divergence between Tier-1 cities and the rest of the country. Each of these trends reinforces the others, creating a negative feedback loop that policy interventions have so far failed to break.

The market is now entering a phase where the easy comparisons and pent-up demand from prior policy easing have been exhausted. What remains is the underlying reality of a sector struggling with overcapacity, weak developer balance sheets, and a homebuying public that has fundamentally changed its expectations about property as an asset class. The second half of 2026 will not bring relief; it will bring a deeper test of which developers have genuine resilience and which are merely surviving on borrowed time.

## The May Sales Reversal Is Not a Blip; It Marks the End of the Policy-Driven Bounce

The most important single observation from the May data is that the improvement seen in April was not the beginning of a trend. National sales value fell 9.3% year-on-year in May after a 7.6% decline in April, while volume dropped 13.2% compared to 9.5% the prior month. On a year-to-date basis through May, total sales value is down 13.5% and volume down 10.8%. These are not marginal deteriorations; they represent a clear re-acceleration of the decline.

The significance of this reversal cannot be overstated. The April data had given some observers hope that the combination of policy easing, lower mortgage rates, and selective city-level deregulation was finally gaining traction. That hope is now gone. The May figures demonstrate that the April improvement was likely a function of seasonal adjustments and a temporary release of pent-up demand from buyers who had been waiting for policy clarity. Once that demand was satisfied, the underlying weakness reasserted itself.

What makes this particularly concerning for the second half is the trajectory of secondary home sales. The report notes that real-time secondary sales in high-tier cities began weakening in mid-May and have decelerated even faster in recent weeks. Secondary market activity has historically been a leading indicator for primary market pricing and transaction volumes. When secondary sales slow, it signals that existing homeowners are struggling to find buyers, which in turn puts downward pressure on pricing expectations across the entire market. The report expects secondary home sales to turn negative year-on-year in the third quarter, which would be a direct stressor on primary sales and overall home prices.

The implication for investors is straightforward: the window for positioning based on a cyclical recovery has closed. Any strategy that depended on improving sales momentum in the second half needs to be reassessed. The market is not recovering; it is re-weakening, and the second half will likely be worse than the first.

## Construction Activity Is Collapsing, and Developer Caution Is Now Structural, Not Cyclical

The weakness in sales is being matched, and in some ways exceeded, by the deterioration on the supply side. New starts fell 25% year-on-year in May, and completions fell 20%. On a year-to-date basis, the declines are 23% for both metrics. Real estate investment worsened notably, falling 16.2% year-on-year in the first five months compared to a 13.7% decline through April.

These numbers reflect a developer community that has moved beyond caution into outright retrenchment. The report highlights that land purchases by the top 100 developers dropped more than 40% year-on-year in the first five months of 2026. This is not a cyclical pullback; it is a structural decision by developers to preserve cash, reduce exposure, and wait for clarity that may not come. Developers are not building because they cannot sell what they would build, and they are not buying land because they cannot finance it on terms that make economic sense.

The feedback loop here is critical. Lower construction activity means fewer new units coming to market, which in theory should help clear existing inventory. But in practice, the decline in new supply is being overwhelmed by the decline in demand. Moreover, the lack of new construction has second-order effects on local government land sales, which in turn affects fiscal revenues and the ability of local governments to support the broader economy. The construction slowdown is not just a property sector problem; it is a macro-economic headwind that will persist as long as developers remain in retrenchment mode.

The report expects construction activity to remain lackluster in the second half, and that expectation is almost certainly correct. The combination of weak sales, tight financing conditions, and a deeply cautious developer mindset means there is no catalyst for a supply-side recovery. Investors should assume that construction and investment data will continue to deteriorate, and that any forecasts that assume a stabilization in the second half are likely too optimistic.

## The K-Shaped Market Is Real: Tier-1 Cities Are an Exception, Not a Signal

One of the most striking features of the current market is the growing divergence between Tier-1 cities and the rest of the country. In May, the NBS 70-city home price index showed primary market prices falling 0.2% month-on-month and secondary prices falling 0.3%. But within that aggregate, Tier-1 cities actually saw primary prices rise 0.2% and secondary prices rise 0.4%. This is not a statistical anomaly; it is a pattern that has been building for months.

The report describes this as a potential K-shaped trend, where broadly month-on-month declines across most cities coexist with mild uptrends in select Tier-1 markets. The reason for the divergence is straightforward: Tier-1 cities have better destocking progress, stronger demand fundamentals, and a more resilient buyer base. They also benefit from continued in-migration and concentration of high-value employment. In contrast, lower-tier cities are struggling with oversupply, population outflows, and a buyer base that has been disproportionately affected by the broader economic slowdown.

For investors, this divergence creates both opportunity and risk. The opportunity lies in developers with concentrated exposure to Tier-1 and high-quality Tier-2 markets, where pricing and demand are more resilient. The risk is that the aggregate market data masks the severity of the downturn in lower-tier cities, and that investors who focus only on the national numbers will underestimate the damage being done to developers with weaker geographic profiles.

The critical question that the report raises but does not fully answer is whether the Tier-1 resilience is sustainable. The report notes that secondary home sales in high-tier cities have started weakening since mid-May, even as prices remain positive. If secondary volumes continue to decelerate, the pricing support in Tier-1 markets could erode. The K-shape could become a V-shape for Tier-1 cities if the broader macro environment deteriorates further, or it could hold if the structural advantages of these cities prove durable. This is the key analytical uncertainty that investors need to monitor in the coming months.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: Can Policy Break the Feedback Loop, or Is the Downcycle Structural?

The report makes a compelling case that the second half will be weaker, but it leaves an important question unresolved: what would it take to change the trajectory? The report does not explicitly model the impact of further policy easing, nor does it assess the probability of more aggressive government intervention. This is not a criticism of the analysis; it is a recognition that the current situation may be beyond the reach of conventional policy tools.

The traditional policy response to a property downturn involves lowering mortgage rates, reducing down payment requirements, and easing purchase restrictions. China has already deployed many of these measures, and they have produced only temporary effects. The deeper problem is that the structural drivers of the property market have changed. Households are no longer willing to leverage aggressively to buy property because their expectations about future price appreciation have fundamentally shifted. Developers are unable or unwilling to build because their business models are broken. Local governments are caught between the need to support the market and the need to maintain fiscal discipline.

The report does not address whether more aggressive measures, such as direct government purchases of unsold inventory, large-scale restructuring of developer debt, or explicit fiscal transfers to support homebuyer confidence, could alter the trajectory. These are the questions that the full report would need to address for investors to form a complete view. Without answers to these questions, the analytical framework remains incomplete, and investors are left to make their own judgments about the likelihood and effectiveness of further policy intervention.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Selectivity Is Not a Strategy; It Is the Only Option

Given the outlook for a weaker second half, the report’s recommendation to stay selective is not just prudent; it is the only viable approach. The report explicitly states that the industry risk/reward skews to the downside, and that even a 20% share price pullback since mid-May does not change this assessment. This is a clear signal that broad-based exposure to the property sector is not justified, and that investors must focus on specific names with identifiable advantages.

The decision framework that emerges from the report has three layers. First, geographic exposure matters more than ever. Developers with concentrated exposure to Tier-1 and resilient Tier-2 markets have a fundamentally different risk profile than those with broad exposure to lower-tier cities. Second, balance sheet strength and access to financing are the primary differentiators in a market where credit conditions remain tight. The report’s scoring methodology, which emphasizes financing and leverage metrics, reflects this reality. Third, self-help alpha matters. Developers that can generate growth through operational execution, cost control, and disciplined land acquisition, even in a weak market, will outperform those that are simply riding the market cycle.

The report identifies CR Land as its top pick, followed by C&D, citing solid EPS outlooks, attractive dividend yields, and medium-term re-rating potential. These are not speculative bets on a recovery; they are bets on companies that can generate returns even in a challenging environment. For investors who want exposure to the property sector, this is the right framework. For those who do not, the report’s cautious industry view is a clear signal to stay away.

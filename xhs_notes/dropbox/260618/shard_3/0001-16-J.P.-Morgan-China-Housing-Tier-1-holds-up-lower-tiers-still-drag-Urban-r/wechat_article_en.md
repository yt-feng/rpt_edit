# China's Housing Market Is Not Recovering — It Is Reorganizing Around a Tier-1 Core

The dominant narrative in early 2025 — that China's housing market has finally found a floor — is misleading. A global investment bank's recent housing activity index ticked up in May, and tier-1 secondary markets posted a sequential price gain of 0.2% month-on-month. But these are not the leading indicators of a broad-based recovery. They are the early contours of a structural reorganization: the market is concentrating around a narrow set of high-quality assets in a handful of top-tier cities, while the rest of the country continues to experience a prolonged quantity adjustment. The improvement in tier-1 markets is real but narrow, and it coexists with falling real estate fixed-asset investment, elevated inventories, and continued weakness in household credit demand. Policymakers are not trying to revive the market in the traditional sense. The 15th Five-Year Plan for urban renewal, released in early June, makes this explicit: the goal is stabilization, not revival.

The central question for investors is no longer "when will the market bottom." It is "what kind of market will emerge on the other side of this adjustment." The evidence suggests a K-shaped, tier-split outcome in which tier-1 cities and premium product segments decouple from the rest, while lower-tier cities face a multi-year absorption process. This has profound implications for asset allocation, developer strategy, and policy expectations.

## The May Data Confirms a Narrow, Not Broad, Improvement That Is Driven by Tier-1 Secondary Markets

The headline numbers from May are ambiguous enough to support both optimism and caution. The housing activity index ticked up, driven by a smaller contraction in floor space starts — down 22.9% year-on-year in May versus 28.2% in April — and a less severe decline in completions. But these are improvements from a very low base, not signs of a genuine turnaround. New home sales continued to fall, with a steeper 8.6% year-on-year decline in May versus 6.5% in April, and real estate fixed-asset investment dropped 24.3% year-on-year, worsening from 20.1% in April.

The price data tells a more nuanced story. The NBS 70-city new-home price decline stabilized at 0.20% month-on-month in May, nearly unchanged from April. But this aggregate masks a dramatic divergence. Tier-1 cities posted a sequential gain of 0.2% month-on-month, while lower-tier cities continued to drag. Secondary home prices tell an even sharper story: the national average fall widened modestly to 0.26% month-on-month, but tier-1 price gains held at 0.4%. New-home prices are now down 13.7% from the 2021 peak; secondary prices are down 22.6%.

The so-what here is critical. The tier-1 improvement is being driven by the secondary market, where lower inventory, stronger transaction volumes, better conversion rates, and selective price increases have created a self-reinforcing dynamic. This is not a broad-based demand recovery. It is a rotation within the market: buyers are shifting from primary to secondary, from lower-tier to tier-1, and from standard to premium product. The headline stabilization that some observers point to is actually a compositional effect. As the secondary market takes increasing share from primary sales, total transaction volumes may stabilize or even improve while new-home sales, construction, and developer investment remain under severe pressure.

## The 15th Five-Year Plan for Urban Renewal Signals a Shift from Market Revival to Managed Stabilization

The State Council's publication of the Urban Renewal 15th Five-Year Plan in early June is the most important policy signal of the year, yet it has received less attention than it deserves. Compared to the 14th Five-Year Plan, the new framework moves decisively away from pilot-style, incremental renovation toward a systematic model built around three major categories: livelihood, development, and safety. The plan places stronger emphasis on improving housing quality, building "good houses," upgrading old residential communities, and addressing safety risks such as dilapidated housing and urban villages.

The targets are more ambitious. The planned renovation volume for urban dilapidated housing has doubled, and urban village renovation remains at a high level. But the real shift is in the policy toolkit. The 15th Five-Year Plan gives much greater weight to funding, land policy, and real estate transformation. It details stronger fiscal support through budgetary investment, affordable housing subsidies, special local government bonds, ultra-long special central government bonds, tax incentives, and the potential use of REITs and social capital. Land policy is also more flexible, with support for idle land reuse, mixed-use redevelopment, temporary use, land-use conversion, and lower transaction frictions for stock-asset revitalization.

The strategic implication is clear: policymakers have accepted that the old model of developer-led, debt-fueled expansion is over. The new approach is state-led, fiscally supported, and focused on quality improvement rather than quantity expansion. This is not a stimulus program designed to reflate the market. It is a managed transition designed to stabilize the existing stock, improve urban infrastructure, and gradually absorb excess inventory. The plan positions urban renewal as a key tool for improving livelihoods and supporting high-quality urban growth — not for reviving the property market.

## The Market Remains Trapped in a Time-Inconsistency Problem That Forces Adjustment Through Quantities

China's housing market is caught in what the report accurately describes as a "time-inconsistency trap." Policymakers want to improve affordability and reduce the economy's reliance on property. But they have slowed price clearing to protect household wealth and local government finances. The result is that the adjustment is forced primarily through quantities: falling starts, falling sales, falling land purchases, and falling real estate fixed-asset investment.

This dynamic creates a paradoxical environment. Prices in tier-1 cities are stabilizing or even rising, but this is occurring alongside a continued decline in construction activity and developer investment. The quantity adjustment is doing the work that price adjustment would normally do, but it is doing so unevenly. In tier-1 cities, where demand is relatively resilient and supply is constrained, the adjustment manifests as a shift toward the secondary market and premium products. In lower-tier cities, where inventories are elevated and demand is weak, the adjustment manifests as outright stagnation.

The data on inventory months is sobering. Under-construction inventory has risen to approximately 75 months, and finished inventory to about 7 months. These are multi-year highs. Even with the urban renewal plan's ambitious renovation targets, absorbing this inventory will take years, not quarters. The implication is that real estate fixed-asset investment will remain under pressure for an extended period, and developer balance sheets will continue to be constrained by the need to work down existing stock rather than build new supply.

## The Report's Framework Raises Unanswered Questions About the Durability of Tier-1 Demand

The report provides a compelling diagnosis, but it leaves several critical questions unresolved. First, how durable is the tier-1 secondary market recovery? The report notes that it has been supported by lower inventory, stronger transaction volumes, better conversion rates, selective price increases, and possible wealth effects from IPO activity and firmer equity markets. But these are cyclical supports, not structural ones. If equity markets falter or IPO activity slows, the wealth effect could reverse. If inventory in tier-1 secondary markets begins to rise again, the price momentum could stall.

Second, what is the transmission mechanism from urban renewal to housing demand? The 15th Five-Year Plan's fiscal support is substantial, but it is directed at renovation and infrastructure, not at new housing construction or direct demand subsidies. The plan may improve the quality of existing housing stock and support employment in construction-related sectors, but it is unlikely to generate the kind of demand surge that would clear excess inventory in lower-tier cities.

Third, can the narrow recovery broaden without a fundamental improvement in household credit demand? The report notes that household credit demand remains weak. Without easier credit conditions or a sustained improvement in household income expectations, the tier-1 recovery may remain a niche phenomenon confined to high-income buyers and upgrade demand.

These are not criticisms of the report's analysis. They are the natural next questions that any serious investor should be asking. The report provides the framework, but the answers will depend on how policy, credit, and household behavior evolve over the next 12 to 18 months.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Distinguish Between Tier-1 Exposure, Premium Product, and Everything Else

For investors trying to navigate this market, the report's analysis suggests a clear decision framework with three categories.

The first category is tier-1 city secondary market exposure and premium product segments. These are the areas where the data shows genuine improvement, where demand is supported by structural factors such as constrained supply, high-income demographics, and wealth effects. Investors with exposure to these segments should monitor transaction volumes and inventory levels as leading indicators, but the base case is continued outperformance relative to the broader market.

The second category is lower-tier city exposure and standard product segments. These areas face a prolonged quantity adjustment with no clear catalyst for recovery. Investors should assume that inventory absorption will take multiple years, that price recovery will lag volume recovery, and that policy support will be directed at renovation and quality improvement rather than demand stimulation. The risk is that these assets continue to underperform for an extended period.

The third category is policy-dependent plays: developers with exposure to urban renewal projects, companies that benefit from fiscal spending on infrastructure and renovation, and financial institutions with exposure to affordable housing lending. These are not direct housing market plays, but they offer exposure to the policy response. The key variable to watch is the pace and scale of fiscal execution under the 15th Five-Year Plan.

The overarching strategic implication is that the old approach of treating China's housing market as a single, cyclical asset class is no longer valid. The market is undergoing a structural reorganization that will produce divergent outcomes across cities, product types, and business models. Investors who fail to distinguish between these segments will be exposed to risks they do not fully understand and will miss opportunities that are hiding in plain sight.

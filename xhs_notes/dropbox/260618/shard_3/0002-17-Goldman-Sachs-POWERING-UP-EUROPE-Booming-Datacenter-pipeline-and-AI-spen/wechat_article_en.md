# Europe's Datacenter Pipeline Has Hit 480 GW: The Energy Sector Is Now the Critical Bottleneck for AI Infrastructure

The numbers have become almost impossible to ignore. Europe's datacenter connection requests have surged to approximately 480 GW, equivalent to 1.5 times the continent's entire current power demand. This figure has more than doubled in just six months, rising from 290 GW in January 2026 and from 170 GW just over a year ago. While some portion of this pipeline may be speculative, the sheer velocity and scale of growth signal something far more consequential than a cyclical uptick: a structural transformation of European energy demand that is already reshaping the investment landscape for utilities, grid operators, and infrastructure providers.

The implications extend well beyond datacenter operators and hyperscalers. The five largest Big Tech companies are now targeting nearly $1 trillion in combined capital expenditure by 2027, a near tenfold increase from 2023 levels. This spending is not optional. It is being driven by the competitive imperative to deploy AI at scale, and Europe's need to enhance productivity, reinforce national security, and preserve data sovereignty. The question is no longer whether datacenter demand will grow, but whether Europe's energy infrastructure can keep pace.

The pivotal role of energy has become the central strategic question. Utilities are no longer passive suppliers of electricity. They are becoming the gatekeepers of AI infrastructure, responsible for providing energy, scouting for land and grid connections, and reinforcing the power grids that must absorb this unprecedented demand. The companies that can execute on this mandate will not just benefit from incremental growth; they may be entering a generational earnings super-cycle.

## The Datacenter Pipeline Has Outpaced Even the Most Bullish Forecasts, and the Gap Between Pipeline and Reality Is the Key Risk

The latest estimate of 480 GW of connection requests represents a 60 percent increase from the previous projection of 290 GW. To put this in perspective, Europe's total power consumption in 2025 was approximately 320 GW. The pipeline now exceeds current consumption by 50 percent. Even if only a fraction of these requests materialize, the implications for power generation, grid reinforcement, and utility capex are profound.

The European Data Center Association provides a more conservative but still striking forecast: 20 GW of additional capacity by 2031, nearly tripling existing capacity from 15 GW to 35 GW. Crucially, about 60 percent of these additions are already under construction or have received final investment decisions, with the remainder fully approved. This gives the EUDCA forecast a high degree of visibility. By 2031, datacenters would account for approximately 10 percent of Europe's total power demand, up from roughly 5 percent today.

The gap between the 480 GW pipeline and the 20 GW of confirmed additions is the critical analytical question. It suggests either that a massive wave of speculative applications is inflating the pipeline, or that the market is dramatically underestimating the pace of future buildout. Our view leans toward the latter. The rapid doubling of the pipeline over six months, combined with Big Tech's accelerating capex trajectory, argues that the pipeline is signaling genuine demand rather than speculative noise. The risk for investors is not overbuilding, but underbuilding relative to the infrastructure required.

## Agentic AI Could Multiply Datacenter Energy Demand by a Factor That Most Models Have Not Captured

Most current forecasts for datacenter energy demand assume a linear relationship between AI adoption and power consumption. This assumption may be dangerously conservative. Agentic AI systems, which can autonomously execute multi-step tasks, consume significantly more energy per query than traditional AI chatbots. Research suggests that a single agentic query can require up to 50 times the energy of a standard chatbot query, with more conservative estimates from Microsoft placing the multiple at 13 times.

Our analysis adopts a deliberately conservative assumption of 2 watt-hours per agentic query, compared to 0.3 watt-hours for a standard AI chatbot. Even at this reduced multiple, the implications are substantial. A 10 percentage point increase in the share of queries handled by Agentic AI could boost European datacenter needs by 25 to 30 percent. Under our upside case, this would push total European datacenter capacity to approximately 80 GW by 2035, at which point datacenters would account for roughly 25 percent of total power demand.

The market has not priced this scenario. Most industry experts suggest that Agentic AI will represent 20 to 30 percent of queries in the United States by 2030, with Europe lagging by approximately five years. If this timeline holds, the inflection point for European datacenter energy demand arrives around 2033, but the planning and investment decisions required to meet that demand must be made today. The energy infrastructure needed to support 80 GW of datacenter capacity does not appear overnight.

## The Electrification Super-Cycle Will Create a Structural Earnings Tailwind for Utilities That Most Investors Are Underestimating

The convergence of datacenter demand with broader electrification trends creates what can only be described as a generational earnings opportunity for European utilities. Our hyper-electrification scenario, which assumes delivery of Europe's existing 2030 electrification goals alongside accelerated AI adoption, points to power demand growth of 5 percent per annum by 2029-30. This would require approximately 3.5 trillion euros of investment in power generation and grid infrastructure.

The earnings implications are direct and measurable. Our base case assumes a compound annual earnings growth rate of approximately 9 percent for the main electrification compounders through 2030. Under the hyper-electrification scenario, that figure rises well above 10 percent per annum. The key point is that these are not speculative projections. They are grounded in visible investment cycles, regulatory frameworks, and corporate capex plans that are already underway.

The market is not fully reflecting this potential. The three company clusters that we identify as primary beneficiaries trade at an average price-to-earnings ratio of approximately 12 times by 2030, with an enterprise value-to-EBITDA multiple of roughly 7.5 times. These valuations represent a discount to the broader European utility sector average, despite higher growth prospects. This valuation gap is unlikely to persist as the investment cycle becomes more visible.

## The Report Raises Two Critical Questions That the Data Does Not Yet Fully Answer

Despite the clarity of the pipeline data and the logical force of the electrification thesis, the report leaves two important questions unresolved.

First, how much of the 480 GW pipeline is genuinely executable? Grid connection requests are not binding commitments. They can be withdrawn, modified, or simply never acted upon. The rapid growth of the pipeline suggests that developers are securing grid capacity as a strategic option, but the conversion rate from request to operational datacenter remains unknown. The gap between the 480 GW pipeline and the 20 GW of confirmed additions is wide enough to accommodate both bullish and bearish interpretations. Until we see clearer evidence of conversion rates, this uncertainty will persist.

Second, can European grid infrastructure absorb this demand within the required timeframe? Grid reinforcement is a multi-year process, often constrained by permitting, supply chain bottlenecks, and skilled labor shortages. The 3.5 trillion euros of investment required under the hyper-electrification scenario is a staggering number, and it is not clear that the industry has the capacity to deploy capital at that pace. The risk is not that demand fails to materialize, but that the grid becomes the binding constraint, delaying datacenter construction and capping earnings growth.

These questions are not criticisms of the analysis. They are the natural frontiers of uncertainty in a rapidly evolving landscape. Investors who can form a view on these questions will have a significant informational advantage.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Clusters, Three Different Risk-Reward Profiles

The report identifies three company clusters as primary beneficiaries, each with a distinct risk-reward profile that investors should assess against their own time horizons and risk tolerance.

The first cluster consists of transformative electrification stories: companies where electrification capital expenditure is about to accelerate meaningfully, transforming their portfolios over a three- to five-year horizon. These companies offer the highest potential for earnings acceleration, but they also carry execution risk. The transformation is contingent on successful project delivery, regulatory support, and the ability to secure financing at attractive terms.

The second cluster comprises renewable developers and manufacturers. Developers benefit from rising returns and organic growth opportunities as datacenter operators seek to secure long-term power purchase agreements. Manufacturers benefit from rising orders and improving margins as demand for wind turbines and other renewable equipment accelerates. This cluster offers more diversified exposure but is also more sensitive to commodity prices, interest rates, and policy changes.

The third cluster includes energy security providers: companies focused on grid infrastructure, gas storage, and energy equipment. These companies offer the most defensive exposure, with regulated or contracted revenue streams that provide visibility even in uncertain environments. Their growth is more predictable but also more capped, reflecting the regulatory frameworks that govern their returns.

Investors should map their own preferences against these three profiles. Those with a higher tolerance for execution risk and a longer time horizon may find the transformative electrification stories most compelling. Those seeking more balanced exposure may prefer the renewable cluster. Those prioritizing capital preservation and income may gravitate toward energy security providers.

## The Next Phase of the AI Infrastructure Buildout Will Be Defined by Energy, Not Computing

The first phase of the AI infrastructure buildout was defined by computing hardware: GPUs, networking equipment, and cooling systems. The next phase will be defined by energy. The 480 GW pipeline makes this shift explicit. The companies that control energy supply, grid connections, and infrastructure deployment will become the critical enablers of AI expansion. Their strategic importance will be reflected in their earnings and, ultimately, in their valuations.

The ones that will outperform are those with a structural growth engine — think top alternative asset managers or wealth platforms that can keep absorbing assets. Traditional asset managers and some M&A boutiques? They’re still facing valuation headwinds.
EN_A: Structural growth engine — I like that. So what’s the first key insight?
EN_B: The first insight is that the valuation premium for alternative asset managers is being repriced. Look at the tables — firms like Apollo, KKR, and Carlyle are trading at 10 to 15 times forward earnings for 2026, but their 2027 multiples are expected to compress by 10 to 20 percent. That implies the market expects earnings growth to accelerate and justify current prices. But the question is whether that growth is sustainable.
EN_A: So if their growth doesn’t meet expectations, valuations could take a hit. What’s the second insight?
EN_B: The second insight is about wealth brokers. The report covers LPL, Schwab, Stifel, and others, and their average expected total return is around 18 percent. But here’s the catch — price-to-book ratios vary wildly. Schwab is at over 6 times book, while Stifel is at 2.5 times. The market is rewarding firms that can consistently grow assets and improve operational efficiency, not just those with big brokerage businesses.
EN_A: So investors need to pick the ones with real moats. What’s the third insight?
EN_B: The third insight comes from the M&A boutique sub-sector. The average expected total return here is only about 11 percent — much lower than alternatives or wealth brokers. Firms like Evercore and Houlihan Lokey trade at around 19 times earnings, but their revenue growth expectations are only in the single digits. That suggests the market has already priced in a recovery in M&A activity, but the actual recovery might be slower.
EN_A: Sounds like that sub-sector has a less attractive risk-reward profile. Are there any questions the report doesn’t fully answer?
EN_B: Definitely, at least two big ones. First, can alternative asset managers sustain their fee income growth in a falling rate environment? For example, Ares Capital is at 0.94 times book. Whether those discounts narrow depends on the credit cycle, and the report doesn’t go deep there.

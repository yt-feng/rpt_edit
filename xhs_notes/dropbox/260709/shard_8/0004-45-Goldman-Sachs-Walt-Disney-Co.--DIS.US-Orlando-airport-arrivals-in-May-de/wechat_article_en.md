For those trying to assess Disney on a sum-of-the-parts basis, the parks segment is the most critical variable. It generates the highest margins and the most predictable cash flows. If the demand narrative around parks is shifting, the entire valuation framework needs to be stress-tested.

This is not a story about one month. It is a story about whether Disney's parks business can sustain its premium valuation in a world where domestic air travel is flattening, international recovery is uneven, and consumer spending is becoming more selective. ## The Divergence Between Airport Traffic and Tax Receipts Signals a Fundamental Change in Visitor Economics

The most striking feature of the May data is the split between the two primary demand indicators. Orlando airport deplanements fell 2% year over year, driven by a 3% decline in domestic travelers. Yet Orange County's Tourist Development Tax collections rose 9% to $32.8 million, the highest May figure on record. This is the 14th consecutive month of rising tax revenues. If fewer people are flying in, but tax collections are accelerating, something has changed in the composition or spending behavior of the visitor base.

There are three plausible explanations, and each carries a different implication for Disney. The first is that visitors are staying longer. A smaller number of guests, each extending their trip by a day or two, would generate more hotel nights and thus higher tax receipts. The second is that the mix has shifted toward higher-spending guests. If the decline in domestic arrivals is concentrated among budget-conscious travelers, while luxury and premium segments remain steady or grow, the per-visitor tax contribution rises mechanically. The third is that more visitors are arriving by non-air means, such as driving or rail, which would not appear in airport data but would still generate hotel stays.

For Disney, the most consequential interpretation is the second one. A shift toward higher-spending visitors supports the company's strategy of yield management over volume growth. Disney has been investing heavily in premium experiences, from Lightning Lane passes to exclusive after-hours events to higher-priced hotel inventory. If the data confirms that these investments are working, the market may be undervaluing the revenue per visitor trajectory even as attendance growth moderates. The risk, however, is that this dynamic is fragile. If the high-end visitor base is itself shrinking or becoming more price-sensitive, the tax data could be a lagging indicator that reverses sharply.

## International Visitation Has Stabilized, But the Growth Rate Is Not Enough to Offset Domestic Adjustments

International deplanements at Orlando rose 2% year over year in May, following a similar gain in April. This marks two consecutive months of growth after a period of adjustment. Management commentary has indicated that the pandemic-recovery overlap for international visitation to U.S. parks has cleared, and the data supports that view. The international segment is no longer deteriorating, and it may be inflecting modestly higher.

But the scale of this recovery needs to be put in context. International arrivals account for roughly 14% of total Orlando deplanements in recent months. A 2% increase in that segment adds about 0.3 percentage points to total growth. Meanwhile, the 3% decline in domestic arrivals subtracts roughly 2.5 percentage points. The math is clear: international is a tailwind, but it is not large enough to reverse a domestic adjustment. For Disney's parks segment to show positive attendance growth in the coming quarters, domestic demand must stabilize or improve.

The more strategic question is whether the international recovery has further room to run. The data shows that international visitation has recovered from its post-pandemic normalization but is not yet showing signs of accelerating. The year-over-year comparisons are becoming easier, as the prior-year base included some softness. Yet the growth rate remains in the low single digits. This suggests that structural factors, such as currency exchange rates, visa processing times, and global conditions, are constraining the recovery. For Disney, the international story is one of support, not catalyst.

## The C2Q26 Attendance Estimate Hinges on June, and the Data Pattern Raises More Questions Than Answers

The report estimates that C2Q26 Orlando deplanements are tracking to approximately 1% year-over-year growth, assuming June trends approximate the average of April and May. This estimate is consistent with the forecast for 1% domestic attendance growth at Disney's parks in the same period. The logic is straightforward, but the assumption about June is the weak link.

April deplanements grew 3% year over year. May declined 2%. If June matches the average, that implies June growth of roughly 1%, which would be a deceleration from April but an improvement over May. This is plausible, but it is not supported by any independent data point. The June number is a residual assumption, and it carries outsized weight in the quarterly estimate. A June outcome that is closer to May's decline would push the quarter into negative territory. A June outcome closer to April's growth would make the quarter look solid.

For observers, the critical insight is not the estimate itself but the volatility it reveals. The parks business, which has long been viewed as a stable, recurring revenue generator, is showing quarter-to-quarter swings that are larger than many models capture. This volatility may be the new normal. Consumer behavior is becoming less predictable, and the traditional seasonal patterns that made parks forecasting reliable are breaking down. Analysts who rely on linear extrapolations from a few months of data are likely to be surprised.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Elasticity of Per-Capita Spending Under Volume Pressure

The report provides excellent data on attendance proxies and tax collections, but it leaves a central question unresolved. What happens to Disney's per-capita spending when attendance growth turns negative? The entire perspective on Disney's parks rests on the assumption that revenue per visitor can continue to rise even if foot traffic plateaus or declines. The May data provides a natural experiment to test this assumption, but the results are inconclusive.

Tax collections rose while airport arrivals fell, which is encouraging for the per-capita thesis. But tax data is a lagging indicator, and it reflects hotel stays, not park spending. The correlation between hotel tax receipts and Disney's in-park revenue is strong but not perfect. Visitors staying in off-property hotels or short-term rentals may pay the tax but spend less inside the parks. Conversely, Disney hotel guests may spend more on site but are captured in the tax data only if they stay in taxable accommodations within Orange County.

The report does not break down the tax data by property type or guest origin. It does not model the relationship between airport arrivals and Disney's specific attendance figures, which include annual pass holders, local visitors, and guests staying at Disney-owned hotels who may arrive by car. These gaps matter. Without a more granular view of the visitor mix, it is impossible to determine whether the per-capita spending trend is sustainable or whether it is being artificially boosted by one-time factors such as new ride openings, event calendars, or pricing changes that have not yet faced a demand test.

## A Decision Framework for Observers: Treat Disney's Parks Like a Yield-Maximizing Asset, Not a Volume Business

The May data, when viewed through a strategic lens, suggests that observers should shift their mental model for Disney's parks segment. This is no longer a business that grows by attracting more bodies through the turnstiles. It is a yield-management business that grows by extracting more value from each guest. The distinction is critical for assessment.

If Disney's parks are treated as a volume business, the 2% decline in airport arrivals is a red flag. It implies that the company is losing market share or that demand is adjusting, and the logical response is to lower revenue and EBITDA estimates. But if the parks are treated as a yield-maximizing asset, the same data point becomes less concerning. A smaller number of higher-spending guests can generate the same or greater revenue, and the tax data supports this view.

The decision framework, therefore, has three steps. First, determine whether the decline in domestic arrivals is cyclical or structural. Cyclical declines, tied to macroeconomic conditions or temporary travel patterns, are likely to reverse. Structural declines, driven by demographic shifts, competition from other destinations, or changes in consumer preferences, are more persistent. The May data alone cannot answer this, but the trend over the next two quarters will. Second, monitor the relationship between attendance and per-capita spending. If revenue per visitor continues to rise even as attendance flattens, the yield thesis is intact. If both decline, the perspective shifts. Third, watch the international recovery trajectory. If international growth accelerates to the mid-single digits or higher, it can offset domestic adjustments. If it stalls, the parks segment faces a genuine growth challenge.

This framework leads to a simple conclusion. The May data is not a definitive signal, but it is a watch signal. It increases the burden of proof on Disney's next earnings report. Observers should be looking for evidence that per-capita spending is rising faster than attendance is falling, and that management is confident in the yield strategy. Without that evidence, the current valuation requires careful scrutiny.

The question for observers is not whether Victory Giant can execute on this buildout -- the evidence suggests they can -- but whether the strategic logic holds under multiple scenarios of trade policy, customer concentration, and technology cycles.

## The Cost Premium in Thailand Is Real, but So Is Customer Willingness to Pay for Diversification

The most counterintuitive finding from the factory visit is that higher costs do not translate into margin compression. Victory Giant's Thailand production costs are 10 to 20 percent above mainland China levels, with raw material costs -- particularly copper-clad laminates imported from China subject to tariffs exceeding 10 percent -- being the primary driver. Labor costs in Thailand are lower, but lower labor productivity offsets much of that advantage. Conventional financial analysis would suggest that operating in a higher-cost environment with lower labor productivity is a recipe for margin erosion. Yet management states clearly that customers are willing to pay the premium.

Why? Because the value proposition has shifted. For global-tier clients in AI servers, networking, and high-performance computing, the risk of single-country supply concentration has become an unacceptable business exposure. The willingness to pay a 10 to 20 percent premium for a second production geography is not a sign of irrationality -- it is a rational hedge against supply chain disruption. This dynamic is similar to what we have observed in semiconductor packaging and advanced substrate manufacturing, where customers have accepted higher prices for diversified capacity. The key insight is that the premium is not a permanent cost disadvantage. Management expects gross margins in Thailand to converge with mainland China levels over the medium term through three levers: higher automation, local sourcing of CCLs as Chinese suppliers establish Thailand capacity, and labor productivity improvement as the workforce matures. The current premium is a transitional cost of building a new manufacturing ecosystem, not a structural disadvantage.

## AI PCB Specification Upgrades Create a Window for Capacity Premium Pricing

The Thailand expansion is not simply about replicating existing production in a new location. Victory Giant is positioning its Thailand sites to produce higher-end AI PCBs, which command higher dollar content per board and require more advanced manufacturing processes. This is a crucial strategic choice. By reserving Thailand capacity for AI PCBs, the company is aligning its geographic diversification with the highest-growth segment of the PCB market. The implication is that Thailand is not competing with China on cost for commodity PCBs -- it is competing on capability for premium products.

The timing aligns with a broader industry trend. AI server architectures are driving PCB specification upgrades in layer count, material quality, and signal integrity requirements. Each generation of AI infrastructure demands more sophisticated PCBs with higher value per unit. Victory Giant's revenue projections illustrate this: from approximately 19.3 billion RMB in 2025 to an estimated 87.9 billion RMB by 2028, with EBITDA growing from 6.1 billion to 31.6 billion over the same period. These numbers imply a compound annual growth rate that far exceeds the overall PCB market, suggesting significant market share gains and product mix upgrades. The Thailand capacity is a key enabler of this growth trajectory, as it allows the company to serve customers who require dual-sourcing from non-China locations for their highest-specification products.

## Capacity Expansion Follows Customer Demand, Not Speculative Buildout

A critical risk in any capacity expansion story is the possibility of overbuilding. Victory Giant's approach mitigates this risk in a specific way: capacity expansion in Thailand closely follows customer demand. Management noted that some customers do not have strict requirements for diversifying production sites, implying that the company is not building capacity on speculation but rather in response to confirmed or strongly anticipated customer commitments. This is a disciplined approach that reduces the risk of idle capacity and capital destruction.

However, this customer-driven model introduces a different risk: concentration. If a small number of large customers drive the Thailand expansion, the company's revenue base becomes more concentrated in terms of both geography and customer relationships. The report does not disclose the identity or number of customers driving the Thailand demand, leaving open the question of how diversified the customer base will be in the new facilities. Observers should monitor whether the Thailand customer mix mirrors the China customer base or is skewed toward a few large hyperscalers or OEMs. A concentrated customer base would increase revenue volatility and bargaining power risk, partially offsetting the diversification benefit.

## The Workforce Transition in Thailand Presents Both Risk and Opportunity

Victory Giant currently employs over 1,400 people in Thailand, with a workforce composition of approximately 50 percent local Thai labor, 25 percent from Myanmar, and 25 percent from mainland China. This is a transitional workforce structure that reflects the early stage of the Thailand operations. The high proportion of expatriate Chinese workers is typical for the initial phase of overseas expansion, as experienced managers and technicians transfer knowledge to local teams. Over time, the expectation is that the Chinese expatriate share will decline as local and regional workers gain proficiency.

The workforce composition matters for two reasons. First, labor productivity is currently lower in Thailand than in China, which directly impacts unit costs and throughput. Second, the reliance on workers from Myanmar introduces geopolitical and regulatory risk, as labor mobility across borders in Southeast Asia can be affected by policy changes in any of the three countries. The medium-term goal of converging gross margins depends on improving labor productivity to China levels, which is achievable but not guaranteed. The report does not provide specific productivity metrics or timelines for workforce transition, making this one of the key operational variables to track in future quarters.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating PCB Capacity Diversification Investments

For those evaluating companies like Victory Giant or other PCB manufacturers pursuing overseas expansion, the following decision framework can help distinguish strategic winners from speculative plays. First, assess the customer base. Is the expansion driven by confirmed customer commitments or by general market optimism? Companies that can name specific customers or show contractual commitments have lower execution risk. Second, evaluate the product mix. Capacity built for high-specification AI PCBs has a different risk-return profile than capacity built for commodity PCBs, where pricing power is lower and competition is more intense. Third, analyze the cost convergence trajectory. Can the company articulate a credible path to closing the cost gap with its home-country operations? The three levers Victory Giant identifies -- automation, local sourcing, and labor productivity -- are specific and measurable. Companies that cannot identify similar levers face a longer period of margin compression.

Fourth, consider the geopolitical scenario analysis. What happens to the investment thesis under different trade policy outcomes? The bull case assumes sustained tariffs and customer preference for geographic diversification. The bear case assumes tariff reduction or normalization, which would reduce the premium customers are willing to pay. A robust investment thesis should hold under multiple scenarios, not just the most favorable one. Finally, monitor the workforce transition metrics. The ratio of expatriate to local workers, productivity per employee, and turnover rates in overseas facilities are leading indicators of operational maturity. Companies that can demonstrate improving productivity and declining expatriate ratios are on a credible path to margin convergence.

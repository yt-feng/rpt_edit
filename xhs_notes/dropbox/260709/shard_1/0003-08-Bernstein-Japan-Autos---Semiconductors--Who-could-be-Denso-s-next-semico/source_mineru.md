Since Denso's acquisition proposal for Rohm was withdrawn at the end of April, a number of investors continue to show interest and raise questions about Denso's next steps. We believe M&A remains the most realistic and effective option to achieve the growth targets outlined in its mid-term plan. In this report, we examine Denso's semiconductor business strategy, and assess the strategic options potentially available to the company.

Denso to expand into industrial and consumer semis: Denso's semi business is entirely captive and centered on Power & Analog, including Si-IGBT, SiC, and battery management ASICs. Semiconductor revenue was equivalent to \~JPY 420 bn in FY3/22, with Discrete accounting for \~74% of revenue and ASICs for \~16%. The mid-term plan highlights expansion of its semi business into industrial/consumer electronics. As Denso's minority investments and partnerships with power semi majors have focused on strengthening its auto business, we believe a target with industrial and consumer exposure is key.

Denso's target segments look attractive: While overall semi market growth is driven by AI-related memory and ASIC/ASSP, Denso's target segments still are appealing. Across 9 devices × 7 applications combinations, Denso's target 6 segments (Discrete/Analog × Automotive/Industrial/Consumer) offer reasonable scale, relatively high and stable growth, and room for consolidation. The combined market size is expected to grow from USD 50 bn (2025) to USD 80 bn (2030), implying a CAGR of 10%.

Who could be the best fit? We view Fuji Electric (NC) as the most feasible option in terms of acquisition cost, device/application portfolio and management fit. It has the lowest market cap among the major power semi candidates at \~JPY 2 tn, with a long relationship in IGBT/SiC development and meaningful industrial exposure. For the Rohm/Toshiba/MELCO consortium (All NC), Denso might be able to take a partial stake, but a full acquisition would likely be operationally difficult, in our view. A hostile acquisition of Rohm would also be difficult given the reputational risk. While Onsemi (NC) has an attractive product portfolio, we think fully acquiring Onsemi, Renesas, Infineon/STM (NC) looks financially difficult.

Implications for Japan Autos: Automakers are internalizing H/W-linked S/W layers where Denso has been strong amid the shift to SDVs making expansion in semiconductors important. Cost reduction of power semi is critical for improving Toyota's xEV profitability, which would potentially make Denso's M&A reasonable. But using Toyota stake proceeds for M&A could disappoint investors expecting shareholder returns, while buybacks would be positive for Toyota. Reiterate Market-Perform/Outperform on Denso/Toyota.

Implications for Japan/EU Semiconductors: We are constructive on power semi given the rise of Al power (Link). We think a Fuji Electric transaction would likely have a limited impact on Renesas, as Denso's benefit would be concentrated in power semis. A Rohm/Toshiba structure could be more relevant for Renesas by strengthening Denso's domestic power semi platform and reducing external sourcing. However, Renesas's MCU leadership and broad customer base would likely limit core business risk. The impact on Infineon would likely also be limited. Reiterate Outperform on Renesas and Infineon.

## INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS

## Japan Autos and Auto Parts:

As the industry shifts toward SDVs, automakers are increasingly seeking control over hardware-linked upstream software layers, where suppliers such as Denso have historically captured value through integrated hardware and embedded software offerings. This shift could reduce the scope for supplier-led system design over time, making Denso's expansion into upstream hardware such as semiconductors, as well as non-auto businesses, strategically important. For Toyota, cost reduction of power semiconductors, particularly SiC, is important for improving its xEV competitiveness, which would make a potential acquisition by Denso's of a power semiconductor company strategically reasonable. However, if Denso were to use proceeds from a potential sale of its Toyota stake to fund semiconductor M&A, this could disappoint Denso's investors expecting shareholder returns. Conversely, if Toyota were to buy back those shares, it would be positive for Toyota. We remain Market-Perform on Denso (PT = 2,050.00) and Outperform on Toyota (PT=4,200.00).

## Japan and European Semiconductors:

We are constructive on power semi given the rise of AI power (Infineon/Renesas: CPU renaissance emerges as a second engine for power semi demand, set to outstrip supply). We believe a successful Fuji Electric transaction would likely limit direct impact on Renesas, as the strategic benefit for Denso would be concentrated in power semis rather than in Renesas's core MCU franchise. A Rohm/Toshiba-related structure could be more relevant for Renesas, as it would give Denso a broader domestic power semiconductor platform and potentially reduce external sourcing needs in selected auto discrete and analog areas. However, Renesas remains much stronger in automotive MCUs and has a broader customer base across OEMs and Tier-1s, limiting the risk of meaningful share loss in its core business. For Infineon, the impact would potentially be limited given only 9% sales exposure to Japanese OEMs, although it remains the second-largest automotive semiconductor supplier in Japan by market share. We rate Renesas Outperform with PT = ¥6,300.00 and rate Infineon Outperform with PT = €102.00.

## OVERVIEW OF DENSO'S SEMICONDUCTOR BUSINESS

## Current state of Denso's semiconductor business

Denso's semiconductor business currently spans three categories: MCU & SoC, Power & Analog, and Sensors (Exhibit 1). At present, all semiconductor products are developed primarily for internal use, supporting Denso's auto parts business. In 'Microcomputer and SoC' subsegment, Denso focuses on architecture design and specification development rather than manufacturing. The company works closely with microcomputer and SoC vendors to develop custom chips tailored to its automotive applications, while wafer fabrication is outsourced to foundries such as TSMC and UMC.

Within ‘Sensor’ subsegment, Denso develops semiconductor devices that support its ADAS/AD businesses. While the company continues to procure a significant portion of semiconductor content from external suppliers, it develops and manufactures application devices, including image sensors, millimeter-wave radar chips, and LiDAR-related semiconductors.

'Power & Analog' subsegment is the most important subsegment of Denso's semiconductor business, representing the area where the company has internalized the entire value chain from device design to manufacturing. Denso develops and produces key semiconductors for electrification, including Si and SiC power semiconductors used in xEV inverters as well as battery management ASICs. This vertically integrated model enables the company to maintain control over critical technologies while supporting the growing semiconductor content required for automotive electrification.

Denso's history in power and analog semiconductors dates back several decades. The company began IC research in the 1950s, established its first wafer fabrication facility in 1975, and further expanded its semiconductor capabilities through the acquisition of Toyota's electronic component business in 2020.

Particularly in power semiconductors, Denso possesses a broad range of proprietary technologies (Exhibit 2, Exhibit 3). In Si power semiconductors, the company jointly developed RC-IGBTs with Fuji Electric, integrating IGBTs and diodes into a single device to achieve smaller size and lower power loss, and has deployed the technology in HEVs. In SiC, Denso has proprietary RAF and gas growth technologies for SiC wafer production, enabling the manufacture of high quality, low cost SiC wafers.

## Denso's semiconductor business strategy

Denso identified semiconductors as one of its ‘Societal Value Expansion Domains’ in the mid-term plan announced in Mar-2026 (Exhibit 6). The company aims to expand the application of its semiconductor technologies beyond automotive markets and ‘contribute to a wide range of fields, including industrial equipment and consumer electronics’. Given that Denso currently generates no external semiconductor revenue and derives nearly all of its semiconductor sales from automotive applications, we believe that power semiconductor companies with meaningful exposure to industrial and consumer end markets could be attractive strategic targets for the company.

Denso has historically pursued minority equity investments to strengthen semiconductor supply security and strategic partnerships (Exhibit 7). Investments have included Infineon and Renesas in 2018, JASM (private, TSMC's Japanese manufacturing subsidiary), in 2022, Coherent's SiC wafer business in 2023, onsemi (not covered) in 2024, and Rohm (not covered) in 2025.

Beyond equity investments, Denso has also established a broad range of strategic partnerships. These include Qualcomm for next-generation cockpit systems in 2020, USJC for Si power semiconductor manufacturing at UMC's Japanese fab in 2022, Fuji Electric for joint SiC production in 2024, MediaTek (covered by Mark Li) for SoC development in 2025, and Horizon Robotics (covered by Qingyuan Lin) for ADAS and autonomous driving systems targeting the Chinese market in 2025.

While Denso has built deep relationships with a diverse set of semiconductor companies through minority investments and partnerships, these initiatives have largely been aimed at strengthening its automotive business. In the context of semiconductor M&A, however, acquisition targets that can deliver meaningful inorganic growth beyond the automotive market are likely to be of greater strategic importance, in our view.

## MARKET ATTRACTIVENESS OF DENSO'S TARGET SEMICONDUCTOR SEGMENTS

According to Gartner, the global semiconductor market is expected to grow from USD 809 bn in 2026 to USD 2,153 bn in 2030, implying a CAGR of 21.6% (Exhibit 8). Growth is expected to be driven primarily by memory and ASIC/ASSP markets, which benefit from rising AI-related demand, including discrete GPUs and AI accelerators (Exhibit 9). Combined, these categories are expected to account for more than 80% of the semiconductor market throughout the period from 2026 onward.

By comparison, the discrete and analog markets, which represent Denso's primary areas of focus, are expected to account for only around 5% of the overall semiconductor market. Nevertheless, the combined market size is projected to increase steadily from USD 79 bn in 2026 to USD 111 bn in 2030, making them attractive markets characterized by stable growth rather than rapid expansion.

## EVALUATING DENSO'S POTENTIAL SEMICONDUCTOR M&A OPTIONS

Based on the analysis above, we assess the semiconductor M&A options potentially available to Denso. Given that Rohm was the company's most recent acquisition target, we focus our analysis on major power semiconductor vendors. We exclude analog semiconductor companies from consideration, as the leading players in the segment, such as Texas Instruments and Analog Devices (both covered by Stacy Rasgon), are substantially larger than Denso's realistic acquisition universe. In addition, Denso's semiconductor business remains primarily focused on power semiconductors, making power semiconductor vendors the most relevant strategic targets, in our view. Among Japanese power semiconductor companies, we focus on Fuji Electric, Rohm, Mitsubishi Electric (MELCO, not covered), Toshiba (private), and Renesas, while onsemi (not covered), Infineon, and STMicroelectronics (not covered) represent potential US and European candidates (Exhibit 16).

Fuji Electric appears to be Denso's closest semiconductor partner. In Si power semiconductors, the two companies jointly developed RC-IGBTs, and we believe the majority of Fuji Electric's automotive semiconductor business is currently tied to Denso. The relationship has deepened further through their planned joint production of SiC power semiconductors, which is scheduled to begin in 2H CY2026. Taken together, the long-standing technological collaboration, manufacturing partnership, and existing business ties suggest a particularly high degree of strategic and operational fit between the two companies.

Rohm became a strategic partner of Denso in 2025 through a collaboration focused on analog ICs and automotive semiconductors, accompanied by Denso's acquisition of \~5% stake in the company. In March 2026, Denso submitted a proposal to take Rohm private, which was subsequently rejected by Rohm's management. Rohm has also been working with Toshiba since 2023 to explore collaboration in power semiconductors, although progress has been limited. Following Denso's approach, Rohm expanded the discussions to include Mitsubishi Electric and has been exploring a potential business and management integration among the three companies (link). However, little visible progress has been made over the past two months. For Denso, both Mitsubishi Electric and Toshiba currently maintain conventional business relationships as suppliers of power semiconductors and other components.

Renesas is one of the world's leading automotive MCU suppliers and has long maintained close ties with Toyota and Denso, reflecting its historical origins. Denso increased its ownership stake in Renesas from \~0.5% to 5% in 2018, before gradually reducing it to around 0.1% by 2025. Through a series of acquisitions, Renesas has expanded its presence in analog semiconductors while also strengthening its power semiconductor portfolio. However, the company previously abandoned plans to establish in-house SiC production capabilities.

Among US and European power semiconductor companies, Denso maintains equity relationships with several players. In 2024, Denso acquired a stake in Onsemi and currently owns \~0.05% of the company. The investment was made in connection with their collaboration in ADAS/AD semiconductors. Similarly, Denso acquired a stake in Infineon in 2018 to strengthen cooperation in automotive semiconductors and currently holds \~0.2% of the company. Following the investment, the two companies have reportedly deepened their business relationship through regular management-level discussions and other collaborative activities. STMicroelectronics is the only one of the three major power semiconductor vendors in which Denso does not hold an equity stake. As such, the relationship appears to be limited to a conventional customer-supplier partnership.

To assess strategic fit, we first examine each company's semiconductor portfolio by device category (Exhibit 17). Fuji Electric is effectively a pure-play power semiconductor company, with Discrete devices accounting for 88% of semiconductor revenue. In contrast, Rohm maintains a well-balanced portfolio centered on Analog and Discrete semiconductors while also participating in a broad range of other semiconductor categories, highlighting why the company has attracted Denso's interest.

Mitsubishi Electric derives \~60% of its semiconductor revenue from discrete devices, while also maintaining meaningful exposure to communications-related semiconductors, including Nonoptical sensors and Optoelectronics. Toshiba's portfolio is similarly diversified, with Discrete devices accounting for roughly 40% of revenue and Optoelectronics representing \~19%, resulting in a balanced mix across multiple semiconductor categories.

Renesas stands apart from the other power semiconductor players. Microcomponents account for 42% of its semiconductor revenue, followed by ASSPs at 32%, making Analog and Discrete products relatively minor parts of its overall portfolio.

Infineon and STMicroelectronics also maintain diversified portfolios, although Analog is the largest category for both companies, accounting for roughly 30% of revenue. This portfolio is more comparable to those of Japanese power semiconductor vendors, particularly Toshiba. As a result, onsemi's product mix appears strategically attractive from Denso's perspective.

Finally, we assess Denso's potential strategic options. Our evaluation is based on four criteria: acquisition cost, device portfolio, application portfolio, and strategic and management fit with Denso (Exhibit 19).

We believe the most likely scenario could be a full acquisition of Fuji Electric. Among the potential targets, Fuji Electric has the lowest market cap at \~JPY 2.0 tn. Even assuming a 40% acquisition premium, the implied transaction value of JPY 2.8 tn remains within Denso's estimated funding capacity of JPY 4.0 tn. In addition, the two companies have built a close business relationship over many years. Fuji Electric's focus on power semiconductors, supplemented by selective analog products and meaningful exposure to industrial applications, aligns well with Denso's strategic objective of expanding beyond the automotive market. As a result, Fuji Electric appears to be the most feasible and strategically attractive acquisition candidate.

The second scenario could be for Denso to participate in the ongoing discussions among Rohm, Toshiba, and Mitsubishi Electric regarding the integration of their power semiconductor businesses. Based on a rough calculation using each company's market cap and the proportion of revenue attributable to semiconductors, we estimate the combined entity would be worth \~JPY 3.1 tn and JPY 4.3 tn with a 40% premium. While larger than Fuji Electric, such a transaction would still appear financially feasible for Denso, even under a full acquisition scenario. As such, a partial stake acquisition would appear to be the more realistic structure. From a strategic perspective, the combined portfolio would offer a well-balanced mix across Automotive, Industrial, and other end markets, as well as a broad device portfolio. However, we see meaningful execution hurdles. Rohm initially expanded the discussions with Toshiba and Mitsubishi Electric as a response to Denso's acquisition approach, and we believe management opposition to Denso's participation is likely to remain significant. Even if Denso were able to join the discussions, the process would expand into a four-party negotiation, potentially further complicating talks that have shown little visible progress over the past two months.

The next option could be a majority acquisition of Onsemi. Like Rohm, Onsemi combines a strong power semiconductor business with a diversified device and application portfolio, making it an attractive strategic fit for Denso. However, Onsemi's market cap is \~JPY 6.0 tn. Assuming a 40% acquisition premium, a full acquisition would require \~JPY 8.4 tn, making such a transaction unrealistic for Denso. As a result, a full acquisition would likely be unrealistic, leaving a majority stake acquisition as the most feasible structure. Even then, the challenges associated with managing a large US-listed semiconductor company could represent a meaningful hurdle for Denso.

We view a hostile acquisition of Rohm, as well as acquisitions of Renesas, Infineon/STM, as relatively unlikely scenarios. A hostile acquisition of Rohm would likely create meaningful challenges in post-merger integration and management, while also exposing Denso and the broader Toyota group to reputational risk. Renesas appears even less likely given its large market cap, which effectively rules out a full acquisition. In addition, Renesas remains primarily focused on MCUs rather than power semiconductors, and Denso has gradually reduced its ownership stake over the past eight years, suggesting limited strategic and managerial alignment. Similarly, the size of Infineon and STM makes a full acquisition unrealistic. While minority investments could be conceivable, such investments would be unlikely to materially advance Denso's mid-tem strategy.

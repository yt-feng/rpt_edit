## The Starship Economics Are Not About Rockets; They Are About Unlocking Markets That Currently Do Not Exist at Scale

The report makes a clear distinction between what SPCX already has and what it needs to build. The company's existing launch capabilities are already market-leading. Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy have demonstrated reliability, reusability, and cost advantages that have reshaped the launch industry. But those vehicles are not sufficient for the trillion-dollar vision. They are too small and too expensive per kilogram to enable the massive deployment of satellites and orbital infrastructure that the connectivity and AI businesses require.

Starship is the key. The report's language is precise: the successful deployment of Starship will establish the most affordable and scalable path to unlocking the economic potential of space. That is not a claim about engineering excellence in the abstract. It is a claim about cost structure. If Starship achieves its target of delivering mass to orbit at a fraction of current costs, then the economics of every downstream application change. Satellite constellations become cheaper to deploy and replace. Orbital data centers become feasible. The unit economics of space-based connectivity shift from niche to mass-market.

The so-what here is critical. Observers often focus on whether Starship will work technically. That is the wrong question. The right question is whether Starship can work at the scale and cadence the report assumes. The difference between a successful test flight and a vehicle that can fly hundreds of times per year is enormous. It is the difference between a proof of concept and an industrial capability. The report acknowledges this by emphasizing the ramp from tens of launches in 2027 to thousands by 2031. That ramp is the single most important operational metric to track.

## Connectivity Is Not Just a Satellite Business; It Is a Telecom Infrastructure Play with the Potential to Reshape Global Bandwidth Markets

The connectivity segment is built on Starlink, but the report goes beyond the current direct-to-consumer broadband model. It envisions a full end-to-end mobile service offering that includes direct-to-device connectivity, expanding the total addressable market far beyond rural and underserved areas. The logic is straightforward: if SPCX can deploy a dense enough LEO constellation with sufficient bandwidth, it can compete with terrestrial mobile network operators for a share of the global telecom market.

The numbers in the report support the ambition. Revenue from the connectivity segment is projected to grow from roughly $28 billion in 2027 to levels that would make it one of the largest telecom companies in the world. The gross margins in the segment are expected to be high, driven by the low marginal cost of adding subscribers to an already-deployed satellite network. This is the classic infrastructure business model: high upfront capital expenditure, followed by high incremental margins on usage.

But the report also flags a risk that is easy to overlook. Approval for Starlink to offer broadband and direct-to-device services in additional countries is listed as a key milestone. That is not a technical milestone. It is a regulatory one. The connectivity thesis depends on SPCX gaining access to spectrum and market entry in major economies. Regulatory approval is not guaranteed, and the timelines for such approvals are unpredictable. Those who focus only on the engineering milestones may underestimate the extent to which the connectivity revenue trajectory depends on political and regulatory outcomes.

## The AI Thesis Is the Most Speculative and the Most Consequential: It Combines Enterprise Software, Infrastructure, and Orbital Computing into a Single Bet

The report splits the AI opportunity into two distinct sub-segments: enterprise AI and orbital AI. The enterprise AI business includes the Grok large language model and the Cursor coding assistant acquisition. The orbital AI business is more futuristic, envisioning space-based data centers that can process data in orbit, reducing latency and bandwidth costs for certain applications.

The enterprise AI thesis is grounded in the idea that SPCX can leverage its massive compute allocation and real-time data from its X platform to train and deploy frontier models. The report notes that Grok has yet to take off in a meaningful way, but it points to the next generation of the model, trained on Blackwell clusters, as a potential catalyst. The Cursor acquisition adds strategic value by giving SPCX a foothold in the enterprise AI coding market, which is growing rapidly and has high switching costs.

The orbital AI thesis is more speculative but also more unique. If SPCX can successfully launch and operate AI satellites that process data in orbit, it could address constraints in the terrestrial AI market: power availability, data center space, and latency for time-sensitive applications. The report acknowledges that this is execution-dependent, but the potential economics are attractive because the non-chip infrastructure costs are lower in space than on Earth for certain use cases.

The so-what for observers is that the AI thesis is the most differentiated part of the SPCX story. Every major tech company is building AI. Very few are building orbital AI. If the orbital AI thesis proves out, SPCX could have a moat that no terrestrial competitor can replicate. But the risks are correspondingly high. The technology is unproven at scale, the regulatory framework for orbital data centers is unclear, and the market demand for orbital AI processing is still hypothetical.

## Extreme Vertical Integration Creates a Cost and Scale Advantage That Competitors Cannot Replicate, but It Also Concentrates Risk

The report emphasizes SPCX's extreme vertical integration as a structural advantage. The company designs and manufactures its own rockets, satellites, and ground equipment. It operates its own launch facilities. It controls its own supply chain for critical components. This integration allows SPCX to drive costs down, increase throughput, and deliver at a scale that competitors cannot match.

The Terafab semiconductor facility is a specific example. By bringing chip manufacturing in-house, SPCX can optimize its hardware for its specific use cases rather than relying on off-the-shelf components designed for terrestrial applications. The partnership with Tesla adds another layer of integration, potentially allowing SPCX to leverage Tesla's manufacturing expertise and supply chain.

But the concentration of risk is a double-edged sword. If a single component or manufacturing process fails, the entire system is affected. Vertical integration reduces dependency on external suppliers, but it also means that internal failures cannot be mitigated by switching vendors. The report acknowledges technology, manufacturing, and operating execution risks, but it does not fully explore the scenario in which vertical integration becomes a liability rather than an asset.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Supernormal Growth and the Competitive Response

The report's long-term valuation of $900-plus depends on SPCX maintaining supernormal growth rates for an extended period. The implied revenue trajectory is extraordinary: from roughly $19 billion in 2025 to over $160 billion in 2028, with further growth beyond. That trajectory assumes that SPCX can scale its connectivity and AI businesses without facing meaningful competitive pressure or market saturation.

The report does not fully address what happens when competitors respond. In connectivity, terrestrial mobile operators are not passive. They are investing in 5G and 6G networks, and they have regulatory tools that could slow Starlink's market entry. In AI, the competitive landscape is dominated by companies with enormous compute resources and deep talent pools. The fact that Grok has not yet taken off is a signal that the market is not easy to enter, even for a company with SPCX's resources.

The report also leaves open the question of capital intensity. The cash flow projections show negative free cash flow through 2028, with cumulative capital expenditures exceeding $350 billion over the forecast period. That level of investment requires continued access to capital markets at favorable terms. If the Starship milestones slip or if the connectivity or AI businesses take longer to ramp, the funding requirements could become a constraint.

For readers who want to evaluate the thesis for themselves, the report provides enough data to build a simple decision framework. Three questions are most important.

First, does Starship achieve payload delivery to orbit in the second half of 2026? If it does, the technical risk is significantly reduced. If it does not, the timeline for the entire thesis shifts, and the valuation assumptions become harder to justify.

Second, does Starlink gain regulatory approval in major additional markets over the next 12 to 18 months? If it does, the connectivity revenue trajectory becomes more credible. If it does not, the connectivity segment may be limited to markets where SPCX already has approval, which would cap the addressable market.

Third, does Grok or Cursor demonstrate meaningful traction in enterprise AI? If either product shows accelerating adoption, the AI thesis gains credibility. If both remain niche, the AI segment may be a long-duration option rather than a near-term revenue driver.

These three questions are not exhaustive, but they cover the most critical uncertainties. Those who track these milestones will have a clearer view of whether the $900 path is realistic or whether the $200 base case is the more appropriate anchor.

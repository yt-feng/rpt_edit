EN_A: So, there’s a big new research report out on a major space company that’s getting a lot of buzz. Have you seen it?
EN_B: I have. It’s from a global investment bank initiating coverage with a buy rating and a 200 dollar price target.
EN_A: Two hundred dollars. The stock is around 160, so that’s about 25% upside. But I heard the report also mentions a much bigger number, something like 900 dollars?
EN_B: Exactly. The 200 dollar target is just a near-term milestone. The real thesis is that if the company can prove its key technologies, especially the Starship system, at scale, the stock could eventually move towards 900 dollars or more.
EN_A: So what’s the core question this report is trying to answer? Why do they think it’s worth that much?
EN_B: The core logic is that the company has unrivaled launch capabilities. That gives it a unique key to unlock two trillion-dollar markets: connectivity and artificial intelligence. They see it not just as a rocket company, but as a builder of space infrastructure.
EN_A: Connectivity, that’s Starlink, right? But Starlink has been around for a while. Why is it suddenly a trillion-dollar opportunity?
EN_B: Because the report argues that Starship will dramatically lower launch costs. That allows Starlink to expand from broadband to direct-to-device services, covering billions of people worldwide who still have no internet access. It means they could evolve from a satellite internet company into a dominant global telecom player.
EN_A: And what about AI? How does a space company play in AI?
EN_B: This is one of the most fascinating parts of the report. They break AI into two pieces. One is terrestrial enterprise AI, through acquisitions like Cursor and their own Grok model. The other is something they call Orbital AI.
EN_A: Orbital AI? That sounds like science fiction.
EN_B: It is a bold concept. The report argues that building AI data centers on earth is constrained by power, land, and cooling. In space, you have unlimited solar power and can radiate heat very efficiently. This company has the capability to deploy AI computing clusters in orbit.
EN_A: That’s pretty wild. What are some of the key milestones the report highlights to get there?
EN_B: Quite a few. Starship needs to start delivering payloads to orbit in the second half of this year. They need to demonstrate catching the Starship second stage with the “chopsticks” by the end of 2026. Further out, they want to launch tens of times in 2027, hundreds by 2028, and thousands per year by 2031.
EN_A: Those are extremely ambitious milestones. What about the risks?
EN_B: The report is very clear about them. There are technology risks, manufacturing risks, operational risks, and regulatory risks in every vertical. They even note the company is good at delivering results, but not always on time. That’s why the 200 dollar target is such a heavily discounted version of their long-term view.
EN_A: So, for a regular investor, what’s the one big question the report leaves hanging?
EN_B: The biggest unanswered question, in my view, is the cash flow path. The report shows the company going from billions in losses today to nearly 80 billion in profit by 2028. But the free cash flow is deeply negative for years. How exactly do they finance that transition? The report doesn’t fully detail that bridge.

EN_B: It does. The report's core argument is that solar glass might be approaching a near-term inflection point. After about nine months of inventory buildup and price declines, industry inventories appear to have peaked in mid-June and have since dropped by roughly 10 percent, with the pace of destocking accelerating recently.
EN_A: So what does that mean? Is demand suddenly picking up, or is something happening on the supply side?
EN_B: It's a bit of both. The report suggests the industry could enter a destocking phase in July, supported by a modest recovery in module demand, up 10 percent month-over-month, and declining glass supply. More importantly, current glass prices are at historic lows, meaning the entire industry is burning cash.
EN_A: Burning cash? That's clearly not sustainable. So what about a specific company like Xinyi Solar? Its stock is down about 38 percent since mid-May. Is this a good entry point?
EN_B: The report argues that Xinyi Solar's current valuation at 0.6 times book value offers an attractive risk-reward profile. They believe any near-term price rebound could act as a catalyst for the stock. However, they did cut their target price because glass prices in the first half of the year were weaker than expected.
EN_A: What about other parts of the solar supply chain, like polysilicon? Is it a similar story?
EN_B: Quite the opposite. The polysilicon situation is more worrying. The roughly three-month destocking period appears to be over. As southwestern China enters the wet season, idled polysilicon capacity is gradually coming back online. The report expects July production to increase by 6 to 7 percent month-over-month.
EN_A: So inventory pressure is building up again?
EN_B: Exactly. The report warns that polysilicon prices could even undershoot to 30 RMB per kilogram in the third quarter, which is lower than where they are now. While new energy-efficiency standards have been finalized, the report believes that alone won't be enough to restore industry profitability because weak end-demand remains the key challenge.
EN_A: So solar glass and polysilicon are in completely different situations. Which part of the value chain is doing best right now?
EN_B: According to the report, module manufacturers are currently in the best position. Their margins are positive. Even though module prices have dipped slightly, lower upstream costs have helped them maintain profitability. The report sees module producers as the relative winners in the near term.
EN_A: So what's the investment pecking order? Does the report give a clear ranking?
EN_B: Yes, it does. The order of preference is: LONGi for its BC technology, then Drinda for its space solar business, followed by Xinyi Solar as glass margins near a trough, then GCL Tech which has battery material upside but polysilicon pressure, and finally Tongwei which is most exposed to the polysilicon oversupply.
EN_A: That's a clear hierarchy. And what about the new efficiency standards? Which companies benefit the most?
EN_B: The report views the new module efficiency standards as a potentially more meaningful catalyst for technology leaders like LONGi and Drinda. Based on the data they cite, only 8 out of 20 commercially available TOPCon products currently meet the new requirements. This means the standards should favor higher-efficiency, larger-scale manufacturers and could accelerate industry consolidation.
EN_A: So there are clear winners and losers emerging. But what questions does this report leave unanswered?
EN_B: Quite a few. For example, how sustainable is the solar glass price rebound? That depends on the strength of end-demand recovery, which the report says still needs to be verified. Also, when will polysilicon capacity truly be rationalized, and how strictly will the new energy standards be enforced? These are the key uncertainties the report doesn't fully resolve.

## Alibaba's All-Stack AI Capability Creates a Defensible Moat That Pure-Play Cloud Providers Cannot Replicate

The most underappreciated aspect of Alibaba's cloud business is not its revenue growth rate but the vertical integration of its AI capabilities. The company possesses a full-stack position across the AI value chain that is unmatched among Chinese technology firms. This spans proprietary AI chips developed by T-head, the IT infrastructure of China's largest public cloud platform, the Bailian large language model platform, and the advanced Qwen foundation model. This is not a collection of disparate assets. It is a deliberately constructed stack in which each layer reinforces the others.

The strategic implication is significant. In a market where AI adoption is accelerating but infrastructure supply is fragmented, Alibaba can offer enterprises an integrated solution that reduces integration risk and accelerates deployment timelines. A company seeking to deploy AI workloads does not need to assemble its own stack from different vendors. It can access the entire capability from Alibaba, with the assurance that the chips, the cloud, the model platform, and the foundation model are designed to work together. This is a structural advantage that no pure-play cloud provider in China can match.

The financial consequence is already visible. The MaaS business has surpassed its guidance, and the bank report estimates that cloud margins will improve further from the current level of 11-12%, driven by price increases taken earlier this year and the increasing revenue share of higher-margin MaaS offerings. As AI workloads scale, the marginal cost of serving additional demand declines, while the pricing power derived from an integrated stack increases. This is the classic dynamic of platform economics, and Alibaba's cloud business is entering that phase.

## E-Commerce Earnings Are More Resilient Than Revenue Suggests, But the Growth Engine Has Shifted

The weakness in Alibaba's e-commerce revenue is well documented. An 8% decline in customer management revenue is not a positive signal for a business that has historically been the company's primary profit center. However, the earnings picture is more nuanced than the top-line numbers suggest. The bank report estimates that China e-commerce EBITA, excluding quick commerce, likely declined only 3% year-on-year to CNY 48 billion, despite the 8% revenue drop. The loss from quick commerce likely narrowed significantly to CNY 10.3 billion, compared with a loss of CNY 18 billion in the prior quarter and market expectations of a CNY 12-13 billion loss.

This earnings resilience is not accidental. Alibaba has been managing its e-commerce business for profitability rather than growth, cutting costs, rationalizing investments, and focusing on high-margin segments. The international commerce business, AIDC, likely swung to a profit with EBITA of CNY 658 million, compared with a slight loss a year ago. The overall e-commerce segment, including both China and overseas operations, is estimated to report stable year-on-year EBITA of CNY 38 billion in the June quarter, above the Bloomberg consensus estimate of CNY 36 billion.

The key takeaway here is that Alibaba's earnings power is becoming less dependent on e-commerce revenue growth. The company has demonstrated an ability to maintain profitability in a weak revenue environment through cost discipline and portfolio management. But this is a defensive strategy, not a growth strategy. The real growth engine is now cloud and AI, and observers should be asking whether the e-commerce business can generate sufficient cash flow to fund the AI investments that will drive future returns. The answer, based on the current data, appears to be yes, but the margin of safety is narrowing.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of AI Cloud Margins and the Competitive Response

For all the detail in the bank's analysis, there are several open questions that the report does not fully resolve. The first is the sustainability of cloud margin expansion. The report attributes margin improvement to price increases and a shift toward higher-margin MaaS revenue. But price increases in a competitive market are not guaranteed to stick, especially as other Chinese cloud providers, including state-backed players, ramp up their AI capabilities. The question of whether Alibaba can maintain its pricing power as the market matures remains unanswered.

The second open question is the competitive response from other major Chinese technology firms. Tencent, ByteDance, and Baidu all have significant AI cloud ambitions. The report mentions Alibaba's all-stack advantage, but it does not analyze how that advantage holds up as competitors build their own integrated stacks. The AI cloud market in China is not a winner-take-all market, but it is a market where the top players will compete intensely for enterprise customers. The margin trajectory that the bank forecasts assumes a degree of pricing discipline that may not materialize.

The third question is the capital intensity of the AI cloud business. The report's financial projections show capital expenditure increasing from CNY 85.9 billion in FY25 to CNY 126 billion in FY26, and continuing to rise to CNY 172 billion by FY29. Free cash flow turns negative in FY26 and only recovers to CNY 42 billion by FY29. The question is whether the returns on this invested capital will justify the cash flow drag in the near term. The bank's valuation methodology, which values AliCloud at 7x FY28 price-to-sales, implies a high degree of confidence in those returns. But the data to confirm that confidence is not yet available.

## A Decision Framework for Observers: Three Questions to Answer Before Acting

For those trying to translate this analysis into a portfolio decision, three questions provide a useful framework.

First, do you believe that Alibaba's AI cloud business can sustain 40%+ revenue growth for the next three to four years? The bank's projections imply that cloud revenue will be the primary driver of the company's overall revenue acceleration from 2.7% growth in FY26 to 19.7% growth in FY29. If cloud growth decelerates faster than expected, the entire revenue story collapses. The evidence so far is positive, but the base of comparison is still small relative to the total cloud market.

Second, can Alibaba maintain e-commerce profitability without significant revenue recovery? The June quarter shows that earnings resilience is possible, but it is not sustainable indefinitely. If customer management revenue continues to decline, the cost cuts will eventually hit bone. The quick commerce business is narrowing its losses, but it is still burning over CNY 10 billion per quarter. The path to profitability for that business is not yet clear.

Third, what is the appropriate valuation for a company that is transitioning from a high-margin e-commerce model to a capital-intensive AI infrastructure model? The bank's sum-of-the-parts valuation implies a forward price-to-earnings multiple of approximately 10x for the core business. That is not expensive for a company with 50% earnings growth in FY27 and 34% growth in FY28. But it assumes that the market will re-rate Alibaba as a growth stock rather than a value trap. That re-rating depends on the answers to the first two questions.

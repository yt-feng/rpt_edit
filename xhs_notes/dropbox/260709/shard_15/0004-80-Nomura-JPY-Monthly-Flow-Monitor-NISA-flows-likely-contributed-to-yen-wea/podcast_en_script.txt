EN_A: So the yen has been all over the place recently. There’s a global investment bank report that dives into the actual flow data for June. What’s the big takeaway?
EN_B: The big one is that NISA flows — basically Japanese retail investors buying foreign assets through tax-free accounts — likely added to the yen’s weakness in June. That’s a new factor most people aren’t talking about.
EN_A: Wait, retail investors can move the yen? How big were these flows?
EN_B: The report uses high-frequency data to show that investment trusts, which are the main vehicle for NISA, were net buyers of foreign equities by nearly 800 billion yen in June. When Japanese households buy U.S. stocks, they’re effectively selling yen and buying dollars.
EN_A: So that explains why the yen weakened so fast in June. Will it continue?
EN_B: Probably not at the same pace. Global equity gains have moderated, and June flows were likely boosted by summer bonuses. Plus, the risk of FX intervention is making people more cautious. So the report expects NISA-driven selling of yen to slow from July.
EN_A: Interesting. So retail is now a variable for the yen. What about the big institutional players?
EN_B: They’re split. Banks have sold foreign bonds for six straight months. The reason is that the return on U.S. Treasuries via asset swaps has become less attractive. Meanwhile, trust accounts did a big rebalance in June — they sold 1.3 trillion yen in foreign equities and bought 1.5 trillion yen in foreign bonds.
EN_A: That sounds like GPIF, Japan’s giant pension fund. Are they shifting into domestic bonds?
EN_B: That’s the million-dollar question. And the report says there’s no clear evidence yet that GPIF is actively raising its domestic bond allocation. Their portfolio is still hovering around the 25 percent target for each asset class. But the full report digs deeper into this.
EN_A: So the GPIF story is still a cliffhanger. What about foreign investors? Did they buy or sell Japan in June?
EN_B: They sold big. Foreign investors dumped 5.5 trillion yen of Japanese securities — both stocks and bonds. That’s the first time they’ve sold both since December 2022. The stock selling was concentrated in late June, tied to a tech stock correction across East Asia.
EN_A: And the bond selling? Was that about the BOJ being behind the curve?
EN_B: Exactly. The report points to two fears: the BOJ may be too slow to hike, and the government might pressure the central bank. But there’s a twist — recent headlines suggest the government might soften its language. If the market sees that as more market-friendly, foreign investors could come back to JGBs.
EN_A: So what are the biggest open questions that the full report addresses?
EN_B: Two things. First, life insurers have been cautious about unhedged foreign bonds in their investment plans, but they haven’t actually sold much. If the yen stays weak, could they start buying more unhedged bonds? Second, in May, Japanese investors bought about 3 trillion yen in foreign bonds, mostly U.S. and German bonds, but they sold Australian bonds. The full report breaks down those country-level flows and what they mean.

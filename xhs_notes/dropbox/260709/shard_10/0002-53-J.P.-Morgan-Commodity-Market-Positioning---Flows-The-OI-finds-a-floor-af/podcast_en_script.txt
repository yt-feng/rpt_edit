EN_A: So, the latest global investment bank report on commodities just came out. The headline is that total open interest finally stopped falling after six weeks. What is the big picture here?
EN_B: The big picture is that, as of July 3, the estimated value of open interest across tracked commodity markets ticked up slightly to 1.7 trillion dollars. But this was not driven by new money flowing in. It was mostly a price-driven recovery, especially in petroleum products, natural gas, and precious metals.
EN_A: So the rebound is more about price than conviction? Does that mean the market is still fragile?
EN_B: Exactly. The fragility is real. While the total value went up, the energy sector still saw 13 billion dollars in contract-based outflows, mostly from crude oil. The report flags one key variable that could change everything.
EN_A: What is that variable? Geopolitics?
EN_B: Partly. The market has been pricing in the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz since mid-May, which caused the earlier sell-off. Now, a lot of stranded oil is re-entering the system, which risks a temporary glut, especially since demand from China remains subdued.
EN_A: So what does that mean for crude oil prices in the near term?
EN_B: The report sees near-term pressure. But whether that pressure persists really hinges on two things. First, whether China will resume buying crude. Second, how quickly global inventories get replenished. The base case is that China will eventually come back, but the timing is not clear. That is one of the big open questions in the report.
EN_A: That sounds like a classic cliffhanger. Moving on, precious metals seem to have had a good week. What is happening there?
EN_B: Precious metals were a bright spot. Open interest increased by 3 percent week-over-week to 250 billion dollars. Net managed money positions in gold futures also increased. The report notes that rate-sensitive ETF flows have regained some pricing power as buying from other sectors cooled down.
EN_A: Does that mean gold is about to rally? And what about the Fed?
EN_B: The report is cautious. It argues the Fed will likely stay patient this year, which limits the downside for gold. But reduced demand forecasts point to a more range-bound outlook. Their base case is 4300 dollars an ounce in the third quarter and 4500 in the fourth. But again, that is just the base case.
EN_A: Okay, what about base metals? Copper and aluminum?
EN_B: Base metals were flat overall, but there is a big divergence underneath. Copper and aluminum both saw significant inventory draws. In China, copper inventories have dropped to multi-year lows for this time of year. The report interprets this as a signal of improving Chinese copper consumption, and the US is still pulling metal to COMEX ahead of the tariff decision.
EN_A: So is that a clear bullish signal for copper?
EN_B: It is a constructive signal, but the report does not give a straight buy call. Net long positions in copper actually decreased. So there is still a lot of debate. The report leaves that for a deeper dive in the full version.
EN_A: Good point. Let us touch on agriculture. The report mentions some interesting momentum shifts.
EN_B: Yes. Agricultural markets were mostly flat in value, but the momentum signals are telling a story. For example, CBOT wheat flipped to a buy signal on both short and long-term momentum. But soybeans flipped to a sell on the long-term signal. Gasoline momentum also broke above its positive threshold, which could mean the buying trend is exhausted.
EN_A: So what is the one key takeaway for investors from this report?
EN_B: The report does not give a simple conclusion. It is more like a detailed map showing the temperature of each market. The most important thing to watch is the crude oil market, with the temporary glut and the question of when China will buy. Gold looks range-bound, and copper is showing early signs of tightening. Those are the three themes to track.

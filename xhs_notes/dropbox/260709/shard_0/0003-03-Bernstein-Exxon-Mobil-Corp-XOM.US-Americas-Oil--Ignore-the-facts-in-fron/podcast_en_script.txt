EN_A: So crude oil is trading below 70 dollars a barrel, but gasoline and diesel prices are up more than 50 percent this year. How is that possible?
EN_B: That is exactly the question a recent global investment bank report tries to answer. They say this is the biggest disconnect they have ever seen between crude and its refined products.
EN_A: Disconnect as in the gap between input and output prices?
EN_B: Exactly. They use something called the crack spread. It measures how much profit a refiner makes by turning crude into gasoline and diesel. That spread is now near record highs.
EN_A: So what does that mean? Is crude oil actually undervalued right now?
EN_B: The report does a clever calculation. They back-solve from gasoline and diesel prices to figure out what crude should be worth. And the implied price is above 90 dollars a barrel.
EN_A: More than 90 dollars? That is 20 dollars higher than the current market price.
EN_B: Right. That is the core tension. The market says crude is worth 70 dollars, but the products say it should be worth 90. Something has to give.
EN_A: What is causing this? Is it strong demand or supply issues?
EN_B: The report points to a few things. One is the Russia-Ukraine war which disrupted supply chains and kept refining margins high. But the bigger factor might be government actions.
EN_A: You mean the release of strategic petroleum reserves?
EN_B: Exactly. The report argues that when governments release reserves, it distorts commercial inventory data. That sends a false signal to the market. Plus, the Trump administration's verbal intervention to talk down prices also played a role.
EN_A: So will this disconnect last? Does the report have a view?
EN_B: They think it can persist for a while. Historically, after supply shocks, refining margins can stay elevated for years. And the government's ability to push prices down is not permanent.
EN_A: So where is the investment opportunity?
EN_B: The report is bullish on integrated oil companies, especially ExxonMobil. They have a 182 dollar price target, which is 33 percent upside from current levels. Refining operations can capture excess profits from this price gap.
EN_A: What about pure-play exploration and production companies?
EN_B: They like those too, but prefer names with cost advantages, like Devon Energy and Diamondback Energy. They trade at cheaper valuations and offer more upside leverage.
EN_A: One last thing. Does the report talk about downside risks? What if oil falls further?
EN_B: Here is an interesting point. They argue that when governments eventually need to rebuild strategic reserves, that could create a floor around the mid-60s. So we might already be near the bottom.

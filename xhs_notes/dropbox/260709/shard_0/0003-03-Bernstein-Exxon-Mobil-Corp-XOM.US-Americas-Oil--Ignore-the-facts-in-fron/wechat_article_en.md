## The Historical Crack Spread Relationship Has Shifted, and the Divergence Is Now at a Record Level

For decades, the relationship between crude oil prices and refining margins followed predictable patterns. Prior to the shale revolution, crack spreads averaged approximately 20% of the oil price. In the post-shale era, that ratio increased to roughly one-third. This shift reflected the structural changes in global refining capacity, the rise of light tight oil, and the changing composition of the global crude slate.

What makes the current environment notable is not just the absolute level of crack spreads, but the magnitude of the deviation from the historical model. The back-solving formula—which derives implied oil price from gasoline and diesel prices using the established one-third ratio—has tracked actual oil prices with high accuracy since 2012. During the Russia-Ukraine situation, the crack-implied price exceeded actual prices by roughly 10%. Today, the deviation is more than double that, reaching a level that has never been observed in the data.

This is not a modeling artifact. The crack spread is grounded in physical reality. Refiners must purchase crude and products. When product prices rise faster than crude, refiners capture the spread. The fact that the spread has reached such extreme levels suggests that either product prices are unsustainably high, crude prices are unsustainably low, or both. The historical precedent from the Russia-Ukraine period indicates that elevated crack spreads can persist for years, not months.

## Government Intervention Has Influenced the Price Discovery Mechanism in Ways That May Not Last

The most underappreciated factor in the current oil market is the role of strategic petroleum reserve releases. Governments, particularly the United States, have drawn down strategic inventories at a scale and pace that has fundamentally altered the relationship between commercial inventories and price signals.

Commercial inventory levels—the data that traders and algorithms monitor—have been artificially suppressed by the fact that government reserves are being released into the market. This creates the illusion of adequate supply when, in reality, the underlying supply-demand balance may be much tighter than the price suggests. The market is pricing crude based on a distorted view of available inventories, ignoring the fact that strategic reserves are a finite buffer, not a permanent source of supply.

The verbal intervention by certain administrations adds another layer of complexity. Public statements designed to influence oil prices have a real, if temporary, effect on market psychology. But this is not a sustainable mechanism for price suppression. Eventually, the gap between political messaging and physical market realities becomes too large to ignore. When that happens, the correction can be swift and significant.

The most important implication for observers is the eventual reversal of these policies. Governments will need to rebuild strategic reserves. The scale of this rebuilding could measure in hundreds of thousands of barrels per year—a significant percentage of expected demand growth—and could last for years. This creates a de facto support level under oil prices in the $60s per barrel range, precisely where prices are trading today.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Durability of Elevated Crack Spreads and the Role of Demand Changes

While the analysis convincingly demonstrates the existence and magnitude of the crack spread dislocation, it leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is the durability of elevated crack spreads. The report notes that crack spreads can remain elevated for years after supply shocks, but the current environment is different from the Russia-Ukraine period in one crucial respect: the shock is not primarily to supply, but to the relationship between crude and products.

The question of demand changes is equally unresolved. The report argues that a crack-implied price of $90 per barrel should not be sufficient to reduce demand, but this conclusion depends on assumptions about consumer behavior, economic growth, and the elasticity of gasoline and diesel demand at current price levels. If the global economy slows more sharply than expected, demand changes could occur at lower prices than the model suggests.

The role of non-OECD refining capacity is another area where the analysis is necessarily incomplete. The shift in global refining capacity toward Asia and the Middle East has changed the dynamics of crack spreads in ways that are not fully captured by historical models. The assumption that the one-third ratio will hold going forward may need to be revisited if the global refining map continues to shift.

Finally, the political dimension remains uncertain. The ability of governments to maintain strategic reserve releases and verbal intervention is not unlimited. But the timing and triggers for policy reversal are inherently unpredictable. Observers must weigh the compelling structural argument against the reality that markets can remain in a state of divergence longer than expected.

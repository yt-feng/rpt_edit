Flows through the Strait itself have fallen to 8.3 million barrels per day, about 42% of normal.

The report's analysis of transportation capacity is particularly instructive here. There is no shortage of tankers. The bottleneck is not logistics. It is geopolitical risk. Shippers are hesitating to cross under an unclear ceasefire status, and the recent attacks have reinforced the perception that the Strait remains a high-risk chokepoint.

This leads to a critical analytical conclusion: the speed and magnitude of any future flow recovery will depend almost entirely on Iran's willingness to allow safe passage, not on the availability of vessels. The report's base case assumes that if the 60-day negotiation window holds and the U.S. reinstates the sanctions waiver with security reassurances, flows could normalize by the end of July. That would require a 6.6 million barrel per day increase from current levels. It is a plausible scenario, but it is not a guaranteed one. The downside risk is equally real: if negotiations fail and attacks escalate, Persian Gulf flows could decrease further.

The open question that the report does not fully resolve is what happens if the negotiation process drags beyond the 60-day window without a clear resolution. Markets are pricing in a binary outcome. ## The Russia Refinery Crisis Is a Structural Shock to Product Markets That Crude Supply Cannot Offset

While the market's attention is fixed on the Strait of Hormuz, a more insidious supply disruption is unfolding in Russia. The acceleration of drone attacks on Russian refineries, oil tankers, and midstream infrastructure has taken a staggering toll. Refinery outages have reached 3.8 million barrels per day, which implies that over half of Russia's total refining capacity is currently offline. This is not a seasonal maintenance issue. It is a deliberate campaign of infrastructure destruction.

The consequences are already visible in the data. Russia's refinery runs are down 2.0 million barrels per day year-over-year. Domestic retail prices for gasoline and diesel have risen by over 10% in two months, reaching all-time highs. Fuel shortages are being reported in nearly every region of the country. The Russian government has responded with a diesel export ban, but that is a stopgap measure, not a solution.

The most important structural shift, however, is in Russia's export composition. As its effective capacity to refine crude into products has collapsed, Russia is redirecting more crude for export. Crude exports are up 1.1 million barrels per day from a year ago, while refined product exports have fallen by 0.9 million barrels per day, led by a 0.6 million barrel per day decline in diesel. This is a direct transfer of value from product markets to crude markets. The world is losing a major source of diesel and gasoline supply, and the gap is being filled by additional crude that must now be processed elsewhere.

The implications for refinery margins are profound. European diesel margins versus Brent have climbed to $60 per barrel, compared to $25 per barrel a year ago. U.S. diesel margins have risen to $75 per barrel. These are not cyclical peaks. They reflect a structural reduction in global refining capacity that will not reverse quickly, even if Russian refineries could be repaired, because the attacks are ongoing and the infrastructure damage is cumulative.

The strategic question for observers is whether this divergence will eventually pull crude prices higher as well. If product shortages persist and refinery margins remain elevated, the incentive for global refineries to maximize runs will increase, which should support crude demand. But the transmission mechanism is not automatic. Weak Asian demand, particularly from China, could keep crude markets relatively well-supplied even as product markets tighten. The report's data suggests this tension is likely to persist for at least the next several months.

## The Report Leaves an Unanswered Question About the Interaction Between the Two Crises

One of the most valuable contributions of the report is that it presents two distinct supply crises in parallel: the Strait of Hormuz disruption and the Russia refinery destruction. But it does not fully explore how these two shocks interact. This is not a criticism. The report is focused on tracking high-frequency data, and its authors are appropriately cautious about extending their analysis into speculative territory. But for strategic decision-making, this interaction is the most important unknown.

Consider the following scenario. The Strait of Hormuz negotiations succeed, flows recover to near-normal levels by the end of July, and crude prices ease. At the same time, Russia's refining outages persist or worsen, keeping product markets tight. What happens to global refinery economics? The additional crude flowing out of the Persian Gulf will need to be processed somewhere. But if global refining capacity is already stretched, and if Russian product exports remain depressed, the incremental crude may simply flow into storage rather than being converted into products. The result would be a continued divergence between weak crude prices and strong product margins.

Alternatively, consider the downside scenario. The Strait negotiations fail, Hormuz flows remain depressed, and crude prices spike. In that case, the product market tightness would be exacerbated by higher crude input costs, creating an even more extreme margin environment. Refineries that can secure crude would earn extraordinary profits. Those that cannot would face shutdowns.

The report's data supports both scenarios, but it does not tell us which one is more likely. That is the analytical gap that readers need to fill for themselves, and it is the reason why simply tracking the headline crude price is insufficient for understanding the current market.

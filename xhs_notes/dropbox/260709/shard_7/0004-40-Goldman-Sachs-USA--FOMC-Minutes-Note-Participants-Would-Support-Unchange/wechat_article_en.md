For observers, this creates a regime of conditional certainty: the Fed will act decisively once the data resolves the ambiguity, but until then, the path is wide open.

The base case may be no change in 2026, but the asymmetry of the risks is clear: the Fed has a defined playbook for hiking if inflation persists, and no equivalent urgency to cut if inflation fades.

## The Minutes Reveal a Committee That Has Formalized Two Competing Macroeconomic Scenarios, Each with a Clear Policy Implication

The most important structural feature of these minutes is the way the Committee organized its discussion around scenarios rather than a single modal forecast. "Most" participants discussed a scenario in which inflationary pressures dissipated and inflation "soon" began to return to 2 percent. Almost all of those participants said it would be appropriate to maintain or eventually lower the fed funds rate in that case. Separately, "most" participants discussed a scenario in which inflation remained elevated due to AI-related demand, the Middle East conflict, or tariffs. Almost all of those participants said some policy firming would be warranted.

This is not a debate between hawks and doves. It is a framework in which the same participants could endorse either outcome depending on the data. The Committee has effectively written two policy reaction functions and is waiting to see which one the economy chooses. For observers, this means that the standard approach of forecasting a single rate path and adjusting probabilities around it is insufficient. The correct analytical approach is to model two distinct regimes: a disinflation regime in which the Fed cuts, and a persistent-inflation regime in which the Fed hikes. Portfolio construction must be robust to both outcomes, not optimized for a single base case.

The report's own base case of no change in 2026 is an attempt to split the difference, but it is a fragile equilibrium. If the data tilts even modestly toward the persistent-inflation scenario, the Committee's pre-committed response is a hike. If the data tilts toward the disinflation scenario, the response is a cut. The hold outcome requires that the data remain ambiguous enough to prevent either scenario from gaining a decisive majority. That is a narrow path, and the minutes suggest the Committee knows it.

## Inflation Is Not Just Sticky; It Is Becoming More Broad-Based, and the Sources Are Structural, Not Cyclical

The minutes describe an inflation problem that is evolving in a troubling direction. "Several" participants noted that price pressures had become more broad-based, partly reflecting higher transportation, airfares, petrochemicals, and agricultural input prices. "Several" also noted that services inflation excluding housing remained high. This is not the narrow, supply-chain-driven inflation of 2021-2022 that could be expected to reverse as bottlenecks cleared. This is a broadening of price pressures into sectors that are driven by structural factors: AI-related investment demand, tariffs, and geopolitical disruptions.

"Most" participants noted that above-potential growth resulting from strong AI-related investment demand could contribute to more persistent inflationary pressures. This is a critical admission. The AI investment boom is not a transitory demand shock. It is a multi-year capital spending cycle that adds to aggregate demand at a time when the economy is already at or above potential. If AI investment continues at its current pace, the Fed may face a situation in which the natural rate of interest rises, making the current policy rate less restrictive than it appears. In that environment, keeping rates unchanged is effectively easing, which would be inconsistent with returning inflation to target.

The methodological changes to core PCE announced by the BEA after the June meeting add another layer of complexity. The report's forecast of 3.0 percent core PCE by December 2026 partially reflects these methodological changes, meaning the actual underlying inflation pressure may be higher than the reported number suggests. Observers who rely solely on the published inflation data risk being misled by a statistical artifact. The true inflation trajectory may be closer to 3.5 percent than 3.0 percent, which would put the Fed firmly in the hiking scenario.

## The Labor Market Is Stable but Not Disinflationary, and the Growth Story Is Concentrated in a Narrow Set of Sectors

Participants judged that the labor market remained stable, and "many" noted that it was "not currently a source of inflationary pressures." That sounds reassuring, but it is a statement about the present, not the future. A labor market that is not currently adding to inflation is not the same as a labor market that is helping to reduce it. The Fed needs the labor market to soften further to bring services inflation down, and the minutes offer no evidence that such softening is underway.

The growth story is equally nuanced. Economic activity was growing at a "solid" pace, supported by strong business investment concentrated in AI-related spending. "Most" participants noted that higher equity prices and tax refunds earlier this year had supported consumer spending, especially among higher-income households. This is a growth pattern that is narrow and potentially fragile. AI investment can sustain itself only as long as the expected returns on AI capital remain high. If those expectations shift, the investment cycle could reverse quickly, taking growth with it. Consumer spending supported by equity gains is similarly vulnerable to a market correction.

The combination of narrow growth and broad-based inflation creates a difficult policy problem. If the Fed hikes to combat inflation, it risks affecting the very sectors that are driving growth. If it holds steady or cuts, it risks allowing inflation to become entrenched. The minutes suggest the Committee is aware of this trade-off but has not resolved it. The staff's expectation that the unemployment rate will edge slightly below its longer-run rate in 2028 implies that the Committee sees a prolonged period of above-potential growth as the most likely outcome, which only reinforces the case for eventual tightening.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Threshold for Action and the Role of Financial Conditions

The report provides an excellent framework for understanding the FOMC's two scenarios, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. First, what is the precise threshold for action in either direction? The minutes say "almost all" participants would support a hike if inflation remained elevated, but they do not specify how elevated or for how long. Does "elevated" mean core PCE above 3.5 percent for three months, or above 4 percent for one month? The difference matters enormously for timing.

Second, the report does not address the role of financial conditions in the Fed's reaction function. If equity markets decline sharply or credit spreads widen, the Fed may feel compelled to ease even if inflation remains above target. The minutes mention that higher equity prices supported consumer spending, but they do not discuss the reverse scenario. A tightening of financial conditions could do the Fed's work for it, reducing the need for rate hikes even if inflation is sticky. Conversely, if financial conditions remain loose, the Fed may need to hike to tighten them deliberately.

Third, the report does not fully explore the implications of the AI investment cycle for the neutral rate of interest. If AI-driven productivity gains eventually boost potential growth, the neutral rate may rise, making current policy less restrictive. But if AI investment is largely demand-driven without corresponding productivity gains, it is purely inflationary. The Fed's own models may not yet have incorporated this distinction, leaving a significant source of uncertainty in the rate path.

Second, build a barbell portfolio that performs well in either scenario. In the disinflation scenario, long-duration Treasuries and growth equities would benefit from lower rates. In the persistent-inflation scenario, commodities, inflation-linked bonds, and value equities would perform well. A barbell that combines these exposures reduces the risk of being caught on the wrong side of the regime shift.

Third, monitor the Fed's communication for signals that the Committee is converging on one scenario. The minutes reveal that the Committee is already discussing both paths. The next step is for a majority to express conviction in one path over the other. When that happens, the market will reprice quickly. The time to position is before the consensus shifts, not after.

Fourth, be skeptical of the base case of no change in 2026. The report's own forecast is a statistical compromise, not a strategic conviction. The Fed's reaction function is asymmetric: the threshold for a hike is lower than the threshold for a cut because inflation remains well above target and the labor market is stable. A hold rate path is the most fragile of the three outcomes because it requires the data to remain ambiguous indefinitely. History suggests that ambiguity resolves more quickly than markets expect.

We think that this reflects increased flexibility and adaptability on the supply-side of the economy, perhaps driven by technological innovations that allow broad access to information and real-time communication.

## Renewed Uncertainties—But Continued Resilience

Tensions between the United States and Iran continue to cast a long shadow over the global oil market. Only few weeks ago, oil prices were approaching \$70/barrel and shipments through the Strait of Hormuz looked to be normalizing (Figure 1). More recently, however, the conflict has resurged, and oil prices have jumped back up. This week, Brent prices have moved above \$90/barrel, and market participants are on heightened alert as they await further news from the region.

Clearly, a range of scenarios for oil prices remain in play. If the United States and Iran resume negotiations—and shipments through the Strait resume—we could see oil prices fall back to \$70/barrel. However, if the tensions return in a more sustained way, oil prices stubbornly above \$100/barrel are also possible. With the November midterms fast approaching, our view is that President Trump prefers a path of de-escalation, at least to the extent that Iran is willing to negotiate and follow through on its agreements. Accordingly, our baseline forecast calls for lower oil prices. But for now, this key source of uncertainty persists.

The good news is that the global economy has shown continued resilience. Our forecast for growth this year is running near 2½%, which is down only a few tenths from when the conflict erupted (Figure 2). In recent years, the global economy has shown a remarkable capacity to shake off shocks and continue to record solid growth. We think that this has reflected increased flexibility and adaptability on the supply-side of the economy, driven by technological innovations that facilitate access to information and real-time communication.

This theme is reinforced by the recent performance of the global PMIs (Figure 3). The global services PMI retreated as the tensions erupted, but it has since moved back up and is running comfortably above 50, the breakpoint between expansion and contraction. The manufacturing PMI has been on an upward climb since late last year. This reflects burgeoning AI spending following the release of powerful agentic tools in late-2025. Consistent with this observation, the global electronics PMI has surged upward in recent months (Figure 4). The manufacturing PMI has also reflected more temporary factors, including a surge of inventory demand in an effort to front run disruptions from the conflict.

Despite the higher oil prices, the global economy has been broadly supported by rising AI spending. By our reckoning, US AI investment—where we have the cleanest data—has surged from just under \$300 billion last year (1% of GDP) to \$430 billion in Q1. For 2026 as a whole, industry estimates put likely spending at \$600 billion (nearly 2% of GDP). $^{1}$

In our view, this AI spending has considerable momentum, and it looks robust and likely to continue. However, given the pace of its ascent and the lofty valuations that have emerged, a slackening in the pace of increase—or even a temporary reversal—are plausible downside possibilities that remain on our radar screen. Despite the risks of near-term volatility, we’re convinced that AI is a robust technology that will significantly raise levels of productivity growth over the longer term. $^{2}$

These themes are also echoed at the country level. Figure 5 displays revisions to our growth forecasts since the onset of the US-Iran conflict. Notably, three tech intensive economies—Taiwan, Korea, and Singapore—have seen significant mark-ups in recent months, as soaring AI demand has supported growth. Conversely, our projections have been lowered appreciably for oil importers that don’t produce AI, including the Philippines, Sweden, Chile, and the euro area. Our US forecast is down just a couple of tenths.

Figure 6 reproduces this exercise for our headline inflation forecasts. Our global projection is up $\frac{3}{4}$ ppt from February. This reflects both the direct effects of higher oil prices, as well as indirect pressures due to supply-chain disruptions, increased transportation costs, and lingering second-round effects. $^{3}$

At the country level, headline inflation forecasts for a range of economies including the US, euro area, Vietnam, and Brazil have been raised a full percentage point, while the mark-ups for Thailand and the Philippines are significantly larger. Of those countries posting smaller increases, several of them—including Indonesia, China, and India—have mechanisms in place to constrain the pass-through of higher oil prices to domestic inflation.

Clearly, there are both upside and downside risks to our inflation forecasts, depending on the trajectory of oil prices through year-end. If the US and Iran return to negotiations and oil shipments through the Strait resume, inflation could yet undershoot our projections. However, there are also risks of higher inflation in the event that oil prices return to over \$100/barrel.

In sum, the global economy has absorbed a significant shock with the higher oil prices that followed the Iran conflict and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. As recent developments highlight, this shock remains very much in play. While the resulting pressures and uncertainties have taken a bite out of global performance, the pain to date has been more manageable than we had expected.

In addition, only part of last year's sizable accumulation of petroleum inventories has been reversed, and global inventory levels are generally still ample (Figure 8).

The remainder of our essay draws on this discussion to examine the shifting terrain and associated policy challenges facing global central banks. Relative to our expectations before the conflict erupted, monetary policy looks to be on a less supportive trajectory, as central banks seek to get inflation back to their targets.

## Global Central Banks

As the year began, major global central banks were in the midst of an easing cycle, which was unwinding the rate hikes in the aftermath of the pandemic (Figure 9). Since the onset of the Iran conflict, however, central banks have faced increased inflationary pressures and, in many cases, slippage in efforts to achieve their targets. While this slippage was driven primarily by higher oil prices (Figure 10), many central banks were hesitant to reprise their post-pandemic strategy of “looking through” an evolving supply shock. Although this more muscular response risks “overlearning” the lessons of the previous episode, central bank communication and market pricing have shifted markedly in the direction of tighter policy.

Across our panel of 27 major central banks, the balance between cutters and hikers has moved toward less-supportive policy. For example, during June, five central banks lifted rates, including the ECB, the Bank of Japan, and the Bank of Indonesia. Accordingly, we now expect that twelve of these 27 central banks will be hiking this year, and another seven will be on hold (Figure 11). Of the eight projected cutters, six of them would be easing by 50 bp or less. Only Brazil and Hungary are projected to record more sizable cuts.

This story is distinctly different than before the Iran conflict. Across our panel, seventeen now have less supportive policy paths than in late February, and for nine others the policy path is unchanged (Figure 12). Projected policy rates at year-end are tighter by over 200 bp in Brazil and 100 bp or more in the Philippines, the UK, Indonesia, and South Africa. Only Hungary is slated to see easier policy; in this

case, currency appreciation following Orban's defeat in the presidential election has given the central bank increased scope to provide support.

A convincing (and sustained) resolution of the conflict in Iran could yet bring a markedly lower trajectory for oil prices. But at present, such resolution does not look imminent. While we expect that negotiations will resume, they are likely to continue to be fraught, uneven, and prolonged. Accordingly, pressures in the Strait and corresponding uncertainties for energy markets look poised to remain in play for some time to come.

We conclude this section with brief discussions of the differing policy situations facing the Federal Reserve, European Central Bank, and Bank of Japan. These snapshots provide useful insight into the broad challenges facing central banks around the world.

The Federal Reserve. Since being sworn in, Chair Kevin Warsh has eradicated concerns about a sharp dovish, politically induced shift in Federal Reserve policy. He has underscored his commitment to independence in the setting of Fed policy rates, and he has emphasized the need for the Fed to double down in the pursuit of its 2% inflation target. The Fed has overshot its target for five consecutive years—and is likely to miss it again this year (Figure 13). As such, Warsh has struck a somewhat hawkish tone regarding his policy preferences, although he has rigorously avoided anything that could be construed as “forward guidance.”

Markets are continuing to price in between one and two Fed hikes through the end of the year. In contrast, our US economists remain worried about the durability of the labor market, notwithstanding its stronger performance over the past six months, and envision the Fed cutting rates through the last two meetings of this year and the first meeting of next year.

Chairman Warsh is also thinking deeply about the Fed's underlying approach to monetary policy. He has commissioned five task forces to examine a range of key issues. Of these, we see the groups examining communication and the balance sheet as especially important, since they are open policy questions that the Fed must eventually resolve. Of course, these are also topics where FOMC participants are likely to have deeply held views, so the results of the task forces will hardly be determinative.

The other three groups—on inflation forecasting, productivity, and data protocols—will no doubt also yield relevant insights and influence the broader framework of Fed policy. However, they are complex issues that have implications far beyond the Fed’s specific policy remit.

The European Central Bank. In the face of the recent oil shock, the Governing Council has been focused on not repeating the policy errors of the post-pandemic period, when it waited too long to tighten. With this in mind, the ECB hiked rates at its June meeting, and we anticipate that another hike is forthcoming, either at the meeting later this week or (more likely) in September. While oil prices are down from their spring peaks, policymakers will still see sufficient second-round pressures in the pipeline, as well as persisting risks of higher oil prices, to justify another hike.

As highlighted in Figure 14, headline inflation has clearly moved above the ECB's $2\%$ target. Even so, the current situation is hardly comparable to the eruption in inflation a few years ago. Headline inflation rose to $3.2\%$ in May, but it subsequently retreated back to $2.8\%$ in June as oil prices moderated. In tandem, core inflation has hovered around $2^{1/2}\%$ . As such, we view the ECB's tightening as largely a preemptive strike to reinforce its credibility and ensure that inflation expectations remain well anchored. In this respect, the ECB has chosen a notably more hawkish policy path than the Fed, at least to date.

The Bank of Japan. The BoJ continues its trajectory of gradually normalizing policy. After decades of too-low inflation, Governor Ueda has been comfortable remaining structurally behind the curve. The BoJ's policy has lagged the pace of the economy's reflation to cautiously ensure that the shift in economic conditions becomes fully entrenched. Policy has been geared to avoiding impatience and inadvertently forestalling the achievement of sustained 2% inflation.

However, the intellectual case for the BoJ to hike more aggressively is strengthening. First, the policies of the Takaichi Administration are geared to achieving a “high pressure” economy. Second, conditions in Japan’s labor market have shifted appreciably. For example, the spring wage negotiations have yielded increases in excess of 5% for the past three years. Third, the yen has fallen to historically low levels, and pressures in the bond market appear to be amplifying (Figures 15 & 16). As such, there is an increasingly compelling case for a marked tightening of monetary policy.

At a minimum, Governor Ueda and his colleagues should carefully consider the rationale for tighter policy. A key question is how such a pivot by the BoJ would be greeted by the Takaichi Administration, as well as the extent to which pushback from the Administration would be a constraint on the BoJ. In any event, with the economy's ongoing expansion, the BoJ and the Takaichi Administration will likely need to address this issue in the not-too-distant future.

# North America

United States

Resilient activity, stable unemployment rate, and sticky core PCE inflation have kept the market pricing rate hikes by the Fed over the next twelve months. We continue to think the next Fed move is more likely to be a cut rather than a hike. We expect that core inflation annualizing closer to 2% and YoY core CPI dropping to 2.2% in summer data will allow Fed officials to cut rates if the labor market weakens further. Our base case includes rate cuts in October, December and January to a 2.75–3% policy rate. GDP rose 2.1% in Q1 as consumer spending growth slowed but AI-related investment remained strong. Q2 consumption growth is tracking somewhat stronger with overall GDP likely growing around 2%. We continue to expect solid growth in 2026, due partly to continued strength in AI-related business investment.

Core PCE inflation has been sticky above 3% YoY while core CPI has slowed meaningfully, an abnormal divergence as CPI typically runs stronger than PCE. There has not been much passthrough from energy prices to core inflation so far and the pullback in oil from the highs of the last few months should limit the upside. Core PCE inflation is sticky in part due to rising prices for AI-related goods like computer software and accessories that get a much larger weight than in CPI. Methodological changes to this category and a couple other categories in September should imply revisions lower to annual core PCE by 20-30bp.

Monthly payroll job growth has been very volatile month-to-month over the last year but has picked up on a 6m moving average. Job growth started to slow again in June and we expect the trend to continue in the near term. The unemployment rate has increased gradually over the last couple of years but has stayed within a narrow range around 4.2-4.4% in the last several months. We expect labor demand to remain softer than labor supply in coming months, which would put further upward pressure on the unemployment rate. The labor market continues to be defined by a low hiring rate but also low layoff rate and is not a source of inflationary pressure as wage growth has slowed.

New Fed Chair Warsh has continued to provide no forward guidance on policy rates. Other Fed officials have generally erred on the hawkish side focusing on PCE inflation. Warsh has announced several task forces that will work on various priorities like communication, balance sheet, data sources, inflation frameworks and productivity/jobs.

Quarterly GDP growth could remain volatile this year, impacted by substantial shifts in trade patterns. GDP contracted for two quarters in a row, with the output gap remaining negative. Consumption was surprisingly soft in Q1 and headwinds such as slower population growth and higher rates are further headwinds to consumption this year. Q2 GDP growth is tracking stronger but on average growth remains soft.

The unemployment rate has stayed within a range around 6.5-7.0% over the last year. Wage growth slowed sub-4% after some composition issues pushed it higher for a couple of months. We expect the unemployment rate to remain elevated in the second half of the year as labor demand remains soft despite slower population growth. The unemployment rate around 6.5-7% suggests the output gap remains negative.

Headline inflation slowed to 2.8% YoY in June and core measures were very soft sub-2%. Annual core inflation (the average of CPI-trim and CPI-median) has been close to or under 2% for several months in a row. Data like a jump in CFIB price plans keeps us cautious on some near-term risks of stickier core inflation in Q2, similar to last year. But also like last year, we expect softer demand to ultimately keep core inflation subdued and rate hikes very unlikely this year. Home prices have been falling on an annual basis and forward-looking indicators for home prices have generally been soft.

The Bank of Canada has kept policy rates unchanged in the last several meetings at 2.25%, the lower end of the neutral range. Despite markets pricing some probability of a hike, we expect the Bank of Canada will continue cutting policy rates this year to a terminal rate of 1.75% as activity, labor market and core inflation data remain soft. Rate hikes seem unlikely to us in an environment where home prices are falling and core inflation is at target.

## Euro Area

Still stag-flationary – Despite the oil price volatility, our inflation forecasts are roughly unchanged since the last GEOS, with headline inflation still expected to stay close to 3% YY until early 2027. This partly reflects the reversal of energy tax cuts introduced in the spring, which are keeping energy inflation elevated, and partly as we still expect food prices to accelerate later this year (potential effects of El Nino compounding higher transport costs and fertilisers shortages). We expect some but limited indirect effects on core prices from higher energy prices, mainly in core goods, with core HICP edging higher to 2.5% YY by year-end.

Resilient growth (ex- Ireland) – Volatility in Irish GDP keeps us revising our 2026 GDP forecast for the Eurozone higher this month (by 0.3pp to 0.6%). Still this is weaker growth than in 2025 (at 1.3%) and we attribute the slowdown mostly to the delayed effects of US tariffs on exports in 4Q25 and 1Q26, after a large front-loading earlier last year. Aside from Ireland, Eurozone growth has been remarkably steady at just above 1% annualised rate (and domestic demand around 1.5%) recently. 2Q 26 high-frequency data suggest a similar pace has been maintained, with some slowdown in services offset by a mild recovery in manufacturing. We expect growth to accelerate in 2027 at 1.4% on peak fiscal stimulus. European growth should benefit from ‘autonomy spending’ (defense, green, technological investment) and from the global tech investment cycle.

ECB: Inflatio Delenda Est – The ECB delivered a 25bp ‘hawkish’ hike in June and after the meeting we found little reason to invalidate the plausibility of a second hike this year. The recent volatility in oil prices and lower-than-expected June inflation may have the ECB skipping the July meeting, but the fact that inflation will likely stay close to 3% in 2H26 and that upside inflation risks have clearly intensified again (from AI, El Nino, energy disruptions, etc) we still think another 25bp rate hike remains a base case, with the most likely timing in September.

## Germany

After a first year of apparent paralysis, Germany's government embarked on a reform ‘blitz’ of sorts. A commitment to implement an expert commission's reform proposals in their entirety was followed by a (modest) income tax reform as well as plans to increase labour market flexibility and reduce red tape. A reform to the public health insurance system already agreed earlier this spring is now also set to become law, albeit after tweaks that somewhat blunt the initial draft's impact.

## France

France's domestic demand remains lacklustre compared to Euro Area peers and inflation is accordingly lower. The latter might be, on the face of it, a positive during times of an energy price shock, but the resulting higher real rates leave the economy comparatively more vulnerable to monetary policy tightening. Unemployment is already increasing and with fiscal policy too constraint to provide support, we think matters might deteriorate further in the short term.

## Italy

We reverse some of the recent stag-flationary adjustments to our forecasts, with 2026-27 growth revised up marginally, but inflation essentially unchanged since June (when was revised lower quite substantially). We still see Italian growth supported by NGEU spending in 2026 and in 2027, German fiscal stimulus and European defense investment. We expect the government fiscal conservatism to persist even after the next general election (likely in spring 2027) and demand for BTPs to remain solid, with potential for further credit rating upgrades ahead.

## United Kingdom

UK GDP has, once again, proven resilient. UK output growth surprised to the upside in Q1 at 0.6% QQ. The result has been a mechanical upgrade in our GDP forecast for 2026 to 1.1% - marginally higher than we saw it in February. We are, however, careful about reading too much into this. Q1 outperformance has become something over a trend over the last few years to the point where the seasonal adjustment process has questioned. We think this looks related to deferred consumption and investment from the previous year's Q4, where the budget process has generated an unusual degree of uncertainty in the economy over the last few years and shifted some output from Q4 to Q1. While we expect the ONS seasonal adjustment process to capture this eventually, for now it means that we also assume a weaker profile throughout the year with Q4 2026 growth at just 0.1% QQ to capture this trend.

Questions remain over the path for inflation. The challenges around the inflation forecast remain as they were at the time of the last GEOS: Hormuz; fiscal intervention on energy bills; degree of pass-through into second-round effects. Each of these remains an unknown and, in their absence, we’ve left the forecast largely unchanged. That is to say we continue to expect a surge in non-core inflation with only limited second-round effects in services. We continue to watch two themes as we monitor the forecast. The first is whether or not the government opts to intervene on energy bills. Doing so would mechanically lower Q3 inflation but, absent the demand destruction that higher prices would otherwise bring, risks a strong profile through 2027. The second is Hormuz, where the absence of a resolution presents predominantly upside risks as we approach summer.

Fiscal policy presents a familiar challenge. The Treasury’s trilemma of building spending pressures, the fiscal rules, and a manifesto which inhibits capacity for large tax increases is back in focus. This is all the more salient as the headroom against the fiscal rules continues to be eroded. While the bulk of the pressure has come from the combination of higher borrowing costs and the reversal of a planned fuel duty increase (see: European Economics - Is the energy price shock derailing the fiscal impulse?), further pressures are growing. The Prime Minister has recently announced a further £18bn in defence expenditure which – while absent details such as whether this spending lands in CDEL or RDEL, and what time frame it takes place over – will add to these pressures. The Q3 and Q4 energy price caps will also be important to watch. While we think the Q3 one will pass without intervention (in part because of the fuel duty changes), the government may come under pressure to spend to shield households as we approach winter.

Monetary policy still on hold. We continue to maintain that Bank Rate will be on hold at 3.75% for the remainder of 2026. Our view is a doveish stance compared to the current market curve, however we feel it is justified as many on the MPC have signalled in their recent communications that a hold would be seen as restrictive in light of their expectations of the recent weakness of where underlying growth is headed.

## Japan

The BoJ Tankan released earlier this month showed business sentiment coming in better than expected, with capital expenditure plans and other components also pointing to resilient results. Meanwhile, consumer prices have continued to track below 2% over the past few months, supported by a deceleration in food inflation and government measures to counter high energy prices. However, as the impact of higher oil prices due to the Middle East situation begins to feed through more fully into CPI — typically with a lag of around six months — inflation is expected to rise above 2% again from the summer onward, potentially approaching 3% temporarily toward year-end and into early next year. That said, the more pressing concern in Japan's macro landscape at present is the market reaction to the government's fiscal stance, ahead of the imminent release of the "Honebuto" Basic Policy on Economic and Fiscal Management and Reform for the next year's budget. Concerns over the expansionary fiscal stance of the Takaichi administration, which champions "responsible, active fiscal policy," have continued to weigh on both the Japanese currency and bond market, with selling pressure persisting into July.

For the BoJ's monetary policy as well, market movements in the yen and JGB yields are currently likely to have a greater bearing on policy decisions than near-term economic data releases. We continue to expect two further rate hikes at a pace of once every six months — in December of this year and June of next year — bringing the terminal rate for the current hiking cycle to 1.5%. However, should concerns over yen weakness and the risk of falling behind the curve intensify excessively, the possibility of a faster pace of rate hikes or a higher terminal rate than currently assumed cannot be ruled out. Key risk factors include a further deterioration in fiscal concerns, increasingly aggressive corporate price pass-throughs, and a more hawkish Fed that could trigger further yen weakness. As the BoJ raised rates at last month's meeting, a hold at this month's meeting is widely expected. Nonetheless, attention will be focused on whether any board members advocate for a rate hike and on the updated economic and price outlook in the Outlook Report.

## Australia

The back-and-forth in the Middle East adds to near-term uncertainty to the outlook, namely the inflation outlook, where risks are skewed to the upside. Next week's inflation print for Q2 will be the key data point to determine if the RBA hikes again or not. ## New Zealand

The RBNZ commenced its hiking cycle in July by raising the OCR by 25bps, as widely expected. Solid Q1 GDP and upward revisions to three of the previous four quarters have eliminated the economy's negative output gap. The Bank noted that growth in Q2 had likely improved further. Upward revisions suggest the economy has been more responsive to previous accommodative monetary policy and that expectations for a higher unemployment rate may need to be revised downwards by the RBNZ at the August MPS. Q2 CPI was also broadly in-line with the Bank's view. For example, residential construction costs increased because of higher material prices. With the RBNZ positioned for a neutral rate between $3.00\%$ and $3.50\%$ , the MPC needs to unwind excess accommodation. We expect the MPC to conduct another 25bp hike on Sep 2, lifting the OCR to $2.75\%$ .

## China

GDP growth slowed more than expected in 26Q2. The quarterly rate fell to 4.3%YoY, the lowest reading since 2023. Sequential change as reported by the NBS dropped to 0.9%QoQ SA, also a four-year low. The quarterly miss in 26Q2 serves as a reminder that headline resilience is not assured amid the K-shaped economy. [1] The AI-led new economy continues to run strong. The NBS noted >40% growth contribution from the new economy. Exports beat expectations at 27.0%YoY in June, with IC exports more than doubling at 121.9%YoY. Domestic industrial production also rebounded to 5.3%YoY, and high-tech IP stayed solid at 14.1%YoY. [2] There is no sign of pickup in the old economy. Acceleration of trade-in subsidies and base effect drove retail sales back to growth in June at 1.0%YoY, still a low rate, while the household saving rate stayed elevated at 40.0% in 26H1. The investment slump showed no sign of easing with FAI growth staying low at -10.0%YoY, per our estimate. The green shoots in the property sector seem to have sustained with sales volume and prices holding up in top-tier cities, but this trend is not broadening to other regions yet.

We expect incremental policies ahead following the data miss. The upcoming Politburo meeting could provide more policy signals. We continue to expect a 10bp rate cut from the PBoC and an acceleration in fiscal implementation in our base case. We pencil in the 10bps cut in 26Q3E with the PBoC holding LPRs unchanged in July. We also expect the de facto fiscal austerity to reverse in 26H2 as deployment accelerates, and the rebound of nominal growth to 6.0%YoY in 26Q2 could give the MoF some wiggle room. Targeted measures such as the “Six Networks” initiative to enable AI transition and stabilize investment are likely to be a key focus.

We have trimmed our full-year forecast to 4.6%YoY from 4.7%YoY previously. The miss in 26Q2 is one driver. Potential extreme weather events like typhoons, heavy rains or floods could cause temporary supply disruptions, limiting the pace of the rebound ahead: cargo volume dropped -11.3%YoY in the first three weeks of July amid Typhoon Bavi and other weather events. We expect GDP growth to rebound to 4.5%YoY in 26Q3E and 4.6%YoY in 26Q4E.

## India

Still no alarm on core inflation: Headline inflation rose to a 1.5-year high of 4.38%YY in Jun-26, up 45bps from May-26. This was driven by a sharp jump in food and energy inflation, each contributing \~20-25bps, while core inflation remained unchanged at 3.9%YY. We retain our average FY27 forecasts at 4.7%YY for headline inflation and 4.5%YY for Core CPI.

Monetary policy: August RBI MPC might lower their FY27 average CPI forecast (5.1%YY) by around 20bps, reducing the need for any immediate rate hike action. There is a modest increase in core inflation but from extremely low levels and they remain within RBI's comfort zone. RBI is likely to watch out for the persistence of any second-round effects of the energy shock on the core inflation and in our view, the rate hike possibilities will arise only when core inflation sustains above 4.5%. We expect that this condition is unlikely to be met soon and hence do not foresee a rate hike in 2026. However, there are risks to keep an eye on. First, Impact of renewed hostilities in the Middle East is putting pressure on energy/petchem prices again. Second, disruption to food prices (particularly vegetables/pulses/oilseeds) from potential El Nino shock could become visible with a lag. Despite some improvement in early July, rainfall is still 24% below normal levels. Crop sowing is still down 6%YY as mid-July, with \~54% of total area sown. In an adverse scenario, these supply side shocks can potentially combine with demand side (high retail credit growth), warranting RBI rate action.

BoP and INR: We previously upgraded our FY27 BoP view to a \$45bn surplus from a peak-war deficit of \$50bn, assuming a \$31.5bn saving from a lower net oil import bill and an additional \$55bn inflow from various RBI concessional swap measures. Regarding the former, Brent prices are again hovering around \$90/bbl; every \$10/bbl increase puts at least \$15bn of upward pressure on the annual current account deficit. Regarding the latter, before RBI released the data, market expectations for total inflows were tempering amid reports of taxation-related issues that could reduce the appeal of these products in Western countries (ET, 11-Jun-26). However, RBI has now reported that \$20.7bn was received under these schemes as of 17 July, with \$17.4bn via FCNR(B) deposits. Yet, this inflow is not reflected in FX reserves or banking system liquidity data, raising doubts about offsetting flows (either BoP or FX forwards) or some delay in swap conversion. While initial capital inflow data under these schemes appears upbeat, we maintain our CAD forecast of 0.9% of GDP and a BoP surplus of \$45bn, given uncertainty in the Middle East. Higher oil prices, tempered expectations for RBI scheme-related inflows (at least in 1H-July), general USD strength, and a wider-than-expected June trade deficit have eroded initial INR strength. Brent must return to the \$70s/bbl range and RBI-related inflows must continue to gain momentum the next two months for our revised short-term USDINR view of 94.0 to play out. This will also depend on RBI's reaction function to these flows in terms of reserve accretion versus currency appreciation.

Inflation persistence, reinforced by services, wages, and rising climate-related risks, has pushed Banrep further into a tightening cycle and delayed any discussion of policy easing. The incoming administration has improved market sentiment through a more orthodox policy message and technocratic appointments, but fiscal consolidation remains the dominant challenge. The August budget submission is emerging as the first major test of policy credibility, particularly given the gap between official fiscal projections and independent assessments. Into 2H26, the balance of risks depends increasingly on whether fiscal adjustment, energy-sector stabilization, and inflation control can advance simultaneously, as success on those fronts will determine whether recent gains in confidence can be sustained.

## Euro rates and EGB spreads

Bunds are beginning H2 in a bearish mood with the front-end under pressure from resurgent energy prices amid renewed tensions in the Middle East. While we already thought a second ECB hike was likely this quarter, we still don't think this is the beginning of a fuller tightening cycle. There is a low bar to hike once more, in our view, to complete a ‘measured adjustment’ and with 2.5% depo still seen as neutral. However, we expect greater resistance from within the GC to hike beyond that (unless there is firm evidence that wages are responding) given the risk of demand destruction and with energy effects on inflation set to dissipate with base effects. With that in mind, we believe that front-end pricing is towards the high-

end of the likely range. Similarly, for Bunds, we still think this is a range-trading environment and we prefer to buy the dips above 3.10% with 3% still our target level for 2H26. In essence we are shifting from a mild bearish bias last month to a mild bullish bias this month, with the forecasts unchanged. This is despite the recent uplift to Germany’s multi-year borrowing plans. The market noted the revision via modest bear-steepening (the first of any note since April), but we do not expect this to create a lasting trend towards cheaper valuations. The increase in net supply for Bunds is likely well absorbed, in our view, given Germany’s still strong debt metrics, the shortage of AAA assets and given the extra spending could prove growth enhancing. Turning to EMU spreads, the latest increase in ECB hike premium has contributed to renewed widening. If we’re right that the ECB stops at 2.5% depo, then we think there is scope for some corrective tightening in the coming months. However, we still think the effective floor for spreads is somewhat higher than pre-conflict with an extra headwind from EGB supply in H2 which is set to run a little higher than usual (given the deferral of issuance in the volatility of 2Q26). Further ahead, we expect the market to really begin to focus on the French presidential election from about October with OATs likely to lead the pricing of additional risk premium into the political event risk.

## UK

10yr gilt yields have tracked higher to around 5% at the time of writing, which remains our target level for year-end. The neutral view reflects the competing forces of bearish risk from any change in fiscal approach under the new Prime Minister vs bullish risk from the likelihood that the MPC holds rates steady for 2H26 and disappoints market pricing for hikes. On the politics and fiscal, greater clarity on the extent of the ambition for a policy shift will likely emerge by the 27-30 September Labour Party Conference but the full picture may not be clear until the autumn Budget (for which the OBR requires a minimum of 10 weeks' notice). Additional borrowing seems likely (with the investment rule providing a mechanism to borrow more without breaking the self-imposed fiscal rules). However, there could be relief for gilts if the DMO opts to meet any extra funding need via Treasury Bills as it may seek to expand that market following the consultation earlier this year. There is little doubt, however, that gilt borrowing is a key bearish risk for H2 and could cause an overshoot in yields. Otherwise, the outlook would lean bullish. For sure, rate cuts are off the agenda for now, but we don't expect the BoE to hike. While the message is somewhat confused by the focus on the divides among individual member views, the best collective judgement appears to be that the market has delivered sufficient tightening in response to the oil price shock by taking out the cuts in the pre-conflict baseline. Overall, our forecast speaks to a neutral view on UK duration, albeit with the risk of twist-steepening, and with gilts potentially underperforming Bunds in the second half.

## Commodities

\- Our near-term conviction in the oil and metals price outlook is relatively low, reflecting the fact that the outlook is inherently tied to the actions of both President Trump and the Iranian regime. Having said this, our baseline view is that energy rallies be sold over the summer and metals weakness to be bought.

Indeed, a deal, either US/Iran or ME regional countries / Iran, which sees flows return through the SoH, would return the oil market to its pre-war surplus rapidly, if and when it occurs. This would also see lower inflation pressures, and likely lower real interest rates, as well as stronger cyclical global growth expectations and metals prices (hence buy the dip in gold/copper/aluminium for a Sep-Dec '26 rally).

We interpret recent events as President Trump using military pressure to try to re-negotiate Point 5 of the US-Iran MoU, which was the point that focused on commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz (SoH). The President wants to use the Oman side of the Strait as it sees fit, and Iran doesn't want that (as they likely want to build tracking/monitoring infrastructure for future fees).

We continue to expect the same endgame as before: a diplomatic resolution and normalisation of shipping flows, possibly as soon as this month. In our view, the key question is the timing of “MoU 2,” with a return to diplomacy over the coming weeks our base case.

The expected (eventual) return to diplomacy base case reflects the fact that President Trump has historically shown a preference for strong equity markets, stable bond markets and lower energy-price volatility, meaning US equity and bond market downside provides a limit for high oil prices. For Iran, a return to negotiations would help avoid further economic disruption, infrastructure damage and domestic pressure, while the SNSC had already approved the original MoU terms.

The recent military escalation clearly raises the probability that Iran could suspend engagement with the MoU for weeks or months, potentially until after the US mid-term elections. While not our central scenario, this would likely support higher-for-longer oil prices and could trigger additional strategic stock releases from IEA/OECD countries. For reference, our base case remains that the ongoing escalation falls short of mutual energy and public infrastructure destruction, though this is a bullish risk for oil, it is a tail risk in our view.

## Global Foreign Exchange Outlook

We leave our near-term EURUSD forecast unchanged at 1.13 to reflect USD-positive asymmetry around the Fed's reaction function and US-Iran conflict. G10 relative value expressions look increasingly attractive, though, with G9 USD correlations heading lower. In this context, we forecast upside in EURGBP and NOKSEK, and downside in AUDNZD over coming months.

US midterms are coming into view, as well, where expectations are for a divided government. The FX impact here is less clear. On the one hand, a divided government could drive slightly lower term premium, which could support foreign inflows into US assets. On the other hand, the US fiscal impulse could also fall, and we could see more US-centric volatility around budget discussions and similar areas.

Elsewhere, we took profit on our short EURBRL trade to hedge against seasonal volatility into August. But we remain short AUDBRL as a carry play that helps neutralize macro risk (spot ref: 3.5378, 2:06 PM ET, 7/22).

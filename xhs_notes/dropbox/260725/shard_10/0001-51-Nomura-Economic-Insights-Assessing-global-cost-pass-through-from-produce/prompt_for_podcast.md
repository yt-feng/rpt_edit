## Assessing global cost pass-through from producer prices to consumer prices

With crude oil prices rising again and El Niño conditions now official (see Special Report - Gauging who is most exposed if food prices surge, 15 May 2026), there is a sharp focus on the inflation outlook. For 27 major DM and EM economies, we examine the trajectories of producer price index (PPI) and CPI inflation since the outbreak of the US-Iran war. We then compare them to the PPI and CPI trajectories during the energy shock after the outbreak of the 2022 Russia-Ukraine war. Our main findings are:

\- PPI-CPI pass-through appears more limited and heterogeneous in 2026 than in 2022; the relationship between cumulative PPI and CPI rises is weaker in 2026 than 2022.

\- Initial conditions of healthier margins, weaker consumer demand and less fiscal and monetary support help explain the less aggressive cost pass-through this year.

PPI inflation measures upstream prices in the supply chain and is generally a leading indicator of CPI inflation (Figure 1). Aggregate CPI inflation for the 27 countries (using purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusted shares of world GDP, which cover about $75\%$ of world GDP on a PPP basis) peaked around five months after PPI inflation peaked in 2022. This year, although aggregate PPI inflation moderated slightly in June (based on only partial data), it may have yet to peak, given the renewed oil price spike and El Niño conditions. Assuming the same five-month lag means CPI inflation is unlikely to peak until late this year at the earliest.

\- However, delayed pass-through remains a risk if the PPI shock persists, particularly in countries with a large negative CPI-PPI gap and a historically strong PPI-CPI inflation correlation.

## Now versus 2022

To dig deeper and compare the country-level impact of the energy price shocks in 2022 and 2026 on PPI and CPI inflation, we calculate the cumulative percentage changes in each country's PPI and CPI each month from the start of the two shocks. Both the Russia-Ukraine war in 2022 and US-Iran conflict in 2026 started in February, so we calculate the cumulative percentage changes from the average of January-February 2022 and 2026, respectively (January-February average is used to remove any distortions in Asian data from the moving Chinese New Year holidays).

Looking back at 2022, the cumulative change in PPI peaked within 10 months from the start of the war for many countries, while the cumulative change in CPI remained on an uptrend (see Picture books 1, 2 and 3 in Appendix). But will those increases in producer prices eventually be passed on to consumer prices, and could the limited transmission so far actually signal greater scope for future pass-through now? One camp can argue that firms may have developed stronger muscle memory from the last inflation surge in 2021-22 to swiftly pass on higher costs this time, especially after the failed US-Iran peace truce and as energy prices are now rebounding. Nevertheless, we would argue that the backdrop of this oil price shock is different to the 2022 one, in that: 1) there is no pent-up demand; 2) fiscal space is more limited; and 3) monetary policy stances are nowhere near as loose.

In Figure 7, we compare real private consumption growth, gross public debt and policy rates in 2022 to those in 2026, and highlight in red the year when real private consumption growth is higher, and gross public debt and the policy rate are lower. We rank each country by the gap between 2022 and 2026 for each indicator. For real private consumption, comparing year-on-year growth in 2022 to 2026 may be tricky, given the base effects from pandemic lockdowns and reopening; thus, we compare the sequential growth instead in the table, but we present a time series chart of year-on-year real private consumption growth for each country in 2022 versus 2026 in Picture books 4, 5 and 6 in Appendix. Most countries experienced slower real private consumption growth in Q1 2026 than in Q1 2022, and face higher public debt and a higher policy rate. This is consistent with our hypothesis that tighter monetary policy and less fiscal space mean a less demand-supportive environment, whereby firms may have less pricing power this time around to pass on the cost increases to consumers, and thus they may be forced to compress their margins further. That said, with oil prices rebounding sharply, and if the energy price shock persists, it may be increasingly difficult for firms to continue absorbing these cost increases via margin squeezes.

The upshot is that, if the re-escalation of the US-Iran conflict continues and if the severity of El Niño deepens, leading to further increases in energy and food costs, the outcome could be more stagflationary (higher inflation but weaker growth via profit margin compression) than in 2022, leaving a tougher trade-off for central banks.

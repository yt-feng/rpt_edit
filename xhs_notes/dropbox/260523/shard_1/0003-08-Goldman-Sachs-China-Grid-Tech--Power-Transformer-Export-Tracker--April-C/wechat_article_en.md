# The US Power Transformer Deficit is Narrowing, But Not Closing — And That Creates a Lasting Window for Chinese Exporters

The narrative around US power transformer supply has been defined by one alarming statistic: a 72% demand-local supply gap. For years, this gap has driven a relentless surge in imports, particularly from China, as US utilities scrambled to secure equipment for grid modernization, renewable integration, and data center buildout. The conventional wisdom held that this deficit would persist indefinitely, creating a seemingly endless growth runway for non-traditional suppliers.

That conventional wisdom is now being tested. New data through April 2026 reveals a more nuanced picture. While US transformer exports from China still grew 27% year-on-year in April, this marks a significant deceleration from the 56% growth recorded just one month earlier. The headline numbers are shifting, and the underlying dynamics are even more telling. The question is not whether the US supply gap will close — but how quickly, and what the shape of that closure means for different players in the global grid technology ecosystem.

The core thesis of this analysis is straightforward: the US power transformer shortage is entering a structural transition, not a sudden collapse. The gap will narrow from 72% to an estimated 60% by 2028, driven by domestic capacity expansions. But a 60% gap is still a massive shortfall, and the nature of that remaining deficit will favor suppliers with specific capabilities — particularly those in China who can navigate pricing volatility, product mix shifts, and the rising importance of non-transformer equipment like switchgear. This is not a story of a window closing. It is a story of a window changing shape, and the investors who understand the new geometry will be the ones who capture the next phase of value.

## The Deceleration in Transformer Exports Masks a Rotational Shift in Demand Toward Switchgear

The most striking data point from the April export tracker is not the 27% year-on-year growth in transformer exports — it is the explosive acceleration of switchgear exports. After growing just 5% year-on-year in March, switchgear exports surged to 77% growth in April. This is not a statistical anomaly. It reflects a fundamental rotation in the US procurement cycle.

The logic is clear. US power transformer pricing has been elevated since the 2021-2022 spike, and the PPI for power and specialty transformers has stabilized at roughly double the 2020 level. For utilities and grid operators, the calculus has shifted. Transformer lead times remain extended, and prices have not retreated. In response, procurement teams are shifting capital toward switchgear and other balance-of-system equipment that can be deployed more quickly and at more predictable prices. The US PPI for switchgear and switchboard apparatus manufacturing hit 14% year-on-year growth in April, signaling that pricing power is now migrating from transformers to adjacent categories.

This rotational dynamic has profound implications for investors. The market has rightly focused on transformer supply tightness as the primary investment thesis. But the data now suggests that the most acute phase of transformer scarcity may be passing, even while the broader grid equipment shortage persists in different forms. Companies that are narrowly exposed to large power transformers will face a more challenging growth trajectory. Those with diversified product portfolios spanning switchgear, meters, and secondary equipment are better positioned to capture the next wave of procurement.

## US Domestic Capacity Expansion is Real, But It Will Not Eliminate the Import Requirement

The report estimates that the US power transformer demand-local supply gap will narrow from 72% to 60% by 2028. This is a meaningful improvement, but it still leaves nearly two-thirds of demand unmet by domestic production. The key question is whether the pace of capacity expansion can accelerate further, or whether structural constraints will keep the gap wider for longer.

Several factors argue for the latter. First, the US transformer manufacturing base has been hollowed out over decades, and rebuilding it requires not just capital but specialized labor, supply chain integration, and regulatory approvals for new facilities. The announced expansion plans are real, but they face execution risk. Second, the demand side of the equation is not static. The buildout of data centers, the electrification of industrial processes, and the grid hardening required for climate resilience are all accelerating. Even if domestic supply grows at an optimistic rate, demand is likely to grow alongside it.

For Chinese exporters, this creates a sustained opportunity — but one that requires strategic adaptation. The easy phase of export growth, driven by the sheer magnitude of the US deficit, is giving way to a more competitive phase where pricing, product mix, and relationship management become differentiating factors. The 220-330MVA segment, which saw April pricing decline 14% year-on-year, illustrates the point. While the three-month rolling average still shows 23% year-on-year growth, the monthly volatility suggests that buyers are becoming more price-sensitive and that competition is intensifying.

## Pricing Dynamics Reveal a Market in Transition, Not a Bubble About to Burst

The pricing data in the report offers a window into the market's internal dynamics that is more revealing than the top-line export numbers. US local pricing, as benchmarked to the PPI for power and specialty transformers, has stabilized at a level roughly double that of 2020. This is not a bubble. It is a structural repricing driven by sustained demand, constrained supply, and the long lead times required to bring new capacity online.

The contrast with Chinese export pricing is instructive. For the 220-330MVA segment, Chinese FOB pricing has been volatile, with the three-month rolling average up 23% year-on-year in February-April but with significant monthly swings. This volatility reflects several factors: changes in product mix, raw material cost fluctuations, and the strategic pricing decisions of Chinese manufacturers as they navigate different markets and customer segments.

The raw material picture adds another layer of complexity. Grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES), a critical input for transformer cores, has seen prices inch down from their 2023 peak. Copper, by contrast, experienced a sharp increase in the fourth quarter of 2025 before turning lower in March 2026. These divergent trends mean that Chinese exporters face an uneven cost environment, which will pressure margins for some products while benefiting others. The ability to manage this volatility through procurement strategy, hedging, and product mix will separate the winners from the also-rans.

## The Geographic Diversification of Chinese Exports Points to a Multi-Polar Growth Story

One of the most interesting findings in the data is the geographic dispersion of Chinese transformer exports in April. While the US remains a critical market, the growth was far from uniform. Exports to Africa surged 681% year-on-year, contributing 23% of total export value. North America (including the US) contributed 66% growth and 19% of the total. Asia grew 38% and accounted for 30% of exports. Europe managed only 9% growth, while the Middle East and Latin America saw contractions.

This geographic pattern tells a story of a market that is becoming more complex. The African surge is likely driven by large infrastructure projects and the continent's growing need for grid investment. The relative weakness in Europe may reflect a combination of slower economic growth, existing inventory, or a shift toward other suppliers. The key insight is that Chinese exporters are not dependent on any single market. The US is important, but it is not the only game in town.

For investors, this geographic diversification reduces the binary risk of a US policy change or trade dispute. Even if the US market becomes more restrictive — through tariffs, local content requirements, or other measures — Chinese exporters have alternative growth avenues. The companies that are best positioned are those with the operational flexibility to serve multiple regions and the product breadth to meet varying technical specifications.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Policy Risk and the Shape of the Remaining Gap

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is the trajectory of US trade policy toward Chinese grid equipment. The report's estimates of the supply gap narrowing to 60% by 2028 assume a continuation of current trade patterns. But policy risk is real and could move in either direction. A more protectionist stance could accelerate domestic capacity expansion but also create supply bottlenecks and higher costs. A more open stance could maintain the current import trajectory but at the risk of political backlash.

The second unresolved question is the shape of the remaining 60% gap. Not all transformers are created equal. The gap may be concentrated in the largest, most complex units — those above 500MVA — where domestic capacity is hardest to build. Or it may be more evenly distributed across voltage classes. The answer matters for which Chinese exporters benefit most. Those with capabilities in the highest voltage classes will face less competition but also smaller volumes. Those in the mid-range segments will face more competition but larger addressable markets.

Third, the report does not fully explore the implications of the switchgear acceleration. Is this a temporary shift driven by procurement cycles, or does it signal a permanent change in how utilities allocate their capital budgets? The answer will determine whether the switchgear opportunity is a one-time catch-up or a structural growth trend.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Grid Equipment Cycle

For investors seeking to translate this analysis into actionable decisions, the following framework may prove useful. It is designed to evaluate individual companies based on their exposure to the key variables that will drive performance over the next two to three years.

**First, assess product diversification.** The era of pure transformer plays is ending. Companies with exposure to switchgear, meters, and secondary equipment will benefit from the rotational shift in procurement. The ideal candidate has a product portfolio that covers multiple grid equipment categories, allowing it to capture demand regardless of where the bottleneck appears.

**Second, evaluate geographic exposure.** A company that relies too heavily on any single market — whether the US, Asia, or Africa — carries concentration risk. The strongest positions are those with balanced exposure across multiple regions, combined with the operational capability to shift production and sales efforts as market conditions change.

**Third, analyze pricing power and cost management.** The volatility in export pricing, combined with divergent raw material trends, means that margin stability is a competitive advantage. Companies with long-term customer relationships, contractual pricing mechanisms, and sophisticated raw material procurement will outperform those that are forced to transact on a spot basis.

**Fourth, consider the voltage class sweet spot.** The 220-330MVA segment is the most competitive and price-sensitive. The highest voltage classes offer better margins but smaller volumes. The mid-range presents a trade-off. The optimal positioning depends on a company's cost structure and customer base.

**Fifth, monitor policy risk but do not overestimate it.** Trade policy is uncertain, but the structural need for grid equipment is undeniable. Companies that can serve multiple markets and maintain operational flexibility are best positioned to weather any policy storm.

## The Window is Changing, Not Closing — And the Full Picture Requires Deeper Analysis

The April export data confirms that the US power transformer market is entering a new phase. The days of unlimited demand growth at any price are giving way to a more selective, competitive environment. But the core thesis remains intact: the US cannot satisfy its own transformer demand for years to come, and Chinese exporters will play a critical role in filling the gap.

The opportunity, however, is no longer uniform. It is shifting from transformers to switchgear, from the US to emerging markets, and from volume growth to margin management. The companies that recognize these shifts and adapt their strategies accordingly will be the ones that deliver sustained outperformance.

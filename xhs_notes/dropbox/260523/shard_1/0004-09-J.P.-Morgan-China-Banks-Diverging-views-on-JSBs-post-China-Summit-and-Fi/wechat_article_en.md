# China's Joint-Stock Banks Are No Longer a Monolith: The Divergence in Profit Trajectories Demands a Stock-Specific Strategy

The Chinese banking sector is often discussed in aggregate terms, but the most critical insight emerging from recent meetings with four major joint-stock banks is that the era of uniform performance is over. While all these institutions face the same macroeconomic headwinds—weak retail loan demand, a recovering but fragile net interest margin (NIM), and persistent drag from retail asset quality—their individual profit trajectories are diverging sharply. This is not a sector story; it is a stock-picker's market. One bank is guiding for a meaningful acceleration in profit growth, while another is signaling that it remains in the early stages of a credit deterioration cycle. The difference between these outcomes is not about the macro environment, but about the strategic decisions made over the past several years regarding asset quality cleanup, balance sheet restructuring, and provisioning discipline.

The meetings, conducted during a recent global investment bank's China Summit and Financial Tour, revealed a spectrum of outcomes that challenge the conventional wisdom of a synchronized recovery. On one end, a bank is targeting high-single-digit profit growth for the full year, a stark contrast to the modest single-digit growth recorded in the first quarter. On the other end, a bank is guiding for subdued profit growth, explicitly stating that it has no confidence to call an inflection point in its retail asset quality. This divergence is not random; it is the direct consequence of how each bank managed its loan book and provisioning reserves through the downturn. For investors, the implication is clear: a blanket approach to China's joint-stock banks is no longer viable. The strategic question has shifted from "will the sector recover?" to "which banks have already paid the price for past credit excesses and which are still paying?"

## The Loan Demand Narrative Is Uniform, but the Loan Growth Strategy Is Not

The most striking finding from the meetings is that the weakness in loan demand is a shared experience, yet the response to it is not. Every bank confirmed that April loan contraction was partly seasonal, but the underlying trend is a persistent softness in retail demand, particularly for mortgages. One bank even speculated that the rise of cash payments and higher downpayments, rather than competition from provident funds, is the structural reason for weak mortgage growth despite a recovery in transaction volumes. This is a critical nuance: the issue is not a lack of homebuyers, but a shift in how they are financing purchases, which is fundamentally harder for banks to address.

Against this backdrop, the divergence in loan growth strategies is telling. One bank expressed confidence in achieving its full-year loan growth target, supported by a strong pipeline of corporate loans that reached approximately RMB 230 billion in the first quarter, concentrated in the Greater Bay Area and Yangtze River regions. This bank is effectively pulling forward demand from a strong corporate pipeline. In contrast, another bank guided for a slight slowdown in loan growth, from 5.8% in 2025 to 5.3% in 2026, as it aims for a more balanced mix between corporate and retail, but admits that actual results depend on demand. A third bank saw corporate loan demand exceed expectations in the first quarter, only to see a retreat in April and continued weakness in May, with retail loans contracting across all lines since mid-2025.

The so-what here is that loan growth is no longer a simple function of capacity or willingness to lend. It is a function of where the bank has chosen to position itself. Banks that have a strong corporate franchise in dynamic regions are able to maintain growth, while those that are more dependent on retail are struggling. For investors, the question is not whether a bank will grow its loan book, but whether that growth is coming from segments that are sustainable and profitable, or from a last-ditch effort to meet targets.

## NIM Recovery Is Real, but the Pace and Sustainability Vary by Bank

The consensus on NIM is more optimistic than it was a year ago, but the devil is in the details. Every bank expects NIM contraction to slow or even reverse, but the magnitude and confidence level differ. One bank guided for NIM to be largely flat quarter-over-quarter for the remainder of 2026, with a full-year contraction of only 3-5 basis points, a dramatic improvement from the 13-basis-point contraction in 2025. Another bank already saw a 2-basis-point improvement in the first quarter and aims for stable or mildly improving NIM year-over-year. A third expects a low-single-digit basis point contraction, but is focused on structural optimization—shifting from bills to loans.

The strategic implication is that the NIM recovery is not an automatic tailwind. It is a function of asset mix, deposit pricing power, and the ability to pass on lower funding costs to loan customers. Banks that have successfully cleaned up their balance sheets and reduced their reliance on high-cost wholesale funding are better positioned to benefit from the rate environment. Banks that are still dealing with legacy issues, such as a high proportion of non-performing loans requiring heavy provisioning, will see less of the NIM improvement flow through to the bottom line.

## The Real Divergence Is in Asset Quality: Some Banks Have Cleared the Deck, Others Are Still Digging

This is the most consequential insight from the meetings. The divergence in profit guidance is not driven by revenue optimism, but by a fundamental difference in where each bank stands in the credit cycle. One bank is guiding for a high-single-digit profit growth in FY26, which implies a meaningful acceleration from the modest 1% year-over-year growth in the first quarter. The reason is clear: this bank believes its asset quality cleanup is largely complete. It has brought all off-balance-sheet wealth management products onto the balance sheet and over-provisioned for them by the end of 2025. It is confident that its asset quality is at the best level of the past ten years. The bank's credit costs are on an improving trend, and it expects overall NPL formation to decline year-over-year, leading to lower impairment charges. This is a bank that has already taken the pain and is now reaping the benefit of lower provisioning.

In stark contrast, another bank is still in the early stages of recognizing retail asset quality deterioration. It has no confidence to call an inflection point in retail asset quality, with credit card NPL formation still high and showing no clear improvement. Personal business loan NPL formation rebounded in the first quarter. This bank expects impairment charges to be slightly higher than in 2025, primarily due to retail. Its NPL coverage ratio is only around 140%, and management prioritizes rebuilding that coverage to 150% before expecting any return on equity (ROE) rebound. This is a bank that is still in the penalty phase of the credit cycle.

The other two banks fall in between. One is guiding for stable NPL formation and flattish credit costs, with pressure concentrated in retail but with a manageable overall picture. The other has seen a significant improvement in its NPL formation rate, leading to lower write-off pressure and declining credit costs, but still faces headwinds from a high base in investment income that will pressure revenue growth in the middle quarters.

The so-what for investors is profound. The banks that have already cleared their asset quality problems are entering a virtuous cycle: lower NPL formation leads to less write-off, which leads to lower provisioning, which leads to profit accretion. The banks that are still dealing with deterioration are in a vicious cycle: higher NPL formation requires more provisioning, which drags on profits and prevents them from rebuilding their coverage ratios, which in turn makes them more vulnerable to future shocks.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the Fee Income Recovery

While the meetings provided detailed guidance on loan growth, NIM, and asset quality, one area remains more opaque: the sustainability of fee income growth. Most banks are optimistic on fees, citing recovery in wealth management, custody, and bancassurance. One bank targets positive fee income growth, driven by wealth management which grew 12-14% year-over-year in 2025. Another expects fee income to be "stable and improving," driven by wealth, settlement, and custodian fees.

However, the report does not provide a clear answer on whether this fee recovery is structural or cyclical. The wealth management business is benefiting from a base effect, as the market adjusts to the new regulations that brought off-balance-sheet products onto the balance sheet. But the competition is still intense, and one bank noted that DCM (debt capital market) fees are easing on base effects, but bank card fees remain a headwind. The question that remains unanswered is whether the fee income recovery can sustain itself once the base effects fade and competition intensifies. For investors, this is a critical variable. If fee income growth proves to be a one-time bounce, then the profit divergence story becomes even more dependent on credit costs and NIM, which are more predictable but also more volatile.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Separate the Winners from the Laggards

Based on the insights from these meetings, investors should apply a three-part framework to evaluate any joint-stock bank. This framework moves beyond aggregate metrics and focuses on the strategic choices that determine outcomes.

First, has the bank already completed its asset quality cleanup? This is the single most important question. The banks that have already taken the impairments and over-provisioned for legacy issues are now in a position to benefit from lower credit costs. The banks that are still in the early stages of recognizing retail asset quality deterioration will continue to see provisioning drag on profits. The key metric here is not just the NPL ratio, but the NPL formation rate and the coverage ratio. A bank with a coverage ratio above 200% and a declining NPL formation rate is in a fundamentally different position than a bank with a coverage ratio below 150% and a rising NPL formation rate.

Second, is the bank's loan growth strategy aligned with its risk appetite? The banks that are growing their loan books are doing so primarily through corporate loans in strong regions. The banks that are struggling are those that are dependent on retail, particularly mortgages and credit cards. Investors should assess whether a bank's loan growth is coming from segments where it has a competitive advantage and where credit risk is manageable. A bank that is forced to grow in retail to meet targets is likely taking on more risk than it can manage.

Third, is the fee income recovery sustainable or cyclical? This requires a deeper analysis of the composition of fee income. A recovery driven by wealth management and custody is more sustainable than one driven by a one-time bounce in DCM or bancassurance. Investors should look for banks that are rebuilding their retail teams and product shelves, as this suggests a structural investment in fee income generation rather than a cyclical rebound.

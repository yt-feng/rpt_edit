# The Market Is Misreading Commodity Supply Scares, and the Real Opportunity Lies in the Dislocation

For two days at the China Summit, a persistent narrative has taken hold: that Chinese miners are being unfairly punished by a macro-driven investor base that is repricing risks for higher rates and inflation far too aggressively. But the deeper truth emerging from corporate meetings is more nuanced and far more strategic. The real story is not about whether Chinese equities are cheap or expensive. It is about a series of supply-side dislocations that the market has not yet fully priced, and about a growing divergence between what investors fear and what companies on the ground actually see.

The key takeaway from Day 2 is that several widely feared shortages are not materializing in the way the market expects. There is no sulfur shortage in the DRC. There is no diesel shortage for miners who are willing to pay. Indonesian aluminum supply will not disrupt global markets for at least 12 to 18 months. Yet the market is pricing these risks as if they are immediate and binary. The gap between perception and reality is creating a fertile ground for selective, high-conviction positioning.

The most important signal from the summit is that the most bullish voices are coming not from equity analysts or macro desks, but from the corporates themselves. China Hongqiao, one of the world's largest aluminum producers, is explicitly calling for higher prices over the next 12 to 18 months. CMOC is reaffirming its ambitious copper output targets with no sign of demand destruction. And the investor interest in Chinese mining has never been higher, even as the share prices lag. This is the classic setup for a dislocation trade: the fundamentals are improving, but the market has not yet caught up.

## The DRC Supply Scare Is Overblown, but the Real Cost Pressure Is Structural

The most debated topic at the CMOC meeting was the availability of sulfur and diesel in the Democratic Republic of Congo. A significant portion of the investor base has been concerned that a sulfur shortage would cripple copper production in the region, and that a diesel shortage would further compound operational risks. The company's response was clear and direct: there is no shortage. Most miners in the region operate their own acid plants, and inventories are well stocked into the third quarter. Diesel is available as long as one is willing to pay the market price.

The "so what" here is critical. The market has been pricing a disruption premium into DRC-exposed equities based on a narrative that is not supported by the facts on the ground. But the company also acknowledged that both sulfur and diesel will be persistent cost pressures. Sulfuric acid has moved from roughly 10% of cash costs to 20%, and diesel has shifted from a low single-digit percentage to a high single-digit percentage. This means that while the volume risk is low, the margin risk is real and structural. Investors who are long DRC copper must accept that the cost curve has shifted permanently higher.

The second-order implication is more strategic. If sulfur and diesel costs remain elevated, the marginal cost of production for DRC copper will rise, which in turn should support a higher floor for copper prices. The companies that are best positioned are those with captive acid plants and long-term diesel procurement contracts. The companies that are most exposed are those that rely on spot purchases. This is not a uniform call on the DRC. It is a call on operational differentiation.

The meeting also surfaced a longer-term concern that the report does not fully resolve: the DRC's increasingly U.S.-friendly policy stance. Investors are worried that this could limit further expansion in the country, particularly for Chinese-owned miners. The windfall tax of 50% above $11,500 LME, the local employee stake requirements, and the cobalt quotas are all headwinds that will blunt earnings in 2027 and beyond. The question that remains unanswered is whether the DRC's policy environment will become more restrictive for Chinese capital, or whether the current tensions are a temporary phase of renegotiation. The report does not provide a clear answer, and this uncertainty will continue to cap valuations for DRC-exposed names.

## Aluminum Is the Most Compelling Dislocation in Commodities Today

The meeting with China Hongqiao was arguably the most bullish of the summit, and for good reason. The company sees aluminum prices moving higher from current levels and remaining elevated for 12 to 18 months. The logic is straightforward and data-supported. LME inventories have fallen to critically low levels, with only about 300 kilotons of inventory remaining, of which roughly 200 kilotons is Russian and therefore subject to logistical and sanctions-related constraints. Chinese inventories stand at 1.4 to 1.5 million tons, but that cushion is about to be tested.

Hongqiao argues that it is only a matter of time before China's aluminum exports begin to draw down domestic inventories. With a monthly export run rate of 550 kilotons, the impact should start showing in one to two months. The LME and Rotterdam premiums remain notably higher than the SHFE premium, which means Chinese producers have a strong incentive to export. Once that process begins, domestic inventories will tighten, and Chinese prices will have to rise to realign with global benchmarks.

The report also highlights a critical supply-side argument that is widely misunderstood. There is a widespread belief that Indonesian aluminum smelters will flood the market with new capacity in the near term. Hongqiao's response is devastating to that narrative: it took the company 2.5 years to bring on a 1 million ton alumina refinery in Indonesia. The idea that smelters can bring on 2 million tons of aluminum in one year without sufficient power capacity is, in the company's words, unrealistic. The power constraints in Indonesia are severe, and the timeline for new smelting capacity is measured in years, not months.

The strategic implication for investors is clear. The aluminum market is heading into a structural deficit that will not be resolved by new supply for at least 12 to 18 months. The companies that are best positioned are those with captive bauxite reserves, integrated alumina refining, and access to low-cost power. Hongqiao is actively increasing its bauxite inventories from seven to ten months, which is a defensive move that signals the company expects supply chains to remain tight.

One unresolved question from the report is whether China's 45 million ton capacity target will be breached. Hongqiao believes it is unlikely, but the production run rate is already high. If Chinese output exceeds the target, it could cap the upside for aluminum prices. The market does not yet have a clear answer on this, and it will be a critical data point to watch in the coming quarters.

## The Geopolitical and Policy Risks Are Real, but They Are Not Uniform

The report surfaces three distinct geopolitical and policy risks that are affecting investor sentiment, but they are not all created equal. The situation in Mali is the most acute. The abduction of nine Chinese employees at an Allied Gold mining site, combined with rumors that Zijin has not received NDRC approval for its acquisition, has created a sudden and severe risk premium for companies with exposure to the country. The market reaction was immediate, with Allied Gold falling as much as 10% at one point.

The second-order implication is that Chinese mining companies are facing a growing tension between their global expansion ambitions and the willingness of host governments to accommodate them. Mali is not an isolated incident. The DRC's policy shift, Indonesia's repeated regulatory changes, and the broader trend of resource nationalism are all converging. The companies that will succeed are those that have diversified geographic exposure and the financial flexibility to walk away from deals that do not meet their return thresholds. The companies that are overconcentrated in high-risk jurisdictions will face a persistent valuation discount.

Indonesia's latest policy shock is arguably the most consequential, because it introduces structural uncertainty into the entire Southeast Asian mining complex. The government is now requiring all coal, palm oil, and ferroalloy exports to go through a central state-owned entity, including paperwork, sales agreements, and payment processing. The Jakarta Composite Index fell 4% on the day and is down 34% year-to-date. The policy creates a massive implementation risk. How will the SOE handle the volume? Will payments be delayed? Will foreign buyers accept the new structure? These questions are not yet answered, and they will hang over every investment decision in Indonesian mining for the foreseeable future.

The report also flags a critical watchpoint in chemicals. PetroChina is moving full steam ahead with its capital expenditure, but the real concern is Sinopec, which is the largest incremental capacity adder and has been losing money in chemicals for four years. Sinopec is not at the conference, which means the market has limited visibility into its strategy. JPM's house view is that China's chemicals capex will decline 10% year-on-year in 2026, but that forecast depends on Sinopec making rational decisions. If Sinopec continues to add capacity despite losses, the chemicals sector could face a prolonged period of margin compression.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Dislocation

The report provides enough data to construct a simple but powerful decision framework for investors who are trying to position for the next 12 to 18 months. The framework has three dimensions: supply reality, cost pressure, and policy risk.

First, supply reality. The market is pricing a series of supply disruptions that are not materializing. There is no sulfur shortage, no diesel shortage, and no imminent Indonesian aluminum flood. Investors should overweight the commodities where the supply narrative is most overblown, because the correction will be sharp when the data confirms the reality. Aluminum and copper are the clearest candidates.

Second, cost pressure. The cost curve for many commodities has shifted permanently higher. Sulfur and diesel costs are up. Labor costs in mining jurisdictions are rising. Renewable energy investments are adding to capital intensity. Investors should favor companies that have captive inputs, long-term contracts, and operational flexibility. They should avoid companies that rely on spot procurement and have high fixed-cost bases.

Third, policy risk. The policy environment is deteriorating in several key jurisdictions, but the impact is not uniform. The DRC's windfall tax is a known headwind that is already priced. Indonesia's new export rules are an unknown that is not yet priced. Mali's security situation is a tail risk that is hard to quantify. Investors should limit exposure to companies that have concentrated positions in high-risk countries and should demand a higher risk premium for any company that cannot demonstrate a clear path to repatriating cash.

The unanswered question that runs through all three dimensions is whether the macro environment will cooperate. If global interest rates remain elevated and inflation stays sticky, the demand side of the equation could weaken. The report does not provide a definitive view on this, and it is the single biggest risk to the bullish commodity thesis. But for investors who are willing to take a view, the current dislocation between corporate reality and market pricing offers a rare opportunity to buy high-quality assets at discounted valuations.

# China's Medtech Market Is Not Recovering Uniformly—The Divergence Between Device Types Reveals the Real Strategic Challenge

China remains the most consequential and most misunderstood market for global medtech companies. After three years of relentless healthcare cost-control policies—volume-based procurement (VBP), the "minimum necessary principle," and Buy-China initiatives—the conventional narrative has shifted from panic to cautious optimism. Several multinational corporations reported improving sales trajectories in the first quarter of 2026. Abbott Laboratories saw its Core Lab business in China stabilize after a year of 15-30% quarterly declines. Boston Scientific delivered strong double-digit growth despite VBP headwinds. Olympus expects a recovery in its fiscal year ending March 2027.

But these green shoots are deceptive. A careful reading of the latest earnings commentary from over a dozen global medtech companies reveals a market that is bifurcating along product lines, not geography. The recovery is real in therapeutic devices—particularly cardiovascular and gastrointestinal products where volume expansion has offset price compression. It is absent or worsening in diagnostics, especially in-vitro diagnostics (IVD), where policy-driven price deflation is accelerating and structural demand is being reshaped by domestic substitution. The divergence is not temporary. It reflects a fundamental reordering of China's healthcare priorities: the state is willing to reward innovation that reduces procedural costs, but it is systematically commoditizing test-based revenues.

For investors, the implication is clear. The old approach of treating China medtech as a single exposure—hedging across device types, relying on long-term demographic demand—no longer works. The next phase of the China strategy requires a surgical portfolio view, where the distinction between therapeutic and diagnostic exposure determines whether a company is positioned for recovery or structural impairment.

## The Diagnostic Sector Is Facing a Second Wave of Policy Pressure That Therapeutic Devices Have Largely Absorbed

The most striking pattern in the first-quarter 2026 earnings calls is the asymmetry between diagnostic and therapeutic businesses. Companies with significant IVD exposure in China are reporting deterioration, not improvement. Roche's Diagnostics business in China declined 14% year-on-year in the first quarter, and its global diagnostics growth of 3% becomes 8% when China is excluded. Sysmex, the Japanese diagnostics specialist, saw a staggering 38.5% year-on-year decline in fourth-quarter China sales, driven by distributor inventory adjustments, reagent pricing pressure, and the looming threat of unified test pricing. Revvity chose to divest its entire Immunodiagnostics business in China—roughly 6% of total company sales—explicitly citing continued headwinds from healthcare cost-control policies.

These are not isolated incidents. They reflect a policy environment that is intensifying for diagnostics even as it stabilizes for therapeutics. The "minimum necessary principle"—which limits the number of diagnostic tests per patient encounter—is structurally capping volume growth in a way that VBP for devices does not. In devices, VBP compresses unit prices but often triggers volume expansion as hospitals expand access to previously expensive procedures. Terumo's Neurovascular business in China is a textbook case: VBP-driven price declines were more than offset by volume increases through expanded sales channels. Boston Scientific's double-digit China growth came despite VBP impacts on its Urology and Arterial businesses.

The difference is fundamental. In therapeutic devices, the procedure itself is the value driver, and lower prices can expand the addressable patient population. In diagnostics, the test is a cost center for hospitals, and policymakers have no incentive to expand testing volumes—they have every incentive to consolidate, standardize, and reduce test utilization. The unified test pricing discussions in China, which Sysmex explicitly cited as a source of earnings volatility, represent a potential structural cap on diagnostic revenues that has no analogue in the device world.

This distinction matters because it is not cyclical. The policy trajectory in China is clear: the government is building a sustainable healthcare system that prioritizes cost control over revenue growth for suppliers. Diagnostic companies, which rely on recurring reagent and consumable revenue streams, are more exposed to this structural shift than device companies, which can offset price declines through procedure volume growth and product innovation. Investors who treat "China recovery" as a uniform theme are missing the most important strategic question: is your company's China business a volume story or a price story?

## Localization Is Becoming a Defensive Necessity, Not a Growth Strategy, and the Window for Foreign Companies Is Narrowing

The second major theme emerging from the earnings commentary is the accelerating push for domestic manufacturing and local product development. Olympus explicitly linked its expected China recovery to growth in locally manufactured products. Mindray, the Chinese domestic champion, reported that its market share in immunoassay, biochemistry, and coagulation rose from 10% to 13% in the first quarter, with a target of 20% within three years. The company noted "an accelerating trend of domestic products replacing imported ones at Tier-3 hospitals."

This is not a new trend, but its pace is quickening. The Buy-China policies that were once aspirational are now operational. Tier-3 hospitals—the highest-level institutions in China—are increasingly prioritizing domestic equipment, not just for price reasons but because of regulatory incentives, procurement quotas, and faster service support. For foreign medtech companies, localization is no longer a growth option; it is a defensive requirement to maintain access to the most profitable hospital segment.

Yet localization comes with its own strategic complications. Building local manufacturing capacity requires capital investment, supply chain reorganization, and technology transfer. It also changes the competitive dynamics: a locally manufactured product may qualify for preferential procurement but may also face more direct price comparisons with domestic competitors. Sysmex's plan to shift to direct sales in China reflects this tension—the company is trying to capture more value from its distribution chain even as its pricing power erodes.

The critical question for investors is whether localization can restore growth or merely slow the decline. For companies like Olympus, which has a strong brand and differentiated technology in gastrointestinal endoscopy, local manufacturing may allow it to compete effectively against emerging domestic players. For companies in more commoditized segments—particularly diagnostics—localization may be a necessary condition for survival but insufficient to restore historical margins. The companies that succeed will be those that can maintain technological differentiation even as they shift production onshore.

The window for this transition is narrowing. Mindray's stated goal of doubling its market share in core diagnostic segments within three years is aggressive but credible. If domestic players continue to gain share at the current rate, foreign companies in diagnostics may face a choice between exiting the market—as Revvity did—or accepting permanently lower margins. The strategic calculus is different for each company, but the direction of travel is unmistakable.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: How Will Unified Test Pricing Reshape the Diagnostics Business Model?

The most consequential development flagged in the earnings commentary is the potential introduction of unified test pricing in China's diagnostics market. Sysmex's management explicitly stated that its guidance incorporates the possibility of this new policy being introduced, while acknowledging "uncertainty as to when and how the new pricing policy is implemented." The company described it as a potential source of "earnings volatility."

This is the question the report does not—and cannot—fully answer. Unified test pricing would represent a fundamental shift in how diagnostic companies generate revenue in China. Currently, diagnostic companies earn most of their China revenue from consumables—reagents, test kits, and supplies—that are sold at prices set by the manufacturer. Unified pricing would set a fixed reimbursement rate for each test, effectively capping the revenue that hospitals can generate from diagnostic procedures. This would compress margins for reagent suppliers and potentially shift the business model from volume-based to value-based—or simply reduce the total addressable market.

The implications are profound. If unified test pricing is implemented broadly, it could accelerate the consolidation of the diagnostics market, favoring companies with the lowest cost structures and strongest local supply chains. It could also incentivize hospitals to reduce test utilization further, compounding the impact of the minimum necessary principle. For companies like Sysmex, which derives a significant portion of its revenue from China, the policy could represent a structural impairment to earnings that no amount of volume growth can offset.

The uncertainty around timing and scope makes this the most important variable for medtech investors to monitor. The report's analysis of current earnings commentary provides a useful snapshot, but it cannot predict how quickly or broadly unified test pricing will be rolled out. Investors need to stress-test their China exposure under multiple scenarios: a best case where unified pricing is limited to a few test categories, a base case where it applies broadly but with gradual implementation, and a worst case where it is applied aggressively across the entire diagnostic portfolio.

## The Strategic Framework for Medtech Investors Should Be Based on Product Type, Not Geography or Company Size

The earnings commentary from the first quarter of 2026 allows investors to construct a decision framework that is more granular and more useful than the traditional "China exposure" analysis. The key insight is that the relevant variable is not whether a company sells in China, but what it sells in China.

The framework has three dimensions. First, product type: therapeutic devices versus diagnostic products. Therapeutic devices, particularly those with strong volume growth potential from expanded access, are better positioned to absorb price compression. Diagnostic products, especially those with high reagent revenue exposure, face structural headwinds that may not be cyclical.

Second, policy exposure: companies with significant VBP exposure in devices have already experienced the worst of the price compression. Companies with exposure to unified test pricing in diagnostics are facing a new and potentially more damaging policy wave.

Third, localization readiness: companies that have already invested in local manufacturing and have a credible path to domestic supply chains are better positioned to compete for hospital procurement. Companies that rely on imported products face a growing competitive disadvantage.

This framework suggests that investors should treat medtech companies not as a single China play, but as a portfolio of exposures that require different strategic responses. A company like Boston Scientific, with strong therapeutic device growth and manageable VBP exposure, may be in a fundamentally different position than a company like Roche Diagnostics, which faces both diagnostic headwinds and localization challenges.

The framework also highlights the importance of monitoring domestic competitors. Mindray's market share gains are not just a Chinese story—they represent a shift in the global competitive landscape. As Chinese domestic players improve their technology and build scale, they will increasingly compete with foreign companies not just in China but in emerging markets and eventually developed markets. The China policy environment is effectively subsidizing the development of future global competitors.

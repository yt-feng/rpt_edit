EN_B: The administration is basically swapping out the expiring Section 122 tariff regime for a much broader set of Section 301 tariffs. It’s not just a name change. The legal risks and the scope are completely different.

EN_B: Exactly. Earlier this year, the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, which cut the average US tariff rate by about 2.5 percentage points. But now, with Section 122 expiring on July 24, these new 301 tariffs are set to take its place.
EN_A: How does this new system work? It sounds pretty complex.
EN_B: The report breaks it into two main tracks. One is a forced-labor investigation covering about 60 economies, which is 99% of US trade. The other is a structural excess capacity probe covering 16 economies.

EN_B: For the forced-labor part, USTR proposed a two-tier system. 10% for countries that have anti-forced-labor laws but weak enforcement, like the EU, the UK, and Mexico. And 12.5% for everyone else under investigation. Plus, there is a separate 25% tariff just for Brazil.
EN_A: 25% on Brazil? That sounds like a big deal.
EN_B: It is. Brazil sees the biggest jump. The report estimates their effective rate goes from about 10.5% to 19%. But it also notes that Brazil’s direct trade exposure to the US is relatively low, and strong oil exports help cushion the blow.
EN_A: And what about China? They are already hit the hardest.
EN_B: China’s rate barely moves, staying around 23% to 24%. They already have so many Section 301 and Section 232 duties in place that these new measures are just a minor tweak.
EN_A: The report also mentions something interesting. It says the actual tariff rate collected is much lower than the headline rate. Why is that?
EN_B: That is a key insight. The gap is because of very broad exemptions. Things like energy, pharmaceuticals, electronics, and all USMCA-compliant trade are largely excluded. The report estimates the observed rate in April was only about 6.7%, well below the 10% or 15% headline numbers.
EN_A: So the exemption list is the real battleground. And legally, how is this new system different from the one the Supreme Court just threw out?
EN_B: The report argues it looks like a straightforward effort to recreate the broad IEEPA regime, rather than the targeted, country-specific approach Section 301 has historically used. That creates a big legal risk, because the courts are now focused on strict statutory text.
EN_A: So litigation is likely?
EN_B: Very. The report says it seems inevitable. And the process itself will raise trade policy uncertainty. Plus, there is a big open question that the report doesn’t fully answer.

EN_B: The structural excess capacity probe. The tariff rates for that haven’t been announced yet. And the critical question is: will those tariffs be stacked on top of existing ones, or replace them? The report leaves that hanging.
EN_A: So we could be facing even more tariff stacking?
EN_B: Exactly. That is the core of the next wave of uncertainty.

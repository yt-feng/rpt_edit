# The US Tariff Regime Is Not Weakening — It Is Being Rebuilt Under New Legal Authority

The narrative that US trade policy is retreating is misleading. The Supreme Court’s invalidation of IEEPA-based tariffs earlier this year did cut the average US tariff rate by roughly 2.5 percentage points, bringing it to about 11 percent on a static basis. But this is not a story of liberalization. It is a story of legal architecture being reconstructed. With the Section 122 tariff authority expiring on July 24, the administration is not stepping back from protectionism. It is switching legal vehicles — from emergency powers to Section 301 trade remedies — while preserving, and in some cases expanding, the overall level of tariff pressure on the US economy.

The observed tariff rate — calculated duties divided by actual imports — stood at an estimated 6.7 percent in April, well below the headline average. That gap has led some observers to conclude that tariffs are largely symbolic. This interpretation is wrong. The gap reflects broad exemptions for energy, pharmaceuticals, electronics, and USMCA-compliant trade, not a policy retreat. The administration is deliberately targeting specific sectors and supply chains while shielding politically sensitive inputs. The effective rate is low by design, not by accident. And the new Section 301 framework being proposed is broader and faster-moving than the original China-focused measures from 2017–2018, covering forced-labor enforcement across 60 economies and structural excess capacity across 16.

What makes this moment strategically significant is not the level of tariffs today, but the direction of travel. The administration is replacing a temporary, legally vulnerable tariff regime with a permanent one built on statutory trade law. The court decisions that struck down IEEPA tariffs did not question the administration’s trade policy objectives — they questioned its choice of legal authority. The response has been to find a more defensible statute. This is not a defeat for protectionism; it is an adaptation.

For investors, supply chain planners, and corporate strategists, the implication is clear: US trade policy uncertainty is not fading. It is entering a new phase — one where legal challenges, statutory interpretation, and the sequencing of multiple tariff authorities will determine the real economic impact. The next few weeks, as Section 122 expires and the forced-labor Section 301 tariffs are finalized, will set the baseline for a multi-year regime shift.

## The Shift from Emergency Powers to Trade Statutes Does Not Reduce Tariff Levels — It Changes the Legal Risk Profile

The US tariff regime has undergone a fundamental legal transformation in recent months. The Supreme Court’s February decision invalidating IEEPA-based tariffs removed an estimated 2.5 percentage points from the average US tariff rate. But the administration immediately pivoted to Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a rarely used authority that allows temporary tariffs during balance-of-payments emergencies. That authority now expires on July 24. The next step is a move to Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — the same statute used during the original China tariff escalation.

This is not a linear reduction in protection. It is a substitution of legal authorities, each with different procedural requirements, judicial review standards, and political staying power. Section 301 tariffs are harder to challenge in court than IEEPA tariffs, because the statute gives the US Trade Representative broad discretion to determine whether foreign trade practices are unreasonable or discriminatory. The court decisions that struck down IEEPA tariffs focused on the statutory text of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, which limits presidential action to genuine national emergencies. Section 301 has no such constraint — it is explicitly designed for trade retaliation.

The forced-labor Section 301 investigation covers approximately 60 economies, representing 99 percent of US trade. The proposed remedy is a two-tier tariff structure: 10 percent for jurisdictions with forced-labor bans but weak enforcement, and 12.5 percent for the remaining investigated jurisdictions. Exemptions are broad — covering Section 232 goods, foodstuffs, fuels, pharmaceuticals, gold, semiconductors, electronics, aircraft, and additional machinery. On a static basis, replacing Section 122 with these Section 301 tariffs would keep the global average effective rate near current levels. But the distribution across countries changes significantly.

Brazil faces the largest increase — a separate Section 301 action proposes a 25 percent headline tariff, lifting Brazil’s effective rate from 10.5 percent to 19 percent. China remains the most heavily tariffed economy, staying near 23 percent. The European Union and the United Kingdom see small declines, as the broad Section 122 surcharge lapses and the targeted Section 301 duties cover only part of their trade. Canada and Mexico remain near 3 percent due to USMCA exemptions.

The strategic insight is that the administration is using Section 301 not as a targeted tool, but as a broad-based tariff authority — a departure from its historical use for specific country disputes. This invites litigation, but the legal standard is more favorable to the government. The question is not whether the tariffs will be challenged, but whether the courts will defer to USTR’s discretion or impose a more searching review.

## The Gap Between Headline and Effective Tariff Rates Reveals a Deliberate Strategy of Selective Protection

The observed US tariff rate of 6.7 percent in April is well below the static headline average of 11 percent. This gap is often cited as evidence that tariffs are not biting. In reality, it reflects a carefully calibrated exemption strategy that shields politically sensitive sectors while maintaining pressure on targeted industries and supply chains.

The largest exemptions cover energy, pharmaceuticals, electronics, and USMCA-compliant trade. Under the proposed forced-labor Section 301 tariffs, the exemption list expands further to include aircraft, machinery, and additional foodstuffs. This is not random. These are sectors where tariff increases would cause immediate domestic price spikes, supply chain disruption, or political backlash. The administration is willing to accept lower effective rates in exchange for maintaining the legal and political viability of the tariff regime.

The sectoral pattern is instructive. Aircraft and jet-engine parts, which were fully subject to the Section 122 surcharge, would see their effective rate drop to near 1 percent under the proposed Section 301 framework, because only the non-civil share — roughly 10 percent of value — would be dutiable. This explains why France, with its concentration in airframe exports to the US, sees the largest decline among major European suppliers. Machinery and foodstuffs receive similar treatment.

This selective exemption strategy creates a complex landscape for corporate planners. Companies in exempted sectors face minimal direct tariff impact, but they are vulnerable to second-order effects — supply chain reconfiguration, input cost increases, and regulatory uncertainty. Companies in targeted sectors face direct cost increases, but may also benefit from reduced competition as tariffs shift trade flows.

The key analytical question is whether the exemption structure is stable. The administration could narrow exemptions over time, increasing effective rates without changing headline tariff levels. Alternatively, it could broaden exemptions to manage political pressure. The trajectory of exemptions will be as important as the trajectory of headline rates.

## The Structural Excess Capacity Investigation Represents the Next Wave of Tariff Expansion

The forced-labor Section 301 tariffs are not the end of the story. A separate Section 301 investigation into "structural excess capacity and production" across 16 economies is underway, with comments closed on April 15. The US Trade Representative has indicated that conclusions are expected in "weeks." This investigation targets countries where government policies — subsidies, state-owned enterprises, and industrial planning — have driven production levels above market demand, generating overcapacity that harms US industries.

This investigation is conceptually different from the forced-labor inquiry. It is not about labor standards; it is about industrial policy. The targets are likely to include China, but also other economies with significant state-directed manufacturing sectors. The proposed remedies have not been announced, but the scope could be broad — covering sectors such as steel, aluminum, chemicals, solar panels, electric vehicles, and semiconductors.

The critical unknown is whether these tariffs will be stacked on top of existing tariffs. If they are additive, the effective rate on certain products from certain countries could rise significantly. If they are substitutive — replacing existing tariffs rather than adding to them — the impact would be more moderate. The administration has not provided guidance on stacking, and the legal basis for stacking may face challenges.

Investors should monitor this investigation closely, because it represents the most significant potential expansion of US tariff coverage since the original China Section 301 tariffs. The timeline is compressed — conclusions in weeks, implementation potentially within months. The sectors at risk are those where global overcapacity is most pronounced and where US domestic producers have the most political influence.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Multiple Tariff Authorities and the Legal Risk of Stacking

The report provides a detailed mapping of how forced-labor Section 301 tariffs would replace Section 122 tariffs, and how Brazil-specific Section 301 tariffs would add incremental pressure. But it leaves several critical questions unanswered.

First, the structural excess capacity investigation could produce tariffs that are stacked on top of existing Section 301 tariffs, Section 232 tariffs, and the baseline HTS rates. The report acknowledges this question but does not resolve it. If stacking occurs, the effective rate on Chinese exports could rise well above 25 percent, and the impact on other targeted economies could be substantial. If stacking is prohibited by statute or by court ruling, the administration may need to choose which tariff authority to prioritize.

Second, the legal risk of the new Section 301 tariffs is unclear. The courts have already demonstrated a willingness to strike down tariffs based on statutory interpretation. While Section 301 gives USTR broad discretion, the forced-labor investigation stretches the statute's historical application. The question is whether the courts will defer to USTR's determination that inadequate forced-labor enforcement constitutes an "unreasonable" trade practice that burdens US commerce. This is a novel legal theory, and its fate is uncertain.

Third, the political sustainability of the exemption structure is an open question. Broad exemptions reduce the effective tariff rate and limit economic disruption, but they also reduce the protective effect for domestic industries. As domestic producers lobby for narrower exemptions, the administration will face pressure to tighten the exemption list. The trajectory of exemptions will determine whether the effective rate converges toward the headline rate or remains significantly below it.

These questions are not academic. They will determine the real economic impact of the tariff regime over the next 12 to 24 months. Investors and strategists should monitor court filings, USTR announcements, and Congressional reactions for signals on these issues.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the New Tariff Regime

For corporate strategists, supply chain planners, and portfolio managers, the evolving US tariff regime requires a structured approach to risk assessment. The following framework is designed to translate the policy analysis into actionable decisions.

First, map your exposure by legal authority. The US tariff regime now has multiple layers: baseline HTS rates, Section 232 tariffs on metals and derivatives, Section 301 tariffs on China, the new forced-labor Section 301 tariffs, and the potential structural excess capacity tariffs. Each layer has different legal foundations, exemption structures, and political dynamics. Companies should calculate their effective tariff rate under each scenario — not just the headline rate.

Second, assess exemption vulnerability. The gap between headline and effective rates is driven by exemptions. Companies currently benefiting from exemptions should assess the political and legal stability of those exemptions. Are they statutory or discretionary? Are they subject to periodic review? Do they depend on specific product classifications that could be reinterpreted? Exemptions that appear stable today could narrow tomorrow.

Third, model the stacking scenario. The most significant risk is that the structural excess capacity tariffs are stacked on top of existing tariffs. Companies with exposure to sectors targeted by multiple authorities — steel, aluminum, chemicals, EVs, semiconductors — should stress-test their supply chains and pricing models under a stacked tariff scenario. This is the tail risk that could produce the largest economic impact.

Fourth, monitor legal developments. The courts are now an active participant in trade policy. Every new tariff authority is likely to face legal challenge. The outcome of these challenges will determine the durability of the regime. Companies should track not just the headline policy announcements, but also the legal arguments and court decisions that follow.

Fifth, prepare for policy sequencing. The administration is moving quickly. The forced-labor Section 301 tariffs will be finalized by July 24. The structural excess capacity investigation could conclude within weeks. The Section 232 derivative tariff structure continues to expand. The pace of change means that companies cannot wait for clarity — they must build flexibility into their planning processes.

## The New Tariff Regime Is More Permanent and More Complex Than the Old One

The US trade policy landscape has entered a new phase. The emergency powers that defined the first wave of tariff escalation have been replaced by statutory trade authorities that are harder to challenge and more permanent in design. The forced-labor Section 301 tariffs represent the first step in this transition, but the structural excess capacity investigation will define the next phase.

For investors, the key insight is that US tariff rates are not falling — they are being restructured. The effective rate may remain near current levels in the near term, but the legal and political foundation is shifting toward a more durable, more complex regime. The exemptions that keep effective rates low are a feature, not a bug — they allow the administration to maintain high headline rates while managing domestic political risk.

The questions that remain unanswered — stacking, legal risk, exemption stability — will determine the real economic impact. But the direction of travel is clear: the US is building a permanent tariff infrastructure that will shape trade flows, supply chains, and investment decisions for years to come.

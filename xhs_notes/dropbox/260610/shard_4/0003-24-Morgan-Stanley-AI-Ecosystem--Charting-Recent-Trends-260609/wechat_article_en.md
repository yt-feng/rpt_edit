# Hyperscaler Capital Intensity Has Already Surpassed the Dot-Com Peak, and the Real Earnings Impact Has Not Yet Arrived

The narrative around artificial intelligence investment has shifted from speculation to a matter of record. The numbers now in public filings tell a story that is more dramatic than any market commentary could capture. Hyperscalers -- the handful of companies driving the global AI buildout -- are now spending more than 40 percent of their sales on capital expenditures. This ratio has already surpassed the peak of the dot-com fiber buildout, which topped out at 32 percent. By 2027, a global investment bank's analysts project that figure will reach 44 percent. This is not a cyclical uptick. It is a structural reallocation of capital that has no precedent in modern corporate history.

The scale of financial commitment extends far beyond what appears on balance sheets as traditional capex. Long-term purchase commitments across the AI ecosystem have reached approximately one trillion dollars. Lease commitments not yet started now exceed $800 billion. These off-balance-sheet obligations represent operating leverage that is not yet visible in standard leverage ratios but will crystallize over time. The question is not whether this spending will happen. It is whether the revenue will materialize fast enough to absorb the coming depreciation charges.

Depreciation is the next margin watch item. The four largest hyperscalers are expected to record more than $520 billion in combined depreciation over the next three years. That figure will compress reported earnings unless sales revise upward or non-depreciation costs decline meaningfully. But there is a timing nuance that many observers miss. Much of the capital currently being deployed sits in Construction in Progress, an asset category that delays the recognition of depreciation. This means the earnings impact of today's spending will hit income statements on a lag. Investors who focus solely on current margin trends are looking in the wrong direction.

The implications for portfolio strategy are clear but uncomfortable. The market is pricing AI infrastructure as if the revenue trajectory is locked in. The data suggests otherwise. The gap between capital commitments and realized revenue is widening, and that gap will eventually demand resolution through either accelerated sales growth or margin compression. The analytical challenge is to determine which scenario is more probable and how the risk is distributed across the ecosystem.

## The Capital Intensity Ratio Has Broken All Historical Benchmarks, and the Trend Is Still Accelerating

The most striking data point in the report is the projected hyperscaler capex-to-sales ratio. In 2026, the figure is expected to reach 36 percent. In 2027, it rises to 44 percent. In 2028, it remains elevated at 42 percent. To put this in context, the dot-com fiber buildout peaked at 32 percent. The current cycle has already exceeded that level and shows no sign of peaking.

What makes this comparison meaningful is not just the magnitude but the composition. During the dot-com era, fiber infrastructure was largely a speculative bet on future demand. The current AI buildout is driven by real, measurable demand from enterprise customers, cloud tenants, and AI-native firms. The report shows that AI-related spending will account for more than 50 percent of all Russell 1000 capex by 2026, with hyperscalers contributing approximately 90 percent of that total. This is not a sector spending spree. It is a concentration of investment into a small group of companies that are placing a coordinated bet on the same technological thesis.

The risk is not that the thesis is wrong. The risk is that the timing of returns does not match the timing of spending. When capital intensity rises this fast, the margin for error in revenue forecasting shrinks dramatically. A 10 percent shortfall in projected sales for a hyperscaler operating at 44 percent capex-to-sales would translate into a direct hit to free cash flow and a likely reassessment of the entire investment cycle.

## Off-Balance-Sheet Commitments Create a Hidden Layer of Operating Leverage That Will Magnify Any Revenue Miss

The headline capex numbers are striking, but they tell only part of the story. The report documents that long-term purchase commitments across the AI ecosystem have reached approximately $1 trillion, while lease commitments not yet started exceed $800 billion. These are contractual obligations that will convert into cash outflows over time, regardless of whether the underlying assets generate the expected returns.

The use of finance leases is particularly important. By financing assets through leases rather than direct purchases, hyperscalers can report lower headline capex while still committing to the same economic outlay. The report notes that finance leases push headline numbers higher when included, meaning the true capital intensity is even greater than the capex-to-sales ratio suggests. This is not an accounting trick. It is a legitimate financing strategy. But it does obscure the full magnitude of the investment from investors who focus only on cash capex.

The consequence is a form of operating leverage that is not captured by traditional metrics. When purchase commitments and lease obligations are added to balance sheet debt, the total leverage of the hyperscaler group becomes substantially higher than standard debt-to-EBITDA ratios would indicate. If revenue growth slows, these fixed commitments will compress margins with little room for adjustment. The companies have essentially pre-committed to a level of spending that assumes a specific revenue trajectory. Any deviation from that trajectory will have amplified consequences.

## Remaining Performance Obligations Have Nearly Tripled, Indicating That Customers Are Locking In Capacity at a Pace That Exceeds Revenue Recognition

The report shows that remaining performance obligations, or RPO, among major AI compute players have nearly tripled over the past year, reaching more than $2 trillion. RPO represents contracted revenue that has not yet been recognized. It is a forward-looking indicator of demand visibility. A tripling in RPO suggests that customers are committing to long-duration contracts at an accelerating rate.

This is the most bullish data point in the report. It indicates that the demand side of the AI ecosystem is not hypothetical. Enterprises, AI startups, and cloud tenants are signing contracts that lock them into multi-year commitments. The revenue is coming. The question is when.

But there is a nuance that warrants caution. RPO growth does not necessarily imply that revenue will grow at the same rate. Contracts can include cancellation clauses, ramp periods, and variable pricing. Moreover, the concentration of RPO among a small number of hyperscalers creates counterparty risk. If one of the major customers faces financial distress or changes its procurement strategy, the impact on the supplier's RPO could be sudden and material. The report does not provide a breakdown of RPO by customer concentration, but the interconnected nature of the ecosystem, as illustrated in the report's flowcharts, suggests that a small number of players account for a disproportionate share of the commitments.

## Depreciation Will Become the Dominant Margin Variable, but Its Impact Is Delayed by Construction in Progress

The most underappreciated dynamic in the AI investment cycle is the role of Construction in Progress. When hyperscalers spend on data centers, servers, and networking equipment, the capital does not immediately hit the income statement as depreciation. It first sits in CIP, an asset category that is not depreciated until the assets are placed into service. This means that the earnings impact of today's spending is deferred.

The report estimates that the four largest hyperscalers will record more than $520 billion in depreciation over the next three years. That is a staggering number. But the actual cash spending that will generate that depreciation has already occurred or is occurring now. The depreciation charge is simply being recognized on a lag. Investors who look at current net income margins and conclude that the investment cycle is manageable are missing the fact that a wave of depreciation is building beneath the surface.

When that wave hits, it will compress reported earnings unless revenue grows fast enough to absorb it. The math is unforgiving. If depreciation increases by $170 billion annually, and revenue grows at 15 percent, the gross margin impact is manageable. But if revenue growth slows to single digits, the margin compression becomes severe. The report does not model this scenario explicitly, but the data suggests it is a risk that deserves serious attention.

## The Report Leaves a Critical Question Unanswered: What Happens If the Revenue Cycle Peaks Before the Depreciation Cycle Does?

The report provides an extraordinary amount of data on the supply side of the AI ecosystem. It documents capital spending, lease commitments, purchase obligations, and RPO growth with impressive granularity. What it does not fully address is the demand-side sustainability.

The implicit assumption in the data is that revenue will continue to grow at a pace that justifies the current level of investment. But that assumption is not tested. The report does not model a scenario in which enterprise AI adoption slows, or in which competition drives down pricing for cloud and AI services, or in which the technology itself evolves in a direction that renders current infrastructure investments less valuable.

These are not hypothetical risks. They are the normal uncertainties of any technology cycle. The dot-com buildout was followed by a period of overcapacity that took years to absorb. The fiber that was laid during that cycle eventually became valuable, but only after a painful period of write-downs and consolidation. The AI buildout may follow a similar pattern, or it may not. The point is that the data in the report makes a strong case for the investment cycle but does not provide a framework for stress-testing it.

This is the analytical gap that investors need to fill. The report gives you the inputs. It is up to you to build the scenario analysis.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the AI Infrastructure Investment Cycle

For investors trying to make sense of this data, the following framework may be useful. It is not a recommendation. It is a way to organize the information into actionable questions.

First, assess the capital intensity trajectory for each hyperscaler individually. The aggregate numbers are useful, but the dispersion matters. Some companies are investing more aggressively relative to their revenue base. Others are more conservative. The risk profile is not uniform.

Second, track the ratio of RPO growth to revenue growth. If RPO is growing faster than revenue, it suggests that the pipeline is building and future revenue visibility is improving. If RPO growth slows while capex remains elevated, it signals a potential mismatch.

Third, model the depreciation curve. Estimate when the current CIP will convert into depreciable assets and calculate the impact on net income under different revenue growth scenarios. This is the single most important analytical exercise for anyone invested in hyperscaler equities.

Fourth, monitor the off-balance-sheet obligations. Lease commitments and purchase orders are not debt, but they function like debt in terms of their claim on future cash flows. A company with $100 billion in debt and $200 billion in lease commitments is more leveraged than its balance sheet suggests.

Fifth, watch for changes in financing structure. The report notes that incremental capex revisions are now accompanied by capital raises and innovative financing. If the capital markets become less accommodating, the pace of investment may slow. That would be a signal that the cycle is maturing.

This framework does not provide a single answer. It provides a method for updating your view as new data arrives. The AI ecosystem is evolving too fast for static analysis. The only defensible approach is a dynamic one.

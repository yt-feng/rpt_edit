# The Semiconductor Equipment Cycle Is Shifting from Euphoria to Execution — And That Changes Everything

The narrative around semiconductor production equipment has quietly pivoted. Six months ago, the dominant emotion was exuberance, fueled by AI-driven demand and memory cycle recovery. Today, after attending the AI Summit in Taipei and Computex, a more nuanced picture emerges: investor interest remains robust, but the tone has shifted from "how high can it go" to "how sustainable is this, and who wins at each layer." This is not a bearish signal. It is a maturation of the cycle.

For the serious investor, the critical question is no longer whether semiconductor equipment spending will grow. The evidence strongly suggests it will. The question is which subsegments will capture disproportionate value as the industry confronts two structural forces: the relentless scaling of AI memory demand and the physical limits of copper interconnect technology. The companies that solve these bottlenecks will command premium valuations. Those that merely ride the wave will not.

This article synthesizes the key strategic insights from recent industry gatherings and translates them into a decision framework for portfolio positioning. It does not summarize every data point. Instead, it makes an argument about where the equipment cycle is heading and why the next 12 to 24 months will separate the leaders from the laggards.

## AI Memory Demand Is No Longer Speculative — Vera CPU Alone Could Absorb Over 10% of Global DRAM Output

The most concrete data point to emerge from recent supply chain checks is the memory demand implied by Vera, a new CPU architecture. According to supply chain analysis, a single Vera CPU can accommodate 1.5TB of LPDDR5X memory — triple the capacity of its predecessor, Grace. At projected production volumes of 2.5 to 4.0 million units, Vera alone would require between 4 and 6 million GB of LPDDR5X bit capacity.

To put that in perspective: that is more than 10% of total global DRAM bit demand from 2025. One product. One architecture. One customer segment. This is not a forecast of gradual growth; it is a step-function increase in memory intensity per compute unit.

The implication for semiconductor production equipment is direct and powerful. DRAM manufacturers will need to add significant wafer fabrication capacity to meet this demand. Based on production at the 1c nanometer node, a rough estimate suggests the equivalent of 100,000 to 200,000 wafer starts per month of additional capacity. That is not a rounding error. That is the kind of demand that drives multiyear capital expenditure cycles.

What makes this particularly significant is the timing. Memory spot prices have recently turned upward again, reinforcing investor conviction that the current cycle has legs. Three months ago, there were legitimate concerns about memory cycle sustainability as spot prices declined. Those fears have receded. The combination of agentic AI expansion and specific product launches like Vera creates a demand floor that extends well into 2027.

The strategic takeaway is straightforward: front-end wafer process equipment names should continue to see major benefits. The memory cycle is not just alive; it is being structurally reshaped by AI workloads that consume exponentially more memory per compute operation.

## The Copper Wall Is Coming — And It Will Reshape the Back-End Equipment Landscape

If the memory story is about volume, the interconnect story is about physics. And physics is about to force a major technological transition.

The concept of the "copper wall" was a central theme at Computex, and it deserves careful attention. For decades, electrical signal transmission via copper has been the workhorse for short-distance connections within data centers — between racks, within racks, and even within semiconductor packages. Copper is reliable and cost-effective. But it has a fundamental limitation: transmission speed and distance are inversely proportional.

At 100G per lane, copper can transmit signals up to 5 meters. At 200G per lane — increasingly common today — that distance falls to 2.5 meters. At 400G per lane, targeted for adoption around 2028, the effective distance shrinks to 1.25 meters. Since standard rack heights are approximately 2 meters, it becomes physically impossible to fully connect racks using copper at 400G signaling.

This is not a theoretical problem. It is a practical bottleneck that will force data center architects to shift from electrical to optical interconnects for rack-to-rack and eventually rack-internal connections. The transition to co-packaged optics is no longer a speculative technology trend. It is an engineering necessity driven by the laws of physics.

For the semiconductor production equipment industry, this creates a new growth vector in back-end processes. Co-packaged optics will require new testing paradigms, new assembly techniques, and new substrate technologies. The testing total addressable market for CPO is projected to reach $100 million in 2026, grow to $300-700 million by 2028, and exceed $1 billion thereafter as CPO is adopted in scaled-up applications.

The companies positioned to benefit span multiple equipment categories. In testing, the demand for wafer insertion testing — both level 1 and level 2 — will increase significantly due to the emphasis on ensuring known-good-die reliability. In assembly, hybrid bonding technology for optical engines will drive demand for precision equipment. In substrates, the placement of switch ASICs and optical engines onto larger boards will increase substrate size and complexity, benefiting exposure equipment manufacturers.

The copper wall is not a distant threat. It is a near-term catalyst that will begin to materialize in production volumes by 2027-2028. The equipment companies that have already invested in CPO-related capabilities will see this transition as a multi-year growth driver.

## Investor Sentiment Has Normalized, Creating a Window for Selective Positioning

One of the most striking observations from recent investor meetings is the shift in sentiment. Compared with the euphoria of late 2025 and early 2026, interest in semiconductor production equipment appears more subdued — but also more informed. This is not a negative signal. It is a sign of market maturation.

The earlier phase of the cycle was characterized by broad-based enthusiasm, where almost any equipment company could attract investor attention. Today, the conversations are more granular. Investors are asking specific questions about guidance, competitive dynamics, and timing of catalysts. They are differentiating between companies that benefit from secular trends and those that are merely cyclical.

This shift creates a strategic opportunity. When sentiment is uniformly bullish, it is difficult to find mispriced assets. When sentiment normalizes, the market begins to distinguish between genuine structural winners and companies that were simply carried along by the tide.

Consider the case of Kokusai Electric. The company has historically been viewed as a NAND stock, but its revenue composition has shifted dramatically. NAND accounted for approximately 40% of equipment sales at its peak in fiscal year 2023, but fell to roughly half that level by fiscal year 2026 as the NAND wafer fabrication equipment market contracted. Today, DRAM dominates at 44% of sales, with logic and foundry contributing another 26%.

The company's guidance for the second half of fiscal year 2027 appears conservative, with an implied 17% sequential decline. Management has acknowledged upside risk, noting that flat sequential growth would lift full-year revenue from ¥280 billion to ¥300 billion. Further upside could come from emerging Chinese customers and additional DRAM demand. With lead times extended to eight months, the second half shipment profile should be largely determined by the time first quarter results are announced, creating a clear catalyst for potential upward revision.

This is precisely the kind of situation where normalized sentiment creates opportunity. The market is pricing in conservative assumptions. If the company delivers upside, the stock should re-rate.

## The Report Raises Two Critical Questions That Remain Unanswered

No research report can answer every question, and the most valuable analysis often lies in identifying what is not yet known. This report leaves two open questions that are worth careful consideration.

First, how will the competitive landscape evolve in co-packaged optics testing? The report notes that the competitive landscape is "still not settled" and that a "100-0 situation" — analogous to the GPU market — is unlikely. But the absence of a dominant player today does not mean one will not emerge. The testing requirements for CPO are complex, and the company that can demonstrate the most reliable, cost-effective solution at scale will capture disproportionate market share. Investors need to monitor not just the growth of the CPO testing TAM, but the relative positioning of the key competitors.

Second, what is the realistic adoption timeline for CPO in hyperscale data centers? The report projects significant TAM growth from 2028 onward, but adoption curves are notoriously difficult to forecast. Will hyperscalers adopt CPO aggressively once the copper wall becomes binding, or will they find workarounds — such as shorter rack configurations or hybrid copper-optical solutions — that delay the transition? The answer will determine whether the CPO equipment opportunity materializes as a sharp inflection or a gradual ramp.

These questions do not invalidate the thesis. They simply highlight the areas where additional due diligence is required before making concentrated bets.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in the Equipment Cycle

For investors looking to translate these insights into portfolio decisions, a structured framework is essential. The following approach organizes the available information into actionable categories.

**Layer 1: Secular Demand Drivers.** The strongest conviction should be reserved for companies exposed to structural demand that is independent of any single product cycle. Memory intensity driven by AI is the clearest example. The Vera CPU data point is powerful, but it is not an isolated event. Every major AI accelerator architecture is moving toward higher memory bandwidth and capacity. Companies with exposure to DRAM wafer fabrication equipment — particularly in deposition and etch — should benefit regardless of which specific product wins in the marketplace.

**Layer 2: Technology Transition Catalysts.** The copper wall creates a forced migration to optical interconnects. This is not a discretionary upgrade; it is a physical necessity. Equipment companies that have developed capabilities in CPO testing, hybrid bonding, and advanced substrate lithography are well positioned. The key is to identify companies where CPO exposure is not fully reflected in current valuations.

**Layer 3: Company-Specific Catalysts.** Within the broader equipment universe, individual companies have idiosyncratic drivers that may not be correlated with the macro cycle. Kokusai Electric's guidance revision potential, Tokyo Electron's pricing power, and Advantest's testing leadership in AI GPUs are examples. These catalysts can provide alpha even in a market that has already priced in the cycle recovery.

**Layer 4: Risk Monitoring.** The primary downside risk is a slowdown in AI semiconductor investment. If hyperscalers reduce their capital expenditure plans, the entire equipment ecosystem would be affected. A secondary risk is competitive displacement — particularly in testing, where market share dynamics can shift rapidly. Investors should monitor both factors and adjust positioning accordingly.

This framework is not a recommendation to buy or sell any specific security. It is a method for organizing the available information into a coherent investment thesis.

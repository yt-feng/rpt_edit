# Why the Best Way to Play Emerging Markets Right Now Is to Stop Funding Them in Dollars

The most important decision for an emerging market investor today is not which EM currency to buy. It is which currency to borrow. After a decade of anchoring EM exposure to the US dollar, the evidence is now clear: the traditional USD-funded approach is leaving returns on the table and exposing portfolios to unnecessary tail risk. The strategic shift toward diversified funding—specifically using a basket of G3 currencies or the Canadian dollar as a single-currency alternative—has produced consistently superior risk-adjusted returns across multiple time horizons, including during periods of USD strength. This is not a tactical hedge. It is a structural improvement to how EM exposure should be constructed.

The macro environment reinforces the urgency. The US economy is outperforming its peers, core rates are repricing, and the debate over further Fed tightening has reignited. This combination is pushing the dollar higher once again. For EM investors, the instinct is often to reduce exposure when the dollar strengthens. That instinct is understandable but increasingly outdated. The fundamentals of the EM asset class—improving fiscal and monetary credibility, attractive carry, and limited global investor positioning—remain intact. The problem is not the asset. It is the liability side of the trade. If the dollar is a headwind, the solution is not to abandon EM. It is to change the funding currency.

This article argues that a diversified funding approach is now the most robust framework for capturing EM returns. It examines why the traditional USD-funded model underperforms, why the Canadian dollar deserves attention as a single-currency alternative, and why a basket approach offers the most consistent results. It also identifies what the data does not yet tell us and translates the findings into a practical decision framework for allocators.

## Funding EM Exposure in Dollars Has Been a Structural Drag on Risk-Adjusted Returns, Not Just a Cyclical One

The most striking finding from the long-run data is not that USD funding underperforms during dollar bull markets. It is that USD funding underperforms across almost all market regimes. Over the full sample from January 2010 to May 2026, a long EM currency position funded in USD delivered an annualized return of just 1.9 percent with volatility of 7.3 percent, producing a Sharpe ratio of 0.26. By contrast, funding the same EM exposure in euros yielded a Sharpe ratio of 0.48. Funding in yen produced 0.49. Funding in Canadian dollars produced 0.58. Every single alternative funding currency improved risk-adjusted returns.

The magnitude of the drawdown reduction is even more telling. The maximum drawdown for USD-funded EM exposure was negative 28.8 percent. For CAD-funded exposure, it was negative 12.3 percent. The worst three-month period for USD funding was negative 15.5 percent. For CAD funding, it was negative 8.2 percent. The worst twelve-month period for USD funding was negative 18.4 percent. For CAD funding, it was negative 8.4 percent. These are not marginal improvements. They represent a fundamentally different risk profile for the same underlying EM exposure.

Critically, this advantage persisted across the five-year and three-year horizons, suggesting the benefit is not simply a consequence of the long USD bull market from 2010 to 2022. Even as the dollar weakened in parts of the recent cycle, the relative performance of non-USD funding remained superior. The implication is structural: the dollar carries a volatility and drawdown penalty that is not compensated by higher returns when funding EM exposure.

## The Canadian Dollar Stands Out as the Most Effective Single-Currency Funder, Offering a Unique Balance of Return and Tail-Risk Protection

Among individual funding currencies, CAD is the outlier. Over the full sample, CAD-funded EM exposure delivered an annualized return of 3.6 percent with volatility of just 6.3 percent. That volatility is the lowest among all single-currency funders. The Sharpe ratio of 0.58 is the highest among individual currencies and competitive with the basket approaches. The maximum drawdown of negative 12.3 percent is the lowest among all single funders by a wide margin.

What makes CAD unique is its correlation structure. The Canadian dollar is a commodity currency, closely tied to oil prices and global trade dynamics. Yet it does not introduce the same left-tail risk as other commodity-linked currencies. AUD-funded EM exposure, for example, produced a Sharpe ratio of 0.45 but suffered a maximum drawdown of negative 18.5 percent and a worst twelve-month return of negative 17.8 percent. CAD, by contrast, kept its worst twelve-month drawdown to negative 8.4 percent. This suggests that CAD offers a more balanced exposure: it improves return relative to USD while avoiding the extreme volatility that makes JPY and CHF funding hazardous during risk-off episodes.

With the exception of the last one-year period, CAD-funded EM exposure has produced higher Sharpe ratios than any other single funding currency across all time frames examined. This consistency is rare in financial markets and suggests a genuine structural advantage rather than a data-mining artifact.

## A Basket Approach Delivers the Most Consistent Results Across Time, Outperforming Even the Best Single Currency in Risk-Adjusted Terms

While CAD is impressive, the basket approaches are more robust. Over the full sample, the equally-weighted basket of all six DM currencies produced a Sharpe ratio of 0.57, a maximum drawdown of negative 12.6 percent, and a hit rate of 53.4 percent. The non-USD basket performed similarly with a Sharpe ratio of 0.59. The G3 basket of USD, EUR, and JPY delivered a Sharpe ratio of 0.54.

The real test is consistency across different market regimes. When the analysis is segmented into one-year, three-year, and five-year windows, the basket approaches maintain their advantage. The G3 basket, for instance, produced a Sharpe ratio of 3.6 in the last one-year period, the highest of any strategy. Over the last five years, it delivered a Sharpe ratio of 1.9, again the highest. This suggests that the basket approach is not dependent on a particular market environment. It works when the dollar is strong. It works when the dollar is weak. It works when volatility is high and when it is low.

The implication is that diversification across funding currencies is not just a hedge. It is a return enhancement. The reason is intuitive: different funding currencies have different correlations with EM currencies and with global risk factors. By combining them, the investor reduces the idiosyncratic risk of any single funding currency while preserving the carry and appreciation potential of the EM side. The cost is higher transaction complexity, but the data suggests the benefit is worth it.

## What the Data Does Not Yet Tell Us: The Open Questions That Demand Further Analysis

The evidence for diversified funding is compelling, but several important questions remain unanswered. First, the analysis does not account for transaction costs. Basket approaches involve multiple currency pairs and rebalancing, which would incur higher costs than a simple USD-funded position. The magnitude of these costs could erode some of the advantage, particularly for smaller or less frequent traders. The report acknowledges this limitation, but it does not quantify the break-even transaction cost at which the basket advantage disappears.

Second, the analysis is backward-looking and covers a period that includes the COVID shock, the commodity super-cycle, and the inflation surge. It is reasonable to ask whether the relationships observed will hold in a regime of persistent US outperformance and higher-for-longer rates. The basket approach has worked during USD strength, but the sample includes only one sustained dollar bull market. A longer or more extreme dollar rally could test the robustness of the finding.

Third, the analysis does not explore the optimal weighting within the basket. The equal-weight assumption is a useful starting point, but it is almost certainly not optimal. A dynamic weighting scheme that adjusts funding currency exposure based on carry, volatility, or correlation signals could potentially improve results further. This is a natural extension for investors with more sophisticated execution capabilities.

Fourth, the analysis focuses on EM currencies as an asset class. It does not examine whether the funding currency choice interacts with specific EM sub-asset classes—rates, credit, or local currency bonds. It is possible that the optimal funding strategy differs depending on whether the investor is taking FX risk, duration risk, or credit risk. The report's findings are a starting point, not a complete solution.

## A Decision Framework for Allocators: How to Choose Your Funding Strategy Based on Your Constraints

For allocators considering this approach, the choice of funding strategy should depend on three factors: execution capability, risk tolerance, and time horizon.

If you have the ability to trade multiple currency pairs and rebalance regularly, the G3 basket is the most robust option. It has delivered the highest Sharpe ratios over the last one, three, and five years. It reduces drawdowns significantly relative to USD funding. It is the closest thing to a free lunch in this analysis. The main cost is operational complexity, but for institutional investors with dedicated FX execution, this is manageable.

If you are constrained to a single funding currency, the Canadian dollar is the best choice. It offers the highest Sharpe ratio among individual currencies, the lowest volatility, and the smallest drawdowns. It is particularly attractive for investors with a long-term horizon, as its advantage is most pronounced over multi-year windows. The risk is that CAD is correlated with oil prices and global trade, so a sustained downturn in commodities could reduce its effectiveness.

If your mandate requires you to benchmark in USD or if your liabilities are dollar-denominated, you may face constraints that make non-USD funding difficult. In that case, the evidence suggests you should at least consider hedging your USD exposure or using a partial basket approach. Even a 50 percent allocation to non-USD funding could meaningfully improve your risk-adjusted returns without deviating too far from your benchmark.

The worst option, based on the data, is to do nothing. The traditional USD-funded EM trade has been a poor risk-adjusted proposition for over a decade. Continuing to fund EM exposure exclusively in dollars is not a neutral decision. It is an active choice to accept lower returns and higher drawdowns.

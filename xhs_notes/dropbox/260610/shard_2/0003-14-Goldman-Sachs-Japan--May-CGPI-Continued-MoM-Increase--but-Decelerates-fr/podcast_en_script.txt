EN_A: Japan just released its latest corporate goods price index report for May. What is the big takeaway?
EN_B: The headline is that domestic CGPI rose 0.9% month-on-month in May, which is a clear deceleration from April's massive 2.8% jump. But on a year-on-year basis, it actually accelerated to 6.3%, the fastest pace since March 2023.
EN_A: So April was just a one-off spike, and things are cooling down? That sounds positive.
EN_B: Exactly. April's 2.8% month-on-month increase was the sharpest since 1980, excluding tax hikes. That was driven by a wave of one-time price revisions by companies. By May, that temporary effect faded, so the monthly number naturally came down.
EN_A: But why did the year-on-year number go up?
EN_B: That is a base effect. Because prices were much lower a year ago, the comparison makes the May number look higher. This is a good reminder that you cannot just look at one metric in isolation. You need both monthly and annual data to get the full picture.
EN_A: Which sectors were the main drivers?
EN_B: Petroleum and coal products were still the biggest contributor, up 3% month-on-month. But that is a huge slowdown from the 11.8% jump in April. Chemicals, and electricity, gas and water, followed a similar pattern. They are still rising, but at a much slower pace.
EN_A: Does that mean the impact from the Middle East situation is fading?
EN_B: Not necessarily. The report says the impact is still there, but the rate of increase moderated. One interesting detail is that naphtha prices were flat in May after surging 83% in April. Since naphtha prices are typically reviewed every three months, the next adjustment could come in July. So this might just be a pause, not a trend reversal.
EN_A: What about import prices? That is directly tied to the weak yen.
EN_B: Yen-based import prices rose 2.7% month-on-month in May, far below April's 8.7%. But the year-on-year figure is still a whopping 25.5%. Petroleum, coal and natural gas were the main drivers. However, ethylene prices, part of chemicals, actually fell 4.1% in May after surging 69% in April. And import prices for food and machinery even turned negative.
EN_A: So import price pressures are easing too?
EN_B: Yes, but it is a partial easing. The core issue is that the weak yen is still a factor, just not as extreme as in April. One question the report does not fully answer is whether a potential naphtha price hike in July could reignite overall price pressures.
EN_A: And what about export prices? A weak yen should be good for exporters.
EN_B: Correct. Yen-based export prices rose only 0.4% month-on-month, but the year-on-year increase was 20.6%. Electrical and electronic equipment stood out, with a 3.7% monthly gain and a 35.6% annual gain. This shows Japanese companies are both benefiting from the weak yen and passing on some costs.
EN_A: So what does this mean for the Bank of Japan? Should investors worry about a policy shift?
EN_B: That is the second big question the report does not fully unpack. While price pressures eased in May, the acceleration in the year-on-year numbers and persistently high import costs mean the BoJ still faces a tough balancing act. The full report goes deeper into how these producer prices will feed into consumer inflation and what that means for monetary policy.

EN_B: My pleasure. If you want to explore those open questions, like the potential impact of the July naphtha repricing or the BoJ's policy path, the full report has the details.

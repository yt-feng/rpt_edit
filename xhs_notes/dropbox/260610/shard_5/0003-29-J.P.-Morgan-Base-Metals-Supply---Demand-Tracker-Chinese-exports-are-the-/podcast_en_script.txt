EN_A: So the latest base metals supply and demand tracker from a global investment bank just came out. What is the big picture here?
EN_B: The big picture is that we are seeing a structural mismatch across metals. For copper, the tightness is being driven by US tariff expectations and a massive inventory restock in China. For aluminium, Chinese exports are actually acting as the relief valve for the rest of the world.
EN_A: So what is the single question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It is really asking whether China is tightening global supply or easing it. And the answer is different for each metal.
EN_A: Let us start with copper. The report mentions a big divergence between apparent consumption and end-use consumption in China. That sounds confusing.
EN_B: It is. In April, China apparent copper consumption jumped 9 percent year on year. But the bank own end-use indicator dropped 4 percent.
EN_A: What does that mean?
EN_B: It means a lot of copper was imported, but it was not actually consumed. It went from exchange warehouses into the supply chain. This is because downstream buyers went on strike in late 2025 and early 2026 when prices were too high. Now they are forced to restock.
EN_A: Can that restocking momentum continue?
EN_B: It has already slowed in recent weeks as copper prices stayed between 13,500 and 14,000 dollars per tonne. The report does not give a clear answer on when this restock ends. That is something to watch.
EN_A: And what about the US tariff review? The deadline is June 30.
EN_B: That is another key variable. The report thinks the Trump administration is more likely to go with a phased tariff starting at 15 percent in 2027, rather than a big one-time hit. The goal is to keep all the copper already imported into the US onshore as a strategic reserve.
EN_A: So what does that mean for global markets?
EN_B: It means the US will keep pulling copper away from the rest of the world for the next few quarters. If China import window also opens, the non-US market gets even tighter.
EN_A: Now let us talk about aluminium. The report title says Chinese exports are the relief valve for non-China tightness.
EN_B: Exactly. LME aluminium inventory is down to 330,000 tonnes, very low. But China visible inventory is still near multi-year highs at about 1.4 million tonnes. The arbitrage for Chinese semi and finished product exports is wide open.
EN_A: So China is exporting a lot to fill the gap?
EN_B: Yes. In the last six weeks, only about 90,000 tonnes have been drawn from Chinese warehouses. But the drawdown accelerated in mid-May. Chinese producers are highly incentivized to run near capacity because aluminium prices are high. April output grew 3.4 percent year on year.
EN_A: But the report also flags a risk from Guinea bauxite supply.
EN_B: Right. China gets 75 percent of its bauxite imports from Guinea. April imports were still up 13 percent year on year. But if Guinea supply gets disrupted, it becomes a serious bottleneck for Chinese production. That could even change China export policy if domestic supply security becomes critical.
EN_A: Does the report give a clear view on that risk?
EN_B: Not yet. It says they are monitoring. That is one of the open questions you need the full report for.
EN_A: What about zinc? The report says Chinese concentrate imports fell, but refined output stayed strong.
EN_B: That is a classic mismatch. April concentrate imports dropped nearly 10 percent year on year because spot treatment charges turned negative. But smelters are still running on inventory built up in the first quarter, so output grew 5 percent.
EN_A: What does that mean?
EN_B: If concentrate markets stay tight, smelters will eventually have to cut production. Meanwhile, demand is very weak. Galvaniser operating rates are low, construction activity is dead. China apparent zinc demand in April was down 12 percent year on year.
EN_A: And nickel? The report says inventory build has stagnated.
EN_B: Yes. LME stocks came down a bit from the February peak, but SHFE stocks kept rising. Indonesia policy framework is squeezing producer margins because ore benchmarks are higher. The market is waiting to see if Indonesia adds more ore quotas this summer.

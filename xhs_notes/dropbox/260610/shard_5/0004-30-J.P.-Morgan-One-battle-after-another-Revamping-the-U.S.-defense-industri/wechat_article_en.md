# The U.S. Defense Industrial Base Must Integrate Speed, Scale, and Software or Risk Losing Its Strategic Edge

The United States remains the world's preeminent military power, but that status is increasingly fragile. The nation's defense industrial base—the network of contractors, suppliers, and manufacturers that produces military hardware and software—has been shaped by three decades of consolidation, cost pressure, and a singular focus on exquisite, high-performance platforms. That model, which served the post-Cold War era well, is now dangerously misaligned with the realities of modern conflict. The wars in Ukraine and the Middle East, combined with the rise of China's Anti-Access/Area-Denial (A2AD) strategy, have exposed a fundamental truth: technological superiority alone no longer guarantees military advantage. What matters now is industrial capacity, cost discipline, speed of deployment, and the ability to iterate software and hardware in real time. The U.S. defense industrial base must undergo a structural transformation that integrates commercial-sector agility, scalable production of attritable systems, and software-defined warfare capabilities. Without this shift, the U.S. risks losing its apex position not because its weapons are inferior, but because its industrial model cannot adapt fast enough.

The current structure is the product of deliberate policy choices dating back to the end of the Cold War. In 1993, the so-called "Last Supper" gathering of defense executives set in motion a wave of mergers that reduced over fifty prime contractors to just five dominant firms by 2000: Lockheed Martin, Boeing, Northrop Grumman, Raytheon, and General Dynamics. These primes now control more than 60% of contract value. The logic was efficiency and cost reduction in an era of shrinking defense budgets. But the unintended consequence was a dramatic narrowing of the supplier base, particularly among Tier 2 and Tier 3 firms, and a procurement system optimized for a handful of flagship programs like the F-35 fighter and Ford-class aircraft carriers. These platforms are extraordinarily capable, but they are slow to develop, expensive to produce, and difficult to scale. In a world where adversaries are deploying low-cost drones, precision-guided munitions, and software-enabled kill chains, the U.S. industrial base is optimized for a war that is no longer being fought.

This is not an argument for abandoning high-end platforms. The exquisite tier of U.S. military capability remains essential for deterrence and power projection. But it must be complemented by a new tier of systems that are scalable, attritable, and software-defined. The challenge is that these two tiers—the exquisite and the expendable—have evolved on parallel tracks, with little integration. The policy impetus for change has already begun, driven by the rise of new defense technology companies and a growing recognition within the Pentagon that the acquisition system must move faster. But execution remains the critical constraint. Without institutional reform, sustained investment in production capacity, and a genuine fusion between the defense and commercial sectors, the U.S. risks falling into a strategic trap: possessing the world's most advanced weapons, but lacking the industrial depth to use them effectively in a protracted conflict.

## The Post-Cold War Consolidation Created Four Compounding Vulnerabilities That Undermine Military Readiness

The consolidation of the defense industry was not an accident; it was an explicit policy response to fiscal pressure and strategic uncertainty. But the resulting structure has created four interconnected vulnerabilities that are now strategically acute.

First, production depth has been dangerously shallow. The thinning of Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers means that a single bottleneck in a specialized component—whether a microchip, a bearing, or a rare-earth magnet—can stall entire programs. During World War II, the U.S. engaged more than 800,000 firms, most of them civilian, in defense production. Aircraft output surged from about 6,000 in 1939 to roughly 237,000 in 1944. That extraordinary surge capacity no longer exists. The supply chain is efficient for peacetime production but brittle under surge demand.

Second, the acquisition system is too slow. Major defense programs now take eight years or more from concept to fielding. In an era where commercial technology cycles are measured in months, this pace is strategically unsustainable. By the time a new platform is deployed, the threat environment has often shifted, and the technology may already be obsolete.

Third, industry concentration stifles competition and innovation. With only five primes controlling the vast majority of contract value, there is limited incentive to challenge existing program structures or pursue radically different approaches. New entrants face enormous barriers to entry, including security clearance requirements, complex procurement regulations, and the sheer scale of capital needed to compete.

Fourth, supply-chain fragility is embedded in the system. Globalized, multi-tiered supply chains are efficient but difficult to de-risk. The U.S. defense industrial base has significant dependencies on China for critical materials, including rare earths and certain semiconductors. In a conflict scenario, these dependencies become strategic vulnerabilities.

These vulnerabilities compound each other. Shallow production depth means that a single supplier disruption can cascade through the entire system. Slow acquisition means that the system cannot respond quickly to new threats. Industry concentration means that there are few alternative sources of supply. And supply-chain fragility means that those sources may be compromised by geopolitical pressure.

## The Nature of Modern Warfare Has Shifted from Platform Superiority to System Integration and Speed

The wars in Ukraine and the Middle East have provided a stark demonstration of how modern warfare is evolving. The most effective systems in these conflicts have not necessarily been the most expensive or technologically advanced. Instead, success has often come from the ability to integrate sensors, data, and weapons into a rapid, adaptive kill chain. Drones, both loitering munitions and reconnaissance platforms, have proven highly effective because they are cheap, expendable, and can be deployed in large numbers. Command-and-control architectures that can process vast amounts of data and translate it into timely decisions have become decisive.

This shift has profound implications for the defense industrial base. The traditional model of developing a single, exquisite platform that dominates a mission area is being replaced by a model that emphasizes system-of-systems integration, software-defined capabilities, and rapid iteration. The ability to "sense, make sense, and act" now depends on processing data at unprecedented speed and translating it into decisions that can be executed in seconds, not hours.

China's Military-Civil Fusion strategy has already embraced this model, integrating commercial technology companies into its defense supply chain and scaling production of drones, missiles, and naval vessels at a rate that the U.S. cannot match. China's shipbuilding capacity alone is orders of magnitude larger than that of the United States. Its missile production is similarly scaled. And its supply chain reach extends globally, including into U.S. defense programs.

This is not a call for the U.S. to abandon its high-end platforms. The F-35, for example, remains a critical asset for air dominance and data fusion. But it must be understood as part of a larger system that includes cheaper, attritable drones that can serve as forward sensors, decoys, or strike platforms. The U.S. needs both the exquisite and the expendable, and it needs them to be integrated into a single operational architecture.

## The U.S. Must Build a Dual-Tier Industrial Model That Integrates Exquisite Platforms with Scalable, Attritable Systems

The most important strategic question facing the U.S. defense industrial base is how to integrate two very different production models. The first model, which dominates today, is optimized for low-volume, high-cost, high-performance platforms. The second model, which is emerging, is optimized for high-volume, low-cost, attritable systems that can be produced at scale and iterated rapidly.

These two models are not inherently in conflict. In fact, they can be complementary. The exquisite platforms provide the sensor and command-and-control backbone that enables the attritable systems to operate effectively. The attritable systems, in turn, provide the mass and persistence that the exquisite platforms lack. But for this complementarity to work, the two tiers must be designed together, not evolved separately.

This requires a fundamental shift in how the Pentagon thinks about procurement. Instead of treating each program as a standalone project, the acquisition system must think in terms of families of systems that span both tiers. That means investing in modular architectures, open standards, and common data links that allow different platforms to communicate and coordinate. It also means creating incentives for primes to incorporate commercial technology and for new entrants to scale production.

The rise of new defense technology companies is a promising development. These firms bring commercial-sector speed, software expertise, and manufacturing agility. Some have already developed production capacity at meaningful scale. But there are genuine questions about whether they can move from innovative prototypes to production at the volumes required for major military operations. The history of defense innovation is littered with companies that could build a better mousetrap but could not build a thousand of them.

Incumbent primes face their own set of challenges. Some are actively resisting change, protecting existing program structures and business models. Others are embracing adaptation, investing in new capabilities and partnerships. The most likely outcome is a mixed picture, with some primes successfully transforming and others slowly fading. The policy challenge is to accelerate the transition without disrupting current readiness.

## Institutional Reform Remains the Critical Bottleneck, and the Report's Framework Raises More Questions Than It Answers

The report identifies the policy impetus for change and outlines a direction of travel, but it is less clear on the specific mechanisms that will overcome institutional inertia. The Pentagon's acquisition system is famously resistant to reform. Budget cycles, congressional oversight, and the entrenched interests of primes and their suppliers create powerful forces for the status quo.

The report highlights several institutional challenges, including the need for faster acquisition cycles, greater use of commercial-off-the-shelf technology, and more flexible contracting models. But it does not fully address the political economy of reform. How do you create incentives for primes to cannibalize their own profitable programs? How do you manage the transition without creating gaps in readiness? How do you ensure that new entrants can navigate the security clearance and regulatory requirements that have historically been barriers to entry?

These are not trivial questions. The report's framework is useful as a strategic direction, but it leaves open the most difficult implementation questions. For example, the report notes that "commercial-first and software-centric policies are gaining momentum, but execution remains a heavy constraint." This is an understatement. Execution is the entire challenge. Without a clear plan for overcoming institutional resistance, the policy impetus may amount to little more than aspirational language in Pentagon strategy documents.

The report also does not fully address the question of cost. Shifting to a dual-tier model that includes scalable, attritable systems will require significant upfront investment in new production capacity. The U.S. defense budget is already under pressure, and the political will to increase spending on production capacity—as opposed to new platforms—is uncertain. The report implicitly assumes that the cost savings from attritable systems will offset the investment, but this is not guaranteed.

## A Decision Framework for Investors and Strategists: Four Questions to Assess the Trajectory of the U.S. Defense Industrial Base

For readers trying to make sense of this transformation, a structured decision framework is essential. The following four questions can help assess whether the U.S. defense industrial base is on the right trajectory.

First, is the acquisition system becoming faster? The most important metric is the time from concept to fielding for major programs. If this cycle time is shrinking, it suggests that institutional reforms are taking hold. If it remains at eight years or more, the system is not adapting.

Second, are new entrants scaling production? The rise of defense tech startups is encouraging, but the critical test is whether they can move from prototypes to production at meaningful volumes. Look for companies that have secured multi-year production contracts, not just development awards.

Third, are incumbents adapting or resisting? The behavior of the five primes will be a leading indicator. Are they investing in new capabilities, forming partnerships with new entrants, and restructuring their business models? Or are they lobbying to protect existing programs and procurement structures?

Fourth, is the supply chain becoming more resilient? The U.S. defense industrial base needs to reduce its dependence on single sources and foreign suppliers, particularly for critical materials. Progress on supply-chain diversification is a positive sign. Continued concentration is a warning.

These questions do not have easy answers. The transformation of the U.S. defense industrial base is a multi-decade project, and the outcome is far from certain. But by tracking these four indicators, investors and strategists can assess whether the system is moving in the right direction or drifting toward crisis.

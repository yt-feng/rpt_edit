EN_A: So gold prices have been really volatile lately. Have you been following Lao Pu Gold? It seems like they raised prices right before gold dropped.
EN_B: Exactly. That is the perfect case study. Lao Pu raised prices by 20 to 30 percent on February 28th, when gold was above 5,000 dollars an ounce. Then gold corrected by more than 20 percent, and now it is sitting around 4,100.
EN_A: That timing could not have been worse. Did sales take an immediate hit?
EN_B: They did. On Tmall, their flagship store sales dropped 63 percent year over year in the second quarter. Compare that to 155 percent growth in the first quarter. The 618 shopping festival was especially tough.
EN_A: 63 percent down. That is brutal. What about offline stores?
EN_B: Offline performed better. New store openings helped, with store count up low teens percent year over year in the first half. But there is a clear divergence between channels. The ones with higher reseller exposure did much worse.
EN_A: So Lao Pu is caught between falling gold prices and falling sales?
EN_B: Not exactly. Here is the twist. Lower gold prices actually improve their gross margin. If they buy inventory at 4,100 dollars an ounce, their gross margin after discount could hit around 50 percent. Their target is only 40 percent.
EN_A: So lower gold prices mean higher profits? That sounds too good to be true.
EN_B: In theory, yes. But it is more complicated. Lao Pu sells at fixed prices, not by weight. Their premium over weight-based products is now huge, sometimes over 100 percent per gram.
EN_A: Can they sustain that premium? Are customers still willing to pay up?
EN_B: That is exactly the core question. And here is what the report really digs into. Lao Pu is quietly testing lower prices, not through direct cuts, but through new products and layered membership discounts.
EN_A: How does that work in practice?
EN_B: For example, their new pure gold products are priced around 2,100 to 2,200 yuan per gram, about 3 percent cheaper than existing items. And during promotions, members can stack an extra 5 percent discount on top of the usual 10 percent, making it a 15 percent total discount.
EN_A: Has that worked?
EN_B: At their upgraded Shanghai Xintiandi store, weekend queues lasted one to two hours. So lower prices clearly drive demand. But here is the dilemma. If they keep lowering prices, does that hurt their luxury brand positioning?
EN_A: That is a real balancing act. You want sales, but you also want to stay premium.
EN_B: Exactly. And the report flags that this needs close watching. For now, Lao Pu still leads in brand buzz, with more social media followers and engagement than other heritage gold brands. But consumer perception is fragile. You cannot push discounts too far.
EN_A: So what about the stock? Has the market already priced in all this pain?
EN_B: The report thinks so. At 7 times 2026 earnings, the weak demand is largely reflected. If gold stabilizes, or even recovers to 4,900 dollars by year end as the report forecasts, then the worst might be behind us.
EN_A: So is this a buying opportunity?
EN_B: The report maintains a Buy rating, but the target price was cut sharply from 1,108 to 650 Hong Kong dollars. The key variable is gold price direction and how consumer sentiment recovers. The report does not give a definitive answer on that.
EN_A: What other questions are left open?
EN_B: For one, how far will Lao Pu go with its new product pricing strategy? Could that trigger a price war with other heritage gold brands? Also, how much reseller inventory is still being digested? Those are questions that need more data.

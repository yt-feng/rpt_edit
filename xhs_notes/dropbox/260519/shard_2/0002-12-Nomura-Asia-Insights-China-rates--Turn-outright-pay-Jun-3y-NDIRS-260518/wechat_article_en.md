# The PBoC’s Quiet Tightening Has Reached a Tipping Point: Why Front-End Rates Are Now the Only Trade That Makes Sense

For months, the prevailing narrative in China rates markets has been one of relentless liquidity abundance. The 7-day repo fixing drifted lower, swap rates failed to rally, and the market seemed to accept ultra-flush conditions as the new normal. That narrative is now dangerously stale. The People’s Bank of China has withdrawn over RMB 1 trillion of medium- to long-term liquidity in May alone through 3-month and 6-month outright reverse repos, bringing year-to-date net injections to just RMB 290 billion—well below the historical average of RMB 1.18 trillion for this point in the year. The market has not yet priced in the implications.

This is not a forecast of a sudden liquidity crunch. It is a recognition that the risk-reward profile has shifted decisively. The asymmetry that favored receiving rates for much of 2026 has inverted. The 30-year China Government Bond yield, which failed to sustain levels below 2.25% even during the peak of liquidity excess, now faces a new headwind from the global rates selloff. Meanwhile, the front end of the curve remains stubbornly complacent. The trade that makes sense today is to pay in 3-year nondeliverable interest rate swaps, and to remove the long 30-year CGB position that served well in the first quarter.

The question is no longer whether liquidity will tighten. It is how quickly money market rates will adjust, and which parts of the curve are most exposed.

## The PBoC Has Already Reversed the Entire First-Quarter Liquidity Injection, Yet the Market Has Not Reacted

The scale of the PBoC’s May drain is not just large; it is historically unprecedented in its speed. In January and February, the central bank injected a net RMB 2.05 trillion of medium- to long-term liquidity via outright reverse repos, medium-term lending facilities, net CGB purchases, and reserve requirement ratio cuts. That was more than triple the 2020-2025 average of RMB 631 billion for the same period. The market correctly identified this as the primary driver of the super-flush conditions that followed.

What the market has been slower to internalize is how aggressively the PBoC has reversed course. In March and April, net withdrawals totaled RMB 760 billion. That brought year-to-date injections down to RMB 1.29 trillion by end-April—still above the historical average. But May changed everything. The RMB 1 trillion net drain through ORRs alone has pushed cumulative injections to just RMB 290 billion, which is now materially below the average of RMB 1.18 trillion for May in the 2020-2025 period.

The implication is straightforward: the absolute level of liquidity in the banking system has dropped significantly. The outstanding open market operations balance has fallen to near zero, meaning the PBoC has exhausted its ability to withdraw short-term liquidity. The focus must now shift entirely to medium- and long-term liquidity conditions, and those conditions are now tighter than they have been at any point this year.

Why has the market not reacted? The answer lies in the lag between liquidity withdrawal and money market rate adjustment. Historically, money market rates have responded to changes in the cumulative liquidity position with a delay of several weeks. The 7-day repo fixing has yet to rebound, and the 1-year negotiable certificate of deposit yield remains anchored. But the overnight repo rate, DR001, has already begun to edge higher, rising from an April average of 1.23% to 1.27% in May. This is the early warning signal that the front end is about to reprice.

## The Historical Pattern of Rate Cuts Suggests the Window for Policy Easing Has Closed, Not Opened

There is a temptation to interpret the recent decline in the 7-day repo fixing relative to the 7-day OMO rate as a precursor to policy rate cuts. The spread turned negative for the first time since May 2023, averaging -3 basis points in May. In previous years, a narrowing or negative spread has often preceded OMO rate cuts, with six of seven cuts between 2022 and 2025 occurring in the second and third quarters.

But correlation is not causation, and the context today is fundamentally different. In prior cycles, the PBoC allowed the repo fixing to drift lower ahead of rate cuts precisely because it intended to ease. Today, the central bank is actively draining liquidity at a record pace. The negative spread is not a signal of forthcoming accommodation; it is a residual effect of the earlier flush conditions that the PBoC is now unwinding.

Furthermore, the economics team does not expect any rate or RRR cuts in 2026. Government bond supply is set to rise in June through September, with actual and planned issuance in May already falling short of expectations due to slow local government bond supply. When supply picks up, the PBoC typically ensures sufficient liquidity in the banking system, but that does not mean it will accommodate lower rates. It means it will manage the technical conditions to avoid a disruptive spike.

The seasonal pattern is instructive. In 2022 through 2025, liquidity tended to tighten from June onward, consistent with the withdrawal of the initial flush and the ramp-up in bond supply. If this pattern holds, the 7-day repo fixing and broader money market rates are more likely to grind higher than to break lower. The downside in the 7-day repo fixing is very limited.

## The 30-Year CGB Yield Has Lost Its Anchor: Global Rates and Domestic Fundamentals Are Now Competing, Not Aligned

The 30-year CGB yield has been the standout performer in China rates this year, rallying even as global yields sold off sharply. In April, while US, European, UK, Japanese, and Korean 30-year yields all rose, the Chinese 30-year yield fell by 10 basis points. This decoupling was justified by a combination of factors: the low probability of a PBoC rate hike, persistent domestic demand weakness, and strong buying from onshore funds.

That decoupling is now under threat. The global selloff has intensified in May, with US 30-year yields rising 19 basis points, Japanese yields rising 36 basis points, and Korean yields rising 39 basis points. The Chinese 30-year yield has risen only 3 basis points so far, but this modest increase masks a more concerning structural shift. The 30-year CGB yield has repeatedly failed to sustain levels below 2.25%, even during the peak of liquidity excess. At 2.2875%, it is now trading at the upper end of its recent range.

The positioning data is equally revealing. Onshore funds, the key driver of CGB yields, have not yet reduced their 30-year holdings in large size, but buying flows have slowed markedly over the past two weeks. Insurance firms have started to add back 30-year CGBs, but only in small amounts. Large banks and securities firms remain the main sellers. The momentum that drove the rally is fading.

The risk is that the 30-year CGB yield begins to develop a higher beta to global rates, especially after its outperformance in April. If the global selloff continues, the 30-year CGB could see a meaningful correction. The long position that made sense when domestic liquidity was super-flush and global rates were stable no longer offers a favorable risk-reward. The decision to exit the long 30-year CGB leg and turn net paid in 3-year NDIRS is a recognition that the axis of risk has rotated from the long end to the front end.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Magnitude and Timing of the Front-End Repricing

The report makes a compelling case that front-end rates are likely to rise, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. First, how much will the 7-day repo fixing rise? The analysis points to limited downside but does not specify a target range for the upside. If the cumulative liquidity position continues to tighten, the repo fixing could move back toward the OMO rate or even above it, but the pace of adjustment remains uncertain.

Second, what is the transmission mechanism from liquidity withdrawal to swap rates? The report recommends paying in 3-year NDIRS, but the relationship between money market rates and swap rates is not mechanical. If the front-end repricing is slow and gradual, the swap market may not react as sharply as the cash market. The conviction level of 3 out of 5 reflects this uncertainty.

Third, how will the interplay between domestic and global factors evolve? The report identifies the global rates selloff as a risk to the 30-year CGB, but it does not fully explore the feedback loop between global rates and domestic liquidity conditions. If global rates continue to rise, the PBoC may face pressure to allow domestic rates to adjust, complicating the liquidity management picture.

These are not weaknesses in the analysis; they are the natural limits of any forward-looking assessment. The reader who wants to understand the exact contours of the repricing will need to monitor the data closely and adjust positions accordingly.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning in China Rates Over the Next Three Months

For investors navigating this environment, the analytical framework should be structured around three questions:

First, what is the trajectory of the PBoC’s medium- to long-term liquidity operations? The May drain was aggressive, but the central bank has not signaled whether this pace will continue. If the PBoC slows the pace of withdrawals in June, the front-end repricing could be postponed. If it maintains or accelerates the pace, the adjustment could be sharp.

Second, what is the path of government bond supply? The May shortfall in local government bond issuance suggests that supply could be concentrated in June through September. If supply comes in above expectations, the PBoC may need to provide offsetting liquidity, which could limit the upside in front-end rates. If supply is moderate, the tightening bias will dominate.

Third, how correlated are Chinese rates with global rates? The report suggests that the 30-year CGB may have a higher beta to global rates going forward, but this is not a mechanical relationship. If the global selloff is driven by inflation fears that are not relevant to China, the decoupling could persist. If it is driven by a broader reassessment of risk premiums, the spillover could be significant.

The trade that emerges from this framework is to position for a gradual tightening in front-end rates while removing exposure to the long end. The 3-year NDIRS pay is a clean expression of this view, with manageable carry and tail risk. The exit from the long 30-year CGB is a risk management decision that acknowledges the changing external environment.

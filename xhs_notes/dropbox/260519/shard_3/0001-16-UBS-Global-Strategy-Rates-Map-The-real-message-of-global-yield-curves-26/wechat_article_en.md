# The End of Central Bank Gradualism Is Reshaping Global Bond Markets Faster Than Most Investors Realize

The global yield curve is sending a message that markets are only beginning to decode: the era of central bank gradualism is over, and its replacement is more volatile, more expensive, and more fragmented than the consensus expects. In May 2026, US 10-year yields crossed 4.60%, breaking above the 4.50% forecast that many strategists held for the second quarter. This was not a fleeting move. It was the culmination of a structural repricing driven by three forces that now operate in concert: central banks abandoning their gradualist playbook, an energy supply shock that refuses to fade, and the return of Japan as a meaningful driver of global rates.

The conventional narrative has focused on inflation persistence and hawkish central bank rhetoric. That framing is incomplete. What is actually happening is a deeper shift in how monetary policy transmits to long-term interest rates. When policymakers move away from what former Fed Chair Ben Bernanke called a "gradualist approach" and toward something closer to a "cold turkey" strategy, the term structure of interest rates changes in ways that many portfolio allocations have not yet accounted for. The result is a bond market that demands higher compensation for duration risk, not just for near-term policy uncertainty.

This is not a uniform repricing. The dispersion across countries is widening, and the differences matter more than the averages. The euro area is facing a larger adverse terms-of-trade shock that limits how hawkish the ECB can become, while the UK remains acutely vulnerable to political risk and energy import costs. Japan has rejoined the global rates peloton, and the Swiss National Bank is now pricing in two hikes by September 2027. The message from globally traded liquid bond markets is consistent at the surface, but the cross-country divergence in economic impact and central bank reaction functions is deeper than current market pricing suggests.

The strategic question for bond investors is no longer whether rates will stay higher for longer. It is whether the current regime of elevated volatility and steepening curves is the new equilibrium, or whether markets are overestimating the durability of this repricing. The answer determines not just portfolio positioning but the broader outlook for risk assets, credit, and fiscal sustainability.

## The Shift from Gradualism to Cold Turkey Is Redefining How Long-Term Rates Behave

The most important intellectual framework for understanding current bond market dynamics comes from Bernanke's own work on monetary policy gradualism. His argument was straightforward: a gradualist approach to policy adjustments increases the central bank's ability to influence long-term rates and thereby shape economic behavior. The logic was that slow, predictable moves allow the transmission mechanism to work through expectations, anchoring long-dated yields even as short rates change.

That framework is now being tested in reverse. When central banks abandon gradualism in favor of a more abrupt approach, the effect is not symmetric. Long-term rates rise more than short-term rates, and volatility increases across the curve. This is precisely what we observed last week: 10-year yields rose even more than 2-year yields, breaking the pattern of short-end underperformance that had dominated since the Iran conflict began. Investors are demanding higher compensation to hold duration risk, and they are doing so in a context where forward guidance has become less reliable as a policy tool.

The implication is significant for portfolio construction. In a gradualist regime, the steepness of the yield curve was largely a function of the expected path of short rates. In the current regime, term premium is reasserting itself as an independent driver of long-end yields. Investors who have been positioning for curve flattening based on the old playbook may need to reconsider. The data supports this: US 5-year real rates, as implied by Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, rose 33 basis points in May alone, reaching 1.55%. This move was driven by repricing of the Fed in line with stronger US data, but the magnitude of the real rate move suggests that term premium, not just policy expectations, is doing the work.

The question that remains open is how far this repricing can go before it becomes self-limiting. Higher long-term rates should eventually restrain economic activity, which would in turn reduce the need for further tightening. But the transmission lag is uncertain, and the energy supply shock complicates the feedback loop. If central banks are forced to choose between fighting inflation and supporting growth, the cold turkey approach may prove unsustainable. Markets are not yet pricing this tension.

## Japan's Return to the Global Rates Peloton Adds a New Layer of Cross-Border Transmission

For years, Japan was the outlier that global bond investors could safely ignore. The Bank of Japan's yield curve control and persistent deflation meant that Japanese government bonds traded in their own universe, largely disconnected from the dynamics driving US Treasuries or European government bonds. That era is over. Japan has rejoined the global rates peloton, and its influence is now material.

Last week provided a vivid demonstration. A total of 29 basis points of Fed hikes is now priced by the March 2027 meeting, but US 10-year yields still rose to 4.60%, crossing the second-quarter forecast of 4.50%. The move was not solely a US story. It was amplified by the repricing of Japanese rates and the resulting cross-border flows. When Japanese yields rise, Japanese investors who had been searching for yield abroad face a different calculus. The carry trade that had been supporting demand for US and European bonds becomes less attractive, and the resulting capital flows can amplify moves in recipient markets.

The mechanism is not well understood by most market participants. It is not simply that Japanese yields are rising in isolation. It is that the correlation structure of global bond markets is shifting as Japan normalizes. For years, the low correlation between Japanese and US rates provided diversification benefits to global bond portfolios. That diversification is eroding. As Japan re-joins the global rates cycle, the co-movement of yields across developed markets is increasing, which means that shocks are transmitted more quickly and with less attenuation.

The strategic implication is that investors can no longer treat Japan as a passive source of demand for US and European bonds. The BOJ's policy normalization, even if gradual, changes the supply-demand dynamics for duration globally. Hedge funds have been positioning for the move higher in US rates, and April's US CPI validated that positioning. But the sustainability of these positions depends on whether the cross-border transmission from Japan continues to amplify the move or whether it eventually becomes a stabilizing force as Japanese yields reach levels that attract domestic buyers.

## The UK Bond Market Remains the Most Vulnerable to the Intersection of Political Risk and Energy Costs

If there is one market where the new regime of higher volatility and steeper curves is most dangerous, it is the United Kingdom. The lesson from the Liz Truss mini-budget of 2022 has not been forgotten by the market, but it may be underpriced in current levels. The report estimates that around 90 basis points of additional term premium was priced into 10-year gilts following that episode. However, at that time the Bank of England was in the middle of a rapid tightening cycle, which was also pushing up long-term rate expectations.

The current environment is different in ways that make the UK more vulnerable, not less. The UK is an energy importer with inflation running above target for a sustained period. The bond market is especially sensitive to higher oil prices and higher US rates, both of which are present today. The report's estimate that renewed fiscal concerns could add roughly 25 to 50 basis points to the term premium at the 10-year tenor from a level of approximately 5% is a reasonable baseline, but the upside tail is larger than the downside.

The key variable that the market may be underestimating is the interaction between fiscal policy and monetary credibility. In the 2022 episode, the BoE was able to intervene to stabilize the gilt market because the shock was clearly political and temporary. In the current environment, the sources of upward pressure on yields are more structural: energy costs, global rate repricing, and the end of central bank gradualism. If fiscal concerns re-emerge, the BoE may have less room to respond without compromising its inflation mandate.

The trading implication is clear: receiving June BoE and avoiding long 10-year UK positions is the prudent stance. The report's advice not to go long UK 10-year bonds is based on the view that political risks had been underpriced. That assessment remains valid. The question that is not fully answered is what catalyst would trigger the next repricing. It could be a fiscal event, a further spike in energy prices, or simply the cumulative effect of higher US rates transmitting through the global term premium. The uncertainty itself is a reason to be cautious.

## The Euro Area Faces a Different Constraint: A Larger Terms-of-Trade Shock Limits How Hawkish the ECB Can Become

The euro area presents a contrasting case that illustrates why the global rates story is not a single narrative. While US yields are being pushed higher by strong data and Fed repricing, the euro area is facing a larger adverse terms-of-trade shock. This asymmetry has direct implications for the path of ECB policy and for the spread between US and German yields.

The report maintains a long position in 10-year bunds with a stop at 3.25%, reflecting the view that the euro area economy cannot sustain the same level of rate increases as the US. The logic is sound: the terms-of-trade shock from higher energy prices hits the euro area harder than the US, which is a net energy exporter. This limits how hawkish the ECB can become without triggering a recession that would make inflation control even more difficult.

The spread between US and German 10-year yields is expected to rise to 150 basis points from the current level of 144. This is a bet on divergence, not convergence. It reflects the view that the US economy can tolerate higher rates while the euro area cannot. The data supports this: US real rates are rising sharply, while euro area real rates have been more subdued. The month-to-date moves show US 5-year real rates up 25 basis points, while euro area 5-year real rates are up only 5 basis points.

The open question is whether the ECB can maintain its hawkish posture without breaking something in the periphery. The report remains neutral on Italy, France, and Spain against Germany, but it would not lean against further tightening in spreads. The European Commission is reviewing the escape clause for fiscal rules, but a repeat of the pandemic-era fiscal support is unlikely. Near-term European government bond issuance could lead to some modest widening of sovereign rates versus swap rates, but the report did not pick up material concerns on funding markets in recent client meetings.

The strategic takeaway is that the euro area offers a relative value opportunity for those who believe the ECB will ultimately be forced to back down. The report's position of receiving July ECB and paying June 2027 Euribor versus receiving June 2028 Euribor is consistent with this view. The market is pricing a more hawkish ECB than the fundamentals justify, and the eventual repricing could be significant.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the New Regime and the Risk of a Policy Error

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several critical questions unresolved. The most important is whether the current regime of higher long-term rates and steepening curves is self-correcting or self-reinforcing. If higher yields eventually slow the economy and reduce inflation, then the current repricing is a temporary adjustment to a new equilibrium. But if higher yields increase the fiscal burden, squeeze private sector credit, and trigger a downturn, then the regime could become destabilizing.

The report does not address the fiscal sustainability question in detail. It notes that the European Commission is reviewing the escape clause for fiscal rules but does not model the implications of sustained higher rates for debt dynamics in the euro area periphery or the UK. This is a meaningful gap. If the term premium rises by 50 basis points across developed markets, the interest cost for highly indebted governments increases significantly. The market may eventually force a fiscal adjustment that itself becomes a source of economic weakness.

Another unresolved question is the role of the energy supply shock. The report provides a timeline of oil pricing but does not fully integrate the energy shock into its central bank reaction function analysis. If energy prices remain elevated, the trade-off between fighting inflation and supporting growth becomes more acute. Central banks may be forced to choose between credibility and stability, and the market is not pricing the probability of a policy error.

Finally, the report does not address the implications of its analysis for credit markets and risk assets. If the bond market is signaling a regime shift, the transmission to equities, credit spreads, and currencies is likely to be significant. Investors who focus solely on rates may miss the broader portfolio implications.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the New Rates Regime

For investors trying to translate the report's analysis into actionable decisions, a structured framework is essential. The following three-step approach can help navigate the current environment.

First, distinguish between countries based on their exposure to the three key drivers: energy imports, fiscal vulnerability, and central bank credibility. The US is relatively insulated on energy but faces the most aggressive Fed repricing. The euro area is vulnerable on energy but has more room for the ECB to pivot. The UK is vulnerable on all three dimensions and should be treated as the highest-risk market. Japan is a wild card that affects all others through cross-border transmission.

Second, assess the shape of the yield curve as a signal of regime stability. In a gradualist regime, the curve flattens as the central bank raises short rates and long rates remain anchored. In the current regime, the curve is steepening because term premium is rising. A sustained steepening is a signal that the market doubts the central bank's ability to control inflation without breaking the economy. If the curve begins to flatten again, it would suggest that the market expects the central bank to succeed or to pivot.

Third, position for divergence rather than convergence. The report's trades reflect this philosophy: long bunds versus short gilts, receiving ECB versus paying Fed, and steepeners in Japan and the UK. These are not bets on a single global narrative. They are bets that the cross-country differences in economic fundamentals and central bank reaction functions will widen before they narrow.

The most important risk to monitor is the interaction between energy prices and fiscal policy. If oil prices spike again, the UK and euro area will face the most pressure. If fiscal concerns re-emerge in either region, the term premium could rise sharply, validating the report's cautious stance on gilts and its neutral posture on European periphery spreads.

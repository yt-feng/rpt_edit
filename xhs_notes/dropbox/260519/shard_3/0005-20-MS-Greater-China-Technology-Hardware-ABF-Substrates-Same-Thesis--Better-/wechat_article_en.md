# The ABF Substrate Thesis Is Not Just Intact -- It Is Accelerating, and the Market Has Not Priced In the Full Gap

The semiconductor supply chain has a habit of confusing a cyclical pause with a structural ceiling. For the past two years, the ABF substrate market has been caught in precisely that misreading. After the post-pandemic correction wiped out the euphoria of 2021-2022, many investors assumed the substrate story had peaked. Capacity was coming online. PC demand had normalized. The shortages were over. That narrative, however, is already outdated. The data now points to a fundamentally different conclusion: the ABF substrate market is entering a structural undersupply that is more severe than previously modeled, driven not by pandemic distortions but by the sustained, multi-year buildout of AI infrastructure.

A recent deep-dive analysis from a leading global investment bank has revised its supply-demand model upward, now projecting a 22 percent undersupply gap by 2030, up from an earlier estimate of 15 percent. This is not a marginal revision. It represents a material shift in the trajectory of pricing, margins, and competitive dynamics across the entire ABF substrate ecosystem. For investors who have been watching from the sidelines, the window for entry is narrowing, not widening. The thesis is the same, but the numbers are better. And the implications extend far beyond the three Taiwanese suppliers that have already rallied sharply this year.

The critical insight is not simply that demand is rising. It is that the composition of demand has changed so dramatically that the old supply-demand models, built around PC cycles and general server upgrades, no longer apply. The ABF substrate market is now an AI-infrastructure market. And that shift has profound consequences for which suppliers win, how pricing behaves, and where the bottlenecks will appear next.

## The Supply-Demand Gap Is Widening Because the Demand Mix Has Fundamentally Shifted Away from PCs Toward AI Infrastructure

The most important structural change in the ABF substrate market over the past decade is not the total volume of substrates shipped. It is the end-market composition. In 2015, PC-related demand for CPUs and GPUs accounted for approximately 70 percent of total ABF substrate value. By 2025, that share had collapsed to roughly 24 percent. By 2030, the analysis projects it will fall below 15 percent. In its place, a new demand cluster has emerged: servers, AI GPUs, AI ASICs, and networking equipment. These four segments combined represented only about 10 percent of the market in 2015. By 2025, they had grown to roughly 60 percent. By 2030, they are expected to account for more than 75 percent of total ABF substrate value.

This is not a gradual evolution. It is a redefinition of the market. The substrate requirements for AI accelerators and high-performance networking are significantly more demanding than those for PC chips. They require larger package sizes, more layers, tighter tolerances, and higher thermal performance. Each AI GPU or ASIC consumes far more substrate area and complexity than a standard PC CPU. As AI workloads shift from training to inference -- the so-called AI diffusion phase that the analysis identifies as Period VII -- the substrate intensity per chip will continue to rise.

The supply-demand model reflects this. The updated forecast shows total ABF substrate demand growing at a 22.2 percent compound annual rate from 2025 through 2030, up from a prior estimate of 17.9 percent. Supply, meanwhile, is expanding at a slower pace, constrained by the long lead times for greenfield capacity additions and the physical limits of debottlenecking existing facilities. The result is a widening gap that becomes most acute in 2029 and 2030, when the undersupply ratio climbs from roughly 15 percent in the old model to 22 percent in the new one.

The so-what is clear: pricing power is shifting decisively from buyers to sellers. The analysis now assumes ABF substrate pricing will grow 15 to 20 percent year-over-year in 2026 and more than 20 percent in 2027, with further increases in 2028 as the imbalance persists. For suppliers with the right capacity and customer relationships, this is not a one-time repricing. It is a multi-year margin expansion cycle.

## Capacity Expansion Is Happening, but It Is Not Keeping Pace, and the Most Efficient Additions Are Coming from Debottlenecking, Not Greenfield Builds

One of the more counterintuitive findings in the updated analysis is that the supply-demand dynamics for 2026 through 2028 remain broadly similar to the prior forecast, despite higher demand assumptions. The reason is that one of the major suppliers, Unimicron, has managed to squeeze out significantly more capacity from its existing facilities than previously expected. Through a combination of utilizing all available floor space and improving production efficiency, the company is on track to add roughly 40 percent more capacity year-over-year by the end of 2026, compared to an earlier plan of only 10 percent growth.

This is a meaningful development, but it also reveals a deeper constraint. The capacity being added is largely debottlenecking -- incremental gains from existing plants rather than new factories. Greenfield capacity expansions typically take two or more years to come online. The industry is still working through the investment wave that began in 2021 and 2022, when capital expenditure jumped from roughly $2.9 billion to $5.5 billion. That wave is now producing output, but the next wave of investment has not yet been committed at scale.

The analysis does not explicitly model a second investment cycle, but the logic is inescapable. If the undersupply gap reaches 22 percent by 2030, the industry will need to begin planning new capacity well before then. The lead times are long, the capital requirements are large, and the technology is becoming more complex. Suppliers that can commit early and execute reliably will be rewarded with long-term customer agreements and pricing premiums.

The risk, of course, is that capacity additions eventually overshoot. That happened after the pandemic, when the industry added capacity just as demand normalized, leading to a two-year down-cycle. But the current demand drivers -- AI training, AI inference, cloud networking, and edge computing -- are not a pandemic spike. They are a structural buildout of computing infrastructure that will span the rest of the decade. The downside risk is real, but the base case is that the industry will remain undersupplied for longer than most expect.

## The Supply Ecosystem Is Broadening, and Smaller Suppliers Are Gaining Access to Premium Customers for the First Time

A second-order implication of the widening shortage is that customers are no longer willing to rely solely on the top-tier suppliers. The analysis notes that end-customers are increasingly allocating demand to smaller scale suppliers and even working with new entrants. This is a significant shift. In a balanced or oversupplied market, customers can afford to be selective. In a tightening market, they prioritize continuity of supply over supplier concentration.

The evidence is specific. Kinsus, a supplier not covered by the analysis, is now working on a broader range of substrates for Nvidia. Zhen Ding Technology, which was previously rated Equal-weight and is now upgraded to Overweight, is pursuing qualification for future TPU substrates in addition to its existing role as a key supplier for China-dedicated AI chips. This broadening of the supply base is not a sign of weakness among the leaders. It is a sign that demand is outstripping the capacity of even the largest suppliers.

For investors, this creates an interesting dynamic. The market has already re-rated Unimicron and NYPCB, both of which have more than doubled since the initial upgrade in February 2026. The upside from current levels, based on the revised price targets, is still substantial -- 49 percent for Unimicron and 62 percent for NYPCB. But the real incremental opportunity may lie in the second-tier suppliers that are now being pulled into the premium segment. Zhen Ding, with a new price target implying 42 percent upside, is the clearest example. The question is whether other suppliers will follow.

The analysis does not explicitly identify which smaller players might benefit next, but the pattern is logical. Qualification cycles for AI substrates are long and demanding. Once a supplier is qualified, the relationship tends to be sticky. The current shortage is accelerating qualification timelines and opening doors that might have remained closed in a normal market. Investors should be watching for announcements of new customer qualifications, capacity expansions, and technology upgrades from suppliers outside the traditional top tier.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Risk of Technology Displacement and the Limits of Debottlenecking

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves several important questions unresolved. The most significant is the risk that alternative packaging technologies could displace ABF substrates in some high-volume applications. The analysis explicitly lists CoWoS -- a chip-on-wafer-on-substrate packaging technology -- as a risk factor, but it does not model the potential impact quantitatively. If CoWoS or similar advanced packaging approaches reduce the substrate area required per chip, the demand forecast could prove too high.

The second unresolved question is the sustainability of debottlenecking. Unimicron's ability to add 40 percent capacity through efficiency gains is impressive, but it raises the question of whether other suppliers can replicate this. If the industry's capacity expansion relies heavily on one company's operational improvements, the supply forecast is vulnerable to execution risk. A single production hiccup or equipment delay could tighten the market further.

The third open question is the role of T-glass, a next-generation substrate material. The analysis notes that T-glass being more constrained than expected is a risk, but it does not provide a detailed assessment of how quickly T-glass capacity can scale or what the cost implications would be. If T-glass becomes a bottleneck for high-end substrates, the pricing dynamics could become even more extreme than the current model projects.

These are not flaws in the analysis. They are the natural limits of any forecast that extends to 2030. The value of the report is not that it predicts the future with certainty. It is that it provides a coherent framework for thinking about the key variables and their interactions. Investors who understand the assumptions can update their own views as new data emerges.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Your Position

For readers trying to translate this analysis into an investment decision, three questions matter most.

First, do you believe the AI infrastructure buildout will continue at its current pace for the next five years? The demand forecast for ABF substrates is effectively a bet on AI compute growth. If you expect AI investment to plateau or decline, the substrate thesis weakens. If you expect it to accelerate, the thesis strengthens. There is no neutral position on this question.

Second, which suppliers have the capacity, technology, and customer relationships to capture the premium segment of the market? The analysis identifies Unimicron and NYPCB as the leaders, with Zhen Ding as an emerging player. But the market is large enough to support multiple winners. The key is to identify suppliers that are not just adding capacity, but adding capacity for the most complex, high-value substrates. Commodity capacity will not command the same pricing premiums.

Third, what is your time horizon? The supply-demand gap widens most sharply in 2029 and 2030. For investors with a two-year horizon, the near-term pricing momentum is the main driver. For investors with a five-year horizon, the structural shortage is the main story. The two time frames imply different risk profiles and different entry points.

The report does not answer these questions for you. It provides the data and the framework. The judgment is yours.

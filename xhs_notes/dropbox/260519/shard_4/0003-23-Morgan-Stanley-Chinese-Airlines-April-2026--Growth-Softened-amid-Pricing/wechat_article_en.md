# China's Aviation Recovery Is No Longer Uniform: The Low-Cost Carrier and International Routes Are Redrawing the Competitive Map

The April 2026 traffic data for Chinese airlines reveals a market that has fundamentally split. Domestic revenue passenger kilometer growth for the Big Three carriers decelerated sharply to between negative 1 percent and positive 3 percent year on year, down from a robust 4 to 12 percent range in the first quarter. This is not a temporary blip. It is the first clear signal that pricing inflation, driven by capacity discipline and rising ticket prices, is beginning to cap volume growth in the country's largest aviation market. Yet while domestic demand softens, two distinct bright spots have emerged: the low-cost carrier model, exemplified by Spring Airlines, and the international recovery, which is proving more resilient and structurally more interesting than most investors anticipated.

The conventional narrative that China's aviation recovery is a rising tide lifting all boats no longer holds. The data from April 2026 shows a market defined by divergence, not convergence. Spring Airlines posted domestic RPK growth of 18.4 percent year on year, a figure that stands in stark contrast to the near-zero growth of the Big Three. On international routes, the story is equally telling: every major carrier except one posted meaningful year-on-year improvements in passenger load factors of 5 to 9 percentage points. This is not merely a recovery from pandemic-era lows. It represents a structural shift in how Chinese carriers are competing and where the next cycle of profitability will be generated.

The implication for investors is straightforward but uncomfortable. The old heuristic of buying the sector on macro recovery no longer works. The winners and losers are being determined by business model, route strategy, and cost structure. Understanding which carriers are positioned to capture the international transit traffic that is increasingly flowing through China, and which are trapped in a domestic pricing war disguised as capacity discipline, is now the central analytical challenge.

## The Low-Cost Carrier Model Is Outperforming Legacy Carriers on Both Growth and Efficiency, and the Gap Is Widening

Spring Airlines' April performance demands attention not because of its headline growth numbers, though they are striking, but because of what they reveal about the underlying economics. The carrier achieved 18.4 percent domestic RPK growth and 17.8 percent international RPK growth, while simultaneously operating at a passenger load factor of 93.0 percent. That load factor is 4.2 percentage points higher than a year ago and is now materially above pre-pandemic levels. For context, China Southern Airlines, the largest carrier by fleet size, saw its domestic load factor decline by 0.9 percentage points year on year to 85.0 percent.

The strategic implication is clear. Spring Airlines is not merely taking share from legacy carriers through aggressive pricing. It is operating at a fundamentally different level of asset utilization. When a low-cost carrier can achieve 93 percent load factors while still growing capacity at 13 percent year on year, it suggests that demand is not the constraint. The constraint is the legacy carriers' inability to match the cost structure and operational flexibility of the LCC model. This is not a cyclical phenomenon. It reflects a permanent shift in consumer preference toward value-oriented travel that has accelerated since the pandemic.

The question investors must ask is whether the Big Three can respond. Air China, which outperformed its legacy peers with 2.7 percent domestic RPK growth and a 2.2 percentage point load factor improvement, is attempting to defend its position through premium service and network advantages. China Southern, by contrast, appears to be losing ground on both volume and efficiency. The data suggests that the middle ground between full-service and low-cost is becoming increasingly difficult to occupy profitably.

## International Demand Is Resilient, But the Source of That Resilience Is Shifting From Chinese Outbound to China as a Transit Hub

The international passenger data for April contains what may be the most underappreciated insight in the entire report. Every major carrier except one posted meaningful year-on-year improvements in international passenger load factors, with Air China and China Eastern showing gains of 8.6 and 5.1 percentage points respectively. These improvements came despite capacity increases, suggesting genuine demand strength rather than mere supply discipline.

The report notes that China is attracting international transit traffic and gaining direct route share. This is a significant structural development. For years, Chinese carriers have been primarily dependent on Chinese outbound travel demand. The April data suggests that a new demand vector is emerging: travelers connecting through Chinese hubs to reach other destinations in Asia and beyond. This shift, if sustained, would fundamentally alter the competitive dynamics of the industry by reducing the seasonality and cyclicality of international revenue.

However, the data also reveals a notable outlier. China Southern's international passenger load factor declined by 0.3 percentage points year on year, even as its international capacity grew by 12.5 percent. This divergence within the Big Three raises an important question. Is China Southern simply adding capacity faster than demand, or does its route network lack the connectivity to capture the transit traffic that is flowing through Beijing and Shanghai? The answer has significant implications for how investors should evaluate the relative positioning of these carriers.

## The Domestic Slowdown Is a Pricing Story, Not a Demand Story, and That Distinction Matters for Profitability

The deceleration in domestic RPK growth from 4 to 12 percent in the first quarter to near zero in April could easily be interpreted as a demand weakness signal. The report attributes the softening in part to pricing inflation, and this explanation is consistent with the data. When capacity growth is constrained and airlines push through fare increases, volume growth naturally moderates. The question is whether this trade-off is net positive or negative for profitability.

The evidence from the load factor data is instructive. Despite the volume slowdown, domestic load factors for Air China and China Eastern improved year on year, while Spring Airlines' load factor remained above 93 percent. This suggests that airlines are successfully maintaining pricing power. The risk, however, is that the pricing environment becomes self-defeating. If fare increases begin to suppress demand more than expected, airlines could find themselves in a situation where lower volumes more than offset higher yields.

The cargo data adds another dimension to this analysis. International revenue freight tonne kilometers grew 19 percent year on year for China Eastern and 4.2 percent for Air China. This indicates that the broader travel and logistics ecosystem remains healthy, even as passenger growth moderates. The question that the report does not fully answer is whether the cargo strength is a temporary post-holiday phenomenon or a sustainable trend that could provide a meaningful revenue buffer for carriers with diversified operations.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Pricing Power and the Risk of Capacity Overshoot

The April data raises two critical questions that the report cannot yet resolve. First, is the pricing inflation that is currently capping domestic volume growth a deliberate strategy by airlines to maximize revenue, or is it an unintended consequence of capacity constraints that will eventually break? The answer depends on whether airlines have the discipline to maintain current capacity levels, or whether the temptation to add seats will eventually overwhelm pricing power.

Second, the international load factor improvements are impressive, but they come against relatively easy comparisons. The report shows that international RPK versus 2019 levels ranges from 8.4 percent for Air China to 33.1 percent for China Eastern on a year-to-date basis. This means that international traffic has not fully recovered to pre-pandemic levels for most carriers. The question is whether the load factor improvements can be maintained as capacity continues to grow and as the comparison base normalizes.

The most intriguing unresolved question concerns China Southern. The carrier's underperformance in both domestic and international load factors, despite being the largest carrier by fleet size, suggests either a structural disadvantage or a strategic misstep. Is China Southern's route network poorly positioned for the current demand environment, or is the carrier making a deliberate choice to prioritize market share over load factor? The answer to this question will determine whether the current underperformance is a buying opportunity or a warning signal.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Four Questions to Determine Which Carriers Are Positioned for the Next Cycle

For investors seeking to translate this data into actionable analysis, four questions provide a useful framework.

First, what is the carrier's exposure to the low-cost segment? The April data makes clear that the LCC model is generating superior growth and efficiency. Carriers with material LCC subsidiaries or the ability to compete on cost will have a structural advantage. Carriers trapped in the legacy full-service model without a clear differentiation strategy will face increasing pressure.

Second, is the carrier's international network positioned to capture transit traffic? The report suggests that China is becoming a transit hub, not just an origin market. Carriers with strong hub connectivity in Beijing and Shanghai, and with partnerships that provide feed traffic, will benefit disproportionately. Carriers with primarily point-to-point international routes will be at a disadvantage.

Third, can the carrier maintain pricing power without destroying volume? The domestic slowdown is a pricing story, but pricing power is not infinite. Carriers that can maintain load factors while growing yields will generate superior returns. Carriers that sacrifice volume for yield, or yield for volume, will underperform.

Fourth, what is the carrier's cargo strategy? The cargo data shows meaningful divergence, with China Eastern posting 19 percent international RFTK growth while China Southern grew only 2.7 percent. In an environment where passenger revenue growth is moderating, cargo can provide a meaningful buffer. Carriers that have invested in cargo capacity and infrastructure will have an additional revenue stream that their peers lack.

Applying this framework to the April data suggests that Spring Airlines is best positioned across all four dimensions, while Air China has advantages in transit traffic and pricing power. China Eastern's strong cargo performance and improving international load factors suggest it may be undervalued. China Southern's across-the-board underperformance raises the most questions.

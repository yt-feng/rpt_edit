# Memory Cost Inflation Is About to Rewrite the Enterprise Hardware Earnings Playbook

The coming earnings season for enterprise hardware and networking companies will deliver a deceptive calm. April-quarter results are likely to land in-line or slightly above consensus, supported by AI demand and a pull-forward in PC and storage purchases as customers rush to lock in prices before memory costs surge further. But this apparent strength masks a structural shift that will define the second half of the year and into fiscal 2027: the sharpest memory cost inflation in a decade is creating a demand-destruction dynamic that most investors have not fully priced in.

A global investment bank report covering HP Inc, Dell Technologies, Everpure, and NetApp reveals a consistent pattern across the sector. The immediate quarter looks manageable. Companies have used strategic pricing, long-term agreements, and product reconfigurations to buffer margins. But the underlying math is unforgiving. DDR contract pricing is expected to rise nearly 60% quarter-over-quarter in calendar Q2, with NAND flash up 40%. These are not marginal increases. They are tectonic shifts in the cost structure of every device that contains memory, which is essentially every enterprise hardware product.

The critical insight from this analysis is that the market is focused on the wrong variable. Investors are watching AI server revenue and aggregate demand. They should instead be watching operating margin trajectories and the second-half guidance revisions that are already baked into the data. The companies that can protect margins through mix shifts and services revenue will separate themselves from those that cannot. The ones that rely on hardware gross margins will face a reckoning.

## The April Quarter Looks Solid, But It Is a Demand Pull-Forward, Not Organic Growth

The April-quarter results across the four companies examined share a common driver: customers are buying now to avoid paying more later. This is not demand creation. It is demand acceleration. When memory prices rise this quickly, procurement teams at enterprises and resellers make rational decisions to front-load purchases. The report estimates that CDW alone saw approximately $100 million in revenue pull-forward during the March quarter across PC, server, and storage. Everpure acknowledged that orders booked late in its fiscal year and a pull-in during its first quarter would account for roughly 8 points of revenue growth, or about $80 million.

This matters because pull-forward creates a statistical illusion of strength. Revenue looks good in the current quarter, but it borrows from future quarters. When the pull-forward exhausts itself, and when the higher prices finally hit end-customer purchase decisions, demand can fall off a cliff. The report explicitly warns about this risk, noting that increased prices from OEMs trying to manage gross margins could lead to disappointing second-half outlooks.

For HP Inc, the report expects in-line PC revenue driven by customers ordering ahead of higher memory costs, alongside strategic actions such as pricing adjustments and product reconfigurations. But it also flags that PCs face the risk of demand destruction from sharply higher average selling prices. The report expects HP to reiterate its full-year EPS guide of $2.90 to $3.20, but with a bias toward the low end. More importantly, investor conversations suggest expectations have already drifted to a $2.70 to $2.80 range. The gap between visible consensus and where the market is actually trading is the first warning signal.

Dell Technologies presents a slightly different picture. Stronger AI server revenue and modest PC upside should drive earnings per share above the company's guidance, with the report estimating approximately $3.05 versus a guide of $2.90 plus or minus 10 cents. But the report makes a crucial distinction: the market is already pricing in a solid beat for the April quarter. What matters for the stock is not the immediate print but the commentary on core demand in the second half, particularly outside of AI-optimized servers. If the core commercial and infrastructure businesses show signs of weakness, the AI server premium will not be enough to carry the narrative.

NetApp looks relatively well-positioned on demand, with storage industry checks showing that demand has held up despite memory cost headwinds. The report expects in-line revenue around $1.9 billion, toward the high end of the company's guidance range. But the critical number is the initial fiscal 2027 EPS guidance. The report expects a range of $8.25 to $8.55, which falls below the Visible Alpha consensus of $8.59. The gap is driven by flattish product gross margins as memory costs compress hardware profitability, partially offset by a favorable mix shift toward public cloud revenue.

Everpure is the most exposed to the memory cost dynamic. The report expects modest revenue upside for the April quarter, including at least $40 million in pull-forward. But product gross margins are expected to come in at 65.5%, reflecting higher memory costs. The real risk is in the second half, where the report sees the company potentially revising its fiscal 2027 gross margin expectations lower. The initial margin trajectory that suggests the first quarter is the trough and margins should improve sequentially may prove too optimistic if memory costs remain elevated.

## Memory Cost Inflation Is Not Cyclical This Time; It Is Structural and Self-Reinforcing

The standard playbook for memory cost cycles is to wait for supply to catch up with demand. But the current cycle has unusual characteristics that make it more dangerous for hardware vendors. The report's data on DDR and NAND pricing shows an acceleration that is without recent precedent. DDR contract pricing is expected to increase nearly 60% quarter-over-quarter in calendar Q2 and 379% year-over-year. NAND flash is expected to rise 40% quarter-over-quarter and 278% year-over-year.

These are not normal cyclical adjustments. They reflect a supply-demand imbalance that has been exacerbated by the AI buildout, which consumes massive amounts of high-bandwidth memory and has diverted production capacity away from traditional DRAM and NAND. The result is that enterprise hardware vendors are competing for a constrained pool of memory components at prices that are rising faster than they can pass through to customers.

The report notes that vendors have tightened pricing quotes from 60-90 days to 30 days or less. This is a clear signal that the environment has shifted from predictable cost management to crisis-mode procurement. When pricing windows shrink, it becomes impossible for hardware companies to give their customers fixed pricing for any meaningful duration. That uncertainty itself becomes a demand headwind, as enterprises delay purchases waiting for price clarity or simply decide that the economics no longer work.

The self-reinforcing nature of this dynamic is what makes it dangerous. Higher memory costs force OEMs to raise prices. Higher prices cause some customers to delay or cancel purchases. Lower volumes then put pressure on fixed cost absorption, which compresses operating margins further. Companies that try to hold the line on pricing to protect market share will see their margins crushed. Companies that raise prices aggressively will lose volume. There is no good outcome in the short term.

## The Companies That Can Decouple Revenue from Memory Cost Exposure Will Win

The report provides enough data to construct a clear framework for evaluating which companies can navigate this environment and which cannot. The key variable is not whether a company has AI exposure. It is whether the company's revenue and margin structure allows it to absorb memory cost inflation without destroying its earnings power.

The first dimension is revenue mix. Companies with high exposure to hardware that contains significant memory content, such as PCs and standard servers, will face the most pressure. Companies with a higher mix of services, software, or public cloud revenue will have natural buffers. NetApp is the clearest example of this dynamic. Its public cloud revenue provides a margin mix benefit that partially offsets the compression in product gross margins. That is why the report expects NetApp to deliver solid growth even as its product margins remain flattish.

The second dimension is pricing power. The report's analysis of HP Inc shows that even with strategic actions such as long-term agreements and product reconfigurations, the company is still expected to see its PC segment margins fall below its long-term guide of 5% to 7%. That suggests that pricing power is limited. When your customers include large enterprises that can delay purchases or switch vendors, passing through a 60% memory cost increase is not feasible.

The third dimension is margin structure within the business. HP Inc benefits from a Printing segment that generates margins above 18%, toward the high end of its long-term guide of 16% to 19%. That high-margin revenue provides a cushion that allows the company to absorb some of the PC margin pressure. But the report makes clear that the PC segment is the focus because of its revenue mix and memory exposure. The printing business can offset some damage, but it cannot eliminate it.

Dell Technologies presents a different challenge. Its AI server revenue is a growth driver, but the report notes that lower AI operating margins limit the EPS upside. High revenue growth with low margins is not a winning formula when the rest of the business is under margin pressure. The market may be giving Dell credit for AI server growth, but the report suggests that the core commercial and infrastructure businesses need to hold up for the stock to work.

## What the Report Leaves Unanswered: The Duration and Elasticity Questions

Every rigorous analysis has boundaries, and this report leaves two critical questions open. The first is duration. The report provides pricing forecasts through calendar Q3 2026, showing NAND pricing continuing to rise but at a decelerating rate. But it does not answer when memory costs will peak and begin to normalize. If memory costs remain elevated for four to six quarters, the demand destruction will compound. If they peak within two quarters and begin to decline, the damage will be contained. The difference between these scenarios is the difference between a cyclical earnings reset and a structural impairment of business models.

The second open question is demand elasticity. The report notes the risk of demand destruction from sharply higher ASPs, but it does not provide a quantitative estimate of how much demand will fall for each percentage point of price increase. This is understandable, because elasticity is notoriously difficult to measure in real time. But it is the central unknown in the earnings outlook. If enterprise customers are relatively price-insensitive because they need to refresh hardware for Windows 11 or AI workloads, the volume impact may be modest. If they are highly price-sensitive and can delay purchases, the volume decline could be severe.

The report's data on storage demand from the VAR survey provides some clues but not a definitive answer. The survey shows strong demand for storage, with 48% of respondents citing strong demand and 44% citing very strong demand. But the survey also shows a decline in demand for Everpure specifically, with only 66% of respondents noting strong or very strong demand, down from 76% in the prior survey. This divergence suggests that some segments are more exposed to price sensitivity than others, but the overall picture remains ambiguous.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask After Each Earnings Call

The report provides the raw material for a structured decision framework. Investors evaluating these companies should focus on three questions after each earnings call, and the answers will determine whether the risk is priced in or not.

First, what is the company's actual memory cost pass-through rate? Companies will report revenue and gross margins, but the key metric is the percentage of memory cost increases that they have been able to pass through to customers in the form of higher prices. If pass-through is above 80%, the margin compression will be manageable. If it is below 60%, the earnings risk is material. This number will not be disclosed explicitly, but it can be inferred from the relationship between reported memory cost increases and gross margin changes.

Second, how much of the current revenue is pull-forward? Companies will report year-over-year growth rates, but investors need to estimate how much of that growth is borrowing from future quarters. The report's estimates for CDW and Everpure provide a methodology. If a company reports 10% revenue growth but 5 points of that is pull-forward, the organic growth is only 5%. That changes the narrative significantly.

Third, what is the trajectory of operating margins, not gross margins? Gross margins will compress because of memory costs. That is inevitable. The question is whether companies can offset that compression through operating expense leverage, mix shifts, or services growth. The report shows that HP Inc's printing margins can partially offset PC margin pressure. NetApp's public cloud mix can partially offset product margin compression. Companies that have these offsets will deliver better earnings outcomes than those that do not.

The companies that can answer these three questions favorably will emerge from this cycle with stronger competitive positions. The ones that cannot will face multiple compression as the market reprices their earnings power downward.

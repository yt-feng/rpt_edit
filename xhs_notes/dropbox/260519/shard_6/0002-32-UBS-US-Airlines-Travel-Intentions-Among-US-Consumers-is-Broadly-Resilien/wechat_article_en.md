# US Consumer Travel Demand Is Not Softening — It Is Restructuring, and That Rewrites the Airline Investment Thesis

The most dangerous assumption in airline investing today is that a slight year-over-year moderation in travel intentions signals the beginning of a cyclical downturn. The 12th wave of a major global travel intentions survey, conducted in March and early April 2026, reveals a far more nuanced reality: US consumer travel demand remains remarkably resilient, with leisure travel intentions holding at 82.8% and business travel intentions declining only modestly from 35.0% to 32.6%. These numbers are not the story of a weakening consumer. They are the story of a consumer who has reprioritized travel, reallocated spending, and begun to value different attributes in the airline product. The investment implication is clear: the market is likely mispricing airlines that have invested in brand equity, loyalty programs, and premium seating, while overestimating the risk to demand from fuel price volatility.

Why now matters is straightforward. The survey was conducted during a period of elevated jet fuel prices and heightened geopolitical uncertainty. If travel demand were truly fragile, we would have seen a sharp pullback. Instead, we saw a 30-basis-point decline in leisure intentions and a 240-basis-point decline in business intentions. Both remain above their March 2024 levels. The pattern is not collapse. It is normalization from a post-pandemic peak, and normalization that is occurring from an extraordinarily high base. For institutional investors, the critical question is not whether demand is falling, but whether the composition of demand is shifting in ways that advantage certain carriers over others.

The evidence from this survey suggests a structural shift is underway. Price remains the most important factor in ticket purchasing, cited by 77% of respondents. But the survey also reveals that airline brand has gained 600 basis points in importance over the past three years, and seat class has gained 900 basis points. These are not marginal changes. They represent a fundamental repricing of what consumers value. The airlines that have spent the last decade building premium products, loyalty ecosystems, and transatlantic networks are the ones best positioned to capture this shift. The airlines that compete primarily on price face a more difficult path.

This article interprets the survey findings through a strategic lens, identifies the key forces reshaping demand, and translates the data into a decision framework for investors. It also highlights what the survey does not tell us — and why those gaps matter.

## The Apparent Moderation in Travel Intentions Conceals a Strengthening of Core Demand

A surface reading of the survey data suggests caution. Leisure travel intentions among US consumers declined from 83.1% to 82.8% year over year. Business travel intentions fell from 35.0% to 32.6%. But these aggregate figures mask a critical internal dynamic: the share of respondents who said they are "very likely" to take a leisure trip actually increased by 20 basis points to 62.1%. The moderation came entirely from the "somewhat likely" category. This is not a consumer who is losing interest in travel. This is a consumer who has moved from tentative planning to committed booking.

The same pattern appears in spending intentions. The share of respondents expecting to increase leisure travel spending by up to 10% rose by 120 basis points to 22%. The share expecting to increase spending by 11% to 40% declined by 150 basis points to 22%. The net effect is a flattening of the spending growth curve, not a reversal. Consumers are still spending more, but the rate of acceleration is moderating. This is consistent with a consumer who has already made travel a budget priority and is now optimizing within that commitment.

The business travel data reinforces this interpretation. The share of respondents planning to spend nothing on business travel fell by 300 basis points to 27%. That is a remarkable improvement from 37% in March 2024. Business travel is not disappearing. It is consolidating among a smaller, more committed base of travelers who are willing to spend. The share planning 11% to 40% spending increases grew to 17%, and the share planning increases of up to 10% rose to 16%. The headline moderation in business travel intentions is driven by a decline in the "somewhat likely" category, not by a collapse in committed travelers.

The strategic takeaway is that demand is not weakening. It is concentrating. The travelers who remain are more committed, more willing to spend, and more discerning about product quality. For airlines, this means the battle is no longer about capturing the broadest possible base of casual travelers. It is about winning the loyalty of a smaller but higher-value core.

## Brand and Seat Class Have Become Decisive Competitive Differentiators, Favoring Network Carriers

The survey's most striking finding is the rapid rise in the importance of airline brand and seat class as factors influencing ticket purchases. Airline brand was cited by 49% of respondents, up 600 basis points from three years ago. Seat class was cited by a similar magnitude of increase, rising 900 basis points over the same period. These are not incremental shifts. They reflect a structural change in how consumers evaluate airline products.

Price remains the dominant factor at 77%, but its share has been stable. The growth is happening in the secondary factors. This suggests that consumers have internalized higher airfares as a permanent feature of the market and are now optimizing for value rather than simply seeking the lowest price. Value, in this context, means a combination of brand trust, service quality, and cabin experience.

The implications for airline strategy are profound. Network carriers with strong loyalty programs, premium cabins, and extensive international route networks are the natural beneficiaries. These airlines have invested heavily in brand differentiation and customer retention. Low-cost carriers that compete primarily on price face a more challenging environment, as the price-sensitive segment of demand is not growing as fast as the value-conscious segment.

The survey also provides specific geographic evidence. Intentions to travel to Europe moderated year over year, declining 600 basis points to 36%. But this remains well above the 26.2% reading from 2019 and is up 350 basis points from two years ago. Transatlantic travel has structurally re-rated. The carriers with the largest and most profitable transatlantic networks are the primary beneficiaries. Similarly, intentions to travel to the Caribbean increased by 470 basis points to 23.4%, highlighting the importance of sun-and-leisure routes.

The pattern is clear: consumers are not just traveling more. They are traveling differently. They are choosing destinations that require longer flights, they are paying more attention to which airline they fly, and they are placing greater value on the in-flight experience. This is a tailwind for airlines that have premium products and strong brand equity.

## The Survey Raises Unanswered Questions About the Durability of AI Adoption in Travel Booking

One of the more intriguing findings from the survey is that approximately 12% of US respondents reported using AI assistants for travel booking. Of those, 44% said they trust these tools "a fair amount," and 17% said they trust them "a great deal." Only 31% said they do not trust them at all or very much.

These numbers are early but significant. They suggest that AI adoption in travel is not a fringe phenomenon. It is a growing channel that could reshape the competitive dynamics of airline distribution. If consumers begin to delegate booking decisions to AI tools, the factors that drive airline selection could change. AI tools may prioritize price, schedule convenience, or loyalty status differently than a human traveler would. They may also be more susceptible to manipulation by airlines that optimize their digital presence for algorithmic discovery.

The survey does not provide enough granularity to assess the impact of AI on airline revenue or market share. It does not reveal which AI tools are being used, whether they are general-purpose assistants or travel-specific platforms, or how their recommendations differ from human decision-making. These are critical gaps. If AI adoption accelerates, the airlines that invest early in AI-compatible distribution strategies could gain a structural advantage. Those that ignore the trend risk being disintermediated.

For investors, the unanswered question is whether AI will become a deflationary force in airline pricing by increasing price transparency, or whether it will become a tool for airlines to drive loyalty by integrating with their own booking platforms. The survey data is a signal to watch this space closely, but it is not yet conclusive.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Interpret Survey Signals Across the Airline Landscape

The survey data can be organized into a three-part framework that helps investors assess which airlines are best positioned for the current demand environment.

The first dimension is demand quality. Investors should look beyond aggregate traffic numbers and focus on the composition of demand. Airlines that serve a higher share of committed, high-spending leisure travelers and recovering business travelers are better positioned than those that depend on casual or price-sensitive demand. The survey shows that committed travelers are growing as a share of the total, and they are willing to spend more on premium products. Airlines with strong loyalty programs and premium cabins are capturing this shift.

The second dimension is brand and product differentiation. The survey shows that brand importance has risen 600 basis points and seat class importance has risen 900 basis points over three years. Airlines that have invested in product quality, cabin segmentation, and customer experience are creating a competitive moat. Airlines that compete primarily on price face increasing pressure as the price-sensitive segment of demand does not grow as fast as the value-conscious segment.

The third dimension is network structure. The survey shows that transatlantic travel intentions, while moderating, remain well above pre-pandemic levels. Caribbean travel intentions are rising. These are network-intensive routes that favor carriers with large international operations. Airlines with strong transatlantic and Caribbean networks are benefiting from structural demand shifts that are not easily replicated by smaller carriers.

Applying this framework, the survey data supports a favorable view of airlines with strong brand equity, premium products, loyalty programs, and extensive international networks. The data is less supportive for airlines that rely primarily on domestic price competition.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Fuel Prices or a Broader Consumer Slowdown Would Change the Trajectory

The survey was conducted during a period of elevated fuel prices, and the report attributes the slight moderation in travel intentions to fuel costs and geopolitical concerns. But the survey does not provide a clear threshold for how much fuel prices would need to rise before demand breaks. It also does not address the possibility of a broader consumer spending slowdown driven by factors outside the travel industry, such as labor market weakness or a credit cycle downturn.

These are not minor omissions. If fuel prices continue to rise, the current equilibrium between demand and pricing could shift. Airlines may be forced to raise fares further, and the 77% of consumers who cite price as the most important factor may begin to trade down or cancel trips. The survey shows that demand is resilient, but resilience has limits. The report does not model those limits.

Similarly, the survey does not address the potential impact of a recession on business travel. Business travel intentions have already moderated, and a recession could accelerate that trend. The survey data shows that committed business travelers are still spending, but the base of committed travelers is small. A broader economic downturn could erode that base quickly.

For investors, these gaps mean that the survey data should be interpreted as a snapshot of a favorable demand environment, not as a guarantee of continued strength. The framework described above helps identify which airlines are best positioned to weather a downturn, but it does not eliminate the risk.

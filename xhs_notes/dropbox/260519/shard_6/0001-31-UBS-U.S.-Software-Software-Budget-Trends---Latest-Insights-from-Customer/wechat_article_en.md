# The Software Sector's Budget Realignment Is Deeper Than AI Disruption — And It Is Already Hitting Results

The most immediate threat to enterprise software companies today is not the medium-term risk of AI-driven obsolescence. It is the near-term reality of a flattening IT budget trajectory, a pronounced shift in spending mix, and an increasingly assertive customer base that is rewriting contract terms in real time. Based on approximately 25 recent conversations with enterprise IT executives and procurement officers, the evidence suggests that the software sector is already absorbing a demand shock that is more structural than cyclical, and that this pressure has likely been embedded in reported results for the past one to two quarters.

Investor debate has been heavily concentrated on how AI might disrupt software business models over a three-to-five-year horizon. That debate is important, but it has obscured a more immediate dynamic: the aggregate IT spending environment is becoming more constrained, and within that envelope, the allocation of dollars is shifting decisively away from traditional SaaS and application software toward AI infrastructure, data enablement, cybersecurity, and cloud compute. The result is a bifurcated market in which a narrow set of vendors are benefiting from robust demand while a much broader set are facing headwinds that go beyond typical enterprise buying cycles.

This is not a recession narrative. It is a reallocation narrative with recession-like consequences for certain segments. The data points from customer conversations are consistent: roughly 80 percent of executives cited stable or slowing IT spending growth for 2026, with the most frequently deprioritized line item being spending on SaaS and application software. The rationale is not that enterprises are cutting IT budgets outright in most cases, but that they are funding AI investments by containing seat growth, limiting add-on module purchases, and squeezing existing vendors on price and contract duration. For software companies whose business models depend on expanding seat counts or upselling modules, this environment represents a structural challenge that will not resolve when macro uncertainty fades.

The argument that follows is not a prediction of a downturn across the entire software sector. It is a framework for understanding which segments are being funded, which are being squeezed, and how the dynamics of customer behavior are shifting in ways that reward infrastructure and data-centric vendors while punishing seat-based application vendors. The implications for portfolio construction and company selection are significant, and they are not fully captured by current valuation dispersion.

## The IT Budget Outlook Has Shifted From Growth to Containment, and the Pressure Is Most Acute for SaaS and Application Software Vendors

The most striking finding from the recent customer conversations is the breadth of caution around IT spending growth. Of the 14 conversations that addressed the overall budget outlook, only three cited healthy growth, while eleven described stable or shrinking budgets. This is not a uniform picture — some large financial services and fintech customers reported budget increases of 10 to 15 percent, driven by AI initiatives and business growth — but the modal response is one of incremental constraint.

The drivers of this caution are worth parsing carefully. Three factors were cited most frequently: the impact of AI investment on the rest of the budget, geopolitical uncertainty affecting customer-facing businesses, and slowing headcount growth. The first of these is the most structurally significant. Enterprises are not simply adding AI spending on top of existing budgets; they are reallocating. One executive described the dynamic explicitly: "AI investments are requiring us to double down on cost reduction strategies in other areas to fund these solutions." Another noted that the uncertainty created by tariffs, fuel prices, and inflation has shifted the planning horizon from long-term cost savings to short-term cost cutting.

The consequence for software vendors is that the environment for selling incremental seats, modules, or upgrades has become markedly more difficult. This is not a temporary pause. The pattern of containing application software spend while funding AI, data, and security has been consistent across multiple quarters of customer conversations. The UBS software team has maintained a cautious view on SaaS and application software for some time, and these checks reinforce that stance. The risk is that this cautious posture, which has already become consensus among investors, may need to persist for longer than anticipated because the underlying budget dynamics are not improving.

What makes this environment particularly challenging is the combination of budget pressure and increased customer assertiveness. Procurement officers are not just saying no to new spending; they are actively renegotiating existing contracts, shortening durations, and pushing back on pricing. The degree of pushback is rising, especially for seat-based applications. This is a structural shift in buyer behavior that will not reverse quickly, even if macro conditions improve.

## The Spending Mix Is Shifting Decisively Toward AI, Data, Security, and Cloud Infrastructure, Leaving Application Software as the Primary Source of Funding

The headline story of the past year in enterprise IT spending is not the absolute level of budgets but the dramatic change in their composition. Across the customer conversations, the pattern is unmistakable: AI is the dominant priority, cited by over 80 percent of executives as a major area of increased investment. Data investments to enable AI, cybersecurity, and cloud infrastructure spending are the next most frequently cited priorities. The deprioritized categories are equally clear: internal headcount growth, external IT staffing and systems integration, and, most consistently, SaaS and application software.

This is not a subtle shift. It is a reallocation of budget dollars at a scale that is reshaping the competitive dynamics of the software industry. The logic is straightforward: enterprises are treating AI as a transformative investment that requires funding, and the most accessible source of that funding is the application software budget. Seat-based applications, in particular, are viewed as discretionary relative to infrastructure, security, and data.

Several customer anecdotes illustrate the mechanism. One executive noted that the biggest gating factor to enabling AI is data quality, which is driving increased investment in data management and data catalog tools. Another described a "hockey stick ramp" in AI adoption since November, accompanied by a massive purchase of Copilot licenses. A third described a deliberate strategy of consolidating spending on a single cloud provider while reducing the long tail of point solution vendors. In each case, the pattern is the same: AI is the catalyst, but the budget adjustments extend far beyond AI itself.

The implication for investors is that the traditional correlation between IT spending growth and software sector revenue growth may be breaking down. Even in an environment where aggregate IT spending is stable or growing modestly, application software vendors can experience revenue pressure if their share of the budget is being reallocated elsewhere. This is precisely what the customer conversations suggest is happening. The vendors that are benefiting are those tied to AI infrastructure, data platforms, security, and cloud compute. The vendors that are being squeezed are those whose revenue depends on seat growth or module expansion within application suites.

## The Transition to Usage-Based Pricing Will Accelerate, but It Will Encounter Significant Customer Resistance and Lengthen Sales Cycles

One of the most important structural trends emerging from the customer conversations is the rapid transition from seat-based to usage-based pricing models. This transition is widely viewed as inevitable, driven by the nature of AI workloads, which are better suited to consumption-based pricing than per-user licensing. However, the customer feedback suggests that this transition will not be smooth. Enterprises are already responding to usage-based pricing by optimizing or cutting seats, implementing firm usage guardrails, and elongating deal negotiations.

The tension is straightforward: vendors want to capture the upside of AI-driven usage growth, while customers want to contain costs and avoid unpredictable bills. One executive described the dynamic as a "push-pull" in which vendors are trying to move to consumption models while customers are pushing back on pricing terms. Another noted that the shift to seat-plus-usage models is creating uncertainty in procurement, leading to shorter contract durations and more frequent renegotiations.

For software companies, this creates a strategic dilemma. The shift to usage-based pricing is necessary to align with how customers want to consume AI capabilities, but it also introduces revenue volatility and lengthens the time to close deals. Companies that manage this transition well — by offering predictable pricing, clear usage caps, and transparent billing — may gain competitive advantage. Companies that attempt to force the transition without addressing customer concerns may face extended sales cycles and increased churn.

The customer conversations suggest that the transition is already affecting deal dynamics. Several executives mentioned that they are taking a "wait and see" approach to AI-related software purchases, preferring to test capabilities before committing to long-term contracts. This cautious posture is likely to persist until pricing models stabilize and customers gain confidence in their ability to forecast usage and costs. For vendors, this means that the near-term revenue impact of the pricing transition may be negative, even if the long-term opportunity remains significant.

## The Report's Framework Raises Unanswered Questions About Durability, Timing, and the Role of Enterprise Platform Consolidation

While the customer conversations provide a clear picture of current budget dynamics, they leave several important questions unanswered. The most significant is whether the current reallocation of spending toward AI is a one-time adjustment or an ongoing structural shift. If enterprises are simply front-loading AI investments and will return to normal application software spending patterns once their initial AI infrastructure is in place, then the pressure on application software vendors may be temporary. If, however, AI investments represent a permanent reallocation of budget share, then the competitive dynamics of the software industry will be fundamentally altered.

The customer conversations provide suggestive but not conclusive evidence on this question. Some executives described AI as a new budget line item that is being added on top of existing spending, while others described it as a reallocation from other categories. The distinction matters, but the data is not granular enough to determine which pattern is more common. Similarly, the conversations do not fully address the role of enterprise platform consolidation. Several executives mentioned a strategy of consolidating spending on large platform vendors like Microsoft, SAP, and Salesforce while reducing the number of point solution providers. This trend, if widespread, would benefit the largest platform vendors at the expense of smaller application companies, but the customer conversations do not provide enough detail to assess its magnitude.

Another open question is the timing of the current budget pressure. The conversations suggest that the pressure has already affected results for the past one to two quarters, but it is unclear whether the environment will improve or deteriorate in the second half of the year. The geopolitical uncertainty cited by many executives is inherently unpredictable, and the impact of tariffs, fuel costs, and inflation on enterprise IT budgets may persist for several more quarters. The report's framework is valuable for identifying the current dynamics, but it does not provide a clear forecast for when the environment might improve.

## A Decision Framework for Navigating the Software Sector: Prioritize Infrastructure and Data Vendors, Be Selective on Application Software, and Monitor Pricing Model Transitions

For investors and corporate strategists, the customer conversations suggest a clear decision framework. The first principle is to prioritize vendors whose revenue is tied to AI infrastructure, data platforms, security, and cloud compute. These categories are receiving the most incremental budget dollars and are likely to continue doing so as enterprises scale their AI investments. The second principle is to be highly selective on application software, favoring vendors with large installed bases, platform consolidation tailwinds, or unique AI capabilities that justify premium pricing. The third principle is to monitor the pricing model transition closely, as companies that manage the shift to usage-based pricing effectively may emerge stronger, while those that struggle may face extended headwinds.

The framework also implies a set of risk factors to watch. For application software vendors, the key risk is continued seat compression and pricing pushback. For infrastructure and data vendors, the key risk is that AI investment enthusiasm moderates or that customers consolidate spending on a smaller number of hyperscaler platforms. For all vendors, the risk of lengthening sales cycles and shorter contract durations is real and may persist for several quarters.

The most important insight from the customer conversations is that the current environment rewards discipline. Vendors that maintain pricing discipline, invest in AI capabilities that differentiate their offerings, and manage the transition to usage-based pricing thoughtfully are likely to outperform. Vendors that rely on seat growth, add-on module sales, or long-term contracts to sustain revenue may face increasing pressure. The budget environment is not uniformly negative, but it is increasingly selective, and the winners and losers are being determined by the quality of their strategic positioning rather than by the overall level of IT spending.

The name is bond (again) - rising bond yields weigh on portfolios

Last week, US inflation prints surprised to the upside while US activity data remained solid. Global bonds sold-off driven by the more reflationary macro backdrop and spillovers from UK and Japan (Exhibit 1). While there is little US macro data this week, there are several speaking engagements with Fed officials, including events with Governors Waller and Barr and Presidents Paulson and Barkin. In Europe there are inflation prints and flash PMIs.

The 2-month correlation between US equities and US 10y yields turned the most negative since the late 1990s (Exhibit 2). The equity market's response to rising bond yields has tended to be closely linked to the source, speed, and starting level of the yield move. Since the start of the Middle East war and the ensuing energy price shock, markets have seen inflation rather than growth as the main source of macro shocks: the relative vol of our PC1 “Global Growth” and PC2 “Monetary policy” factors dropped alongside more negative equity/bond yield correlations (Exhibit 3). Likewise, the equity correlations with front-end inflation pricing (which is directly affected by higher energy prices) have turned sharply negative similarly to that with 10y real rates, while the correlation with forward inflation remains positive

(Exhibit 4). Rising yields due to better growth are usually digested well by equities as growth optimism can help buffer discount rate headwinds, whereas in an inflation-led rate environment the equity/bond yield correlation turns negative. Unlike the past, higher growth expectations due to micro (e.g. AI investment) rather than macro catalysts have supported equity prices since April.

Even when the source is benign, the pace of the rate move can itself become a headwind: historically, equities struggle to digest sharp, disorderly rises in yield. The duration sell-off had been largely orderly until recently, but last week's move — with US 10y yields rising more than 2 standard deviations — is a reminder not to fade right tail risk in rates too early (Exhibit 5). The level of rates plays a role too, albeit a weaker one: historically, US 10y yields above 5% or real yields above 2-2.5% have been associated with more negative equity/bond-year correlations. A more robust indicator is the gap between 10y real rates and LT consensus real GDP growth expectations (now 2.1%) — when positive, it signals tighter monetary policy, which is a more challenging backdrop for equities.

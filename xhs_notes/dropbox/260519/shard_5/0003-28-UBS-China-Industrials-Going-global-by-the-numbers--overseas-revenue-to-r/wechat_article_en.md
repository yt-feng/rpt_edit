# Chinese Companies Are Not Just Exporting Anymore: Overseas Revenue Will Reshape A-Share Profitability by 2030

The most important shift in Chinese equity markets is not a policy pivot or a consumption recovery. It is the structural rebalancing of revenue sources. By 2030, overseas markets will account for 25% of total revenue for non-financial A-share companies, up from 19% in 2025. This is not a marginal trend. It represents a fundamental change in how Chinese industrial companies generate value, compete globally, and deliver returns to shareholders.

This projection, based on bottom-up estimates from analysts covering 17 exposed sub-sectors, carries a powerful implication for gross profit. Under reasonable assumptions, A-share gross profit could increase by 35% by 2030 relative to 2025 levels, driven by faster overseas revenue growth and higher overseas gross margins. The mechanism is straightforward: overseas revenue grows faster than domestic revenue, and it earns higher margins. The combination is a profit multiplier that most investors have not yet priced in.

The going-global theme has been discussed for years, but the data now supports a thesis that is more concrete and more urgent. The overseas revenue share of non-financial A-shares expanded by two percentage points in just one year, from 17% in 2024 to 19% in 2025. That acceleration is not accidental. It reflects a combination of structural competitive advantages, supply chain resilience, and strategic corporate decisions to build production capacity outside China.

The Middle East conflict has added a further catalyst. While the immediate concern has been energy supply, the longer-term effect is global supply chain disruption. Chinese companies, having demonstrated remarkable supply chain resilience during COVID, are now positioned as preferred suppliers in a world that values reliability over cost alone. This is a competitive moat that will persist beyond any single geopolitical event.

## The Overseas Revenue Shift Is Accelerating, and the Gross Profit Impact Is Larger Than the Revenue Numbers Suggest

The headline figure is that overseas revenue will reach 25% of total non-financial A-share revenue by 2030. But the real story is in the gross profit sensitivity. The analysis shows that if overseas revenue contribution reaches 25% by 2030, with stable overseas gross margins and a 5% domestic revenue compound annual growth rate, A-share gross profit could increase by 35% compared to 2025 levels.

This multiplier effect exists because overseas gross margins are structurally higher than domestic margins for most Chinese industrial companies. The domestic market has been characterized by intense price competition, overcapacity, and compressed margins. Overseas markets, by contrast, often allow for premium pricing, especially when Chinese companies offer differentiated products or superior supply chain reliability.

The gap is not just a theoretical construct. In 2025, overseas gross profit already accounted for 21% of total gross profit for non-financial A-shares, up from 18% in 2024. That is a three-percentage-point increase in a single year. If the revenue trajectory holds, the overseas gross profit share will continue to rise faster than the revenue share, because the margin differential is persistent.

The implication for portfolio construction is clear: companies with high and growing overseas revenue exposure, combined with expanding overseas margins, offer a structural earnings growth advantage that is independent of domestic macroeconomic conditions. This is not a cyclical trade. It is a secular shift.

## Export and China+1 Are Complementary Drivers, but the Strategic Emphasis Is Shifting Toward Localized Production

The report identifies two primary drivers of overseas revenue growth: exports and China+1 strategies. Among the 17 sub-sectors analyzed, analysts identify 14 as likely to be primarily export-driven over the next five years. But the more interesting finding is that China+1 is becoming increasingly important, especially for sectors where tariff barriers, local content requirements, or customer proximity matter.

China+1 refers to the strategy of maintaining production in China while building additional capacity in other countries, typically in Southeast Asia, but increasingly in Europe, Africa, and Latin America. This is not about relocating production away from China. It is about adding geographic diversification to serve regional markets more effectively and to hedge against trade policy risks.

The sector-level data reveals a clear pattern. Passenger vehicles, auto parts, power equipment, and new chemical materials are pursuing both export and China+1 strategies simultaneously. The overseas revenue growth for these sectors is coming from two sources: direct exports from China and production at overseas facilities. This dual approach reduces risk and increases flexibility.

The regional breakdown is equally instructive. Europe and ASEAN are identified as the major contributors to overseas revenue growth, which is consistent with monthly export monitor data. But Africa is emerging as the next key growth driver, supported by improving macroeconomic conditions projected for 2026-2027 and favorable commodity prices. For construction machinery and mining equipment companies, Africa represents a frontier market with significant upside.

The strategic implication is that investors should differentiate between companies that are merely exporting and companies that are building genuine global operating footprints. The latter group has higher barriers to entry, more sustainable revenue streams, and greater margin expansion potential.

## Power and Auto Supply Chains Are Leading the Going-Global Charge, with Wind Equipment and New Chemical Materials Posting the Fastest Growth

Not all sectors are going global at the same pace. The report identifies power and auto supply chains as the most aggressive in expanding overseas revenue. Analysts forecast that Chinese wind equipment and new chemical materials companies will post overseas revenue compound annual growth rates exceeding 25% during 2025-2030, accompanied by overseas gross margin expansion.

Wind equipment is a particularly instructive case. The sector is pursuing a China+1 strategy, building production capacity in Europe to serve local renewable energy demand. This is driven by both policy incentives in European markets and the technical competitiveness of Chinese wind turbine manufacturers. The overseas gross margin expansion reflects higher pricing power in European markets and improving capacity utilization at overseas plants.

New chemical materials follow a similar pattern. Companies in this sub-sector are investing in overseas production facilities, particularly in Europe and Asia, to serve local customers in industries like automotive, electronics, and packaging. The gross margin expansion is driven by product differentiation and the ability to command premiums for specialty materials.

The auto supply chain story is broader. Passenger vehicle companies are pursuing both exports and China+1, with particular focus on ASEAN, Europe, and Latin America. Auto parts companies are expanding overseas capacity to serve both Chinese OEMs going global and local automakers. The gross margin expansion in auto parts is linked to order ramp-up and capacity utilization improvements at new overseas plants.

For investors, the key question is not which sectors are going global, but which companies within those sectors have the operational capability to execute overseas expansion profitably. The report provides a list of most-preferred names, but the analytical challenge is to assess execution risk, capital allocation discipline, and the sustainability of margin expansion.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Trade Policy Escalation or Geopolitical Fragmentation Could Alter the Trajectory

No analysis can predict the future with certainty, and this report is no exception. The projections assume a continuation of current trends, but there are two major uncertainties that could alter the trajectory.

The first is trade policy escalation. The report acknowledges that the Middle East conflict could disrupt global supply chains, but it treats this as a tailwind for Chinese companies. A more aggressive scenario would involve targeted tariffs on Chinese goods in key markets like Europe and the United States, or the imposition of local content requirements that limit the ability of Chinese companies to serve those markets through exports. The China+1 strategy mitigates this risk, but only if the overseas production capacity is sufficient to replace export volumes.

The second uncertainty is geopolitical fragmentation. If the global economy splits into competing blocs, Chinese companies may face restrictions on technology transfer, market access, and capital flows. The report assumes that Chinese companies can continue to build overseas capacity and access foreign markets, but this assumption may be tested in a more fragmented world.

The report does not model a downside scenario in which overseas revenue growth slows or margins compress due to these factors. Investors should stress-test their own assumptions about the pace and sustainability of overseas expansion, particularly for companies with high exposure to markets that are politically sensitive.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Evaluate Going-Global Exposure

For investors who want to act on this thesis, the report provides a rich set of data, but the real value lies in applying a structured framework to individual stock analysis. Based on the report's logic, three questions should guide investment decisions.

First, what is the source of overseas revenue growth? Companies that rely solely on exports face higher tariff and logistics risks than companies that are building overseas production capacity. The report distinguishes between export-driven and China+1-driven growth, and investors should favor the latter, especially for sectors where trade barriers are rising.

Second, what is the trend in overseas gross margins? The report shows that most sub-sectors are experiencing overseas margin expansion, driven by higher prices and rising capacity utilization. But the magnitude varies. Sectors like pharmaceuticals, health care equipment, and auto parts are seeing significant margin expansion, while others like commercial vehicles and two-wheelers are seeing flat margins. Investors should focus on companies where margin expansion is driven by sustainable factors like product differentiation, not by temporary factors like favorable exchange rates.

Third, what is the regional exposure? The report identifies Europe, ASEAN, and Africa as key growth regions, but each has different risk profiles. Europe offers high margins but faces regulatory and political risks. ASEAN offers volume growth but may have lower margins. Africa offers frontier growth but carries operational and currency risks. Investors should assess whether a company's regional exposure matches their risk tolerance and return expectations.

These three questions provide a systematic way to evaluate going-global exposure. The report contains the data needed to answer them for specific companies, but the analytical work of applying the framework to individual portfolios remains the responsibility of each investor.

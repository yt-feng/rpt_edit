A $35\%$ relative ROE premium already separates Asia's most AI-ready banking markets from the least. Does this reflect genuine AI foresight? We don't think so. We see this as a reflection of macro resilience and the "safety trade" that has driven Asian banks performance since last year through a period of uncertainty. AI, however, could introduce real optionality. If macro conditions were to shift and preference for safety were to fade, but AI were to deliver on the efficiency gains promise, we believe this can continue to support the outperformers.

# Six structural factors to assess AI readiness

Not all Asian banks are equal in their ability to convert AI investments into efficiency gains. Studying two decades of cost efficiency journeys across 13 markets in close collaboration with the UBS Asia banks team reveals 6 structural factors that matter: Tech Culture, IT Infrastructure, Efficiency Room, Labour Flexibility, Competitive Urgency and Scale. On a scorecard based on these factors, banks in Korea, Singapore, Australia & Thailand emerge as better positioned. Banks in the Philippines, India (PSUs in particular), Taiwan and Japan face more structural headwinds.

# AI premium - Prescient Call or a Beautiful Coincidence?

A 35% relative ROE premium separating Asia's top five AI ready banking markets from bottom five might sound like a bold and prescient market call on AI efficiency. Using a narrower lens makes this even more intriguing. For the most AI-ready banks in the top 5 markets versus the least AI-ready banks in the bottom 5, the valuation premium widens to 40%. Is the market making a prescient AI call at the market level as well as at the stock level? We don't think so. This is not a prescient AI call. This is more of a beautiful coincidence. The same markets sit atop both AI readiness and macro resilience framework ("safety trade" playbook) i.e. banking markets with high dividend yields, FX stability and defensive characteristics that have attracted flows since last year and outperformed given the preference for safety among investors in a period of heightened uncertainty. AI however could introduce a real optionality. Even if the macro shifts and preference for safety were to fade, AI optionality can provide a reason to hold on to the winners.

# Efficiency gains only partially offset revenue pressure

Asian banks have faced structural revenue headwinds driven by demographics-led pressure on net interest income (NII) and persistent challenges in scaling fee income over the last two decades.

While operating cost discipline over the last two decades has been meaningful, it has not been sufficient to fully offset this revenue erosion. Evidence of higher technology investments by the banks resulting in clear efficiency gains is also at best mixed. This raises the question whether the ongoing and future AI investments will yield any different results? Can AI help unlock the next leg of sustainable efficiency gains for Asian Banks?

Structural pressures like demographics have led to a 45bps decline in overall net interest income to assets for Asian Banks. Efforts to pivot to fee income have only delivered limited relief. Regulatory constraints, structural market features and competitive intensity made it challenging to scale fee income, resulting in an overall decline of 15bps in fee income to assets for Asian Banks in aggregate. Overall revenue to asset fell by close to 60bps for the sector in the last 20 years. We discussed these in greater detail and the varying journeys of Asian banking markets in two of earlier reports -

- Q-Series "Silver wave: How an ageing population is reshaping Asian..."   
- APAC Focus: Asian Banks "The Pivot to Non-interest Income – how...".

Against this backdrop, banks in the region leaned heavily on cost discipline to protect profitability. Operating efficiency improved steadily over the past two decades, with total operating expenses to assets declining \~40bps over the last 20y on average for Asian Banks, but only partially offsetting the revenue erosion. Please see our companion report for a more detailed discussion on these trends (After two decades of cost discipline, what's next for Asian banks?).

This trend of non-staff expense reduction being more important is even more pronounced in DM markets in Asia than EM. Branch rationalisation, payment infrastructure modernisation and technology consolidation were likely the main drivers of non-staff cost reduction.

These may or may not come with equivalent staff costs benefits, which due to less labour flexibility can be difficult to achieve in many Asian markets. Significant branch reductions (2012-2022) and launch of real time rails in Korea (early 2000s), Big Four consolidation and retirement of the state-based data centers (through 2000s) in Australia, KBank and SCB consolidation (2016-17 onwards) and PromptPay launch in Thailand (2016), India's UPI (2016), Singapore's IFAST (2014) are a few examples.

# P&L impact of these initiatives might be limited

- Investments in customer personalization are best viewed as defensive rather than revenue accretive. While improved engagement and cross-sell are often cited as benefits, the more tangible outcome is the prevention of customer attrition and share loss. As personalization becomes ubiquitous, its absence is more likely to result in revenue leakage than its presence driving incremental topline growth.   
- Productivity-oriented use cases, particularly in software development, operations, and document processing, offer clearer scope for cost efficiencies in the medium term, although quantification remains challenging at this stage. Some banks even refer to AI as "Augmented intelligence" suggesting limited scope for headcount reduction which continues to be politically challenging in many Asian markets. Gains are more likely to accrue gradually through headcount avoidance, faster turnaround times, and reduced error rates rather than explicit cost reduction. As such, near-term margin uplift may be limited. Benefits might manifest more as a structural hedge against cost inflation over the medium term.   
- Lastly, AI investments in fraud detection and risk analytics have a more direct bearing on earnings stability rather than earnings expansion. While improved data ingestion and real-time analytics could modestly enhance credit underwriting outcomes, the primary benefit lies in mitigating downside risks – lower fraud losses, earlier identification of asset quality deterioration, and reduced tail-risk events. In this sense, these capabilities help contain potential future costs that could otherwise materialise in the absence of such investments, supporting more resilient returns through the cycle.

While localized efficiency gains can be significant – such as reducing lending decision times or automating customer messaging – these are not yet headcount-reduction stories in most cases. They are primarily capacity-release (same headcount, higher throughput) and cost-avoidance (not hiring) stories, which likely gets reflected in cost/income overtime gradually rather than immediate opex cuts. Specifically see the

comment from HSBC above.

There is also a risk that just like technology investments in the past, any resulting cost savings today will be reinvested back in additional capital expenditure required for larger data infrastructure, technological upgrades, cybersecurity, and compliance to remain competitive against peers and digital-native disruptors, further diminishing the P&L impact.

# Medium-term: From implementation to costs savings

Nevertheless, there remains potential for AI to drive material cost savings for banks in the medium term given the large amount of data and human capital intensive nature of the business. Furthermore, based on the findings from McKinsey (report here), operations can represent \~60–70% of a bank's cost base, making operational AI the biggest cost lever for banks if scaled end-to-end.

ICBC in China has said that they have reached a milestone where the workload handled by AI is equivalent to more than 40,000 full-time employees, illustrating the massive scale of operational displacement possible.

Furthermore, as banks move past the initial high capex phase of building data infrastructure, these "digital workers" can scale with very low marginal cost, eventually potentially allowing banks to scale their revenues without a linear increase in headcount or physical infrastructure.

# AI Readiness Scorecard For Asian Banks

As will become clear from the detailed discussion below there were six factors that were critical determinants of whether a banking sector was able to deliver efficiency gains successfully. These we believe will also determine the track record for AI related efficiency gains in future. These factors form the basis of the AI readiness scorecard that we present for Asian banking markets below –

- Tech Culture – This captures whether banks approach technology as a genuine strategic tool for productivity improvement or more as a compliance and capability exercise. Markets where technology investment is held accountable to cost-out targets – rather than feature delivery milestones – are likely to show better efficiency outcomes.   
- Competitive Urgency reflects the degree to which banks face an existential market threat that forces genuine follow-through on efficiency commitments. Technology investments without competitive pressure tends to be additive rather than substitutive   
- Efficiency Room measures the quantum of addressable opex inefficiency remaining in the system. High scores here reflect currently elevated cost ratios relative to peers.   
- Labour Market Flexibility is also an important precondition for AI-driven opex improvement. AI can automate processes, but efficiency ratios can only improve if the headcount those processes required is actually reduced or not replaced. In certain markets cultural norms, union structures, political economy, or state ownership objectives can prevent this.   
- IT Systems and Data Infrastructure determines whether AI can be deployed effectively in the first place. AI tools require modern API-accessible core systems, clean and well-governed data, and also high quality national-level data – digital identity, credit bureaus, open banking frameworks – to generate meaningful outputs. Markets burdened by old legacy systems might need to upgrade before AI investment delivers returns; those with modern infrastructure can deploy immediately and at lower integration cost.   
- Scale reflects the reality that meaningful AI investment is capital-intensive and subject to significant economies of scale – the costs can only be justified above a certain asset threshold. In our framework, this doesn't play a big role since almost all banks that we consider appear to have the scale that might be needed to make these investments, making the other five factors more critical.

Disclaimer: While based on the past experience and backed by quantitative evidence in each market, these views are inherently subjective and only reflect one analytical perspective. These are by no means a definitive assessment. Other observers might rank the markets differently. The framework is largely intended as a structured framework for comparative thinking, not necessarily a definitive forecast of AI outcomes across markets.

# Japan

- Total opex / assets for the megabanks sits towards the top of DM group just below the Asian average. There has been little improvement over the last 15y or so. This reflects the structural challenges in the market.   
- Staff costs are paradoxically the lowest in the DM group but again like overall costs, and unlike most other Asian markets, have not shown any meaningful improvement over the decades. The level appears to be affected by the large asset base and sheer size of the Japanese banks that compresses the ratio. Lifetime employment norms, powerful union cultures and deep social expectations add to the challenge in driving efficiencies through headcount reduction.   
- Non-staff costs remain elevated and likely reflect the legacy IT maintenance burden. The three mega banks have announced ambitious technology transformation programs – MUFG committing Y1tn in tech investments over 2021-25, SMFG partnering with SBI Holdings for digital distribution, and Mizuho publishing a standalone Digital Transformation Report following the FSA's 2021 administrative action – all signal that management intent is genuine and regulatory pressure is real. The challenge remains execution and speed.   
- Figure 33 highlights the restructuring challenge in Japan. 33 branches per 100k adults – the highest density among all 13 Asian markets – and a line that has remained flat for decades. On the contrary every other DM market has seen branch rationalisation, with Australia leading in the last decade. Japan's branch network has not changed despite the mounting revenue pressure building up over the years (low interest rates, demographics).

# What drives the scores for Japan banks (1=highest, 5=lowest):

- Tech culture – a score of 3 for the mega banks in aggregate given the recently announced ambitious technology plans which all suggest genuine intent. FSA is also pushing hard for organisational change but the progress has been slow. Mega-banks (MUFG, SMFG, Mizuho) are all making progress albeit from a low base.   
- Competitive urgency – a score of 4 alongside Taiwan. Just like in Taiwan, significantly less neobank disruption threat due to an aging demographic. Lifetime employment norms reduce internal urgency within organisations. Only Asian market where loans per branch has continued to decline (Figure 34).   
- Efficiency room – scores 2 alongside Indonesia and Taiwan. High opex to assets ratio suggest room for inefficiency to come down especially on non-staff costs driven by legacy IT maintenance overhead. Less room than India and Philippines where both cost lines are simultaneously elevated. See Figure 25 above.   
- Labour flexibility – a score of 4 alongside India and China. Lifetime employment norms, powerful internal union cultures, and deep social expectations introduce some rigidity in the labour market. In China, state direction creates a constraint, in Japan it appears to be more cultural and contractual. Natural attrition over the next decade is one pathway. See Figure 35 above.   
- IT & data infra - a score of 4 given legacy IT burden. COBOL expertise remains valued over cloud engineering at major institutions. See Figure 29.

# Australia

- One of the clearest downward trends in the DM group – total opex / assets declining steadily over the years – clear evidence of the restructuring journey over the years. The overall ratio and staff costs are now converging with Singapore declining from a much higher level suggesting genuine structural transformation. Sharpest downward trajectory for branch per capita from 30+ to 15 among the DM group reflecting sustained restructuring programs without political bottlenecks.   
- Both staff and non-staff lines have declined with the latter showing a sharper fall reflecting the data center consolidation and branch rationalisation in the 2000s.   
- The heavily unionised nature of workforce and over branched nature of banking in Australia changed after the crisis in the early 90s. Several banks faced heavy losses and had to transform. In some ways similar to Korea, Australian banks had to focus on cost discipline driven by the crisis. This also likely allowed for more political permission for restructuring.   
- Worth mentioning here are the two landmark financial inquiries: the Campbell Committee reforms of the early 1980s and the Wallis Inquiry of the late 1990s. Both aimed to inject competition, market discipline, and modern regulation into a previously tightly controlled system, thereby improving banking efficiency over the long term.   
- The unique mortgage brokerage channel, superannuation driven shareholder discipline are also factors unique to Australian banks that help gradually improving efficiency ratio in recent years.

# What drives the scores for Australia banks (1=highest, 5=lowest):

- Tech culture – scores 2 alongside China and Thailand. CBA is a genuine global AI leader. The Royal Commission drove process standardisation and enabled automation. Scores below Korea and Singapore because tech culture appears to be more concentrated at the Big Four.   
- Competitive urgency – scores 2 alongside Singapore and Thailand. Neobank competition is emerging. The mortgage broker channel – intermediating almost two-thirds of new originations – creates permanent and structural margin pressure that forces cost discipline. # China

- Opex / assets has fallen sharply and sits at the lowest level among all Asian markets. This is true for both staff and non-staff costs which both are the lowest in the region.   
- This is partly a reflection how SOE banks compensation is managed. Since 2015, executive and senior staff pay has been effectively capped as part of the SOE reform agenda, as well as front line wage growth has been tightly managed. This contrasts with India where there are bipartite wage settlements and these are negotiated with unions with often politically generous outcomes.   
- On non-staff opex, there are specific reasons why Chinese banks are able to maintain one of the lowest level - a lot of the digital infrastructure was built with state subsidy or shared cost arrangements, and individual banks didn't have to bear the full investment cost the way they had to in other markets (like Japan or Taiwan).   
- Banks also effectively outsource the customer facing digital layer to fintechs (like WeChat or Alipay) avoiding the significant UX / digital buildout cost that banks in Malaysia and Taiwan have to bear themselves.

# Singapore

- Total opex to assets sits at middle of the pack in the DM group and broadly stable level. Staff costs tend to be higher than the average DM peer in one of the highest cost of living and expensive cities in the world. Non-staff costs have remained quite consistently low and among the lowest in Asia.   
- Both staff and non-staff costs have nudged a bit higher in the recent years, despite the rhetoric of ongoing tech investments, reflecting the lack of evidence that tech investments have resulted in meaningful reduction in opex intensity. Staff costs increase is likely a result of the ongoing WM push given the strong inflow of capital and heating competitive intensity.   
- Singapore trends offer an interesting example. Despite the numerous "world's best digital bank" awards for DBS, the efficiency ratio for the sector have not shown any meaningful improvement. WM push not only explains the numerator but also denominator. A growing WM business doesn't always reflect in higher asset growth as the assets can also sit off balance sheet. For this reason, cost to income, while not usually the ideal metric given the income volatility for banks, might be a more relevant metric in this case.

# What drives the scores for Philippines banks (1=highest, 5=lowest):

- Tech culture – scores 4 alongside India and Taiwan. Universal banks (BDO, BPI, Metrobank) are increasingly investing and BSP is one of the more progressive ASEAN regulators. Sector fragmentation (hundreds of rural, thrift, cooperative banks) prevents aggregate investment at scale. Similar ranking as India but without India Stack.   
- Competitive urgency – scores 3 alongside many peers. Six digital bank licences (Tonik, Maya, UNObank) are creating some competitive pressure. However, the financial inclusion mandate still driving physical network expansion – partially offsetting efficiency urgency.   
- Efficiency room – scores 1 alongside India – highest non-staff costs in EM Asia and staff costs second highest, with no improvement visible despite years of investment. A portion of inefficiency might be structural - inter-island cash logistics, typhoon and disaster infrastructure - costs that AI cannot remove.   
- Labour flexibility - scores 3 alongside Indonesia, Malaysia, and Taiwan. No lifetime employment norm. But BPO competition drives up banking wages, limiting the savings AI would otherwise generate through reduced headcount. Social sensitivity around visible bank job cuts in a market with high underemployment adds political friction. Overall moderate – better than India, China or Japan.   
- IT & data infra – scores 4 alongside India and Taiwan. Lacking clean integrated infrastructure – tech investments have been rising but still look early. BSP's open finance framework is improving the data layer but from a weak starting point.

# Vietnam

- Second lowest opex to assets in the EM group but this is more a denominator story than genuine efficiency improvements. Vietnam has been the only market in Asia where banking system assets have consistently grown at a double digit pace in the last few years, well above most EM peers.   
- Non-staff costs are remarkably low and second only to China in the EM group. This partly reflects the challenges of SOE ownership and lack of significant tech investments for the sector overall. Some private banks like TCB stand out though. The recent decline continues to be largely a denominator story.   
- Staff costs are also one of the lowest in the EM group and reflect the very low level of banking sector wages.   
- A challenge for the market in future to drive further efficiency gains remains the quality of the data - Vietnam lacks the digital ID or data sharing frameworks that Indonesia and India have. The national credit bureau (CIC) coverage is also limited.

# Are markets pricing in the AI-driven efficiencies?

Is the market starting to price in some of these differences in AI readiness among Asian banks? Or do investors remain sceptical over execution and timing. AI readiness don't appear to be a part of the investment debates for most Asian banks yet and as a result the findings below look quite intriguing. We don't think its the AI benefits that are starting to get priced in yet. Its more the "safety trade" that has explained the performance of banks since last year. But we believe with an AI optionality on top, which means that even if the macro shifts and the safety trade winners looks less attractive, but if AI delivers, the outperformance of the winners could still continue.

We compare the historical relationship between P/B valuations and ROE for the top 5 and bottom 5 markets identified in the AI readiness scorecard above. In figure 37 the grey dots represent the monthly datapoints for relative PB to relative ROE relationship over the last two decades (post GFC) and seem to fit in straight line, as would be expected.

The most recent values are shown by the red and green dots. The green dots represent the most recently 12 months, and the red dots the 12m prior. The breakout from this historical pattern, the latest green dot is at a 35% premium to its fair value spot on the trend line, suggesting that the market has already started to pay a significant premium for the most AI ready markets.

Interestingly these five markets also sit atop of the "safety trade" scorecard (i.e. FX stability + high dividend yield) that as we have discussed at length in the past helps explain the performance of Asian banks and the difference between the outperformers vs underperformers since the beginning of last year. See these notes for some of that discussion –

• Asian Banks "2026: Déjà vu or Déjà new?"

Is the market making a prescient call on AI readiness or is this the safety trade in disguise? This is a debate at best.

Nevertheless, in either case banks need to deliver on these efficiency gains for this to sustain. The safety trade could fade over the medium term and for this valuation gap to sustain the promises of efficiency gains through AI will need to be delivered on. On the other hand, if this is AI readiness being already priced in (not our base case), the promise needs to be delivered.

# Valuation Method and Risk Statement

The main risk for Asian banks is inflation in countries with current account deficits and low FX reserves as higher interest rates would hurt margins for banks with high LDRs and hurt credit quality. Also, asymmetric increase in deposit rates and fewer rises in lending rates could hurt current high loan margins. Banks with equity/asset ratios below $5\%$ and ROAs below $1\%$ are vulnerable to economic and regulatory risk, especially if still growing loans by more than $15\%$ per annum, as they will encounter capital shortages.

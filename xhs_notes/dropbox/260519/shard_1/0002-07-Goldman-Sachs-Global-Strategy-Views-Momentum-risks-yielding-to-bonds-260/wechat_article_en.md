# The Equity Rally Is Running on Momentum Alone, and Bonds Are About to Call Time

Equity markets are making new highs even as the Straits of Hormuz remain closed and the growth-inflation mix deteriorates. This is not a contradiction. It is a carefully constructed illusion propped up by extraordinary earnings in two sectors: technology and energy. The global economy is growing, yes, with nominal GDP estimated at 5.9% this year versus 4.7% last year, and that provides a tailwind for revenues. But the real story is narrower. In the United States, bottom-up consensus estimates for S&P 500 earnings per share in 2026 and 2027 have each risen by 8 percentage points so far this year. That is remarkable in a world where analysts are usually too optimistic and spend the year cutting estimates. This time, they are raising them.

Yet the cause of these revisions is not broad-based economic strength. It is the concentrated effect of rising expectations for AI capital expenditure and higher energy prices. These two forces have driven the majority of positive earnings revisions globally. In most years, analysts' consensus estimates decline over time. This year, they have gone the other way. And that has been enough to keep the market aloft.

But here is the problem: the same forces that have driven the market higher have also made it dangerously concentrated. The S&P 500 has returned roughly 10% year-to-date in 2026, and technology, media, and telecommunications stocks have accounted for 85% of that return. Korea, riding the chips boom, has surged nearly 80% this year after similar advances last year. The rally is not broad. It is a bet on a handful of stories. And when momentum is the only thing holding a market together, the arrival of a competing narrative can be devastating.

## The Momentum Trade Has Become the Market, and That Is a Fragile State

The leadership of technology and energy has driven a powerful rise in the Momentum factor across every major region. The speed of the rebound in technology has been the most dramatic driver in recent weeks, reflecting a valuation opportunity following the sharp de-rating earlier in the year. But the recovery in technology, combined with the rise in commodity-related sectors, has made the equity rally exceptionally concentrated. The Momentum rallies across regions are a reflection of strong underlying profit growth, but they also raise the risk of a correction if the growth and inflation mix deteriorates further.

This is not a hypothetical risk. The correlation of equities to bond yields has turned negative. If oil disruptions continue into the second half of the year and inflation expectations rise further, there is a real risk of a speed bump for equity markets. The market is currently pricing in a benign scenario where earnings growth continues to accelerate and inflation remains contained. That scenario is now being tested by real-world events.

The concentration of returns also means that the market is vulnerable to a reversal in any one of its pillars. If technology earnings disappoint, or if energy prices fall, the entire rally could unwind quickly. The momentum trade is not diversified. It is leveraged to a narrow set of outcomes. And leverage works in both directions.

## Investor Sentiment and Positioning Have Reached Levels That Historically Precede Corrections

The Risk Appetite Indicator has risen above 1.1, placing it in the 99th percentile since 1991 and at its highest level since 2021. That is not a neutral data point. It is a warning. Rising optimism is also reflected in the surge in retail participation, particularly in the United States. Retail trading volumes have risen by 28% since mid-April, and a basket of retail favorite stocks has rallied by 29% over the same period.

At the same time, equity markets have risen despite the rise in bond yields, pushing down equity risk premia. This makes equities more vulnerable to disappointments on growth or inflation. When risk premia are compressed, the margin for error shrinks. A small miss on earnings or a slightly higher-than-expected inflation print can trigger a disproportionate sell-off.

The speed of the adjustment in bond yields is particularly important. Sharp bond yield moves have historically coincided with negative equity returns. The surge in government borrowing is an additional factor pushing up longer-dated yields across bond markets. Political developments can rapidly undermine confidence in government funding as governments compete to raise money in an environment where capital spending is rising in the private sector. A sharp increase in bond yields from current levels presents a meaningful risk for equity investors.

This is not a forecast of imminent collapse. It is a statement about the fragility of the current configuration. The market is priced for perfection, and perfection is rarely delivered.

## The Old Playbook of US, Technology, and Growth Outperformance Is No Longer Reliable

For nearly 15 years, equity returns followed a repeated pattern driven by three factors: geography, sector, and factor. The United States consistently outperformed other regions. Technology outperformed other sectors. The Growth factor outperformed Value. Over this period, the technology sector experienced a boom in earnings as the world accelerated its transition to a digital world, benefiting software and cloud computing. These companies were largely detached from the sluggish growth in economies around them and benefited from the capital expenditure installed during the internet boom a decade earlier and from very low interest rates.

Slow-growing old economy businesses suffered from overcapacity, sluggish nominal economic growth, and private sector deleveraging. Rather than accruing value for shareholders, many were seen as value traps. That pattern is now breaking down.

What has changed? First, the rise in bond yields and inflation. After years of ultra-low interest rates, we have seen a significant rise in long-term bond yields. Only a handful of years ago, a quarter of 10-year government bond yields had a negative yield. Even 30-year yields in Germany and Japan were around zero, and in other markets around 1%. They are now between 3.5% and 6%, with the US moving above 5% for the first time since 2007.

The impact of this rise in long-term interest rates, reflecting a combination of rising term premium and higher government debt, has both reduced the value of very long-duration growth within equity markets and undermined the valuation of defensive and quality parts of the equity market. These segments, which are most sensitive to interest rates, were largely valued as bond proxies. When bonds become competitive again, those proxies lose their appeal.

Second, the increase in capital spending has been dramatic. In the decade and a half after the Global Financial Crisis, there was little appetite or incentive for companies to invest heavily in capital expenditure. Growth and returns were low, and there was excess capacity in many older economy industries. The launch of large language models and growing demands for critical infrastructure have shifted the balance in favor of capital spending.

In the United States alone, S&P 500 companies have reported year-over-year capital expenditure growth of 38% so far in the first quarter of 2026, compared with just 1% for buybacks. This is a remarkable shift from the trend over the decade and a half following the financial crisis, when companies were generally rewarded for buybacks and penalized for capital expenditure. The explosion of spending on capital expenditure by the major hyperscalers in the US has been the biggest driver. So far this year, consensus estimates for the spend of the top five have increased by around 80 billion dollars to 755 billion dollars, 80% higher than a year ago. The result has been a significant boost to the earnings of AI infrastructure companies, including chip manufacturers.

## The Report Raises Questions It Does Not Fully Answer: How Much of This Earnings Growth Is Real, and How Much Is a Mirage?

The report documents that earnings revisions have been positive across every S&P 500 sector in the past month and that earnings have been strong across the main regions. But it does not fully address the sustainability of this earnings growth. The extraordinary growth in technology and energy earnings is driven by a feedback loop: AI companies spend on infrastructure, which boosts the earnings of chip manufacturers, which in turn justifies more spending on AI infrastructure. This circular logic is powerful as long as it continues, but it is vulnerable to a break at any point.

If the hyperscalers decide to slow their capital expenditure, the earnings of AI infrastructure companies will decline sharply. If energy prices fall, the earnings of energy companies will follow. The report notes that earnings revisions have been positive, but it does not answer the question of how much of this revision is driven by genuine demand growth versus cost pass-through or one-time factors.

There is also the question of valuation. The report shows that equity risk premia have compressed, making equities more vulnerable to disappointments. But it does not provide a framework for determining how much compression is justified by the change in the growth and inflation mix. Investors are left to decide for themselves whether the current risk premium is adequate compensation for the risks they are taking.

Finally, the report notes the rise in retail participation and the surge in retail favorite stocks. But it does not analyze whether this retail participation is a sign of genuine conviction or a symptom of FOMO-driven speculation. The answer to that question has significant implications for the sustainability of the rally.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Ask Before Acting on This Analysis

For readers trying to translate this analysis into action, three questions provide a useful framework.

First, what is your time horizon? If you are a long-term investor, the shift in the drivers of equity returns suggests that the old playbook of buying US technology and growth stocks may no longer be optimal. The rise in capital spending and the increase in bond yields have changed the relative attractiveness of different sectors and regions. You should be asking whether your portfolio is positioned for a world where value and cyclical stocks perform better than growth and defensive stocks.

Second, how much concentration risk are you willing to tolerate? The current rally is heavily concentrated in technology and energy. If you are overweight these sectors, you are making a bet that the feedback loop between AI capital expenditure and chip earnings will continue indefinitely. That is a bold bet. You should have a clear view of what would cause that feedback loop to break and how you would respond.

Third, what is your view on bond yields? The report makes clear that the correlation between equities and bond yields has turned negative and that sharp moves in bond yields have historically coincided with negative equity returns. If you believe bond yields will continue to rise, you should be reducing your exposure to long-duration equities and defensive stocks that are sensitive to interest rates. If you believe bond yields will stabilize or fall, the current equity risk premium may be attractive.

These three questions do not provide a single answer. They provide a structure for thinking about the problem. The right answer depends on your specific circumstances, risk tolerance, and views on the macroeconomy.

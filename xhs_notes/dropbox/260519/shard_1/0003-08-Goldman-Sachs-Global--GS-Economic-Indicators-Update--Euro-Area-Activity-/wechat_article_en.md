# The War in Iran Has Already Fractured the Global Growth Narrative, and the Euro Area Is Its First Casualty

The geopolitical shock that began with the onset of the war in Iran is no longer a theoretical risk to be modeled in scenario analyses. It is now a measurable drag on the global economy, and the data has already begun to confirm the damage. According to the latest update from a major global investment bank's proprietary economic indicators, the first clear signal of deterioration is not in the United States, nor in China, but in the Euro Area. Activity data across the currency bloc has surprised decisively to the downside since the conflict began, and the implications extend far beyond a single quarter of softness.

This is not a routine data fluctuation. The bank's MAP indicator, which aggregates the surprise value of economic data releases worldwide, has turned sharply negative. The 90-day moving average of this index has fallen from a positive reading of 2.8 in May 2025 to a negative 3.5 by May 2026. This is a swing of over six standard deviations in the direction of disappointment, and the inflection point aligns precisely with the start of the conflict. The narrative that markets have been telling themselves—that the war would be contained, that energy disruptions would be manageable, that the global expansion would remain intact—is now being contradicted by hard economic data.

The strategic question for investors is no longer whether the war will impact growth, but how deep the slowdown will be, where the next fault lines will appear, and which policy responses are most likely to succeed. The data from this report provides a powerful lens through which to assess these questions, but it also leaves critical gaps that every serious investor must wrestle with. The Euro Area is flashing red, but the full picture of global contagion is still being painted.

## The Euro Area's Activity Data Has Collapsed Relative to Expectations, Signaling a Recession Risk That the Rest of the World Has Not Yet Fully Priced

The most striking finding from the current activity indicator (CAI) data is the stark divergence between the Euro Area and the rest of the developed world. For April 2026, the Euro Area's spot CAI stands at just +0.8% month-on-month annualized, a level that is essentially stagnant. Germany, the bloc's manufacturing powerhouse, is already in negative territory with a reading of -0.6%. This is not a temporary soft patch; it is a structural deceleration that has been building for months.

Compare this to the United States, which continues to show resilience with a spot CAI of +3.0%, or Japan at +3.2%. The Euro Area is the outlier, and the reason is not difficult to identify. The region is far more exposed to energy price shocks, has less fiscal flexibility to cushion the blow, and is more dependent on trade with a Middle East that is now engulfed in conflict. The bank's MAP indicator confirms that this weakness is not a forecasting error but a genuine surprise relative to what economists had expected. The Euro Area is underperforming not just its own history, but the consensus view of where it should be.

The so-what for investors is clear. A recession in the Euro Area is now a base-case scenario, not a tail risk. This has direct implications for European equities, for the euro exchange rate, and for the European Central Bank's policy trajectory. The ECB will almost certainly be forced to cut rates more aggressively than currently priced, even as inflation remains above target. The stagflationary dynamic that many feared has arrived, but only in one region so far. The question is whether it will spread.

## Financial Conditions Are Tightening Unevenly Across Economies, and the US Dollar Is Acting as a Shock Absorber That Exacerbates Divergence

The financial conditions index (FCI) data in the report reveals a critical asymmetry. Globally, financial conditions tightened by 6.7 basis points in the most recent week, driven primarily by higher long-term interest rates and weaker equity markets. But the distribution of this tightening is far from uniform. The United States saw conditions tighten by 13.8 basis points, while the Euro Area tightened by only 7.4 basis points. At first glance, this might suggest that the US is bearing the brunt of the financial stress. But this interpretation is misleading.

The US FCI tightening was overwhelmingly driven by long rates and the exchange rate. The dollar strengthened significantly, which is itself a tightening mechanism for the US economy but an easing mechanism for the rest of the world. For the Euro Area, the sovereign spread component was a major contributor to tightening, reflecting rising concerns about fiscal sustainability in peripheral economies. The dollar's strength is effectively exporting deflation and financial strain to the Euro Area, making it harder for the ECB to stabilize the region.

The strategic implication is that the Fed's policy stance is now the single most important variable for global financial conditions, even more so than the war itself. If the Fed holds rates steady or cuts only reluctantly, the dollar will remain strong, and the Euro Area will continue to suffer. If the Fed cuts aggressively, the dollar weakens, and the Euro Area gets some relief. The report's FCI data suggests that the market is already pricing a path that is unfavorable for Europe. Investors need to decide whether they believe the Fed will prioritize domestic stability over global spillover effects.

## The Global Growth Engine Is Shifting Away from China and Toward India, but the Transition Is Too Slow to Offset the Euro Area Drag

The CAI data for emerging markets shows a fascinating and potentially decisive divergence. India's spot CAI surged to +7.9% in April 2026, up 1.3 percentage points from the prior week, and its three-month average stands at an impressive +7.0%. In contrast, China's spot CAI fell sharply by 2.3 percentage points to +4.3%, and its three-month average dropped to +5.6%. The emerging market aggregate, at +3.8%, is being pulled in opposite directions by these two giants.

This data confirms a narrative that has been building for some time: India is emerging as the primary growth engine for the developing world, while China's post-reopening momentum is fading faster than expected. The war in Iran is likely accelerating this shift, as China is more dependent on Middle Eastern energy imports and faces greater geopolitical risk in its trade routes. India, by contrast, is more domestically driven and benefits from lower commodity prices relative to its terms of trade.

The problem is scale. India's economy is roughly one-fifth the size of China's in nominal GDP terms, and about one-third on a purchasing power parity basis. Even a strong Indian performance cannot fully compensate for a meaningful slowdown in China, let alone a recession in the Euro Area. The global economy is now in a situation where the two largest developed market regions—the US and Euro Area—are moving in opposite directions, while the two largest emerging markets are also diverging. This is an unusually fragmented growth landscape, and it makes portfolio construction exceptionally difficult.

The report's data leaves an open question that is central to the investment outlook: Can India sustain its current growth trajectory without triggering overheating? The CAI data shows that India is already running well above its potential, and the bank's wage and inflation trackers suggest that price pressures are building. If the RBI is forced to tighten policy to contain inflation, the Indian growth story could stall just as the rest of the world needs it most.

## The Wage-Price Spiral Is Not Dead, but It Is Now a Regional Phenomenon That Central Banks Cannot Ignore

The report's wage tracker and inflation data provide a nuanced picture of the inflation outlook. In the United States, the wage tracker has moderated from its 2022 peak of over 5% to around 4.5% in 2024, and core PCE inflation has fallen from above 5% to a forecast of 2.5% in 2025. The US appears to be on a path toward a soft landing, with inflation gradually converging to target without a sharp rise in unemployment.

The Euro Area and UK, however, tell a different story. Euro Area wage growth remains elevated at around 5%, and core inflation is forecast to remain above 2% through 2025. The UK is even worse, with wages near 7% and core inflation still above 4%. The jobs-workers gap data in the report shows that labor market tightness remains acute in both regions, suggesting that wage pressures will persist.

This creates a policy trap for the ECB and the Bank of England. If they cut rates to support growth, they risk entrenching inflation. If they hold rates high to fight inflation, they deepen the recession. The report's data suggests that the ECB is likely to choose growth over inflation, given the severity of the activity data shock. But this is a high-risk bet. If inflation proves stickier than expected, the ECB could lose credibility, and the euro could come under severe pressure.

The strategic takeaway for investors is that the inflation narrative has become regionalized. The US is winning the inflation fight. The Euro Area and UK are not. This means that yield curve strategies, currency positioning, and sector allocation must be tailored to each region's unique inflation dynamics. A one-size-fits-all approach to duration or currency risk will likely underperform.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Transmission Mechanism from the War to the Real Economy Remains Opaque

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves a critical gap that every investor must fill for themselves. The MAP indicator and CAI data show that activity has surprised to the downside since the war began, but they do not explain the precise transmission mechanism. Is the weakness primarily due to higher energy prices, which are squeezing consumer spending and industrial margins? Or is it due to uncertainty, which is causing firms to delay investment and hiring? Or is it due to financial conditions tightening, which is reducing access to credit for households and businesses?

The answer to this question has enormous implications for portfolio strategy. If the primary channel is energy prices, then the solution is for oil to fall, and investors should be positioning for a decline in crude. If the primary channel is uncertainty, then the solution is for a ceasefire or diplomatic resolution, and investors should be positioning for a geopolitical de-escalation. If the primary channel is financial conditions, then the solution is for central banks to ease, and investors should be positioning for rate cuts.

The report provides the data to measure the damage, but it does not provide a decomposition of the causes. This is not a criticism of the analysis; it is an inherent limitation of any high-frequency economic indicator. The CAI and MAP are designed to tell you what is happening, not why. Investors who rely solely on these indicators without a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics risk making allocation decisions based on correlation rather than causation.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Navigate the Three-Speed Global Economy

Given the fragmentation evident in the data, a simple framework is essential. The global economy is now operating at three distinct speeds. The United States and India are in the fast lane, growing above potential with manageable inflation. The Euro Area and China are in the slow lane, with the former at risk of recession and the latter decelerating. The UK and Japan occupy a middle ground, with decent growth but persistent inflation.

For equity investors, this framework suggests a barbell strategy. Overweight the US and India, where growth is strongest and policy is most supportive. Underweight the Euro Area and China, where the headwinds are most severe. For fixed income investors, the framework implies a preference for US duration over European duration, as the Fed has more room to cut than the ECB. For currency investors, the framework suggests a long dollar bias against the euro and the pound, but a short dollar bias against the rupee.

The report's FCI impulse data provides a useful cross-check. The impulse from financial conditions to GDP growth over the next four quarters is negative for Norway, Sweden, Australia, and New Zealand, but positive for the US, Canada, and Japan. This suggests that the US economy is actually getting a tailwind from easier financial conditions, while commodity-linked economies are facing headwinds. The data reinforces the view that the US is the safest haven in a turbulent world.

The most important open question is whether the US exceptionalism can persist. The US FCI is tightening in the most recent week, and the dollar is strengthening. If this trend continues, the US economy could begin to feel the pinch in the second half of 2026. The report's data only covers through early May, and the situation is evolving rapidly. Investors need to monitor the weekly FCI updates closely, because a sustained tightening in US financial conditions would change the entire global outlook.

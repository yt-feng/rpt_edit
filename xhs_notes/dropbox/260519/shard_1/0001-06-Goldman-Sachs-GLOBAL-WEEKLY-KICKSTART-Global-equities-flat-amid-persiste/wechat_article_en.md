# The Rotation Toward Heavy Assets Is Not a Trade—It Is a Structural Realignment of Global Equity Strategy

Global equities were flat this week, but beneath the surface a decisive rotation is underway. Emerging markets declined 2.5%, underperforming developed markets, while the US eked out a modest 0.2% gain. Momentum factors continued to lead, supported by Energy and Technology sectors, large caps, and strong earnings revisions. Yet the most consequential signal in the data is not the week's headline performance. It is the accelerating shift from asset-light, high-obsolescence business models toward asset-heavy, capital-intensive ones—a rotation that a global investment bank report has codified as the HALO effect.

This is not a defensive pivot. It is a strategic repricing of how investors should think about competitive advantage, earnings durability, and terminal value in a world shaped by geopolitical fragmentation, supply chain realignment, and artificial intelligence disruption. The report's analysis of Japan's equity market offers a powerful lens into a phenomenon that is likely to spread across developed and emerging markets alike. The Growth versus Value P/E premium, which peaked at roughly 140% in 2022, has compressed to approximately 60%. Since late 2025, HALO—Heavy Assets, Low Obsolescence—stocks have consistently outperformed their LAHO counterparts. The question is not whether this rotation has legs. The question is whether your portfolio is positioned for a world in which the scarcest resource is not innovation, but capacity.

The macroeconomic backdrop reinforces the case for structural change. The report's forecasts show a world of moderating but resilient growth, with US GDP expected at 2.1% through 2027, Euro area growth decelerating to 0.7% in 2026, and China settling near 4.7%. Inflation remains sticky enough to keep real rates elevated. The US 10-year yield is projected to decline only gradually to 4.1% over twelve months. In this environment, the premium once awarded to asset-light disruptors—companies that could scale without building physical infrastructure—is being reassessed. When capital is no longer cheap, and when supply chains are being rebuilt for resilience rather than efficiency, owning the physical assets that underpin production and distribution becomes a source of competitive advantage, not a drag on returns.

What follows is an analysis of the HALO thesis, its implications for sector allocation, the unresolved questions it raises, and a decision framework for investors who need to act before the rotation becomes consensus.

## The collapse of the Growth premium signals that asset-light business models are losing their valuation advantage

The most important chart in the report is not a price index. It is the trajectory of the Growth versus Value P/E premium in Japan, which peaked near 140% in 2022 and has since fallen to roughly 60%. This is not a mean-reversion trade. It is a structural repricing of what investors are willing to pay for growth that lacks tangible backing.

For over a decade, asset-light companies commanded a significant valuation premium. The logic was straightforward: software platforms, digital marketplaces, and subscription-based models could scale with minimal incremental capital, generating high returns on invested capital and seemingly endless total addressable markets. Low interest rates made future cash flows highly valuable, and the absence of physical assets made these businesses flexible and adaptable. But the environment has shifted. Geopolitical risk has made supply chains a board-level concern. AI disruption is threatening the moats of many asset-light businesses, particularly in software and content, where the marginal cost of production is approaching zero and competitive differentiation is narrowing. Meanwhile, the cost of capital has risen, and the premium for visibility and durability has increased.

Asset-heavy sectors—Utilities, Transport, Materials—benefit from durable capacity that cannot be easily replicated or disrupted. Their assets are long-lived, their revenue streams are often contracted or regulated, and their capital expenditure creates barriers to entry. The report's Japan equity strategists expect the geopolitical and policy backdrop to remain supportive of further HALO outperformance. This is not a Japan-specific story. It is a global template for how investors should rethink the relationship between asset intensity and valuation in a higher-cost, higher-risk world.

## The HALO framework identifies which sectors win and which lose in the new regime

The report's distinction between HALO and LAHO is analytically powerful because it cuts across traditional style boxes. It is not Value versus Growth. It is not Cyclical versus Defensive. It is a classification based on two dimensions: the weight of physical assets on the balance sheet and the rate at which those assets become obsolete.

HALO sectors—Utilities, Transport, Materials, and select parts of Energy and Industrials—share a common structure. Their assets are heavy, long-lived, and difficult to replicate. Their obsolescence risk is low because the physical infrastructure they own is essential regardless of technological change. A power grid, a railway network, a chemical plant: these assets do not become obsolete because a new software platform emerges. They may require maintenance and upgrading, but their fundamental economic function remains stable.

LAHO sectors—Technology, Consumer Discretionary, parts of Communication Services—face a different reality. Their assets are often intangible: intellectual property, brand equity, user bases. These assets can generate high returns, but they are also vulnerable to rapid obsolescence. AI is accelerating this vulnerability. A software company's competitive advantage can erode in months if a new model or platform renders its product redundant. The report's data show that asset-light sectors face margin and terminal value pressure from AI and changing capital needs. This is not a cyclical headwind. It is a structural challenge to the valuation models that have supported high multiples for asset-light growth stocks.

The performance data in the report reinforces this divergence. Energy has returned 28.7% year-to-date, Technology 22.2%. But these are different kinds of returns. Energy's gains are driven by physical capacity constraints and commodity prices. Technology's gains are driven by AI enthusiasm, which may be more fragile. Utilities, despite a negative week, have returned 4.6% year-to-date with far lower volatility. The question for investors is not which sector has performed best, but which performance is more sustainable.

## The report's forecasts reveal a meaningful gap between top-down and bottom-up earnings expectations

One of the most striking data points in the report is the comparison between the investment bank's top-down earnings growth estimates and consensus bottom-up estimates. For the S&P 500, the top-down estimate for 2026 EPS growth is 12%, while consensus bottom-up is 21%. For the STOXX 600, the gap is 10% versus 14%. For TOPIX, it is 7% versus 11%.

This gap matters because it reveals a structural optimism embedded in bottom-up forecasts that top-down analysis does not support. Bottom-up estimates are typically more optimistic because analysts are incentivized to be bullish on the companies they cover. Top-down estimates incorporate macro constraints—GDP growth, interest rates, margin pressure, and currency effects—that individual analysts may underweight. When the gap is as wide as it is today, it suggests that either the macro outlook is too pessimistic, or that earnings expectations are too high and will need to be revised downward.

The implications for the HALO thesis are direct. If earnings expectations are too high for the broad market, the stocks most vulnerable to disappointment are those with the highest valuation multiples and the most optimistic growth assumptions. These are disproportionately asset-light companies. HALO stocks, with their lower multiples and more predictable earnings, are less exposed to earnings disappointment. They may not offer the upside of a 21% earnings growth story, but they also do not carry the same risk of a downward revision cycle.

For 2027, the gap narrows but does not disappear. Top-down estimates for the S&P 500 are 10%, versus 14% bottom-up. For TOPIX, the estimates converge at 11% versus 12%. This convergence over a longer horizon is consistent with the idea that bottom-up optimism tends to correct over time. But the persistence of the gap in the near term means that the market is pricing in a level of earnings growth that the macro environment may not deliver.

## One section the report does not fully answer: how to distinguish between durable asset heaviness and stranded asset risk

The HALO framework is powerful, but it has a limitation that investors must address. Not all heavy assets are created equal. Some are durable and essential. Others are vulnerable to technological substitution, regulatory change, or shifts in consumer behavior.

The report does not provide a systematic way to distinguish between these two categories. It groups Utilities, Transport, and Materials together as HALO sectors, but within each of these sectors, there are assets that may become stranded. A coal-fired power plant is heavy and has low obsolescence in the sense that it will continue to generate electricity for decades, but its economic viability is declining as carbon pricing and renewable energy reshape the power market. A railway network in a region with declining population may have low obsolescence but also low utilization. A chemical plant built for a specific supply chain configuration may become uneconomic if trade patterns shift.

The unresolved question is this: what additional criteria should investors use to identify the HALO stocks that will outperform from those that will merely survive or decline? The report's framework is a useful starting point, but it needs to be supplemented with an analysis of asset quality, regulatory exposure, and the potential for technological disruption even within asset-heavy industries. Investors who apply the HALO label too broadly risk owning assets that are heavy, low-obsolescence, and low-return.

## A decision framework for applying the HALO thesis to your portfolio

For investors who want to act on the HALO thesis, a structured decision process is essential. The report provides the conceptual foundation, but implementation requires answering four questions.

First, what is your time horizon? The HALO rotation is structural, not tactical. It is driven by shifts in geopolitics, capital costs, and technology that will unfold over years, not weeks. If your investment horizon is less than twelve months, the HALO thesis may underperform during periods of risk-on enthusiasm for technology stocks. If your horizon is three to five years, the structural case is compelling.

Second, which HALO sectors are best positioned in your region? The report focuses on Japan, where the HALO effect is most advanced. But the thesis applies differently in different markets. In the US, Energy and select Industrials may be the primary beneficiaries. In Europe, Utilities and Transport may offer more attractive valuations. In emerging markets, Materials and Energy are likely to lead. The key is to identify sectors where asset heaviness is combined with pricing power and regulatory stability.

Third, how do you manage the risk of asset stranding? Not all heavy assets are good investments. The HALO thesis requires due diligence on the quality and durability of the assets themselves. Investors should favor assets that are essential to economic activity, difficult to replicate, and supported by long-term demand trends. Assets that are heavy but exposed to technological substitution or regulatory headwinds should be avoided or hedged.

Fourth, what is your exit trigger? The HALO rotation will not last forever. It will end when the conditions that drive it reverse: when geopolitical risk declines, when capital becomes cheap again, or when AI disruption stabilizes and asset-light models regain their valuation premium. Investors should define in advance what conditions would cause them to reduce their HALO exposure. The report's data on the Growth versus Value P/E premium is a useful monitoring tool. When that premium begins to expand again, it may signal that the rotation is ending.

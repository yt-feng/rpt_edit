# China’s SME Lending Policy Has Reached an Inflection Point: Near-Term Pain Is the Price for Medium-Term Yield Recovery

China’s financial regulator has fundamentally changed the rules of the game for small and medium enterprise lending. The National Financial Regulatory Administration’s May 2026 Notice on MSE Financial Services removed the long-standing explicit encouragement of loan volume growth, replacing it with a mandate focused on credit structure optimization and asset quality improvement. This is not a minor adjustment. It is a structural pivot that ends a decade-long policy era defined by the “two increases, two controls” framework and the race to expand inclusive finance at any cost.

The timing is no coincidence. The policy shift comes as small enterprise credit metrics are deteriorating sharply. PMI data for small enterprises has fallen below 50, indicating contraction, while the interest coverage ratio for small industrial firms has dropped to levels not seen since the early pandemic. The divergence between large and small enterprises in accounts receivable turnover days has widened to a record gap. The regulator is effectively acknowledging what the data already shows: the volume-first approach has run its course, and the cost of sustaining it has become too high.

For investors, this pivot creates a clear trade-off. In the near term, asset quality pressure will intensify as loan growth decelerates and weaker borrowers lose access to refinancing. But in the medium term, the easing of irrational pricing competition should allow inclusive loan yields to recover from their current distressed levels. The implications vary dramatically across banks, and the divergence will define the winners and losers in Chinese banking over the next two to three years.

## The End of the Volume Race Means Near-Term Asset Quality Deterioration Is Unavoidable

The regulatory pivot from quantity to quality is not a gentle course correction. It is a deliberate decision to absorb pain now in exchange for sustainability later. The 2018 “two increases, two controls” framework forced banks to grow inclusive SME loans faster than overall loan growth and to expand the borrower base year over year. That hard requirement was softened in 2023 but remained directional. The 2026 Notice removes the growth target entirely and substitutes it with an emphasis on asset quality improvement. This is the closest thing to an admission that the previous policy created unintended consequences.

The data supports this view. From 2022 to 2025, the weighted average new inclusive finance lending yield contracted by 120 basis points to 3.31 percent, while the NPL ratio for personal business loans—a key sub-category of inclusive finance—rose by 70 basis points to 1.66 percent. The economics of SME lending have deteriorated to the point where risk-adjusted returns are negative for many banks. The NPL ratio for personal business loans has been trending up since the first half of 2023, even as the overall loan NPL ratio continued to decline. This is a clear sign that the quality deterioration is concentrated in the SME segment, not a broad-based phenomenon.

When growth targets are removed, the natural consequence is a slowdown in new lending. SOE banks have been the primary drivers of inclusive finance growth, with their contribution to total inclusive loans rising from less than 2 percent of total loans in early 2019 to over 6.5 percent by early 2026. As these large banks pull back, the weakest borrowers—those who relied on continuous refinancing to stay afloat—will face repayment stress. The near-term asset quality pressure is therefore not a hypothesis. It is a mechanical outcome of the policy change.

## The Divergence Between Large and Small Enterprises Has Reached a Critical Threshold

The macro data underpinning this policy shift is stark and often underappreciated. Manufacturing PMI for large enterprises stood at 51.1 in May 2026, indicating expansion, while PMI for small enterprises fell to 48.5, signaling contraction. This gap has persisted for years, but it has widened materially since the pandemic. More telling is the divergence in financial health metrics.

The interest coverage ratio for small industrial enterprises has dropped to approximately 5.0 times, while large and medium enterprises have maintained a ratio above 9.0 times. This means small enterprises have far less capacity to service their debt. Meanwhile, accounts receivable turnover days for small enterprises have stretched to over 90 days, compared to roughly 75 days for larger firms. Small enterprises are being squeezed from both sides: their customers are paying later, and their lenders are becoming less willing to extend credit.

This structural weakness is not cyclical. It reflects a fundamental shift in the competitive dynamics of the Chinese economy, where large enterprises have greater pricing power, better access to capital, and stronger bargaining positions with suppliers and customers. The SME lending policy was, in part, designed to offset this imbalance. But the data now suggests that subsidized credit alone cannot solve the underlying problem. The policy pivot is an acknowledgment that continuing to push credit into a deteriorating segment only delays the reckoning and increases the eventual cost.

## Banks with Demonstrated Asset Quality Control Will Benefit Most from the Yield Recovery

The medium-term opportunity lies in the recovery of inclusive loan yields. With growth targets removed and competition from SOE banks likely to moderate, pricing should stabilize and potentially improve. But not all banks are positioned equally to capture this benefit.

The key differentiating factor is asset quality trajectory. Banks that have already shown improvement in their personal business loan NPL ratios are best positioned to benefit from a yield recovery, because they do not need to simultaneously provision for rising defaults. Two banks stand out in this regard. China Construction Bank and Shanghai Pudong Development Bank both reported declines in their personal business loan NPL ratios in 2025 on a year-over-year basis. This suggests they have already absorbed the worst of the credit deterioration and are now operating from a cleaner base.

For these banks, an improvement in lending yield—even a modest one—flows directly to the bottom line. The economics of their inclusive finance portfolios can shift from negative to positive without requiring a dramatic improvement in underwriting. The question is how quickly the yield recovery materializes. The report suggests the magnitude will be small in the near term, as banks remain risk-averse and SME debt-servicing capacity has not yet recovered. But the direction is clear, and the inflection point is visible.

On the other side of the ledger, Postal Savings Bank of China faces the most significant headwinds. It has the highest exposure to inclusive finance loans among all covered banks, and it continued to see both lending yield contraction and rising NPL ratios in 2025. Its inclusive finance portfolio is too large to pivot quickly, and its asset quality trajectory has not yet turned. For PSBC, the near-term pain will be more acute and the medium-term recovery more uncertain.

## The Regional Bank Story Is More Complex: Short-Term Pain, Long-Term Opportunity

For city and rural commercial banks, the policy pivot creates a more nuanced dynamic. These banks have historically been the primary lenders to local SMEs, but they have been crowded out by aggressive competition from large state-owned banks. The data shows that from 2020 to 2025, the compound annual growth rate of inclusive finance loans at large SOE banks far exceeded that of most regional banks. ICBC grew its inclusive finance book at a 38 percent CAGR, while many regional banks grew at half that rate or less.

The policy pivot should, in theory, benefit regional banks by removing the irrational pricing pressure from large competitors. As SOE banks pull back, regional banks with genuine SME service capabilities should be able to regain market share in their core operating regions. But the near-term challenge is asset quality. The report notes that no inflection point has yet been observed in personal business loan NPL ratios across covered regional banks. The credit withdrawal by large banks could amplify repayment stress for borrowers who were previously cross-subsidized by cheap SOE credit.

Among regional banks, Chongqing Rural Commercial Bank appears best positioned. Its personal business loan NPL ratio contracted by 49 basis points in the second half of 2025, likely supported by local macro recovery and better underwriting capacity. Changshu Rural Commercial Bank, by contrast, faces the largest near-term downside risk. It has the highest inclusive finance exposure among all covered banks at over 41 percent of total loans, and its personal business asset quality continues to deteriorate. The long-term read-through for CSRCB is constructive—its deep local knowledge and “loan factory” credit model should allow it to regain share as competitive dynamics normalize—but the near-term data does not yet support a positive view.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Magnitude and Timing of the Yield Recovery

The report makes a compelling case that inclusive loan yields will improve in the medium term, but it leaves several critical questions open. First, the magnitude of the yield recovery is uncertain. The report suggests it will be “moderate” and “small” in magnitude, but it does not provide a specific estimate. Given that inclusive finance lending yields have fallen by 120 basis points over three years, a recovery of even 30 to 50 basis points would be meaningful, but it is not clear whether that is achievable.

Second, the timing is ambiguous. The report uses the phrase “medium term” without defining it. Does this mean six months, twelve months, or two years? The answer depends on how quickly banks adjust their pricing behavior and how soon SME debt-servicing capacity improves. The macro data does not yet show a clear recovery in small enterprise profitability, which suggests the timeline could be longer than markets currently expect.

Third, the interaction between asset quality deterioration and yield recovery is not fully modeled. If near-term NPLs rise faster than expected, banks may need to hold more provisions, which would offset any benefit from higher yields. The net impact on bank profitability depends on the relative speed and magnitude of these two forces. The report identifies which banks are better positioned, but it does not quantify the net earnings impact.

Fourth, the policy itself is not static. The 2026 Notice represents the current view, but regulatory priorities can shift again if economic conditions deteriorate further or if SME credit availability becomes a political concern. The removal of growth targets could be temporary. Investors need to consider the possibility that the policy pivot is reversed or modified before the medium-term benefits materialize.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Evaluate Any Bank

The report’s analysis can be distilled into a simple decision framework. For any bank with meaningful inclusive finance exposure, investors should ask three questions in sequence.

First, what is the bank’s asset quality trajectory for SME loans? Banks that have already shown improvement in personal business loan NPL ratios are better positioned to benefit from a yield recovery, because they do not need to simultaneously increase provisions. Banks with deteriorating asset quality will face a double squeeze: higher credit costs and only a delayed benefit from higher yields.

Second, what is the bank’s exposure to inclusive finance relative to its total loan book? Banks with higher exposure will feel the impact of the policy pivot more acutely, in both directions. High exposure amplifies the near-term pain from asset quality deterioration, but it also amplifies the medium-term benefit from yield recovery. The key is to assess whether the bank’s asset quality trajectory is good enough to survive the near-term pain and reach the medium-term benefit.

Third, does the bank have a competitive advantage in SME lending that will allow it to regain market share as large banks retreat? For regional banks, this means local knowledge, relationship-based underwriting, and efficient credit processes. For national banks, it means scale, data analytics, and diversified revenue streams. Banks that lack a clear competitive advantage may find that the policy pivot simply reduces the size of their SME lending business without improving its profitability.

Applying this framework, the banks that score well on all three dimensions are those with improving asset quality, moderate exposure, and strong competitive positions. The banks that score poorly are those with deteriorating asset quality, high exposure, and no clear competitive moat. The report’s preferences align with this logic: CCB and SPDB on the positive side, PSBC and CSRCB on the cautious side.

The policy pivot in China’s SME lending market is a defining moment for the banking sector. The near-term pain is real, but the medium-term opportunity is equally real for the right banks. The key is to distinguish between banks that are absorbing losses from a position of strength and those that are absorbing losses because they have no other choice. The divergence will be sharp, and the next twelve to eighteen months will reveal which banks have the underlying credit quality and strategic positioning to emerge stronger.

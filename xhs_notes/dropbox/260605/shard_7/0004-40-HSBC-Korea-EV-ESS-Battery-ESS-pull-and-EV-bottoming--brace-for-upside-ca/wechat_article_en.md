# Korea’s Battery Cycle Has Bottomed: The Real Catalyst Is Not EV Demand, But a Structural Pivot to US Energy Storage

The narrative around Korean battery makers has been dominated by one word for the past eighteen months: weakness. Electric vehicle sales growth slowed, inventory piled up, and the market punished the sector with brutal multiple compression. But the cycle has turned. The evidence from the first quarter of 2026 is not a story of recovery—it is a story of redefinition. The operating losses at both Samsung SDI and LG Energy Solution, while still negative, came in materially better than consensus expectations. SDI reported an operating loss of KRW 156 billion versus consensus expectations of KRW 271 billion. LGES reported an operating loss of KRW 208 billion, essentially in line with consensus but with a sequential improvement in margins. These numbers are not yet good, but they are better than feared. And in a cyclical industry, that is often the first signal of a structural turn.

The deeper argument, however, is not about EV demand snapping back. It is about the energy storage system market in the United States, which is undergoing a tectonic shift toward Korean suppliers. This is not a minor tailwind. It is a structural reordering of the global battery supply chain, driven by US policy, geopolitical risk, and the insatiable energy demands of artificial intelligence data centers. The Korean battery makers are not just surviving the EV downturn. They are repositioning their manufacturing capacity, their capital allocation, and their strategic focus toward a market that offers higher margins, longer contracts, and policy-protected returns.

The conventional wisdom holds that Korean battery makers are hostage to the EV cycle. The data suggests otherwise. The US ESS market, which is projected to grow from 128 gigawatt-hours of cumulative installations in 2025 to 458 gigawatt-hours by 2030, is becoming the primary earnings driver for these companies. And Korean suppliers are positioned to capture the majority of that growth. The question for investors is no longer whether the cycle has bottomed. It is whether they are positioned for the structural shift that follows.

## The US ESS Market Is Structurally Rotating Toward Korean Suppliers, and the Policy Architecture Makes This Irreversible

The most important development in the global battery industry is not a technology breakthrough. It is the US One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which has created a regulatory environment that effectively taxes Chinese-made batteries out of the US ESS market while subsidizing domestically produced alternatives. The math is straightforward. A Chinese-made battery pack costs roughly USD 90 per kilowatt-hour at the factory gate. But by the time it lands in the United States, it faces a 43.4 percent tariff, bringing the effective cost to USD 129 per kilowatt-hour. It then loses access to the 30 percent Investment Tax Credit and the Advanced Manufacturing Production Credit of USD 35 to 45 per kilowatt-hour. The result is that a US-made Korean battery, despite a higher base cost of USD 180 per kilowatt-hour, delivers a total ESS project capex that is approximately 16 percent lower than the Chinese alternative.

This is not a marginal advantage. It is a decisive structural moat. And it is reinforced by the Foreign Entities of Concern restrictions embedded in the OBBBA, which progressively tighten the allowable Chinese content in batteries eligible for tax credits from 55 percent in 2026 to 75 percent by 2030. Chinese suppliers are attempting to circumvent these restrictions by establishing supply chains in Southeast Asia, but that introduces its own set of risks around quality, scale, and geopolitical scrutiny. Korean suppliers, with existing US manufacturing footprints and no FEOC designation, are the direct beneficiaries.

The market share implications are stark. Current estimates project that Korean makers will capture 10 percent of the US ESS market in 2025, rising to 37 percent in 2026, 51 percent in 2027, and 63 percent by 2028. This is not a gradual trend. It is a step-function shift. And it is happening because the US market is not just growing—it is actively excluding the dominant Chinese suppliers that have historically controlled the global battery supply chain.

## The Fungibility of EV and ESS Cell Production Gives Korean Makers an Operational Pivot That Competitors Cannot Match

One of the underappreciated structural advantages of the Korean battery ecosystem is the high degree of manufacturing fungibility between EV and ESS cell architectures. The same production lines that were built to serve automotive OEMs can be reconfigured to produce ESS batteries with relatively modest retooling costs and timelines. This is not true for all battery chemistries, but it is true for the lithium-ion chemistries that dominate both markets.

This operational flexibility matters enormously in the current environment. The EV market is oversupplied, with utilization rates running below breakeven for most producers. The ESS market, by contrast, is undersupplied, with demand accelerating faster than new capacity can be brought online. Korean makers are responding by converting EV capacity to ESS production, effectively arbitraging the demand gap between the two markets. This capacity conversion not only improves utilization rates and unit economics but also accelerates the timeline for Korean suppliers to capture US market share.

The strategic implication is that Korean battery makers are not simply waiting for the EV cycle to recover. They are actively redeploying their most valuable asset—manufacturing capacity—toward the highest-return opportunity. This is the kind of capital discipline that separates companies that survive cyclical downturns from those that emerge stronger on the other side. And it is a dynamic that is largely absent from the consensus narrative, which remains focused on monthly EV sales numbers and inventory data.

## AI Data Center Expansion Is Creating a New Demand Vector for ESS That Is Only Beginning to Be Priced In

The connection between artificial intelligence and energy storage is not immediately obvious, but it is becoming one of the most important demand drivers in the battery industry. AI data centers consume enormous amounts of electricity, and their power demands are growing at an accelerating rate. US data center energy demand is projected to rise from approximately 150 terawatt-hours in 2024 to nearly 390 terawatt-hours by 2030. This growth is straining grid infrastructure and creating a massive need for behind-the-meter energy storage solutions.

The specific estimates are telling. Between 2026 and 2030, the average annual increase in AI data center power demand is projected at 21.5 gigawatts. Of that, roughly 27 to 30 percent will need to be covered by energy storage, with weighted average storage durations of 4.3 to 4.7 hours. This translates to an annual incremental ESS demand of 25 to 31 gigawatt-hours from AI data centers alone. To put that in context, the total US ESS market in 2024 was approximately 37 gigawatt-hours of installations. AI data center demand alone is adding a market roughly the size of the entire current market every year.

This is not a forecast. It is a structural necessity. Data centers cannot operate with intermittent power. They require reliable, high-quality electricity around the clock. Energy storage is the bridge that allows them to integrate renewable generation, manage peak demand, and maintain uptime. And the Korean battery makers, with their US production footprints and policy advantages, are the primary suppliers for this application. The market is only beginning to price in this demand vector, which suggests that current earnings estimates for 2027 and 2028 may prove conservative.

## The Earnings Estimates Tell the Story: Consensus Is Still Behind the Curve on the ESS Recovery

The most compelling evidence that the market has not fully absorbed the ESS thesis comes from the earnings estimates themselves. For Samsung SDI, operating profit estimates for 2027 and 2028 stand at 44 percent and 23 percent above Bloomberg consensus, respectively. For LG Energy Solution, the divergence is 17 percent and 12 percent above consensus. These are not small gaps. They reflect a fundamental difference in how the market and the analysis view the trajectory of earnings recovery.

The source of this divergence is instructive. Consensus estimates are still heavily weighted toward the EV cycle. They assume that earnings recovery will be driven primarily by a rebound in EV demand, improved utilization rates, and pricing discipline. The alternative view is that the recovery will be driven disproportionately by ESS, where volumes are growing faster, margins are higher, and the policy environment is more supportive. The difference in outcomes between these two scenarios is not marginal. It determines whether these companies return to peak earnings by 2028 or whether they exceed them.

The estimate revisions themselves are also revealing. Samsung SDI’s 2028 operating profit estimate was raised by 24 percent in the most recent update, driven almost entirely by higher ESS expectations. LGES’s 2028 operating profit estimate was raised by 33 percent. These are not incremental adjustments. They are meaningful re-ratings that reflect a structural shift in the business mix. And they suggest that the earnings power of these companies in a normalized environment may be significantly higher than the market currently discounts.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of the US Policy Regime and the Risk of Chinese Price Warfare

No analysis is complete without identifying the questions it leaves open. The most important unresolved issue is the sustainability of the US policy regime. The OBBBA and the tariff structure that underpins the Korean advantage are political constructs. They can be modified, delayed, or repealed. If the US were to lower tariffs on Chinese batteries or relax FEOC restrictions, the competitive dynamics would shift dramatically. Korean suppliers would lose their cost advantage, and the market share projections would need to be revised downward.

The second unresolved question is the potential for Chinese price warfare. Chinese battery makers have historically been willing to operate at thin margins to gain market share. If they decide to aggressively cut prices in the US market, even with the tariff burden, they could erode the Korean cost advantage. The analysis assumes that Chinese suppliers will not engage in irrational pricing, but that assumption has been wrong before. The risk is that Chinese producers, facing overcapacity at home, decide to dump product into the US market at below-cost prices, forcing Korean makers to either match the pricing or cede share.

The third open question is the pace of technology transition. The report highlights high-voltage mid-nickel and solid-state batteries as long-term optionality, but it does not fully address the risk that a technology discontinuity could disrupt the current competitive landscape. If a Chinese or Japanese competitor achieves a breakthrough in solid-state manufacturing economics, the entire battery value chain could be reshuffled. Korean makers are investing in these technologies, but they are not the only ones. The timing and scale of the next chemistry transition remain uncertain.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Positioning

For investors trying to translate this analysis into a portfolio decision, three questions provide a useful framework.

First, do you believe the US policy regime on Chinese batteries will remain intact through 2028? If the answer is yes, then the structural shift toward Korean suppliers is not just probable but near-certain. The market share projections become a baseline, not a stretch case. If the answer is no, then the thesis is significantly weakened, and the stocks should be evaluated on EV cycle dynamics alone.

Second, do you believe AI data center energy demand will grow as projected? This is a bet on the continued expansion of AI infrastructure, which is itself a function of compute demand, energy availability, and regulatory approval for new data center construction. If AI demand stalls or shifts to more energy-efficient architectures, the ESS demand tailwind diminishes. But if the current trajectory holds, the incremental demand from data centers alone justifies the current valuation.

Third, which stock fits your risk profile? Samsung SDI offers a more concentrated ESS pivot with higher earnings upside relative to consensus, but also higher volatility and a smaller market cap. LG Energy Solution offers a broader EV beta play with more diversified revenue, larger scale, and lower upside relative to consensus but also lower downside risk. The choice between them is a choice between conviction and optionality.

The report provides detailed estimates and target prices for both stocks, but the framework above is what determines whether those estimates are achievable. Investors who answer yes to the first two questions and align their stock selection with their risk tolerance are positioned for the structural shift. Those who answer no should stay on the sidelines until the uncertainties resolve.

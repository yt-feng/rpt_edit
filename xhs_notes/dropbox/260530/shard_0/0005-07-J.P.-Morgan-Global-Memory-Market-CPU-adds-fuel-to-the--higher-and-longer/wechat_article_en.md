# Memory Is No Longer a Cyclical Commodity. The Upcycle Has Become Structural, and the Market Is Underpricing Its Longevity.

For decades, the memory industry was defined by a brutal boom-bust rhythm. Investors timed entries around inventory corrections and exits around supply gluts. That framework is now obsolete. The global memory market is undergoing a transition that is not merely cyclical but structural, driven by an AI demand wave that is broadening from GPU-centric workloads into CPU-driven orchestration, state management, and edge inference. The implications for total addressable market, pricing stability, and valuation methodology are profound.

A global investment bank report now projects the memory TAM reaching USD 1.3 trillion by 2027 and USD 1.7 trillion by 2028, representing upward revisions of 37-53% versus prior models. This is not a forecast built on extrapolation of a single product cycle. It reflects three converging forces: the emergence of AI CPU memory demand as a material new leg, a worsening supply-demand deficit that challenges the industry's ability to keep pace, and a shift in how hyperscalers treat memory as a strategic asset rather than a procurement line item.

The market cap of the memory ecosystem has already surged over 130% in the first half of 2026 alone. Yet the sector continues to trade at a discount to its earnings power. The central question for investors is no longer whether memory is in an upcycle, but whether the valuation framework that served the industry for the last decade can capture the magnitude of what is unfolding.

## The CPU Memory Demand Leg Is Real and Material, Broadening the AI Compute Architecture Beyond GPU-Only Narratives

The dominant narrative in AI semiconductors has centered on GPU compute intensity. That story is incomplete. As AI workloads scale from training clusters to inference-heavy production environments, the CPU has re-emerged as a critical compute node for orchestration, state management, and API execution. This is not a theoretical shift. The report documents strong traction in Vera CPU adoption and anticipates major CSP customers launching their own in-house CPU designs from the second half of 2027 onward.

The data is striking. The CPU-to-GPU ratio has moved from 5.4:1 in 2023 to 3.2:1 in 2025, and is projected to reach 2.4:1 by 2028. This trend is the primary driver behind a 20-22% upward revision in server-grade DDR and LPDDR5 bit demand for fiscal years 2027 and 2028. By 2028, AI CPU DRAM demand is forecast at 17 million GB, representing 24% of total market demand. That is a structural shift, not a cyclical blip.

Why this matters for investors: the memory TAM is no longer a function of GPU unit growth alone. Every new CPU architecture designed for AI inference and workload coordination adds a parallel demand stream that is less correlated with GPU replacement cycles. This diversification reduces the single-point-of-failure risk that has historically plagued memory demand forecasts. It also implies that even if GPU upgrade cadences slow, the CPU memory leg can sustain demand growth.

## Supply-Demand Tightness Is Set to Worsen, but Long-Term Agreements Are Reshaping the Pricing Paradigm

The report projects a clear and worsening supply-demand deficit across both DRAM and NAND. DRAM bit demand growth is forecast at 32.5% in 2026 and 34.4% in 2027, while bit shipment growth trails at 22.9% and 21.7% respectively. The gap is even more pronounced in NAND, where demand growth of 23% in 2026 outpaces supply growth of 19.8%, and the divergence widens in 2027.

Historically, such tightness would trigger aggressive price increases. The report acknowledges this theoretical upside risk, noting that DRAM and NAND prices have already risen 220-250% year-over-year. However, the pricing dynamic is evolving. The industry is shifting toward long-term agreements, or LTAs, which stabilize ASPs and reduce spot market volatility. The report expects a more stable pricing trend supported by an increasing mix of LTA contract sales.

This is a critical inflection point. If LTAs become the dominant pricing mechanism, memory suppliers gain earnings visibility that was previously impossible. The report estimates memory operating profit margins reaching the high 70% range by 2027 and sustaining near those levels through 2028. That is a margin profile that rivals the most profitable segments of the semiconductor ecosystem, including AI semi and foundry.

The strategic implication: investors should not wait for a pricing blow-off top to take profits. The transition to LTA-based pricing suggests that peak margins may be more durable than in prior cycles. The risk of a sharp mean reversion is lower, precisely because the pricing mechanism has changed.

## HBM Is Driving a Fundamental Reallocation of Wafer Capacity, Creating a Bottleneck for Conventional DRAM

High Bandwidth Memory has been the headline growth story, but the structural consequences for the rest of the DRAM market are only now becoming clear. The report introduces a revised HBM supply-demand view with TAM upward revisions of 17-21% through 2028. Key drivers include ASIC demand acceleration, with ASIC bit mix rising from 33% in 2026 to 39% in 2027, and a conservative assumption on HBM4E content reflecting a longer 12Hi product lifecycle.

The most consequential data point, however, is the wafer allocation trend. DRAM wafer capacity allocated to HBM is projected to rise from 24% in 2026 to 31% by 2028. This is not a marginal shift. It represents a structural diversion of capacity away from conventional DRAM products at a time when overall demand is accelerating. The report explicitly notes that this adds greater pressure on conventional DRAM supply-demand dynamics.

The report also flags an important pricing dynamic: the dollar-per-bit crossover between HBM and non-HBM products. As that crossover approaches, memory makers have a clear economic incentive to prioritize HBM production and to become more vocal about raising HBM ASPs. The report forecasts a 10% year-over-year like-for-like increase and a 30% blended increase in HBM pricing next year.

The second-order implication is that investors in conventional DRAM-exposed names may face a structural headwind even as the overall market expands. The wafer allocation shift means that non-HBM supply growth will be constrained, which supports pricing for those products, but volume growth will be capped. Understanding which memory makers have the most flexible wafer allocation strategies will be a key differentiator.

## Memory Has Become a Strategic Asset Within CSP Capex, but Its Rising Share Raises Investor Concerns That Must Be Addressed

The most underappreciated structural change in the memory industry is its rising strategic importance within hyperscaler capital expenditure. Memory as a percentage of total CSP hardware capex has moved from the mid-teens at the beginning of the AI investment cycle to over half today. The report projects this share reaching 73% by 2027.

This is not merely a reflection of higher memory prices. It reflects a fundamental shift in how memory is viewed within AI compute systems. As the report notes, stable procurement is imperative for AI service operations to deliver faster and higher quality service. Memory is no longer a commodity to be procured at lowest cost; it is a strategic asset that directly impacts inference performance, latency, and overall system utilization.

However, this rising share has created unease among investors. Many have expressed concerns about whether memory's value share within CSP capex can sustain further increases. This skepticism is one of the primary reasons memory stocks continue to trade at a discount to earnings, even as margins reach record levels.

The report addresses this concern directly. Higher overall CSP hardware capex, supported by evidence of NVIDIA's 2030 spending outlook of USD 3-4 trillion, should help alleviate fears. If the total capex pie expands sufficiently, memory can maintain or even increase its share without crowding out other components. The risk factors to monitor are delayed utility infrastructure readiness and slower-than-expected new AI business model adoption, both of which could compress the overall capex envelope.

For investors, the key question is whether memory's value share has peaked or whether it can stabilize at elevated levels. The report's framework suggests that stabilization is the more likely outcome, but the path is contingent on continued CSP capex growth. This is a macro-driven variable that deserves close monitoring.

## Enterprise SSD Is Emerging as the Dominant NAND Demand Driver, with Technology Shifts That Could Tighten Supply

NAND has historically played a secondary role in the AI narrative, but that is changing. Enterprise SSD is emerging as a powerful demand driver, with customers showing a clear preference for high-density and high-speed solutions. The report estimates the eSSD market will exceed 500 exabytes in 2026, representing 43% of total NAND bit demand, and grow to over 1,100 exabytes within two years at a 52% CAGR.

The value implications are even more striking. Given the ASP premium for enterprise SSD, the report estimates the eSSD value TAM could exceed USD 300 billion in the next two years, making it larger than the HBM TAM. This is a reminder that NAND, often viewed as the less attractive memory segment, has its own AI-driven growth vector.

The technology transition within NAND is also tightening supply dynamics. The report notes growing traction for SLC-based high-speed SSD on top of mainstream TLC, with various solutions expected to hit the market from the second half of 2026. SLC has a higher bit-per-wafer trade-off compared to TLC and QLC, meaning that a shift toward SLC reduces effective supply even if wafer starts remain constant. This dynamic, combined with capex spending that remains below historical peak levels, creates a constructive supply-demand backdrop for NAND.

The strategic insight: NAND investors should focus on the eSSD adoption curve and the technology mix shift. Companies with strong positions in high-speed SSD controllers and SLC solutions are likely to benefit disproportionately.

## The Report's Own Framework Raises Unresolved Questions About Valuation, Competitive Dynamics, and the Sustainability of Margin Expansion

Despite the compelling thesis, the report leaves several meaningful analytical questions unanswered. These are not weaknesses in the analysis but rather open frontiers that investors must navigate themselves.

First, the valuation framework is in transition. The report notes that memory stocks trade at a discount to earnings and suggests that a new valuation approach is necessary, referencing an LTA-based framework. But the contours of that framework remain undefined. How should investors value a memory company whose earnings are increasingly supported by long-term contracts rather than spot pricing? Should the sector command a multiple expansion toward foundry or AI semi levels? The report does not provide a definitive answer, and the path to a new framework is described as "patchy."

Second, the competitive dynamics with Chinese memory producers are unresolved. The report projects that Chinese DRAM volume share will rise from 6% in 2025 to 8-11% by 2028, and NAND share from 12% to 12-16%. Capacity shares are even higher, reaching 18% for DRAM and 19% for NAND by 2028. However, the report argues that inferior product mix limits value share gains, with DRAM and NAND value shares at 10% and 12% respectively by 2028. The key monitoring points are HBM wafer allocation in China and equipment export restriction policy updates, including the MATCH Act. The question is whether these constraints are durable or whether Chinese producers can eventually climb the product stack.

Third, the sustainability of operating margins at 75-77% is unprecedented in the memory industry's history. The report acknowledges this by labeling it a "new norm." But new norms are difficult to prove in real time. What happens if CSP capex growth slows, or if memory's value share within that capex begins to compress? The report's own data shows that margins were negative in 2023 and only 26% in 2024. The jump to 76% by 2026 represents a step change that has no historical precedent.

These open questions are not reasons to dismiss the thesis. They are the analytical gaps that separate a consensus view from a differentiated one. Investors who can form a conviction on these questions will be better positioned than those who rely solely on the headline numbers.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Filters to Navigate the Memory Opportunity

Given the structural shift underway, investors need a framework that goes beyond simple sector exposure. The following three filters can help differentiate between opportunities within the memory ecosystem.

Filter one: assess earnings visibility through LTA exposure. The transition to long-term agreements is the single most important change in the industry's business model. Companies with higher LTA coverage should command a valuation premium because their earnings are less exposed to spot price volatility. Conversely, companies that remain heavily dependent on spot markets carry higher risk even if the demand backdrop is favorable.

Filter two: evaluate wafer allocation flexibility. The shift toward HBM is creating a structural supply constraint for conventional DRAM. Memory makers with the ability to flex wafer allocation between HBM and non-HBM products will be better positioned to capture value across the cycle. Those with rigid allocation or heavy concentration in segments facing capacity diversion may face volume headwinds.

Filter three: monitor CSP capex as the macro variable that determines the ceiling. Memory's rising share of CSP hardware capex is the most debated topic among investors. The bull case rests on continued expansion of the overall capex envelope. The bear case is that memory share has peaked and will compress as CSPs optimize their procurement. Investors should track CSP capital expenditure guidance, utility infrastructure timelines, and the pace of new AI business model adoption as leading indicators.

These three filters provide a structured way to evaluate individual stocks within the memory ecosystem, from DRAM and NAND producers to equipment suppliers and controller solution providers.

EN_A: So the Hong Kong property sector has been underperforming the Hang Seng Index recently. What is going on?
EN_B: Yes, the sector has fallen about 3% since last Friday. The trigger was a crackdown by Chinese regulators on illegal cross-border securities trading through brokers like Futu. Investors are worried this could lead to tighter capital outflow controls from mainland China.
EN_A: So the market is worried that mainland buyers won't be able to move money into Hong Kong to buy property. How real is that fear?
EN_B: So far, it looks like a precautionary sell-off. There is no evidence of imminent tightening. But if it does happen, it would impact some demand, especially for luxury primary homes in areas like Kai Tak.
EN_A: How big is the mainland buyer segment in the Hong Kong market anyway? Are they really that important?
EN_B: That depends on how you define "mainland Chinese." According to Centaline data, by value, so-called mainland buyers account for 32% of transactions. But here is the catch. That data uses Mandarin pinyin of last names. So it includes mainlanders who already live in Hong Kong, and even some local Hong Kongers with names like Wang.
EN_A: So the actual impact could be much smaller than the headline number suggests?
EN_B: Exactly. We estimate that mainland-based mainland Chinese account for only about 15% of transaction value, or roughly 10% by volume. Even in a worst-case scenario where all that demand disappears, full-year transaction value would still grow 12% year on year. So the market may be overreacting.
EN_A: How does that break down by district and property type?
EN_B: Luxury primary homes would be hit the hardest, especially in Kai Tak. We estimate 40% to 50% of buyers there are mainland-based. Next would be Wong Chuk Hang. Developers with more diversified businesses, like SHKP, Sino Land, or Cheung Kong, are more defensive.
EN_A: If capital controls do tighten, does that change your upcycle thesis for the housing market?
EN_B: Not at all. Our upcycle does not rely solely on mainland-based buyers. There are many other tailwinds. Population growth from incoming talent, very low vacancy rates, a strong financial services industry, and genuine demand from mainlanders who already live in Hong Kong.
EN_A: Tell me more about that last group. The mainlanders already in Hong Kong.
EN_B: We estimate there are about 200,000 to 300,000 mainland Chinese households who have lived in Hong Kong for less than seven years. They had to pay an extra stamp duty until early 2024. Since then, only about 15,000 of them have bought a home. That is just 5% to 8% penetration. So there is still a lot of pent-up demand.
EN_A: And what about home prices this year? Are they rising too fast?
EN_B: Year to date, prices are already up 9%, faster than we expected. Our full-year forecast is 10% to 15%, so there is still room for another 1% to 6% growth in the second half. Interestingly, if capital controls slow down price growth a bit, that actually reduces the risk of the government imposing more cooling measures.
EN_A: Like higher stamp duties?
EN_B: Exactly. In February, the government already raised the stamp duty on properties worth over 100 million Hong Kong dollars from 4.25% to 6.5%. If prices keep rising too fast, they could easily extend that higher rate to properties worth 50 million. So a slow bull market is actually a good thing.
EN_A: What are the bigger risks you worry about, beyond capital controls?
EN_B: The biggest risk is a stock market crash. The Hang Seng Index has historically been highly correlated with Hong Kong home prices. Another is an unexpected cut to talent scheme quotas. And a near-term overhang is the risk of rate hikes. If the US raises rates, Hong Kong might follow. But the report argues that rates are not the dominant factor for home prices.

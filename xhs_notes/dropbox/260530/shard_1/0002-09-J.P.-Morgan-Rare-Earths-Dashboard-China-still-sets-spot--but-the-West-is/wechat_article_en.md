# The Rare Earth Market Has Split in Two: China Sets the Spot Price, But the West Is Now Paying for Security

The rare earth market is no longer a single market. It has bifurcated into two distinct pricing regimes, and investors who treat it as one will misprice risk. China still controls the marginal ton of neodymium-praseodymium oxide that clears the global spot market, but a parallel pricing structure is now emerging outside China, anchored by government-backed offtake agreements at USD 110 per kilogram. This is not a temporary divergence. It is a structural shift driven by the April 2025 export controls, which proved that China's leverage extends far beyond oxide supply into metal making, alloying, and magnet manufacturing. The immediate disruption was severe: monthly Chinese magnet exports to the United States fell 92 percent in May 2025, from 584 tonnes to just 46 tonnes. Volumes recovered as licensing approvals began flowing, but the controls were never lifted. The message was unmistakable.

The strategic question for investors is no longer whether China will use rare earths as a geopolitical tool. It already has. The question is how to value ex-China supply chains that are being built on strategic pricing floors, not on marginal spot economics. The answer depends on execution risk, and the evidence from existing hydrometallurgical plants is sobering. Of 14 hydromet plants examined by an independent expert, nine never reached design capacity. The gap between a feasibility study and a functioning separation plant is where most equity value is lost. This article argues that the rare earth investment debate has shifted from policy support to metallurgical execution, and that the winners will be those who can deliver separated material at commercial scale outside China. The rest will be capital traps.

## The April 2025 Export Controls Were a Watershed, Not a Spike

The conventional narrative after April 2025 was that the export controls caused a price spike that has since faded. This is true but misleading. NdPr oxide did spike to approximately USD 120 per kilogram in April 2025 before settling back to around USD 103 per kilogram. But the pattern that matters is not the price level; it is the structural change in how the market operates. Before April 2025, the global rare earth market was integrated and largely priced on Chinese domestic spot. After April 2025, it is split. The controls targeted seven medium and heavy rare earths, not NdPr, but the disruption was immediate because China controls not just oxide but the downstream processing steps. Magnet exports collapsed not because NdPr was blocked, but because the licensing requirements for magnets and metal products created an administrative bottleneck that took months to resolve.

The deeper implication is that China's leverage is not about any single element. It is about the fact that China controls approximately 90 percent of rare earth processing capacity, including the separation, metal making, and magnet manufacturing steps that transform oxide into usable materials. Even if NdPr oxide were freely traded, a country that cannot process it into magnets is still dependent. The April controls demonstrated this dependency with surgical precision. The recovery in exports from July 2025 onward shows that customers can adapt to licensing, but it also shows that China retains the ability to turn the tap on and off. The controls were not removed. They remain in force as a standing threat. Any investor who assumes the market has returned to pre-April normalcy is ignoring the permanent change in the operating environment.

## Strategic Pricing Floors Are Now the Anchor for Ex-China Supply, Not Spot Markets

The most important development in rare earth pricing over the past year is not the level of the Chinese spot price. It is the emergence of explicit strategic pricing floors for ex-China supply. The US Department of Defense agreement with MP Materials established a USD 110 per kilogram floor for NdPr. Lynas Rare Earths subsequently secured a similar floor in its revised offtake agreement with JARE, also at USD 110 per kilogram. Lynas's agreement with the US government incorporates a comparable pricing anchor. These are not market prices. They are government-underwritten floors designed to make ex-China production economically viable even if Chinese spot prices fall below that level.

This matters because the economics of ex-China rare earth production are fundamentally different from Chinese production. Chinese rare earth mines and processing plants benefit from lower environmental costs, established infrastructure, and decades of operational experience. An Australian or American project must compete against that cost base while also bearing the risk of political disruption and higher capital costs. Without a pricing floor, most ex-China projects would be uneconomic at Chinese spot prices. With a floor, they become viable for a defined volume of contracted output. The floor does not cover all production. It covers the volume that governments are willing to underwrite. But it creates a pricing bifurcation: the Chinese spot market for marginal tonnes, and a strategic pricing regime for government-backed ex-China tonnes.

The current spot price of approximately USD 103 per kilogram sits just below the USD 110 floor. This is a critical observation. It means that the strategic floor is not a hypothetical backstop. It is already acting as a de facto price anchor for ex-China supply. If Chinese spot prices fall further, the floor becomes more important, not less. It guarantees that contracted ex-China producers receive a price that covers their costs, even if the Chinese market clears lower. This is exactly the kind of structure that can attract long-term capital to projects that would otherwise be too risky. But it also creates a dependency on government willingness to maintain those floors, which is a political risk that investors must assess.

## The Equity Debate Has Shifted From Policy Support to Execution, and the Track Record Is Poor

The first wave of rare earth equity investment was driven by policy announcements. Governments declared critical mineral strategies, allocated funding, and signed offtake agreements. The second wave, which is now underway, is driven by execution. Can these projects actually build separation plants that work? The evidence from existing hydrometallurgical facilities is not encouraging. An independent hydrometallurgist found that nine of 14 hydromet plants never reached their design capacity. Most feasibility studies assume full ramp-up within 12 to 24 months, but the historical record suggests that is optimistic by a wide margin.

For Lynas Rare Earths, the key execution questions are the ramp-up of the Kalgoorlie processing facility, the ability to deliver meaningful heavy rare earth volumes, the negotiation of downstream conversion agreements, and the succession of the CEO. Lynas is the only producer in the coverage universe that is already making separated rare earths at commercial scale outside China, with heavy rare earth production underway. That gives it a significant advantage in execution credibility. But it does not eliminate the risk. Kalgoorlie has been delayed before, and the integration of heavy rare earth separation adds technical complexity.

For Arafura Rare Earths, the debate has shifted from funding to construction and hydrometallurgical execution. The company is targeting approximately 4,440 tonnes of NdPr per year from its Nolans project, but it has not yet demonstrated that it can operate a separation plant at commercial scale. The feasibility study assumes a ramp-up profile that the historical data suggests is aggressive. For Iluka Resources, the Eneabba project with approximately 5,500 tonnes of NdPr capacity is the key value driver, but capital intensity, offtake arrangements, and multi-feed commissioning risk remain live issues. Governments can help de-risk capital by providing loans, guarantees, or offtake agreements. They cannot de-risk metallurgy. If a separation plant does not work, no amount of government support will make it produce rare earths.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Sustainability of Strategic Pricing and the Real Cost of Ex-China Supply

The report provides a clear framework for understanding the current state of the rare earth market, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. The first is the sustainability of the strategic pricing floor. The USD 110 per kilogram floor is currently underwritten by the US government and embedded in commercial offtake agreements. But what happens if the political landscape changes? A future administration could decide that critical mineral subsidies are not a priority. The floor is not a legally permanent structure. It is a policy choice, and policy choices can be reversed. The report does not model the scenario in which the floor is removed or allowed to lapse, and that scenario is worth serious consideration.

The second unanswered question is the real all-in cost of ex-China rare earth production. The report notes that feasibility studies assume rapid ramp-ups, but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of operating costs for each project. The strategic floor of USD 110 per kilogram may be sufficient to cover cash costs at steady-state production, but it may not be sufficient to cover the full capital cost of building the plant, including cost overruns and delays. If the true all-in cost is closer to USD 130 or USD 140 per kilogram, then even the strategic floor may not be enough to generate acceptable returns. Investors need to stress-test the cost assumptions in the feasibility studies, not just accept them.

The third question is the role of heavy rare earths. The April 2025 controls explicitly targeted dysprosium, terbium, and other heavy rare earths, and Lynas has started producing dysprosium outside China. But the report does not fully explore the pricing dynamics for heavy rare earths or the potential for supply constraints in that segment. Heavy rare earths are critical for high-performance magnets used in defense and advanced manufacturing applications. If heavy rare earth supply becomes the binding constraint, the value of ex-China heavy rare earth production could be significantly higher than the current pricing suggests. The report touches on this but does not develop the thesis.

## A Decision Framework for Rare Earth Investors: Three Questions Before Committing Capital

Investors evaluating rare earth opportunities should apply a structured decision framework based on three questions. The first question is about pricing: what price realization does the project assume, and how does that compare to the strategic floor and the Chinese spot price? Projects that rely entirely on Chinese spot pricing are vulnerable to a decline in that price. Projects that have contracted offtake at or above the strategic floor have more revenue certainty. The key metric is the proportion of expected production that is covered by floor-protected contracts. The higher that proportion, the lower the pricing risk.

The second question is about execution: what is the track record of the management team in building and operating hydrometallurgical facilities? The report's finding that nine of 14 hydromet plants never reached design capacity is a powerful warning. Investors should look for teams that have actually operated separation plants, not just built feasibility studies. They should also examine the contingency built into the capital cost estimate and the schedule. If the feasibility study assumes a 12-month ramp-up, the investor should assume 24 months and recalculate the returns accordingly.

The third question is about government dependency: how much of the project's value depends on continued government support? This includes direct financial support, offtake agreements, and regulatory approvals. The strategic floor is a form of government support. So are loans from export credit agencies and defense department contracts. These are real sources of value, but they are also sources of political risk. Investors should assess the probability that government support continues over the life of the project, and they should consider what happens to the project's economics if that support is reduced or withdrawn.

## The November 2026 Policy Cliff Is the Next Catalyst That Could Reshape the Market

The report identifies a specific date that deserves more attention than it typically receives: 10 November 2026. This is the scheduled expiry of the suspension of China's October 2025 "second wave" export controls. If the suspension is not extended, amended, or withdrawn, the broader controls could return. The April 2025 controls remain in force regardless, so the baseline is already restrictive. But the October controls would add additional restrictions on rare earth processing technology, magnet technology, and foreign-made products that use Chinese rare earth inputs or know-how.

The November 2026 cliff creates a natural catalyst for the market. As the date approaches, customers will need to decide whether to stockpile, accelerate supply chain diversification, or accept the risk of disruption. Producers with ex-China capacity will be in a stronger position to negotiate contracts. Governments will face pressure to extend or expand their support for domestic production. Investors should watch the policy signals from both China and the US in the months leading up to November 2026. The outcome of that policy decision could determine the trajectory of rare earth prices and equity valuations for the next several years.

The rare earth market is no longer a simple commodity market driven by supply and demand for magnets and EVs. It is a strategic market where pricing is increasingly set by government policy, and where execution risk is the primary differentiator between winners and losers. Investors who understand this shift and apply a rigorous decision framework will be better positioned to navigate the volatility. Those who treat rare earths as just another metal cycle are likely to be surprised.

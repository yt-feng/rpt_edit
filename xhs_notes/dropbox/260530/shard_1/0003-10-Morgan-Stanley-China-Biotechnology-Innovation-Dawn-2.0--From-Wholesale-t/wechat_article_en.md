# China Biotech's Next Re-Rating Depends on Value Capture, Not More Deal Volume

The first re-rating of China biotechnology was about global relevance. Outbound licensing proved that assets originated in China belong in the global innovation pool. That debate is now settled. The market has moved on from asking whether China can innovate to asking who captures the economic value of that innovation. The next leg of sector performance will reward companies that retain more risk-adjusted net present value, not those that simply sign more licensing agreements.

The transition from wholesale to bespoke deal structures is the defining strategic shift in China biotech today. Straight licensing, where an originator sells global rights for upfront payments and milestones, has been the dominant model. It served its purpose. It validated the thesis. But it leaves too much value on the table for companies with the capacity to carry assets further. The evidence is clear: only about 25 percent of China licensing deals involve late-stage or commercial assets, compared to roughly 50 percent for US biotech M&A transactions. That gap represents a discount that is rational but contestable.

Several converging forces now make this discount compressible. Balance sheets are stronger. Median runway for listed China biotech companies has expanded from 2.6 years in fiscal 2023 to 4.9 years by fiscal 2025. Licensing income now drives approximately 70 percent of sector funding. Domestic commercial engines are generating revenue. Global trial experience is accumulating. Regulatory harmonization continues to improve. And artificial intelligence is accelerating asset quality convergence. The question is no longer whether China biotech can retain more value, but which companies have the combination of risk capacity and asset scarcity to do so.

## The Structural Discount in China Licensing Is Not Permanent, But It Requires Proof of Risk Absorption

The valuation gap between China out-licensing and US biotech M&A is not arbitrary. It reflects a rational allocation of risk. When a Chinese company licenses an early-stage asset to a multinational, the MNC absorbs development risk, regulatory risk, chemistry manufacturing and controls risk, translatability risk, and commercialization risk. The discount on retained value is the price of transferring those risks. The market prices this correctly.

What is changing is the ability of select China companies to absorb more of those risks themselves. Longer cash runways mean fewer forced transactions. Licensing income provides a buffer that reduces dependency on upfront payments. Domestic revenue from approved products generates operating leverage. Experience conducting global multi-regional clinical trials builds credibility with regulators and partners alike.

The mechanism for value capture is straightforward. Companies that can carry assets into later-stage development before externalizing retain a larger share of economics. They negotiate from strength rather than necessity. They can structure co-development agreements, NewCo formations, or profit-sharing arrangements that preserve upside. The report estimates that NewCo and co-development structures could lift retained NPV by roughly 35 to 41 percent compared to straight licensing, when risk, dilution, and obligations are manageable.

But this is not a universal opportunity. It applies only to companies that have the financial capacity to delay externalization and the asset quality to command better terms. For the rest, licensing remains the rational endpoint. The market must distinguish between those two populations.

## Risk-Bearing Capacity and Innovation Scarcity Are the Two Axes That Determine Value Capture

Two factors determine whether a China biotech company can move up the value capture ladder. The first is risk-bearing capacity. This is a composite measure that includes cash position, licensing income, domestic commercial revenue, and overseas clinical execution experience. Companies that score high on this dimension can fund later-stage development themselves, reducing the need to sell rights early.

The second factor is innovation scarcity. This measures whether an asset is first-in-class, best-in-class, or globally differentiated to the point where partners have few substitutes. Scarcity commands better terms because the partner's alternative is not a different licensing deal but no deal at all. Where scarcity is high, the originator can demand co-development economics, equity participation, or retained territorial rights.

These two axes create a natural framework for stock selection. Companies that score high on both dimensions are positioned to become value-retaining leaders. They can move from wholesale licensing toward co-development, NewCo structures, and eventually M&A-like economics. Companies that score high on risk capacity but low on scarcity may still improve deal terms incrementally, but they lack the differentiated assets needed for structural shifts. Companies with scarcity but weak risk capacity are validation stories waiting to happen, but they remain dependent on partners for development funding.

The report identifies four companies as repeat monetizers where value capture is becoming visible: Keymed, Innovent, BeOne, and Abbisko. Each represents a different test case for the thesis. Keymed tests whether NewCo structures can be repeatable beyond upfront licensing. Innovent tests co-development economics through US profit share, ex-US royalties, and retained influence. Abbisko tests whether licensing-led value capture can work through a single differentiated asset validated by a major partner.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Geopolitical and Commercialization Boundaries

The report is analytically rigorous on deal structures, risk allocation, and company-level positioning. But it leaves several important questions unresolved. The most significant is the geopolitical boundary. US-China tensions create an overhang that is difficult to model and impossible to hedge completely. Even as regulatory harmonization improves, the political risk of technology transfer restrictions, investment screening, or market access barriers remains real. The report acknowledges that geopolitics and US commercialization still cap near-term value capture, but it does not fully explore what happens if those constraints tighten rather than ease.

A second unresolved question is the commercialization capability of China biotech in the US market. Licensing and co-development structures still depend on partners to execute in the world's largest pharmaceutical market. No China biotech company has yet demonstrated the ability to build a meaningful US commercial presence from scratch. The report's framework assumes that value capture improves as companies carry assets further, but carrying an asset to US regulatory approval is different from successfully commercializing it. The skills required for clinical development and regulatory affairs are different from those required for pricing, access, sales force deployment, and payer negotiation. Until at least one China biotech company proves it can commercialize in the US independently, the ceiling on value capture remains unclear.

A third question concerns the sustainability of the NewCo model. NewCo structures can lift retained NPV, but they also introduce new risks: dilution from follow-on financing, governance leakage as control shifts to new investors, and exit uncertainty. The report shows that NewCo can narrow the discount to M&A, but it does not fully address the conditions under which NewCo value is realized versus destroyed. If NewCo exits underperform or if secondary offerings dilute originators heavily, the retained economics may prove illusory. The market needs to see a full cycle of NewCo formation, development, and exit before it can price this structure with confidence.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Navigating the Transition

Investors evaluating China biotech today need a framework that goes beyond headline deal value and upfront payments. The report provides the raw material for such a framework, but it requires synthesis into actionable criteria. Here is how to think about it.

First, separate deal optics from economic reality. Headline total deal value is the most misleading metric in biotech licensing. It is dominated by long-dated commercial milestones that have low probability of realization. The real signals are total residual value retained, net upfront value after cost-sharing and retained obligations, and quantified retained economics such as royalties, profit-sharing, and post-dilution NewCo ownership. If a company announces a multi-billion-dollar deal but cannot articulate what it retains after risk allocation and obligations, assume the retained value is low.

Second, assess control retention. Governance rights, development influence, and commercialization role matter more than the structure label. A "strategic collaboration" that gives the partner all decision rights is not fundamentally different from a licensing deal. A co-development agreement where the originator retains meaningful influence over trial design, regulatory strategy, and commercial planning is genuinely different. Read the economics through the lens of control.

Third, evaluate the credibility of the monetization path. In licensing, this means partner quality and milestone probability. In NewCo, it means investor quality, financing visibility, and exit pathway. In co-development, it means whether the incremental economics compensate for the added funding and execution burden. The same structure can be value-creating for one company and value-destructive for another, depending on risk capacity and asset scarcity.

Fourth, look for repeatability. One-off headline success is not proof of platform quality. Companies that can execute multiple value-capture transactions across different assets demonstrate that their capabilities are institutional rather than asset-specific. Repeat monetizers are the investable subset because their model is replicable and their execution risk is diversifiable.

Finally, maintain a healthy skepticism about retained value until it is realized. The gap between announced deal terms and actual cash flows is wide. Milestones are probabilistic. Royalties are subject to commercial performance. NewCo equity is subject to dilution and exit timing. The market should demand evidence of realization before awarding valuation premiums for deal sophistication.

## The Unresolved Questions That Make This Thesis Worth Following Closely

The Innovation Dawn 2.0 thesis is compelling because it identifies a structural shift that is already underway. Licensing momentum is accelerating. Balance sheets are improving. Regulatory alignment is progressing. Asset quality is rising. The conditions for value capture are better today than they have ever been for China biotech.

But the thesis also rests on assumptions that have not yet been proven. Can China biotech companies truly internalize global development risk at scale? Will the NewCo model deliver realized returns that justify the complexity? Can any China biotech company build a US commercial presence that breaks the dependency on partners? And how much of the value capture opportunity is already priced into current valuations?

These questions are not weaknesses of the report. They are the natural consequence of a thesis that is ahead of the data. The report provides the framework for answering them. The answers will come from watching the next wave of pivotal MRCT readouts, the next set of NewCo formations and exits, and the next generation of deal structures.

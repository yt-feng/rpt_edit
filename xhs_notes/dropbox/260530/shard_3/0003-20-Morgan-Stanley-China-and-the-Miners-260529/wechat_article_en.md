# China’s Demand Slowdown Is Not a Collapse: Why the Mining Supercycle Is Shifting, Not Ending

The narrative is tempting to write off. China’s April industrial production data landed at 4.1% year-on-year, a sharp deceleration from March’s 5.7%. Property new starts plunged 27.1% year-on-year. Fixed asset investment fell 9.4%. Retail sales hit a record low outside of pandemic lockdowns. For anyone tracking the Australian mining sector, these numbers look like a flashing red warning. But the story beneath the surface is far more nuanced, and for strategic investors, it points to a critical rotation rather than a broad retreat.

The core thesis is this: China’s demand for mineral resources is undergoing a structural rebalancing away from property-driven bulk commodities and toward energy transition metals and export-linked industrial production. This shift is not a short-term cyclical wobble. It is a multi-year transformation that will reward exposure to copper, aluminium, and select coal assets while punishing over-reliance on iron ore and steel. The April data confirms the transition is accelerating, and the market is still pricing many miners for the old cycle.

The energy shock that hit China in April—weighing on energy-intensive sectors and consumer confidence—is a temporary amplifier of a deeper trend. The oil price spike accelerated the slowdown in discretionary consumption and property, but the underlying drivers of export-led manufacturing and AI-related capital expenditure remain intact. The second half recovery that economists are forecasting will not lift all boats equally. The key question for investors is not whether China is slowing, but which commodities will benefit from the new growth architecture and which will be left behind.

## The Property Collapse Is Now a Structural Drag on Iron Ore, and the Data Shows No Bottom Yet

April’s property data was unambiguously dire. New starts fell 27.1% year-on-year, worsening from March’s 17.1% decline. Floor space sold dropped 10.3%. Completions fell 19%. Fixed asset investment in property plunged 20.1%. The secondary market sales bounce in top-tier cities during March and April has already faded sharply in May, and the divergence between high-tier and lower-tier cities is widening. Lower-tier markets continue to weaken in both listings and prices.

This matters because property has been the single largest driver of steel demand in China for two decades. With new starts now in a sustained double-digit decline, the implications for iron ore are severe. Crude steel output fell 2.8% year-on-year in April, and domestic steel apparent consumption dropped 3.1%. Pig iron production is down 4% year-to-date. Iron ore port inventories, while slowly drawing, remain elevated. The ban lift on seaborne cargoes from one major producer may ease some supply tightness, but it does not change the demand reality.

The critical insight here is that the property adjustment is not a cyclical downturn that will resolve with a policy stimulus. It is a structural deleveraging of the largest asset class in China. The government has shown no appetite for a broad-based property rescue. Selective gains in top-tier secondary markets are not a precursor to a national recovery. For iron ore investors, the question is not whether demand will recover to prior peaks, but at what lower equilibrium level the market will settle. The April data suggests we are not there yet.

## Aluminium Is the Quiet Winner of China’s Capacity Ceiling and Supply Disruption

While most commodity production data in April was weak, aluminium stood out. Production rose 3.1% year-on-year to 3.9 million tonnes, driven by capacity resumption in Liaoning. But the more important number is that total operating capacity in China has reached 45.3 million tonnes, which is at the government-imposed ceiling. There is limited upside from here.

This is a structural positive for aluminium pricing and for miners with exposure. China’s capacity cap means that any demand growth—whether from power grid expansion, electric vehicle manufacturing, or construction—must be met by imports or by drawing down inventories. April’s aluminium and product exports surged 23% month-on-month and 15% year-on-year to the highest level since November 2024, driven by supply disruptions in the Middle East that tightened ex-China markets and widened the export arbitrage.

The paradox is that Chinese aluminium inventories rose to all-time highs even as exports surged. This suggests that domestic production is still outpacing demand in the near term, but the ceiling on capacity means this imbalance is temporary. As the second half recovery takes hold and hydro power generation picks up, the inventory build should reverse. For investors, aluminium offers a rare combination of supply constraint and demand tailwind from energy transition and grid investment. The solid margins above 8,600 renminbi per tonne that smelters enjoyed in April are likely to persist.

## The Coal Story Is More Complex Than Headlines Suggest, and the Risk Is Underappreciated

Coal production fell 1% year-on-year and 12.5% month-on-month in April to 386 million tonnes. The month-on-month drop reflects the seasonal end of the winter heating season. But the year-on-year decline, combined with a 13% drop in coal imports, tells a more interesting story. Total coal imports in the first four months of 2026 were down 2% year-on-year.

The conventional view is that China’s coal demand is in structural decline as renewables expand. That is true over the long term, but the near-term dynamics are more supportive. Thermal power generation rose 3.1% year-on-year in April, though its share of total power generation fell to 62% from 66% in March as hydro power ramped up. The key driver for coal in the coming months is the summer peak consumption season. Power plants are likely to restock in May, which should support coal prices.

More importantly, the report flags potential for gas-to-coal switching in North Asia, which would boost demand for Australian thermal coal. Tight met coal supply following a major mine accident in Shanxi province, which triggered safety inspections, adds further support. For investors, the coal thesis is not about long-term demand growth. It is about near-term supply tightness, seasonal demand patterns, and the fact that the market may be overly pessimistic on coal pricing. The risk is that the market is pricing in a structural decline that has not yet materialized in the physical market.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Lithium and Copper Demand Gap

The report provides detailed positioning on lithium and copper, but it leaves several critical questions unanswered. On lithium, the analyst sees merit in taking profits and is equal-weight on one major producer while underweight on another. The key uncertainty cited is the life-of-mine plan for a major lithium asset. But the deeper question is whether the lithium market is entering a period of structural oversupply or whether the current weakness is a correction within a long-term growth trend.

Global electric vehicle adoption continues to rise, but the pace of growth has slowed in key markets. Battery chemistry is shifting toward lower lithium content alternatives. At the same time, new supply from Africa and South America is ramping. The report does not resolve whether the lithium price has found a floor or whether further downside is likely. For investors, this is the most important unresolved question in the battery materials space.

On copper, the report highlights strong import data in April—copper and product imports rose 9% month-on-month and 3% year-on-year—driven by a pullback in copper prices that encouraged Chinese buyers to re-engage. But copper ore and concentrate imports fell 11% month-on-month and 20% year-on-year, as treatment charges fell. The question is whether this divergence signals a near-term supply chain bottleneck or a more persistent structural issue. The report notes that strong sulphuric acid prices supported smelter margins, but it does not fully explore whether the smelter bottleneck will constrain refined copper production in the coming quarters. Given copper’s critical role in electrification and AI-related infrastructure, this is a gap that deserves deeper analysis.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Mapping Commodity Exposure to China’s Diverging Demand Drivers

The April data provides a clear framework for portfolio construction. The key is to map each commodity to China’s three distinct demand drivers: property and infrastructure, export-led manufacturing, and energy transition.

Property and infrastructure demand is in structural decline. Iron ore and steel are the most exposed. Fixed asset investment in highways fell 18.1% year-on-year in April. Cement production dropped 10.8%. The only support for iron ore in the near term is the possibility of modest steel export growth, but steel exports themselves fell 9% year-on-year in April. For investors, iron ore exposure should be concentrated in low-cost producers with strong balance sheets and the ability to weather a prolonged downturn. The preference for one major diversified miner over others in the report reflects this logic: low-cost cash generation and expansion optionality matter more than volume growth.

Export-led manufacturing demand remains resilient. Electric vehicles, machinery, and technology equipment continue to benefit from global AI-related capital expenditure and trade flows. This supports demand for copper, aluminium, and select specialty metals. The report’s overweight on a base metals-focused miner reflects this thesis. Aluminium, in particular, benefits from both export demand and the domestic capacity ceiling.

Energy transition demand is the wild card. Copper and lithium are the primary beneficiaries, but the timing of demand acceleration is uncertain. The report’s cautious stance on lithium suggests that near-term supply dynamics outweigh long-term demand optimism. For copper, the bull case rests on grid investment, data centre construction, and electrification, but the smelter bottleneck and treatment charge dynamics introduce near-term complexity.

The framework for decision-making is clear: overweight commodities with supply constraints and structural demand tailwinds (aluminium, copper), underweight commodities with structural demand headwinds and ample supply (iron ore, steel), and take a tactical approach to commodities where near-term supply tightness coexists with long-term demand uncertainty (coal, lithium).

## The Unresolved Analytical Questions That Make the Full Report Worth Reading

The parsing of this monthly report reveals patterns, but it also raises questions that only the full report can answer. First, how does the China economics team calibrate its second half GDP recovery forecast, and what are the key risks to that view? The report cuts second quarter GDP tracking to 4.5% year-on-year but expects a recovery toward 5% in the second half. The assumptions about oil price trajectory, export resilience, and property stabilization are critical for commodity demand forecasts.

Second, what is the detailed supply outlook for copper concentrates? The divergence between strong refined copper imports and weak concentrate imports suggests a bottleneck in smelting capacity or a shift in trade flows. The full report likely includes a detailed analysis of treatment charges, smelter utilization rates, and the impact of new smelting capacity in Indonesia and elsewhere.

Third, how does the lithium supply-demand balance evolve over the next 12 to 18 months? The report flags life-of-mine plan uncertainty for a major asset, but it does not provide a comprehensive supply-demand model. For investors considering entry points in lithium, this analysis is essential.

Fourth, what is the scenario analysis for coal? The report highlights potential gas-to-coal switching and met coal tightness, but it does not quantify the demand impact under different winter weather scenarios or industrial production paths. Coal is a high-volatility, event-driven commodity, and the full report likely provides the sensitivity analysis that the monthly data alone cannot.

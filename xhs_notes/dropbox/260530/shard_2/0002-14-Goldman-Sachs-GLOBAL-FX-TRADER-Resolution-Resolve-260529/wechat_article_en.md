# The Dollar Is Not Done Weakening, But the Path Will Be More Divided Than the Consensus Expects

The conventional narrative that the US dollar is poised for a sustained decline is missing a critical structural shift. While the currency weakened last week on familiar drivers—hopes of a US-Iran deal and steady CNY appreciation—the broader picture is far more complex. The dollar is actually 1-1.5% stronger on net since the beginning of March, depending on the index used. This is not noise. It reflects two simultaneous structural forces reshaping global FX markets: an AI boom that is restoring foreign demand for dollar assets, and an energy supply bust that is creating divergent growth outcomes across economies. These forces are not temporary. They represent a fundamental repricing of relative terms of trade that will determine currency returns for quarters, not weeks.

The key insight for serious investors is that the dollar is caught between opposing gravitational pulls. On one side, positive headlines around trade deals and geopolitical resolution create room for depreciation. On the other, the structural underpinnings of dollar strength—better growth outcomes, firmer inflation, and widening front-end rate differentials—remain intact. The result is not a simple bearish dollar call. It is a divided dollar outlook where performance will vary dramatically across currency pairs. The opportunity lies not in betting on the dollar's direction, but in identifying which currencies are best positioned for the resolution or escalation of these competing forces.

This report examines five currencies where the interplay of terms of trade, domestic policy, and geopolitical risk creates asymmetric payoff structures. For each, the analysis goes beyond surface-level narratives to identify the second-order implications that most market participants are missing.

## The Hungarian Forint Has a Clear Appreciation Path That Markets Are Underpricing

Two developments in Hungary this week created a powerful tailwind for the forint that is not fully reflected in current positioning. First, the central bank signaled a June rate cut while acknowledging a significant improvement in the inflation outlook. Second, EUR 16.4 billion in EU funds were unblocked—a figure worth over 7% of GDP. These are not independent events. They are connected through a causal chain that runs from EU fund disbursement to lower country risk to a stronger currency to lower inflation to more policy flexibility.

The critical mechanism here is the direction of causation in the FX-rates relationship. The central bank has explicitly framed its ability to cut rates in the context of a stronger forint, making the currency a binding constraint on monetary easing. This means the correlation between FX and rate differentials is negative: as the forint strengthens, the central bank gains room to cut, which in turn supports growth without undermining the currency. This is the opposite of the standard emerging market dynamic where rate cuts weaken the currency. The EU funds provide the fiscal buffer that makes this virtuous cycle sustainable.

The second-order implication is that the forint's appreciation is self-reinforcing. A stronger forint lowers import prices, helping meet the Maastricht inflation criterion for euro adoption. Meeting these criteria further reduces country risk, attracting more capital inflows. The EU funds, which more than offset the impact of higher energy prices on the current account, provide the external balance support that makes this loop credible. Investors should focus on the asymmetry: the risks are skewed toward further appreciation, not depreciation. The initial target of EUR/HUF 350 is likely conservative.

## The AUD/NZD Cross Is About to Break Decisively Lower as Relative Policy Divergence Intensifies

The Australian dollar-New Zealand dollar cross has stalled after a strong run, and the conditions for a meaningful correction are now in place. Three factors are converging. First, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand has shifted decisively hawkish, with three of six committee members preferring a hike and all members agreeing that increasing the OCR at upcoming meetings would likely be necessary. Our economists now expect two hikes this year. Second, the Reserve Bank of Australia has moved in the opposite direction, pushing back its expected final hike. Third, Australian data has broadly disappointed across inflation, employment, and consumer sentiment.

The market is still pricing these developments incompletely. Rate differentials between Australia and New Zealand are showing signs of having peaked, which historically has been a reliable leading indicator for AUD/NZD direction. Positioning is stretched in favor of AUD, and the New Zealand dollar is well positioned to benefit from any de-escalation in global tensions. The terms of trade backdrop remains supportive for AUD due to elevated energy prices, but this is the opposing impulse that has kept the cross from breaking down sooner.

The key question is which force will dominate: the terms of trade support for AUD, or the domestic policy divergence favoring NZD. The evidence increasingly points to the latter. The RBNZ's hawkish shift is more decisive than the RBA's dovish lean, and the data flow is reinforcing this divergence. A normalization in global energy flows would apply clear downward pressure on AUD/NZD, but even without that catalyst, the risk-reward from a relative data and policy perspective is appealing for shorts. The cross is set for a more meaningful correction lower.

## The Swiss Franc's Haven Premium Will Re-emerge Once the SNB Shifts Its Intervention Bias

The Swiss franc has been contained by the Swiss National Bank's intervention bias, but this constraint is about to break. The franc's safe-haven properties are particularly relevant in the current environment, which combines geopolitical tensions, inflationary pressures, and downside European growth risks. The energy shock has elements of all three. Yet the franc has not fully reflected these haven flows because the SNB has been actively capping gains.

The trigger for change is domestic inflation. Headline CPI is already running above the SNB's most recent Q2 projection, and consensus expects a further increase in the May report. This reacceleration should be enough to shift the SNB's intervention bias back to neutral, uncovering the haven benefits that have been suppressed. The upcoming June 18 SNB meeting is the key event to watch. A shift in language around FX management would be the catalyst for significant CHF outperformance.

However, three risks complicate the picture. First, EUR/CHF carry-to-vol is close to post-2015 highs, weakening risk-reward in outright downside positions. Second, gold's shifting correlation to risk is spilling over to the franc via terms of trade linkages, creating idiosyncratic volatility. Third, the June 14 population cap referendum could trigger a re-rating of Swiss growth if passed, posing modest downside risks. These risks do not invalidate the CHF appreciation thesis, but they argue for a more measured entry point and a focus on relative value rather than outright shorts.

## The Colombian Peso Faces Asymmetric Election Risks Despite Recent Strength

The Colombian peso has rallied strongly, driven by energy prices and a supportive carry backdrop. But this strength creates a vulnerability that most investors are overlooking. The peso now screens close to fair value on the GSDEER model, around USD/COP 3500. This contrasts sharply with the 2022 presidential election, when the peso was deeply undervalued and had significant room to depreciate without breaking through fair value.

The first round of Colombia's presidential election takes place this weekend, with left-leaning Ivan Cepeda well positioned to advance to the runoff. The main uncertainty is which right-leaning candidate will also advance. The signal from polls has become noisier due to changing electoral laws, leaving greater scope for surprises in either round and thus greater FX volatility.

The critical insight is that the starting point of COP strength points to asymmetric risks. When a currency is near fair value, the scope for further appreciation is limited, while the potential for depreciation in the event of a less market-friendly outcome is large. This asymmetry is compounded by the fact that the peso's appreciation trend has been driven by factors that are now fully priced: declining external balance vulnerabilities, the central bank's decisive hiking cycle, and the broader risk-supportive backdrop. A negative election outcome would test whether these factors can withstand a shift in political risk perception. The answer is likely no, and the move could be sharp.

## The Canadian Dollar Faces a Trade Policy Headwind That Is Not Priced in Options Markets

The USMCA review is flying under the radar, but the July 1 deadline is approaching, and the implications for the Canadian dollar are more significant than for the Mexican peso. Historical analysis shows that negotiations with Canada have generally been more contentious than those with Mexico, and USD/CAD tends to rise when trade tensions escalate. Yet markets are pricing limited trade-specific premium beyond standard cyclical drivers.

The reason for this limited premium is instructive. The Bank of Canada has incorporated trade policy uncertainty into its reaction function, both in 2018 and more recently. This means that trade shocks impact USD/CAD more durably through rate differentials rather than as a sustained premium over traditional cyclical drivers. Trade tensions weigh on activity, keep the BoC dovish, and widen rate differentials against CAD. This is a more persistent drag than a one-time risk premium.

Three market implications follow. First, USMCA negotiations are likely to be a more important driver for CAD than for MXN. Second, elevated trade uncertainty should keep the Bank of Canada on the more dovish end of the central bank spectrum, weighing on CAD performance. Third, while trade policy uncertainty has historically had less impact on implied ATM or realized volatility, there is value in low-delta USD/CAD options as the deadline approaches. The tail risk of a USMCA exit is credible, and the market is not pricing it adequately.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between AI Flows and Energy Shocks

The report identifies two structural forces reshaping FX markets—the AI boom and the energy supply bust—but does not fully resolve how these forces interact over time. Both are net positive for the dollar, but they operate through different channels and on different time horizons. The AI boom restores foreign demand for dollar assets, supporting the currency through capital flows. The energy shock creates divergent growth outcomes, supporting the dollar through terms of trade and rate differentials.

The open question is whether these forces are reinforcing or offsetting. If the AI boom accelerates investment in US technology infrastructure while the energy shock depresses European growth, the dollar benefits from both channels simultaneously. But if the energy shock triggers a global recession that reduces demand for US exports, the dollar's safe-haven properties could be tested. The report leans toward the reinforcing interpretation, but the evidence is not yet conclusive.

A second unanswered question is how long the energy shock's growth impact will persist after the Strait begins to open. The report argues that product shortages and elevated prices will continue to drive divergent growth outcomes for some time, but does not specify the mechanism or duration. This is a critical variable for positioning in currencies like AUD and NZD that are sensitive to energy prices.

## A Decision Framework for Positioning Across the Divided Dollar Landscape

Given the complexity of the current environment, investors need a systematic framework for allocating across currencies. The following decision matrix captures the key variables:

First, identify the primary driver for each currency pair. For HUF, it is the virtuous cycle of EU funds, lower country risk, and policy flexibility. For AUD/NZD, it is relative monetary policy divergence. For CHF, it is the SNB's intervention bias and domestic inflation. For COP, it is election risk and fair value proximity. For CAD, it is trade policy uncertainty and the BoC's reaction function.

Second, assess the asymmetry of outcomes. Currencies near fair value, like COP, have larger downside risks than upside potential in the event of shocks. Currencies with clear appreciation paths, like HUF, offer more favorable risk-reward. Currencies caught between opposing forces, like AUD/NZD, require a view on which force will dominate.

Third, consider the correlation structure. The report notes that the FX market remains highly correlated and exceptionally stable. This means that positions should be evaluated not just on their individual merits but on how they interact with each other and with the broader dollar narrative. A portfolio of long HUF, short AUD/NZD, and long CHF provides diversification across different drivers while maintaining a consistent view on the divided dollar outlook.

Fourth, monitor the key catalysts. For HUF, it is the pace of EU fund disbursement and the central bank's rate cut trajectory. For AUD/NZD, it is the relative data flow and RBNZ versus RBA policy decisions. For CHF, it is the SNB's June 18 meeting and the May inflation report. For COP, it is the election results and the runoff dynamics. For CAD, it is the USMCA negotiations and the July 1 deadline.

## Warsh and Fedspeak point to patience

Chair Warsh leaned dovish at the ECB's Sintra forum, which is consistent with our read of his post-FOMC press conference two weeks ago. He remained optimistic about the medium-term disinflationary impact of AI, arguing that while AI is boosting demand in the near term, supply side gains should follow, even if they are harder to measure. In our view, emphasizing hard-to-observe supply improvements gives policymakers more latitude in the reaction function. Warsh also downplayed government statistics, repeating his “echoes of history” line from the June presser.

Warsh noted that inflation risks and expectations have come down over the past four weeks, likely alluding to developments in the Middle East and lower crude oil prices. His framing appeared closer to dovish policymakers, who have attributed elevated inflation partly to energy prices and tariffs.

Warsh did not give any signal of the near-term policy path. He pushed back against forward guidance and expressed skepticism about the dot plot, suggesting the dots are likely to remain in place for now but could be revisited.

Overall, Warsh's comments reduce the likelihood of a July hike. We think he will favor a wait-and-see approach and avoid any policy shift until the Fed receives recommendations from the task forces.

Broader Fedspeak this week also pointed to patience. Although hawkish officials such as Goolsbee, Barkin, Kashkari and Hammack expressed concern about sticky inflation, they did not commit to a July hike. Lower commodity prices also raised a bar for policy tightening in the near term as SF Fed President Daly, a dove, saw lower crude oil prices as “hope for relief” and assessed the current stance of monetary policy as slightly restrictive. This week’s Fedspeak supports our call for the Fed to remain on hold for the foreseeable future.

## King-sized task

Warsh indicated that some members of the announced task forces are likely to be announced next week.

Reportedly, former BOE Governor Mervyn King is likely to co-chair the Fed's communications task force. King's views appear broadly aligned with Warsh's pushback against forward guidance and apprehension about the dot plot. In a June 2024 NBER paper, King argued that "there is no time-invariant policy reaction function" that can describe a central bank's policy intentions. He noted that the private sector does not share the central bank's outlook and mainly needs to understand the reaction function. In his view, forward guidance conflates the two. King was also critical of the dot plot, arguing that a central bank's attempt to forecast its own policy rate under genuine uncertainty can damage credibility. Consistent with Bernanke's BOE review, King argued that forecast

communication should focus more on risks around the outlook than on central tendencies.

Overall, King's reported role reinforces the idea that the communications task force may recommend a less explicit Fed communication strategy.

## SCOTUS ruling favors Cook, but tail risk remains

The Supreme Court ruled that Fed Governor Lisa Cook can remain on the Board, rejecting the Trump administration's bid to remove her. Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Kavanaugh joined the Court's three liberal justices in a 5-4 decision in Cook's favor.

The ruling appears to rest on procedural grounds. The Court did not decide whether the allegations cited by the administration would satisfy the Federal Reserve Act's "for cause" removal standard. Instead, it noted that the ultimate question depends partly on the underlying facts, which the Court did not address.

This leaves some ambiguity around the scope of “cause.” In principle, the administration could restart the process with stronger procedural safeguards. That said, the decision also underscored the Fed’s unique institutional status, emphasizing that both Fed independence and the “appearance of independence” are key to its design.

Overall, the ruling supports Fed independence, but does not fully eliminate the risk of another removal attempt. Following the verdict, President Trump doubled down, promising to take “appropriate action immediately” to prevent Cook from making important decisions. Repeated threats to Fed independence could increase the likelihood that Powell remains on the Board for longer. Moreover, because the case was decided on procedural grounds and left the definition of “cause” unresolved, the administration could also attempt to restart its probe into Powell with proper procedural safeguards. Note, NEC director Hassett in his Bloomberg interview today expressed concerns about Powell staying at Fed, describing it as “extremely unorthox.”

## The June Payrolls Report Was Dovish on Net, But the Underlying Details Reveal a Labor Market That Is More Resilient Than the Headline Suggests

The headline slowdown in nonfarm payrolls to 57,000 is attention-grabbing, but it is misleading if taken at face value. The deceleration was largely driven by a reversal of temporary factors that had boosted the May print. Leisure and hospitality employment fell by 61,000 in June and was revised down by 67,000 over the prior two months, accounting for nearly all of the negative revisions to the headline number. Local government employment also retraced after an outsized gain in May. These are not signs of broad-based weakness; they are statistical payback.

When you strip out these volatile components, the picture changes. Employment growth in the cyclical sector improved, and hiring in large services sectors remained positive. The breadth of job gains was solid. This suggests that the underlying demand for labor is still intact, even if the monthly data is noisy. The three-month average pace of job gains has slowed to 111,000, which is consistent with a labor market that is cooling from unsustainable levels but not collapsing.

The unemployment rate falling to 4.2 percent from 4.3 percent might seem like a straightforward positive, but the household survey details tell a more cautious story. The decline in the unemployment rate was driven by a sharp drop in labor force participation, particularly among the 25-34 age cohort. This segment is often seen as more susceptible to AI-related displacement risk, but the decline was broad across education levels, suggesting it is not solely a technology story. Labor force flows were also negative, with the job finding rate ticking down slightly and measures of layoffs remaining sideways.

The implication is that the improvement in the unemployment rate may not be sustainable. It is not that the labor market is weak; it is that the composition of the improvement is fragile. Alternative data sources are mixed. JOLTS job openings remained elevated in May, but the Conference Board labor differential dropped to a multiyear low. The message is clear: the labor market is in a transition phase, and the Fed should not interpret the June data as evidence of a sustained move lower in unemployment.

## Chair Warsh Is Deliberately Creating Policy Latitude by Emphasizing Hard-to-Measure Supply-Side Improvements and Pushing Back Against Forward Guidance

Chair Warsh's appearance at the ECB's Sintra forum was notable for what it revealed about his thinking. He leaned dovish, consistent with his post-FOMC press conference two weeks ago. He remained optimistic about the medium-term disinflationary impact of AI, arguing that while AI is boosting demand in the near term, supply-side gains should follow, even if they are harder to measure. This is a crucial framing. By emphasizing supply improvements that are difficult to observe or quantify, Warsh is giving himself and the Fed more latitude in the reaction function. If inflation remains elevated but can be attributed to transitory demand-side factors that will eventually be offset by supply-side gains, then the Fed does not need to tighten further.

Warsh also downplayed government statistics, repeating his "echoes of history" line from the June press conference. This is a subtle but important signal. It suggests that the Fed is becoming less reliant on noisy monthly data points and more focused on the broader narrative. He noted that inflation risks and expectations have come down over the past four weeks, likely alluding to developments in the Middle East and lower crude oil prices. His framing appeared closer to dovish policymakers who have attributed elevated inflation partly to energy prices and tariffs.

Most importantly, Warsh did not give any signal of the near-term policy path. He pushed back against forward guidance and expressed skepticism about the dot plot, suggesting the dots are likely to remain in place for now but could be revisited. This is consistent with a broader shift toward strategic ambiguity. The Fed does not want to box itself in. It wants to keep its options open, and that means saying less rather than more.

Broader Fedspeak this week also pointed to patience. Even hawkish officials such as Goolsbee, Barkin, Kashkari, and Hammack expressed concern about sticky inflation but did not commit to a July hike. Lower commodity prices raised the bar for policy tightening in the near term. San Francisco Fed President Daly, a dove, saw lower crude oil prices as "hope for relief" and assessed the current stance of monetary policy as only slightly restrictive. The consensus view among Fed officials is that the best course of action is to wait and see.

## The Likely Appointment of Mervyn King to Co-Chair the Fed's Communications Task Force Signals a Move Toward Less Explicit Forward Guidance and a Greater Focus on Risk Communication

The reported likely appointment of former Bank of England Governor Mervyn King to co-chair the Fed's communications task force is one of the most important institutional developments for monetary policy in years. King's views are well documented and broadly aligned with Warsh's skepticism about forward guidance and the dot plot. In a June 2024 NBER paper, King argued that there is no time-invariant policy reaction function that can describe a central bank's policy intentions. He noted that the private sector does not share the central bank's outlook and mainly needs to understand the reaction function. In his view, forward guidance conflates the two.

King was also critical of the dot plot, arguing that a central bank's attempt to forecast its own policy rate under genuine uncertainty can damage credibility. Consistent with Bernanke's review of the Bank of England, King argued that forecast communication should focus more on risks around the outlook than on central tendencies. This is a fundamentally different philosophy from the one that has guided the Fed for the past decade.

The implication is clear: the communications task force is likely to recommend a less explicit Fed communication strategy. This does not mean the Fed will become opaque or uncommunicative. Rather, it means the Fed will shift from telling markets what it plans to do to explaining how it will react to different scenarios. This is a move from prescription to contingency. For investors, this means that the dot plot may become less important and that Fed speeches and minutes will need to be read with an eye on the balance of risks rather than on the central tendency of forecasts.

This shift has second-order implications for market volatility. If the Fed is less explicit about its intended path, markets will have to infer the reaction function from a wider set of signals. This could lead to more frequent repricing of rate expectations, but it could also reduce the risk of large, discontinuous moves when the Fed deviates from its own forecasts. The net effect is likely to be a market that is more attentive to data and less reliant on Fed guidance.

## The Supreme Court Ruling on Governor Cook Supports Fed Independence But Leaves the Definition of "Cause" Unresolved, Creating a Tail Risk That Could Keep Chair Powell in Place Longer

The Supreme Court's 5-4 ruling that Governor Lisa Cook can remain on the Board was a victory for Fed independence, but it was a narrow one. The ruling rested on procedural grounds. The Court did not decide whether the allegations cited by the administration would satisfy the Federal Reserve Act's "for cause" removal standard. Instead, it noted that the ultimate question depends partly on the underlying facts, which the Court did not address.

This leaves significant ambiguity around the scope of "cause." In principle, the administration could restart the process with stronger procedural safeguards. That said, the decision also underscored the Fed's unique institutional status, emphasizing that both Fed independence and the "appearance of independence" are key to its design. The ruling supports Fed independence but does not fully eliminate the risk of another removal attempt.

Following the verdict, President Trump doubled down, promising to take "appropriate action immediately" to prevent Cook from making important decisions. Repeated threats to Fed independence could increase the likelihood that Powell remains on the Board for longer. Moreover, because the case was decided on procedural grounds and left the definition of "cause" unresolved, the administration could also attempt to restart its probe into Powell with proper procedural safeguards. National Economic Council Director Hassett expressed concerns about Powell staying at the Fed, describing it as "extremely unorthodox."

The strategic implication for investors is that the political risk around Fed leadership is not going away. It is simply being deferred. The unresolved definition of "cause" creates a tail risk that the administration could attempt to remove additional Fed officials, which would further politicize monetary policy. At the same time, the threat of removal could make Fed officials more cautious in their communications and more reluctant to deviate from the administration's preferred policy path. This is a complex dynamic that will play out over months, not weeks.

## The USMCA Renewal and US Trade Policy Shift Toward Annual Reviews Will Institutionalize Volatility and Weigh on Growth, But a Complete Rupture Remains Unlikely

USTR Greer's announcement that the US is opting for a renewal of the USMCA agreement and planning to shift to a system of annual reviews is a significant development. The US could also move to separate bilateral agreements with Canada and Mexico. This is consistent with the expectation that the administration prefers to keep its trade policy tools flexible rather than locked into long-term agreements.

Several trade irritants remain unresolved, but all parties have incentives to strike a deal. A shift to annual reviews or narrower exemptions is likely to institutionalize volatility and weigh on growth, but less so than a complete rupture. The key question for investors is how much uncertainty this creates for supply chains and corporate investment decisions. Annual reviews mean that trade rules can change every year, which makes long-term planning more difficult. Companies will need to build more flexibility into their supply chains, which could increase costs and reduce efficiency.

The net effect is likely to be a modest drag on growth, particularly in sectors that are heavily integrated with Canada and Mexico. But the risk of a complete breakdown in North American trade remains low. All three economies are deeply interconnected, and the costs of a rupture would be too high for any party to bear. The shift to annual reviews is a way for the administration to maintain leverage without triggering a crisis.

First, how will the Fed's shift toward less explicit forward guidance interact with the political pressure from the administration? This could erode the credibility of the Fed's independence, even if the legal framework remains intact.

Second, the report does not fully address the implications of the Fed's new communication strategy for the term premium and the yield curve. The report notes that the shift could reduce the risk of large, discontinuous moves, but it does not quantify this trade-off.

Third, the report does not explore the possibility that the Fed's communications task force could recommend a return to more traditional forms of communication, such as the use of the federal funds rate target range rather than the dot plot. This would be a significant change in the Fed's toolkit and would have implications for how markets price rate expectations.

These open questions are not criticisms of the report; they are the natural result of a thoughtful analysis that identifies the key uncertainties. For investors, the takeaway is that the Fed is entering a period of transition, and the path forward is not yet clear.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Read the New Fed

Given the shift toward strategic ambiguity, investors need a new framework for interpreting Fed signals. Here are four principles to guide your analysis.

First, focus on the balance of risks in Fed communications rather than on the central tendency of forecasts. When the Fed emphasizes risks around the outlook, it is signaling that it is uncertain about the path forward and that policy is likely to be data-dependent. When it emphasizes central tendencies, it is signaling confidence in its baseline view.

Second, pay attention to the Fed's characterization of supply-side factors. When the Fed emphasizes hard-to-measure supply improvements, it is creating room to tolerate higher inflation without tightening. When it emphasizes demand-side pressures, it is signaling that it is more concerned about overheating.

Third, watch the political dynamics around Fed leadership. The unresolved definition of "cause" in the Cook ruling creates a tail risk that the administration could attempt to remove additional Fed officials. Any escalation in political pressure on the Fed is likely to be met with a more cautious and ambiguous communication strategy.

Fourth, do not overinterpret noisy monthly data. The June payrolls report is a perfect example of why single data points can be misleading. The Fed is moving toward a regime where it will place less weight on individual data releases and more weight on the broader narrative. Investors should do the same.

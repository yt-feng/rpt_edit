# China’s Economic Recovery Is Real, But It Is Also Narrow, Policy-Dependent, and Fragile

The latest high-frequency economic data from China presents a picture that is simultaneously encouraging and unsettling. On the surface, the numbers suggest a recovery that is gaining traction: passenger flights are returning toward year-ago levels, traffic congestion has normalized after the Dragon Boat Festival, consumer confidence has ticked upward in May, and port container throughput is running above last year. For the casual observer, these are signals that China’s post-reopening momentum is intact. But a closer examination of the full suite of indicators reveals a far more complex story. The recovery is not broad-based; it is concentrated in specific sectors and regions. It is heavily reliant on policy support that remains uneven in its delivery. And it is vulnerable to structural headwinds that the headline numbers do not capture.

The critical question for investors and corporate strategists is not whether China is recovering, but rather what kind of recovery this is, and how durable it will prove to be. The data suggests we are witnessing a recovery that is being sustained by consumption and mobility in the service sector, while production and investment indicators show signs of stalling. At the same time, the policy environment is sending mixed signals. On one hand, the central bank has introduced new monetary tools and the government has rolled out a steady stream of consumption- and investment-oriented announcements. On the other hand, the anti-corruption campaign has escalated to record levels at the local level, policy bank support has softened, and local government special bond issuance, while ongoing, remains modest in absolute terms. Understanding these contradictions is essential for anyone trying to position for the next phase of China’s economic cycle.

## The Consumption-Led Recovery Is Real But Reaching a Plateau

The most encouraging data points in the tracker all revolve around consumer activity and mobility. Domestic passenger flights have increased toward year-ago levels, and the flight cancellation rate has edged down, suggesting that air travel demand is normalizing. Traffic congestion, after the temporary spike from the Dragon Boat Festival holiday, has settled back to levels consistent with the same period last year. These are not just statistical artifacts; they reflect a genuine return to normalcy in how Chinese citizens are moving and spending.

What makes this trend significant is its breadth across different consumption proxies. The NBS consumer confidence index edged up in May, and the Morning Consult consumer confidence measure also rebounded over the past week. The weighted PMI employment sub-index rose in June, which is a lagging indicator that typically follows improvements in demand. When employment sentiment improves alongside mobility data, it suggests that the consumption recovery is not just a temporary release of pent-up demand but is beginning to embed itself into household expectations.

However, the data also contains warning signs that this recovery may be approaching a ceiling. The 30-city daily property transaction volume in the primary market edged down over the last week and remained below year-ago levels. Real estate has historically been the most powerful multiplier for consumer confidence in China, as it directly affects household wealth perceptions and spending behavior. When property transactions soften even as mobility improves, it indicates that the consumption recovery is not being reinforced by the housing market. The secondary market for property tells a slightly different story, with volumes still above year-ago levels, but the downward trend in the primary market is the more forward-looking signal. A consumption recovery that is not supported by a stabilization in the housing market is inherently fragile, because it relies on disposable income and service sector activity rather than the wealth effect that has historically driven Chinese consumer spending.

## Production and Investment Are Sending a Divergent Signal That Cannot Be Ignored

If the consumption data offers cautious optimism, the production and investment indicators demand a more sober assessment. Steel production edged up only slightly over the past week, and daily coal consumption in coastal provinces remained rangebound, albeit above year-ago levels. These are not the kind of vigorous upward moves one would expect from an economy in a synchronized recovery. Industrial activity appears to be treading water rather than accelerating.

The most telling indicator in this category is the pace of local government special bond issuance. Year-to-date, only RMB 2.04 billion in such bonds have been issued. While this number will grow as the year progresses, the current pace suggests that local governments are not rushing to deploy fiscal firepower. This is consistent with the broader theme of policy restraint. The report notes that policy bank support remained soft in June, and the anti-corruption proxy at the local level rose to a record high in the second quarter. These two phenomena are likely connected. When local officials face heightened scrutiny from anti-corruption investigations, their willingness to approve new projects, sign off on bond issuances, or accelerate infrastructure spending diminishes. The result is a disconnect between central government intentions and local government execution.

The implication is clear: the industrial and investment side of the economy is not receiving the stimulus it needs to break out of its current rangebound state. The consumption recovery is pulling the economy forward, but without a corresponding lift in investment activity, the recovery risks running out of steam. This is particularly relevant for investors who are positioning for a cyclical upswing in industrial commodities, capital goods, or infrastructure-related sectors. The data does not support a bullish case for these areas until there is evidence that local government spending is accelerating in a meaningful way.

## The Monetary Policy Framework Is Evolving, But Transmission Remains the Real Test

One of the most interesting developments in the recent policy landscape is the evolution of the monetary policy framework. The People's Bank of China conducted its first overnight reverse repos at the end of June, with the rate undisclosed. The Lujiazui Forum in mid-June reinforced three policy directions: a more price-based monetary policy framework, reduced emphasis on aggregate credit extension, and rules-based capital opening. These are not minor adjustments. They represent a deliberate shift away from the quantity-based tools that have defined Chinese monetary policy for decades and toward a framework that is more aligned with advanced economy central banking.

The immediate market implications are visible in the interbank market. Repo rates edged up over the last week, and the overnight rate remained below the 7-day open market operation rate. This suggests that the PBOC is allowing short-term rates to float more freely, which is consistent with a price-based framework. The CNY appreciated against the CFETS basket while remaining rangebound against the USD, and the USDCNY fixing implied countercyclical factor dropped in recent weeks. These are signs that the currency is being allowed to respond more to market forces, which is a natural consequence of the policy direction articulated at Lujiazui.

However, the critical question is whether this evolution in the monetary policy framework will translate into better credit transmission to the real economy. The data on policy bank support suggests that it has not yet done so. If the PBOC is moving toward a more market-driven system but the banking sector is still constrained by risk aversion and compliance concerns, the net effect on economic activity could be minimal. Investors should watch for whether the overnight repo operations become a regular tool and whether they lead to a sustained easing in financial conditions for the private sector. Until that happens, the monetary policy evolution remains more of a structural story than a cyclical catalyst.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Anti-Corruption and Fiscal Stimulus

The report provides a valuable, high-frequency snapshot of economic activity and policy, but it leaves one crucial analytical gap unresolved. The anti-corruption proxy at the national level stayed elevated, while at the local level it rose to a record high in the second quarter. At the same time, the report documents a series of policy announcements from the State Council, NDRC, and various ministries that are clearly designed to stimulate consumption, investment, and growth. The obvious question is whether these two forces are working at cross-purposes.

The report does not provide a direct answer to this question, and for good reason: the data to make that causal link does not exist at the weekly frequency. But the pattern is suggestive. When local officials are under investigation or fear investigation, they have strong incentives to delay project approvals, avoid signing contracts, and generally pull back from proactive economic management. This could explain why policy bank support remains soft even as the central government announces new stimulus measures. It could also explain why local government bond issuance has been slow despite the clear need for fiscal support.

For investors, this is the most important unresolved question in the report. If the anti-corruption campaign is acting as a de facto drag on fiscal stimulus, then the effectiveness of any new policy announcements will be muted until the campaign either concludes or local officials adjust their behavior. The report's weekly data on policy bank support and bond issuance will be the most important indicators to watch in the coming weeks. Any sign of acceleration in these metrics would suggest that the transmission mechanism is reopening. Continued softness would imply that the policy environment is more constrained than the headline announcements suggest.

## A Decision Framework for Interpreting the Weekly Data

For readers who need to make investment or strategic decisions based on this data, a simple framework can help cut through the noise. The weekly tracker can be thought of as a three-layer system: the consumption layer, the investment layer, and the policy layer. The consumption layer is currently the strongest, with mobility, flights, and consumer confidence all pointing in a positive direction. The investment layer is the weakest, with production flat and fiscal deployment slow. The policy layer is sending mixed signals, with ambitious announcements on one hand and operational constraints on the other.

The key decision rule is this: a sustainable recovery requires at least two of these three layers to be strengthening simultaneously. Currently, only one layer is clearly strengthening. This means that the recovery is real but fragile, and it is being carried by the consumer sector alone. The most important inflection point to watch for is when the investment layer begins to show signs of life, evidenced by rising steel output, accelerating coal consumption, or a pickup in local government bond issuance. The second inflection point is when the policy layer resolves its internal contradiction, evidenced by a decline in the anti-corruption proxy or a visible acceleration in policy bank lending.

Until one of these inflection points occurs, the prudent strategic stance is to favor sectors that are directly exposed to the consumption recovery, such as domestic travel, retail, and services, while being cautious on sectors that depend on investment and industrial activity. The data does not yet support a broad-based cyclical bet on China. It supports a selective bet on the consumer, with the understanding that even that bet has limits if the housing market and investment activity continue to lag.

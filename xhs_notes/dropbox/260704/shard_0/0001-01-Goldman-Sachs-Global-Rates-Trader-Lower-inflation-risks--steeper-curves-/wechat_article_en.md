# The Inflation Narrative Is Shifting: Why Curve Steepening, Not Yield Compression, Is the Dominant Trade

The market is undergoing a subtle but important regime change. Slower hiring, less aggressive central bank rhetoric, and declining energy prices have together deflated the immediate risk of rate hikes that dominated the first half of the year. Yet this is not the beginning of a sustained bond rally. The data points toward a more nuanced outcome: front-end yields can compress modestly, but long-end yields have limited room to fall. The result is a steepening yield curve that reflects a market caught between easing inflation fears and the structural weight of artificial intelligence capital expenditure, fiscal uncertainty, and sticky term premiums.

The controlling insight is straightforward. The near-term risk of central bank tightening has diminished, but the structural forces that keep long-term yields elevated remain intact. Investors who treat this as a simple "risk-off" or "dovish pivot" narrative will miss the trade. The opportunity lies in curve steepening, not in outright duration extension. Across the United States, Europe, the United Kingdom, and Japan, the same pattern is emerging: front-end relief meets long-end resistance.

The next ten days will be decisive. The June inflation prints in the United States and the Euro area will either validate the benign narrative or reintroduce volatility. But even under the most favorable inflation scenario, the market faces a structural constraint that no single data point can resolve: the financing demands of AI and the persistence of fiscal uncertainty are creating a floor under long-term yields that will not easily give way.

## The Labor Market Has Removed the Urgency for Hikes, but This Is a Necessary Condition, Not a Sufficient One

The June payrolls report delivered exactly what the rates market needed to hear: softer headline growth, downward revisions to prior months, and a narrative that the labor market is cooling without collapsing. When combined with the Federal Reserve's acknowledgment that inflation risks have come down, the argument for urgency in raising rates has effectively collapsed. This matters because it removes the tail risk that a July pause would reintroduce long-end volatility.

The critical point, however, is that one soft payrolls report does not constitute a trend. The decline in the unemployment rate was driven by an outsized drop in the labor force, not by strong hiring. This is month-to-month noise, not a structural shift. The accumulation of milder activity data is necessary to curtail broader hike risk, but it is not sufficient. The market needs to see a sustained pattern, and the next major test is the June CPI report.

The historical pattern is instructive. When the market prices in hikes for the front meeting prior to an eventual on-hold decision, the adjustment in broader policy pricing tends to be directional with the change in inflation expectations. Stable oil prices and benign inflation news can support a gradual erosion of hike risk and eventually push toward a steeper curve. But there is a floor. The hedge value of paying near-term meetings will limit how much the front-end can compress in the near term. The real action will come from the long end, where reduced downside growth risks and ongoing AI capex spending are limiting the scope for yields to rally.

## Long-End Yields Are Capped by Structural Demand for Capital, Not by Monetary Policy

The most underappreciated dynamic in the current environment is the structural demand for long-term capital from artificial intelligence investment. This is not a cyclical factor that will fade with the next data point. It is a multi-year trend that is reshaping the supply-demand balance in the bond market. The scale of capex required for AI infrastructure is enormous, and much of it will be financed through debt issuance.

This creates a fundamental constraint on how low long-term yields can go. Even as front-end pricing benefits from the removal of near-term hike risk, the long end faces a persistent headwind from supply. The market is already pricing this in. The term premium, which had been compressed for years, is normalizing across the G10. This is not a temporary phenomenon. It reflects a structural reassessment of the risk of holding long-duration assets in a world where fiscal deficits are large, inflation is more volatile, and the demand for capital is rising.

The implication for investors is clear. Buying long-duration bonds in the hope of a sustained rally is a bet against structural forces that are not going away. The better trade is to position for curve steepening, where the front end benefits from the removal of hike risk while the long end remains anchored by supply and term premium.

## Traded Inflation Has Become Cheap, but Cheapness Alone Is Not a Reason to Buy

The decline in traded inflation since the US-Iran memorandum of understanding and the June FOMC has been sharp, particularly at the front end. The relationship with oil prices explains nearly the entire move. What is more interesting is that forward inflation pricing has declined more than the drop in oil prices would typically justify. The 5-year, 5-year forward inflation rate is now trading near levels last seen during the initial post-conflict growth scare.

This larger-than-implied decline likely reflects the market's perception that the risk of the Fed falling behind the curve has diminished. The June FOMC signaled a more patient approach, and the market has responded by reducing its inflation premium. From a valuation perspective, forward inflation now looks slightly cheap. But cheapness alone is not a compelling reason to buy.

The problem is that the market is still pricing higher inflation on a one-to-two-year horizon than what economists forecast for 2027. The hawkish Fed assumptions that have kept a cap on inflation pricing are not going to disappear overnight. The case for inflation longs is not as a carry trade. It is as a hedge against the Fed revealing a more dovish reaction function or against an exogenous shock to prices. For investors who do not need that hedge, the risk-reward is not compelling.

## The European Story Is About Carry and Spread Compression, Not About a Rally in Yields

In Europe, the inflation relief has been more pronounced and more sustained than in the United States. Incoming data increasingly points to benign inflation risk, and economists have shaved their forecasts following the June flash report. This should keep front-end pricing in a narrow channel around a September hike, with risks fairly balanced.

The interesting question is what happens beyond the front end. The positive slope in pricing beyond September is hard to justify in an environment where inflation is declining and growth is modest. That premium should decay over time. But the real opportunity in Europe is not in yields. It is in carry and in sovereign credit spreads.

The recent widening of French OATs against Bunds is a case in point. Despite strong tailwinds for sovereign credit, including an improving growth outlook and reduced energy-related fiscal risks, OATs have underperformed. This likely reflects positioning for potential political tensions around the 2027 budget negotiations and the upcoming verdict on Marine Le Pen's eligibility to run in the 2027 elections.

The key insight is that the widening is excessive relative to fundamentals. Consensus expectations for the French deficit were actually falling into June. The reduction in macro and rates volatility should reanchor spreads, particularly at shorter maturities. Long-end spreads are stickier given medium-term fiscal uncertainties, but they also offer value. For investors who can tolerate the political noise, the trade is to buy OATs against Bunds and collect carry while waiting for the spread to normalize.

## The Japanese Conundrum: Fiscal Risk and Intervention-Based FX Management Are Sustaining Term Premium

Japan remains the most structurally interesting market in the G10. The JGB curve continues to steepen, with the 2s10s spread stuck at levels that are difficult to justify based on the cyclical and fiscal backdrop alone. Part of this reflects a global normalization of term premium, but domestic factors are clearly at play.

The Takaichi administration's fiscal stance is the key variable. The release of the Basic Policy this month is potentially pivotal for near-term JGB volatility. Absent a shift toward fiscal consolidation, the risks skew toward further cheapening in the 10-year and 30-year sectors, with mounting pressure on the belly of the curve over time.

The more interesting dynamic is the interaction between FX policy and the bond market. The apparent willingness to rely on FX intervention rather than faster rate hikes to address yen weakness is creating a disequilibrium in Japanese macro assets. By suppressing FX volatility through intervention rather than through more proactive rate policy, the authorities are leaving long-term rates as the main domestic pressure-release valve. This is a deliberate choice, but it comes with consequences. The ongoing fiscal risk and intervention-based FX management will continue to lift term premiums and sustain JGB curve steepening.

For investors, the trade is to position for further steepening in the JGB curve. The front end is anchored by the Bank of Japan's gradual normalization, but the long end faces persistent upward pressure from fiscal uncertainty and the structural demand for capital.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Liquidity and Balance Sheet Question

The report raises but does not fully resolve a critical question about the future path of liquidity and its impact on funding markets. The Federal Reserve's reserve management purchases are scheduled for another reduction, with the pace potentially moving from $10 billion per month to zero or $5 billion. This matters because it directly affects the level of reserves in the banking system.

The report's analysis suggests that a monthly RMP pace of $5 billion would see reserves declining to around 11 percent of bank assets by the first quarter of 2027. This would be consistent with TGCR at or slightly below IORB and SOFR slightly above it. But the path is not linear. The potential intersection with the debt limit and the medium-term uncertainty around changes to the balance sheet approach present two-sided risks.

The open question is whether the market is correctly pricing the transition to a scarcer reserve environment. The early-2027 SOFR-FF pricing is consistent with baseline assumptions, but the distribution of outcomes is wide. A faster reduction in reserves could lead to upward pressure on funding costs, while a slower reduction could keep funding benign for longer. This is a second-order risk that becomes more important as the balance sheet continues to shrink.

## A Decision Framework for the Next Three Months

For investors trying to navigate this environment, the framework should be organized around three questions.

First, what is the inflation trajectory? The next two CPI prints will determine whether the benign narrative holds. If inflation comes in soft, the front end can continue to consolidate and the curve can steepen. If inflation surprises to the upside, the hike risk will return and the curve will flatten. This is the single most important variable.

Second, where is the term premium going? The normalization of term premium across the G10 is a structural trend that is not going to reverse. Investors should assume that long-end yields have a floor, and that curve steepening is the dominant trade. The question is not whether term premium will rise, but how fast and in which markets.

Third, what is the fiscal trajectory? In the United States, the focus is on the debt ceiling and the medium-term fiscal outlook. In Europe, it is on the French budget and the political calendar. In Japan, it is on the Basic Policy and the fiscal stance. Fiscal uncertainty is a persistent source of term premium, and it is not going away.

The trades that emerge from this framework are curve steepeners in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Japan, and sovereign credit longs in Europe, particularly in French OATs at the long end. The common thread is that front-end relief is real but limited, while long-end yields face structural headwinds. The opportunity is in the shape of the curve, not in the level.

# The Fed Will Stay on Hold This Year — But the Data Path to a Hike Is Narrower Than Markets Assume

The consensus view that the Federal Reserve will cut rates in 2026 is wrong. The more relevant debate is not whether the Fed will ease, but whether it can avoid hiking. Based on a detailed analysis of the incoming data flow since the June FOMC meeting, the case for a Fed on hold through year-end remains intact — but the margin for error is shrinking. The central bank's own projections show nine participants anticipate at least one rate hike this year, and the conditions that could trigger such a move are well-defined and increasingly measurable. Investors who dismiss this risk are ignoring the specific data thresholds that would force the Fed's hand.

The June FOMC meeting produced a clear divergence between the median participant's outlook and the actual inflation trajectory. The median FOMC participant expects 4Q/4Q headline PCE inflation of roughly 3.5% to 3.6%, while a cluster of eight participants projects inflation between 3.7% and 4.2%. These forecasts were likely formed before the recent decline in oil prices following the US-Iran Memorandum of Understanding, which has pushed energy prices back toward pre-conflict levels. Our own forecast places headline and core PCE inflation at 3.2% and 3.0% respectively — substantially below the median FOMC projection. This gap matters because it means the Fed may be overstating inflation pressures, and incoming data will determine which view is correct.

The argument for a Fed on hold rests on four pillars: tariff pass-through is ending, shelter inflation is moderating, residual seasonality will fade into year-end, and consumer spending has softened meaningfully. First-quarter consumption was revised down sharply to 0.5% from 1.4%, driven by downward revisions to services spending. Second-quarter consumption is now tracking at 1.9% rather than the 3.0% expected a week ago. Year-to-date, the implication is a consumer growing at just 1.2% in the first half of 2026 — a pace that is inconsistent with an overheating economy. Payroll gains have also moderated, averaging 114,000 per month from January to May and 188,000 over the past three months. Our forecast projects further deceleration to 50,000-60,000 per month over the summer.

Yet the data path to rate hikes is real and well-defined. The Fed will see three key data releases — June through August — before its September meeting. If the data evolve in a specific direction, the case for a hike becomes compelling. Understanding these thresholds is essential for any investor positioning for the second half of 2026.

## Monthly Core Inflation Above 0.3% Would Break the Disinflation Narrative

The most critical threshold for the Fed is the monthly pace of core inflation. The FOMC's projections implicitly assume monthly core PCE and CPI inflation running around 0.3% month-over-month, consistent with tariff pass-through persisting longer or second-round effects from higher energy prices materializing. Our forecast calls for core CPI and PCE inflation running slightly below 0.2% month-over-month on average across the June through August releases. If the actual data come in at or above 0.3% month-over-month, the gap between our forecast and the Fed's would narrow, and the disinflation narrative would weaken substantially.

This is not a hypothetical risk. The composition of core inflation matters as much as the headline number. Core goods inflation has been the primary channel for tariff pass-through, and the evidence suggests this pass-through is ending. But if monthly core goods inflation reaccelerates, it would signal that either tariff effects are more persistent than expected or that demand is strong enough to sustain price increases. Similarly, core services inflation excluding shelter — the so-called supercore measure that the Fed has emphasized — would need to remain contained. A sustained 0.3% month-over-month reading in supercore would indicate that wage pressures are feeding through to prices more aggressively than anticipated.

The energy channel adds another dimension of risk. Oil futures prices point to substantial payback in energy prices during June and July, with energy prices in PCE inflation set to fall by roughly 5.0% and 2.6% respectively. If the US-Iran MOU does not hold and hostilities resume, our forecast for monthly declines in headline CPI and roughly flat readings on headline PCE would not materialize. Instead, headline inflation would remain firm, and second-round effects on core inflation would become more probable. The oil risk premium scenario — where energy prices stay elevated and spill over into broader price pressures — is the single biggest upside risk to our inflation forecast.

## An Unemployment Rate Below 4.0% Would Signal Labor Market Overheating

The labor market threshold is equally clear. The median FOMC participant estimates the longer-run neutral unemployment rate at 4.2%. If the unemployment rate falls below 4.0% by September, the Fed would likely interpret this as evidence that the labor market is much stronger than thought and that a more restrictive policy stance is needed to prevent overheating.

Our forecast projects payroll gains of 50,000-60,000 per month over the summer, which should keep the unemployment rate roughly steady near current levels. But three months of payroll gains averaging 188,000 — the pace seen over the past three months — would push the unemployment rate lower. The difference between 50,000 and 188,000 is the difference between a stable labor market and one that is tightening rapidly.

The quality of payroll gains also matters. Recent payroll data may be overstated due to a catch-up effect in hiring following the implementation of protectionist trade policies last year. If this catch-up effect fades, payrolls will decelerate naturally. But if hiring remains strong because underlying demand is more resilient than expected, the Fed will have to respond. The composition of job gains — whether they are concentrated in low-wage sectors or spread across high-productivity industries — will influence the Fed's interpretation. A broad-based acceleration in hiring across goods-producing and services sectors would be more concerning than a narrow pickup in a single industry.

The unemployment rate threshold interacts with inflation in a way that amplifies the risk. A labor market running below its neutral rate would typically generate faster wage growth, which feeds into services inflation. If both the unemployment rate and core inflation are moving in the wrong direction simultaneously, the case for a hike becomes overwhelming. The Fed would not need to see both thresholds breached; either one alone would be sufficient to trigger a policy response, but both together would make a hike almost certain.

## The Risk Management Argument for Hikes May Override the Data Entirely

The most underappreciated risk in the rate hike debate is that some FOMC participants may not need to see adverse data to vote for a hike. A year ago, the Fed viewed its policy stance as inappropriate because downside risks to employment outweighed upside risks to inflation. Some of the nine participants projecting rate hikes may have reversed this risk management framework. They may now see the distribution of risks as skewed sufficiently toward firmer inflation, and view the current policy stance as inappropriate regardless of what the next few months of data show.

This is a fundamentally different argument from the data-dependent framework that the Fed has emphasized. It suggests that subjective assessments about the distribution of outcomes around the modal path — not the modal path itself — are driving some participants' views. If this is correct, then even data that confirm our benign inflation and labor market outlook may not be sufficient to keep all nine hawks from voting for a hike. The question becomes whether the majority of voters on the committee share this risk management view.

Our assessment is that most FOMC voters prefer the status quo. The Reserve Bank presidents who submitted rate hike projections may not be voters this year, and the Board of Governors — who are permanent voters — have generally been more dovish. But this is an assumption that could be wrong. If even one or two Board members shift toward a risk management framework that favors hikes, the balance of the committee could tip. The September meeting will be the first real test of whether the risk management view has gained traction among voters.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Interaction Between Financial Conditions and the Real Economy

The report tracks financial conditions using a FRB/US-based model that incorporates five variables: the 10-year Treasury yield, S&P 500 returns, the corporate BBB credit spread, the US dollar, and the price of oil. Since hostilities in the Middle East began on February 28, the tightening in financial conditions is equivalent to about a 40-basis-point rise in the federal funds rate. This tightening reversed all of the easing seen earlier this year.

What the report does not fully answer is whether this tightening in financial conditions is already doing the Fed's work for it. If tighter financial conditions are slowing the economy through lower equity prices, a stronger dollar, and wider credit spreads, then the Fed may not need to raise rates even if the data thresholds are breached. The tightening in financial conditions acts as a de facto rate hike, and the Fed may be comfortable letting it operate while keeping the policy rate unchanged.

But the composition of the tightening matters. The tightening since February 28 has been driven primarily by a weaker US dollar and higher 10-year Treasury yields. A weaker dollar is expansionary for the economy — it boosts exports and raises import prices, which feeds into inflation. A weaker dollar tightening financial conditions is a contradiction that the model captures but does not fully resolve. If the dollar continues to weaken, the Fed may need to raise rates to prevent the weaker currency from adding to inflation pressures, even if other components of financial conditions are tightening.

The report also leaves open the question of how tariff refunds and the effective tariff rate will evolve. The effective tariff rate has fallen to 8.3% in first-quarter 2026 data, down from the Liberation Day peak. Core tariffs excluding fuels, gold, and AI-related imports remain closer to 13-14%. If tariff refunds accelerate — as suggested by the proxy of cash withdrawals from Customs and Border Protection — the effective tariff rate could fall further, reducing the inflation impulse from trade policy. But the legal framework for tariffs remains uncertain after the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA authority, and the 150-day window for Section 122 tariffs creates a cliff that Congress may or may not address.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Scenarios and Their Implications

For investors trying to navigate the second half of 2026, the key question is not whether the Fed will hike or hold. It is which data releases will determine the outcome and how to position for each scenario. The following framework translates the report's analysis into actionable decision points.

Scenario One: Benign Disinflation. Core PCE and CPI run at 0.2% month-over-month or below through August. The unemployment rate stays above 4.0%. Oil prices remain stable or decline further. In this scenario, the Fed stays on hold through year-end, and the market can price out rate hike risk entirely. The implications are bullish for duration, supportive for equities, and negative for the dollar. The key risk is that this scenario is already priced in, leaving limited upside if it materializes.

Scenario Two: Inflation Threshold Breached. Core inflation runs at 0.3% month-over-month or above in any of the June through August releases. The unemployment rate remains steady, but the inflation data alone are sufficient to trigger a hike. In this scenario, the Fed raises rates by 25 basis points in September, and the market reprices to reflect the possibility of additional hikes. The implications are bearish for duration, negative for equities, and supportive for the dollar. The key question is whether a single month of 0.3% core inflation is enough, or whether the Fed needs to see a sustained pattern.

Scenario Three: Dual Threshold Breached. Core inflation runs at 0.3% or above, and the unemployment rate falls below 4.0%. This is the nightmare scenario for the Fed and for markets. It would confirm that the economy is overheating on both fronts, and the Fed would likely hike by 25 basis points in September with a strong signal that more hikes are coming. The implications are sharply bearish for duration, deeply negative for equities, and strongly supportive for the dollar. The key question is whether the Fed would hike by 50 basis points in this scenario, or whether it would prefer to move gradually.

The decision framework also requires monitoring the oil risk premium scenario, which is a tail risk that could shift the entire probability distribution. If the US-Iran MOU breaks down and oil prices spike, the inflation thresholds would be breached automatically, and the Fed would have to respond regardless of what the labor market data show. This scenario is binary and hard to predict, but its consequences are so large that it deserves constant monitoring.

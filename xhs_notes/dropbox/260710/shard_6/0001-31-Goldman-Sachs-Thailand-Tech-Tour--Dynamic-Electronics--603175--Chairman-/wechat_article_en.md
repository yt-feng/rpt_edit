# AI PCB Is Not Just a Growth Story: It Is a Structural Reshuffling of Global Supply Chains

The conventional narrative around AI infrastructure spending tends to focus on chip design, data center construction, and energy consumption. But the physical backbone that connects every GPU, every switch, and every optical transceiver is the printed circuit board. And that backbone is undergoing a transformation that is both technological and geographic.

A recent visit to Dynamic Electronics' Thailand factories, combined with site tours across the Prachin Buri industrial corridor, reveals something that most top-down analyses miss: the AI PCB opportunity is not simply about rising demand. It is about a forced migration of production capacity, a shift in product mix toward higher-layer-count boards, and a new set of cost dynamics that will separate winners from laggards. The report from a global research institution's Thailand Tech Tour, which included direct discussions with Dynamic's Chairman, provides granular evidence that the PCB industry is entering a phase where capacity location and technical capability are becoming inseparable competitive advantages.

The research institution's analysis projects the global AI PCB total addressable market to grow at a 140% CAGR from 2025 through 2027, reaching USD 18.3 billion. That number is striking, but the more important question is who will capture that value. The answer, based on the factory visit and management commentary, is not simply the largest manufacturers. It is those who can simultaneously manage the complexity of AI-grade boards, the cost structure of new greenfield plants in Southeast Asia, and the supply chain dependencies that still run through China.

This article unpacks what the factory visit revealed about the strategic logic behind Thailand-based PCB expansion, the unresolved tensions in the industry's shift, and the decision framework that researchers and corporate strategists should be applying today.

## The Thailand Pivot Is Not About Labor Cost; It Is About Customer Mandate and Risk Diversification

The single most important insight from the Dynamic Electronics factory visit is that the decision to invest RMB 3.3 billion in Thailand's P5 and P5A facilities is not primarily a cost optimization move. Thailand's labor costs are higher than China's in certain skill categories, and the supply chain for raw materials like copper-clad laminates and specialty chemicals remains heavily concentrated in China and Taiwan. So why build in Thailand?

The answer lies in customer requirements. Major hyperscalers and server OEMs are increasingly mandating that a portion of their PCB supply come from outside China. This is not a tariff-driven phenomenon alone. It is a structural shift in procurement strategy, driven by geopolitical risk assessment and continuity planning. Management explicitly noted that the Thailand production lines are targeting AI server and optical transceiver applications, which are the highest-growth and highest-specification segments of the PCB market.

The implication is profound: for PCB suppliers, having a Thailand factory is becoming a ticket to play in the AI segment, not a cost-saving experiment. The capital expenditure is front-loaded, the ramp-up periods are painful, and the margins during initial production are compressed by depreciation and lower yields. But without that capacity, a supplier risks being excluded from the most valuable part of the market entirely.

This explains why Dynamic Electronics, despite being a company where automotive PCB still accounts for 54% of revenue, is making a bet that is disproportionately large relative to its current AI exposure. The company is not just adding capacity; it is repositioning its entire portfolio toward a customer base that demands geographic diversification.

## Margin Compression During Ramp-Up Is the Price of Admission, Not a Sign of Weakness

One of the most frequently misunderstood dynamics in capital-intensive manufacturing is the relationship between capacity expansion and profitability. When Dynamic Electronics reported first-quarter 2026 revenues up 28% year on year but margins under pressure from rising depreciation, a superficial reading would flag concern. The deeper reading is that this margin compression is structurally necessary and, for well-capitalized players, strategically advantageous.

The P5 and P5A factories in Thailand are being built for AI PCB products that require higher layer counts, tighter impedance control, and more advanced materials. These boards command higher average selling prices, but they also require more expensive equipment, longer production cycles, and more stringent quality control. During the ramp-up phase, yield rates are lower, fixed costs are spread over smaller volumes, and the learning curve is steep.

Management's expectation that profitability will improve with scale expansion, mix upgrade, and production efficiency is not wishful thinking. It is a pattern observable across every PCB cycle of the past two decades. The companies that emerge stronger from each cycle are those that endure the margin trough to build capacity that competitors cannot easily replicate.

The strategic question for observers is whether a given company has the balance sheet and customer relationships to survive the trough. Dynamic Electronics, with a 54% revenue base in automotive PCB, has a stable cash flow generator that can subsidize the AI ramp. That is a structural advantage over pure-play AI PCB startups that lack diversification.

## The AI PCB Total Addressable Market Growth Hides a Critical Specification Gap

The headline projection of a 140% CAGR for AI PCB TAM through 2027 is attention-grabbing, but it masks a critical detail: not all PCB capacity can serve the AI market. The boards required for AI servers and optical transceivers are fundamentally different from those used in consumer electronics or even traditional enterprise servers.

AI server motherboards and accelerator cards require high-density interconnect designs with layer counts exceeding 20 in many cases. They demand materials with low dielectric loss to maintain signal integrity at high frequencies. They require advanced thermal management features because power densities are much higher than in conventional computing.

This means that the existing global PCB capacity, much of which is optimized for smartphones, automotive, and legacy IT, cannot simply be repurposed. New factories must be built with specific equipment configurations. The Thailand expansion by Dynamic Electronics, as well as similar moves by other major PCB manufacturers, represents a deliberate reallocation of capital toward this specification frontier.

The corollary is that the AI PCB TAM growth will be supply-constrained, not demand-constrained, for at least the next 18 to 24 months. Companies that can bring qualified AI-grade capacity online faster will capture pricing power and customer loyalty. Those that lag will find themselves relegated to lower-margin segments where competition is more intense.

## The Report Does Not Fully Answer How Upstream Material Supply Chains Will Adjust

One of the most significant open questions that emerges from the factory visit and the broader Thailand Tech Tour is about the upstream material supply chain. Thailand does not have a deep ecosystem for high-end PCB raw materials. Copper-clad laminates, prepreg, dry film, and specialty chemicals are still predominantly sourced from China, Taiwan, Japan, and South Korea.

Dynamic Electronics' management acknowledged margin pressure from higher material costs in Thailand. This is not a temporary logistics issue. It reflects a structural cost disadvantage that will persist until a local supply base develops. The question that the report does not fully answer is whether that local supply base will develop organically, or whether it will require policy intervention or joint ventures to materialize.

For researchers and strategists, this creates a second-order implication: companies that can integrate vertically or secure long-term supply agreements with material producers may have a more durable cost advantage than those that rely on spot procurement. The Thailand PCB buildout may end up catalyzing a parallel buildout in upstream materials, but that process will take years and will require significant capital commitments.

Another unresolved question is the labor skill gap. AI PCB production requires technicians who understand advanced process control, automated optical inspection, and electrical testing at higher precision levels. Thailand's labor pool is deep for general manufacturing but thin for these specialized roles. The ramp-up timelines that management discussed implicitly assume that training programs and knowledge transfer from Chinese facilities will be effective. That assumption deserves scrutiny.

## A Decision Framework for Evaluating PCB Suppliers in the AI Era

Based on the insights from the factory visit and the broader industry context, a structured decision framework emerges for evaluating PCB suppliers that are positioning for AI demand. This framework has four dimensions.

First, assess geographic diversification credibility. A supplier that announces a Thailand factory but has no track record of operating outside China faces execution risk. Look for evidence of local management teams, relationships with Thai industrial estate operators, and clarity on regulatory approvals. Dynamic Electronics has existing Thailand operations, which reduces but does not eliminate this risk.

Second, evaluate product mix trajectory. The percentage of revenue coming from AI-related boards today is less important than the trajectory of that percentage over the next three years. Companies that are investing specifically in high-layer-count, high-frequency, and high-reliability boards are positioning for the specification upgrade that the AI TAM growth implies.

Third, stress-test the balance sheet for the ramp-up period. The margin compression phase can last 12 to 18 months per factory. Companies with high debt levels or low free cash flow generation from existing businesses are more vulnerable to a prolonged trough. Diversified revenue bases, such as Dynamic Electronics' automotive segment, provide a cushion.

Fourth, examine customer concentration and relationship depth. AI PCB demand is driven by a relatively small number of hyperscaler and server OEM customers. Suppliers that have design-win engagements with these customers, rather than simply being approved vendors, have stronger revenue visibility. The factory visit commentary suggested that Dynamic Electronics is targeting these customers directly, which is encouraging, but the report does not disclose specific customer names or design-win status.

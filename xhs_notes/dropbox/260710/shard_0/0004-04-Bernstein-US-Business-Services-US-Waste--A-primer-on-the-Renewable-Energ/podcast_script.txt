EN_A: So I came across this really interesting report on the US waste industry, and there is one segment that caught my eye: renewable natural gas. How big is this thing?

EN_B: It is actually the smallest piece of the pie, less than 3% of industry revenue. But here is the kicker: its EBITDA margins are between 40% and 50%. That is incredibly high, and it is becoming a real growth driver for companies like Waste Management.

EN_A: 40% to 50% margins? That sounds almost too good. Why haven't I heard more about it?

EN_B: Because the business model is unique. Almost all that profit comes from something called D3 RINs. These are government-created renewable fuel credits. Waste companies capture methane from landfills, turn it into pipeline-quality gas, and that process generates these credits. They sell those credits to oil refiners who need them to comply with federal renewable fuel mandates. So 90% of the revenue is from the credits, not the gas itself.

EN_A: So it is a policy-driven business. That sounds risky. What happens if the policy changes?

EN_B: Exactly. The price of these RINs is very volatile. It traded as low as 50 cents in 2019 and as high as 3.75 dollars in 2022. Right now it is around 2.70. That creates real earnings volatility. But here is the interesting thing from the report: the program has survived both Republican and Democratic administrations since 2014. The political support is broad, from infrastructure operators to unions to municipal governments.

EN_A: So the policy is sticky. Who are the players, and how are they different?

EN_B: The three big waste companies all invest in RNG, but in different ways. Waste Management builds its own facilities directly. Republic Services does it through joint ventures with companies like BP-backed Archaea Energy. And Waste Connections uses a hybrid model, part direct, part joint venture. Each structure has different implications for margin and risk.

EN_A: And how much does this actually move the needle for these large companies?

EN_B: It is small now but growing fast. For Waste Management, the report projects its renewables revenue could grow from 75 million dollars in fiscal 2023 to over 1.1 billion by fiscal 2028. That would add about 0.7 percentage points to the company's overall growth rate. And because the margins are so high, it is also accretive to overall profitability.

EN_A: That is a significant growth story. But we keep coming back to the policy risk. What is the biggest unknown?

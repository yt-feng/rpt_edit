Last week we published an analysis on New Memory LTAs (Long-term agreements), why they are different and what this means for sustainability of earnings. Today's part 2 dives deeper into Case Studies from the Semiconductor industry to compare and address questions and feedback from investors.

Historically, long-term agreements in semiconductors and memory functioned primarily as a buyer's tool: Customers used LTAs to secure guaranteed volume, while sellers had little structural leverage of their own. Even when suppliers held some negotiating power, contracts were largely limited to two structures: take-or-pay, where a buyer promises to either accept a minimum volume at a pre-agreed price or pay a penalty for any shortfall; and so-called "non-cancelable" orders, a buyer's promise not to cancel delivery that, as later cycles would prove, buyers could and did cancel in practice. Both structures offered sellers little real downside protection, since neither carried an upfront financial commitment to compensate the supplier if a buyer walked away; the only remedy was to sue for damages, a process that was slow, expensive, and often hollow if the buyer was no longer solvent by the time judgment was reached.

Two case studies illustrate exactly how previous LTA structures failed in practice: 1. Microchip / Analog Semiconductors; and 2. Hemlock / Polysilicon. Out of the two case studies, the Hemlock case bears more resemblance to what we see in the New Memory LTAs as there were some legal teeth to the agreement but very much relied on suing its customers to get the money owed to it. The Microchip case study, which is often referenced by Semiconductor investors, was a much looser agreement without any notable legal teeth that customers had little disincentive to walk away from.

We identify three structural differences between legacy semiconductor LTAs and the New Memory LTAs, each addressing a distinct weakness that undermined prior contract structures: 1. Upfront financial commitment: cash deposits, letters of credit, or third-party guarantees, specifically back-end weighted (where the coverage ratio of remaining financial commitment as a portion of remaining purchase obligations increases substantially over time, hence increasing downside protection in the latter part of the contract when it is more needed); 2. Better counterparty quality: financially sound, diversified buyers replace the thinly capitalized or diffuse customer bases that undermined Hemlock and Microchip; 3. Structural demand: sustained AI-driven buildout replaces the temporary pull-in and double-ordering that ultimately undermined Microchip.

The New Memory LTAs provide meaningful but not infinite downside protection. We find that these new memory LTAs are structured in a way which provides significantly higher disincentives to customers to walk away - particularly in the latter part of the contract term. In our previous note (New Memory LTAs), we showed a range of scenarios of future price collapse and how those would impact earnings with and without LTAs. In conclusion, there is significantly more downside protection in these agreements than the previous cases we analyzed, but not infinite downside protection.

## PART I: BLAST FROM THE PAST - CASE STUDIES IN EARLIER SEMICONDUCTOR LTAS

Historically, long-term agreements in semiconductors and memory functioned primarily as a buyer's tool: customers used LTAs to secure guaranteed volume, while sellers had little structural leverage of their own. Even when suppliers held some negotiating power, contracts were largely limited to two structures: take-or-pay, where a buyer promises to either accept a minimum volume at a pre-agreed price or pay a penalty for any shortfall; and so-called "non-cancelable" orders, a buyer's promise not to cancel delivery that, as later cycles would prove, buyers could and did cancel in practice. Both structures offered sellers little real downside protection, since neither carried significant upfront financial commitment to compensate the supplier if a buyer walked away; the only remedy was to sue for damages, a process that was slow, expensive, and often hollow if the buyer was no longer solvent by the time judgment was reached.

Two case studies illustrate exactly how previous LTA structures failed in practice: 1. Microchip / Analog Semiconductors; and 2. Hemlock / Polysilicon. Out of the two case studies, the Hemlock case bears more resemblance to what we see in the New Memory LTAs as there were some legal teeth to the agreement but very much relied on suing its customers to get the money owed to it (spoiler alert, Hemlock won the cases after years of litigation but failed to receive all it was due because of customer insolvency). The Microchip case study, which is often referenced by Semiconductor investors, was a much looser agreement without any notable legal teeth that customers had little disincentive to walk away from.

## CASE STUDY 1: MICROCHIP / ANALOG - NON-CANCELABLE/NON-RESCHEDULABLE SUPPLY PROGRAM LED TO INVENTORY GLUT

During the peak of the 2020-2022 (COVID era) semiconductor shortage, automotive and industrial manufacturers scrambled to secure analog, mixed-signal, and basic MCU supply amid widespread component shortages. As a result, the industry experienced significant demand pull-forward and widespread double ordering, creating an artificially inflated backlog. To safeguard production schedules amid severe component shortages, many customers purchased well beyond their immediate requirements, effectively bringing forward several years of anticipated demand to secure limited manufacturing capacity. At the same time, double and even triple ordering became common, with customers placing overlapping orders across multiple semiconductor suppliers and distribution channels to improve their chances of obtaining product. Collectively, these behaviors distorted order patterns, inflated reported backlog levels, and significantly overstated underlying end-market demand.

Interpreting the surge in orders as evidence of sustained, secular growth, chipmakers expanded capacity plans and increasingly pushed customers to sign long-term agreements (LTAs) to secure future production commitments. Microchip launched its Preferred Supply Program (PSP). This program offered prioritized manufacturing slots in exchange for 12 months of continuous, completely non-cancellable, and non-reschedulable order backlogs.

However, demand for analog chips and microcontrollers is fundamentally tied to end-unit production and does not scale exponentially. Customers need sufficient components to manufacture vehicles, industrial equipment, appliances, and consumer electronics, but once supply constraints ease, there is little need to accumulate significant incremental inventory.

Unlike today's AI-driven memory demand, which is a huge incremental growth driver, much of the analog and MCU demand surge during the COVID-era shortage was driven by supply insecurity. In many cases, customers were stockpiling inventory and placing duplicate orders to secure supply rather than purchasing components to meet a corresponding increase in underlying end-market demand.

Therefore, when consumer demand normalized and the macroeconomic environment cooled, the LTAs collapsed under a massive inventory glut. Customers quickly found themselves with hugely bloated inventories. They did not have the physical space or the balance sheet capacity to take on more delivery. Customers were forced to freeze new orders entirely to burn through existing stockpiles. Instead of forcing customers to take chips they didn't need, giants like Analog Devices (ADI) and Microchip Technology intentionally "undershipped". They allowed buyers to pause or delay their LTA orders so distributors and factories could burn through their massive, hoarded stockpiles. This however, came at a massive short-term cost to the chipmakers:

\- Microchip Technology saw its net sales plummet by over 42% (Q3 FY25 over Q3 FY24) during the height of the inventory correction.

\- Analog Devices experienced a multi-quarter revenue contraction as they deliberately kept sales channels lean to prevent a market collapse.

Lessons learned: Weak contract terms, without any legal teeth to disincentivize customers from walking away, and artificial transient shortages which quickly abated.

## CASE STUDY 2: HEMLOCK / POLYSILICON - TAKE-OR-PAY CONTRACT FAILURE

In the mid-2000s, a severe global shortage of polysilicon sent spot prices soaring to over \$400/kg. To secure production lines, major solar companies like SolarWorld, Kyocera, JA Solar, and Yingli signed 10 to 14-year Long-Term Agreements (LTAs) with the Michigan-based Hemlock Semiconductor, then the world's leading producer of hyper-pure polysilicon. These contracts locked in "predictable" fixed prices (often around \$40–\$60/kg) backed by rigid "take-or-pay" provisions.

Shortly after however, the market fundamentally crashed, due to:

\- Huge Chinese supply expansion: The Chinese government heavily subsidized domestic polysilicon production. Massive factories flooded the market, causing global spot prices to plummet from hundreds of dollars down to below \$15/kg.

\- The trade war: In retaliation for U.S. solar tariffs, China imposed steep anti-dumping duties on American-made polysilicon. This meant some of Hemlock's buyers could not even cost-effectively import the material they were legally required to buy into China for processing.

When the global polysilicon market experienced its catastrophic price collapse, long-term supply agreements completely broke down. Buyers rapidly chose to default rather than honor their contracts, driven by a compounding chain of operational and legal failures:

Polysilicon originally comprised up to approximately 75% of a solar module's total BOM. When market prices crashed from \$400/kg to under \$15/kg, buyers were still contractually locked into paying Hemlock roughly \$40 to \$60/kg. Manufacturing panels with raw materials priced at triple the market rate meant selling every finished product at a massive loss, making intentional contract default the only way for these module makers to survive. These contracts were a threat to these customers' survival.

\- Buyers themselves lacked the financial soundness to buffer the price collapse. Major buyers like Yingli Solar and SolarWorld aggressively expanded using immense short-term bank debt to chase market share. These companies operated on highly leveraged, razor-thin profit margins and suffered consecutive quarters of massive losses.

\- Legal rigidity forced the break. Hemlock's contracts featured rigid liquidated damages clauses, meaning a single missed payment instantly triggered a demand for the entire value of the remaining years. By escalating routine pricing disputes into massive, existential legal battles, Hemlock eliminated any middle ground, leaving fragile buyers with no choice but to trigger a formal default and enter bankruptcy court.

Because the contracts were legally bulletproof but commercially impossible to satisfy, they triggered massive collapses and structural shakeups across the industry:

\- SolarWorld (Sachsen): Even though after years of legal battles Hemlock won a massive U.S. court judgment of roughly \$800 million against them, Sachsen had already declared insolvent and hence the funds were likely unable to be fully collected.

\- Kyocera: Opted for a massive out-of-court settlement in 2018, paying Hemlock a staggering \$450 million (51.1 billion yen) just to tear up the remaining LTA commitments.

\- JA Solar & Yingli: Reached restructured settlements or faced crippling arbitration. JA Solar settled by signing new supply deals.

The legal victory, however, proved commercially hollow. While Hemlock won billions in legal settlements on paper, the actual cash recovery was highly fractured and diluted because many of the target companies simply did not have the money to pay.

Lessons learned: More significant legal teeth, but required the supplier to take its customers to court which took years of litigation and by which time one of its customers was insolvent.

## PART II: KEY DIFFERENCES: OLD SEMICONDUCTOR LTAS VS. THE NEW MEMORY LTAS

We identify three structural differences between legacy semiconductor LTAs and the new memory LTAs, each addressing a distinct weakness that undermined prior contract structures:

\- Upfront financial commitment: cash deposits, letters of credit, or third-party guarantees, specifically back-end weighted (where the coverage ratio of remaining financial commitment as a portion of remaining purchase obligations increases substantially over time, hence increasing downside protection in the latter part of the contract).

\- Better counterparty quality: financially sound, diversified buyers replace the thinly capitalized or diffuse customer bases that undermined Hemlock and Microchip.

\- Structural demand: sustained AI-driven buildout replaces the temporary pull-in and double-ordering that ultimately undermined Microchip.

## UPFRONT FINANCIAL COMMITMENT: REAL TEETH, NOT JUST A PROMISE

The most fundamental difference is the upfront financial commitment present in the New Memory LTAs. Legacy agreements required no backend weighted financial guarantee, so remedy for non-performance depended on winning in court and then collecting from the counterparty. However, under the new structures, cash deposits, letters of credit, and third-party guarantees are posted at inception, converting a legal claim into real, standing collateral. Most importantly, these financial collateral are backend weighted, which means they offer real downside protection especially in outer years, differentiating them from typical prepayments. Sandisk backs an estimated \$69 billion of remaining performance obligations with over \$11 billion of guarantees, most sitting on (escrow-like) third-party balance sheets, while Micron backs its obligations with \$18 billion of cash deposits on its own balance sheet plus \$4 billion in letters of credit.

Because this collateral is held upfront rather than pursued entirely after a breach, the supplier is never left empty-handed while waiting on a lawsuit: if a customer walks away, the supplier can immediately draw on the cash or letter of credit already posted. Moreover, it meaningfully changes the buyer's incentives: a customer tempted to exit if spot pricing drops would forfeit real capital already posted and would also jeopardize a reliable long-term supplier relationship. As a result, continued performance becomes the more sensible choice in most circumstances.

The structure also concentrates protection where it matters most. In both cases, guarantees are weighted toward the back end of the contract, so as remaining obligations shrink over time, the fixed collateral covers a rising share of what's left, making protection strongest in the outer years, exactly when downturn risk is highest.

For additional details on the robust downside protection, please see our recent note: Memory: What do New Memory LTAs mean for sustainability of earnings and multiples? Raising SNDK TP to \$3000.

## FUNDAMENTALLY BETTER COUNTERPARTY QUALITY

The counterparty profile is categorically stronger this time, for two distinct reasons: diversification means a hyperscaler's core business cannot be destroyed by honoring higher memory LTA pricing, and concentration among a few solvent buyers makes any shortfall realistically collectible.

First, memory LTA volumes are likely predominantly with hyperscalers, which are diversified across many unrelated revenue streams, not narrowly dependent on a single commodity input. Sachsen had economics that depended almost entirely on the solar value chain; once the take-or-pay polysilicon price diverged sharply from a collapsing spot market, that single point of exposure was large enough to destroy margins and the business itself. Hyperscalers face no equivalent concentration: their revenue spans cloud infrastructure, advertising, software, and enterprise services, so memory is a meaningful input but only one of many, and its cost is a small fraction of overall earnings rather than an existential threat. Hyperscalers' scale and margin base let them absorb the cost without it threatening the core business' survival, unlike Sachsen.

Second, hyperscalers are few in number and financially sound, which matters for collectability. Hemlock's buyers were solar manufacturers - numerous, dispersed, and in several cases thinly capitalized - which meant that even after winning in court, collection depended on the solvency of a customer base that was frequently under financial distress. Microchip's non-cancelable orders, meanwhile, were spread across tens of thousands of small and mid-sized industrial and automotive customers, leaving the company with no realistic recourse when they simply stopped placing orders. However, for New Memory LTAs, the counterparty is a handful of hyperscalers and other large OEMs with investment grade balance sheets and transparent capital plans, making them more accountable and reliable, so even if there is any shortfall beyond posted collateral, it is realistically collectible rather than hollow.

## LIMITED DEMAND VS. STRUCTURAL SCALING

The demand environment is a critical factor behind why the prior LTA structures failed. Microchip's PSP sat on a shortage-driven spike and double-ordering that reversed into a glut once customers realized they had bought far more analog inventory than they would ever consume. The underlying need was inherently mean-reverting: once inventories normalized, the economic rationale for honoring long-term purchase obligations evaporated.

Memory demand today is fundamentally different because it is being fueled by a structural AI and data-center buildout, not a transient pull-forward, or double-ordering. Hyperscalers are not stockpiling memory out of panick; they are buying it because AI itself runs on data and context, and more memory directly translates into smarter, more capable systems. That is why vendors are effectively sold out on high-end memory through 2026, while hyperscalers and AI platforms are locking in volume and planning memory needs years out, treating HBM/DRAM/NAND as mission-critical inputs for AI capex.

Memory demand also has a fundamentally higher ceiling than analog ever did. Analog and microcontroller demand behaves like oxygen: once a customer has enough to build a car or an appliance, another unit adds no value, so demand hit a ceiling quickly - exactly why Microchip's backlog collapsed once the market rebalanced. However, memory demand scales much better than analogs. Every additional gigabyte of memory expands how much context a model can hold, letting it train and run with larger parameter counts and wider context windows, while in inference specifically, greater memory capacity drives smarter, more personalized, and more accurate outputs, and memory bandwidth determines how many concurrent users a system can serve. Because more memory almost always makes an AI system more capable, demand keeps compounding rather than plateauing the way analog inventory did.

Since memory is so important, the economics discourage walking away. The AI opportunity being gated is measured in trillions of dollars of future revenue and market capitalization, while the cost of honoring an LTA is small by comparison, so hyperscalers have every incentive to accept the supply they can secure rather than risk falling behind a competitor with superior memory capacity. For a hyperscaler, honoring a multi-year memory commitment is simply the rational way to keep pace in the race for smarter AI, and that structural, compounding demand is why the new memory LTAs are sustainable in a way that “non-cancelable” analog orders ultimately were not.

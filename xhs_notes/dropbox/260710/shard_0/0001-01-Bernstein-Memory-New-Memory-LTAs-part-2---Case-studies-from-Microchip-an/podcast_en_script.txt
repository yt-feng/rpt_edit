EN_B: This happened during the pandemic chip shortage of 2020 to 2022. Auto and industrial customers were scrambling for chips, so they signed these "non-cancelable, non-reschedulable" long-term orders. But it was panic buying. Many customers were double-ordering and stockpiling. When demand normalized and the inventory glut hit, these customers had no physical space or balance sheet capacity to take more deliveries. The Microchip contracts had zero upfront financial commitment. So the cost of walking away was basically nothing for the customers. They just walked. Microchip's sales then collapsed by over 42%.
EN_A: So the key lesson is that the contract terms were too soft. No real deterrent.
EN_B: Right. The Hemlock case shows the other extreme. Its contracts were legally very strong, with "take-or-pay" clauses. But when the market crashed, honoring the contract would have meant paying three to four times the market price for raw materials. That would have bankrupted the customers. So they chose to default and let Hemlock sue them. Hemlock won in court, but it took years of litigation, and by the time they won, many of the customers were insolvent. They couldn't collect most of the money.
EN_A: So whether the contracts were too soft or too hard, the old LTAs didn't really protect the supplier. So what changed this time?
EN_B: The report identifies three key structural differences. The first and most important is the introduction of "upfront financial commitments." Customers now have to post cash deposits, letters of credit, or third-party guarantees at the start of the contract. And these are "back-end weighted." That means the coverage ratio of the guarantee, as a percentage of the remaining purchase obligations, increases significantly over time. In the later years, it can cover 75% to 100% of the remaining obligations. So the cost of walking away becomes much higher as the contract progresses. That's real deterrence.
EN_A: So the supplier finally has some real "skin in the game" from the customer, not just a piece of paper.
EN_B: Exactly. The second difference is the quality of the counterparty. The old customers were often financially fragile, like those solar module makers that were dependent on subsidies. The new memory LTA customers are fortress-balance-sheet tech giants. They can easily post tens of billions in cash or letters of credit.
EN_A: So these customers not only have the money, but they also have a much lower incentive to walk away, because AI infrastructure is mission-critical for them.
EN_B: Precisely. That leads to the third difference: structural demand. The old Microchip demand was driven by panic and double-ordering, which was temporary. The new memory demand is driven by the AI and data center buildout. It's structural and sustained. The report says high-end memory capacity is already sold out through 2026 and into 2027. This supply-constrained environment gives customers a very strong incentive to honor their contracts. The cost of being short on chips is far greater than the deposit they put down.

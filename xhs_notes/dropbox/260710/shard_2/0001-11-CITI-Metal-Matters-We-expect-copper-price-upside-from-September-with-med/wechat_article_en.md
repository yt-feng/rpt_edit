## The Distortion in Demand Data Is Masking Genuine Underlying Resilience

May 2025 saw a policy-driven spike in reported solar and wind capacity additions, with implied copper end-use surging roughly 13% year-on-year. The comparison against that outsized base creates an optical weakness that has no bearing on actual consumption patterns. Strip out the solar and wind distortions, and implied global copper end-use grew approximately 1% year-on-year in May, with year-to-date growth of 1.4% on an adjusted basis versus a 2.1% decline on the unadjusted headline.

This matters because it changes the risk assessment for the second half of 2026. The base effect is self-correcting: as we move past the May 2025 spike, year-on-year comparisons will flip from negative to positive. An observer who waits for the headline data to improve before acting will miss the inflection point. The underlying demand picture is tepid but resilient, not weak. The distinction is critical for positioning.

China's domestic EV sales have been sluggish, with retail contracting roughly 15% year-on-year, but this has been offset by a 115% surge in EV exports. The implied copper consumption from EVs still grew approximately 17% year-on-year in May, supported by export channels and ongoing electrification trends. ## The Scrap Supply Response Is Muted, Confirming a Structurally Tight Market

One of the most telling data points in the current copper landscape is the behaviour of China's copper-in-scrap imports. Through May 2026, these imports were essentially flat year-on-year, despite copper prices being significantly higher than the prior year. This is not what one would expect in a well-functioning market where higher prices incentivise increased scrap collection and recycling.

The implication is that the global scrap market is relatively price-inelastic at current levels. The easy scrap has already been collected. The marginal tonne of scrap requires significantly higher prices to bring to market, or it simply does not exist in meaningful quantities. This constraint is more important than most observers appreciate.

Combined with flat mine production anticipated for 2026, the supply picture is remarkably tight. Mine supply is forecast to decline by 0.3% this year, with a disruption allowance of 4% that could easily prove conservative given the operational risks in key producing regions. Refined production growth is expected at just 0.8%, while consumption growth is projected at 2.2%. The result is a market that is essentially balanced in 2026, swinging to a deficit of approximately 400,000 tonnes in 2027 at current prices.

This deficit projection assumes only a modest cyclical demand recovery. If manufacturing activity accelerates or if AI-related infrastructure investment surprises to the upside, the deficit could be significantly larger. The supply-demand arithmetic is supportive at current prices and becomes increasingly bullish at any price below $15,000 per tonne.

## The Tariff Uncertainty Is a Headwind That Will Fade, Not a Structural Risk

The market's preoccupation with potential US Section 232 tariffs on copper cathode imports has created a distortive premium in COMEX copper relative to LME pricing. The June 30 review deadline passed without announcement, and the base case is that no tariff will be implemented. The US administration has strategic incentive to maintain ambiguity, neither imposing tariffs nor ruling them out, which will weigh on sentiment through the summer as market participants gradually fade the tail risk.

This dynamic is well understood. What is less appreciated is that the fading of tariff risk is a one-time adjustment, not a persistent headwind. Once the market fully prices the expectation of no tariff, the COMEX premium will normalise, and the focus will return to the fundamental drivers of global copper pricing. The tariff uncertainty has been a distraction from the structural story, not a refutation of it.

The more consequential risk is the potential for bearish gold price momentum to spill over into copper. Gold has been the dominant macro trade, and a sustained decline in gold prices could drag down the entire metals complex through portfolio rebalancing and sentiment channels. This is a real risk for July and August, but it is a cyclical headwind, not a structural impairment. By September, the macro backdrop is expected to shift decisively in copper's favour.

The significance of these demand segments is that they are not well captured by traditional copper end-use trackers. Third, what is your view on supply? The flat scrap imports and declining mine production suggest a tight supply picture, but supply disruptions are inherently unpredictable. The framework is simple: the bull case rests on the convergence of a dovish Fed, resilient demand, and constrained supply. The bear case rests on a hawkish Fed, a China slowdown, and supply normalisation. The weight of evidence supports the bull case, but the timing requires patience.

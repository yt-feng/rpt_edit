EN_A: So the Japanese government is revising its Basic Policy on monetary policy. What is the big deal?
EN_B: The big deal is that the government is trying to clarify that it is not pushing the central bank to prioritize economic growth over price stability. But the market is not buying it. The revision is a step forward, but there are still some very problematic phrases in the text that are keeping bond investors on edge.
EN_A: What is the core question this report is trying to answer?
EN_B: It is asking whether this revision is a genuine improvement in communication between the government and the market, or if it is still a veiled attempt to influence the Bank of Japan’s independence. The report says the revision itself is a positive move, but it does not fully address the market’s deeper concerns.
EN_A: What are those concerns? You mentioned problematic phrases.
EN_B: The first one is the phrase “without a return to deflation.” When the government puts that in a section about what it expects from the central bank, it sounds like it is setting an extremely high bar. It is not just about hitting the 2% target; it is about proving there is no risk of ever going back to deflation. That standard is not in the Bank of Japan Act.
EN_A: So what does that mean for the market?
EN_B: It means the market reads it as the government asking the central bank to use a much stricter, almost subjective, criterion for judging success. That directly challenges the central bank’s independence.
EN_A: And the second phrase?
EN_B: The second is “a virtuous cycle between wages and prices.” The thing is, the Bank of Japan itself stopped using that phrase back in April. It switched to something like “wages and prices rising moderately in interaction with each other.” Analysts think the old phrase was causing public frustration because people felt wages were not keeping up with inflation.
EN_A: So why is the government bringing it back?
EN_B: That is the problem. The market can interpret that as a signal from the government that it wants the central bank to keep easing until wage growth consistently beats inflation. In a supply-shock driven inflation, that is a recipe for a wage-price spiral. Bank of Japan Governor Ueda actually warned about this risk in a speech in May.
EN_A: How did the bond market react to the revision?
EN_B: Not well. Yields went up across the curve, except for the 30-year sector which had an auction. The market is still on high alert. The revision alone did not do the trick.
EN_A: So what is the real issue here, according to the report?
EN_B: The real issue is that the Basic Policy, which is the government’s main economic guideline, probably should not be getting into the weeds of monetary policy at all. If it has to mention it, it should use a much simpler line that respects the Bank of Japan Act. The report points to what the Ministry of Finance representative said at the June meeting as a good example.
EN_A: Does the report say the government should change it again?
EN_B: No. It does not demand more revisions. But it says something more important. The government needs to fully understand that bond market participants are worried about these specific points. And it needs to base its future policy management on that understanding. Ongoing dialogue matters more than any single text change.
EN_A: For someone listening, what questions does the report leave open?
EN_B: Two big ones. First, the report talks about the change in the “virtuous cycle” language, but it does not fully explain why the Bank of Japan switched its phrasing, or what that switch means for inflation expectations. Second, it says the market is still tense, but it does not predict whether the government will make further changes, or how the bond market will price in the risk if it does not. You would have to read the full report to get that deeper analysis.

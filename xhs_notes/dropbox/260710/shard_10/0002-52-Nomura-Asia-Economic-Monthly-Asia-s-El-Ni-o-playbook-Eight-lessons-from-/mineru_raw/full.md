## Introduction

El Niño has officially formed, and is projected to be a strong-to-very-strong episode, with the potential to morph into a "super El Niño" later this year. For most Asian economies, El Niño results in below-normal rainfall and heatwaves. This can hurt food production and trigger food price inflation. Will this time be different?

The southern oscillation index (SOI) fell to -24.9 in June, well below the -7 threshold that indicates El Niño conditions and among the lowest readings in over 20 years. The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) predicts weather conditions will transition from moderate to strong-to-very-strong El Niño starting in August, with a peak likely only later this year. Although a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) could still emerge as a potential offset, its timing and strength is uncertain, and El Niño conditions are likely to prevail.

## Impact so far in Asia

We are still in the early months, but some initial effects of El Niño conditions are visible. In India, monsoon season rainfall so far was 15% below normal, and crop sowing data show summer crop plantations are 20.8% below year-ago levels, led by rice and oilseeds. Malaysia's Economy Minister said rainfall could be 40-60% lower in several states, and that yields in key commodity sectors could fall by 8-10% during an El Niño event. The impact is also seen in food prices so far in 2026, with increases in the prices of rice, wheat, soybean oil and palm oil, while maize and sugar price changes have been more muted.

## Past El Niño cycles

Over the last 25 years, Asia has experienced seven El Niño episodes, including years of mild, moderate and strong El Niño conditions. During the past mild to moderate El Niño years, the impact on agricultural output has been somewhat negative, and food inflation picked up by 0.6pp, on average. During the severe El Niño years, there has been a more significant impact on agricultural output, although food inflation surprisingly declined, a counterintuitive result explained by three key factors.

## Why the relationship between El Niño and food prices is mixed

The linkage from El Niño to rainfall, from rainfall to food output and from food output to food price inflation is not linear, with several other factors equally important in determining the ultimate impact.

First, oil prices matter since they also drive fertilizer and transportation costs. During El Niño years, food inflation has been relatively contained when oil prices are moderate.

Second, the sensitivity of agricultural output to rainfall has moderated over time, due to increased irrigation, the use of better-quality drought-resistant seeds and a rising share of non-crop activities.

Third, governments are more proactive with trade and supply-side policies since food drives the cost of living. Most EM Asian countries maintain strategic reserves of staple crops, and these buffer stocks are released to manage price fluctuations.

## Eight lessons from 25 years of shocks

With history as a guide, and keeping current starting conditions in mind, we see eight key implications for the region this time around.

1. 2026 food inflation contained by buffers: Despite strengthening El Niño conditions, food price inflation in Asia may stay relatively contained this year. Input costs have moderated from their April peaks. Buffer stocks across key agricultural products are elevated.

2. 2027 inflation risks rise as buffers deplete: Once existing buffer stocks are depleted to stabilize prices this year, the vulnerability will be higher next season.

3. Rice, palm oil, sugar and pulses face the highest price risk: The crops most at risk of higher prices next year include rice, palm oil and sugar.

4. Philippines and India are most exposed: This reflects the larger share of food in their CPI baskets.

5. Stagflation threat: For EM Asian economies, El Niño poses a dual threat: a supply-side inflation shock and a demand-side growth shock.

6. Fiscal policy and supply measures as a first defense: Governments have already begun to take action, focusing on drought mitigation and other measures.

7. Trade protectionism is a major risk in coming months: Asia has often resorted to export restrictions to prevent domestic food prices from rising.

8. A dilemma for central banks: The monetary policy response may not necessarily be hawkish.

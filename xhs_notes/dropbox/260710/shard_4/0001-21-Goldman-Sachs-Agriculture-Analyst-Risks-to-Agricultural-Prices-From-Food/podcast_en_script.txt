EN_B: It's asking why protectionist policies are becoming a major source of upside risk for crop prices and volatility. The report introduces a "Commodity Control Cycle" framework. Basically, concentrated supply increases disruption risk, which then pushes import-dependent countries to adopt more protectionist measures, which fragments markets and makes things even more unstable.
EN_A: Sounds like a vicious cycle. So what are the key insights?
EN_B: First, El Nino. There's a 63 percent chance it develops into a "super" El Nino. And many major exporters of rice, sugar, and palm oil are in regions that get hit harder during El Nino. Even a modest weather shock, or just the fear of one, could trigger precautionary export bans.
EN_A: So even if actual production isn't affected, policy reactions alone can spike prices?
EN_B: Exactly. India did this in 2023 with rice. They imposed a precautionary ban ahead of El Nino concerns, and Asian rice prices shot up, even though domestic production turned out fine.
EN_A: What's the second insight?
EN_B: Energy security. Higher fuel prices and concerns about fuel security can push governments to raise biofuel mandates. That diverts crops like soybeans, sugar, and palm oil from export markets into domestic fuel production. Indonesia, for example, raised its biodiesel blend from 20 percent to 40-50 percent, which directly reduced its exportable palm oil surplus and lifted global vegetable oil prices.

EN_B: They start stockpiling or pursuing self-sufficiency. China is a great example. The report expects China's food self-sufficiency to rise from around 60 percent today to over 90 percent by 2035. That means they are willing to accept much higher production costs in exchange for supply security.

EN_B: That's exactly the paradox. The report has a calculation: if a regional market is half the size of the global market, the same shock delivers twice the price impact. So protectionist policies can actually backfire.

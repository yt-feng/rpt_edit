## Risks to Agricultural Prices From Food and Energy Security Policy

Global agricultural supply is highly concentrated geographically. Across key crops such as soybeans, corn, rice, sugar, and palm oil, the top three exporting countries account for 60-90% of global trade (Exhibit 1), leaving agricultural markets highly exposed to localized weather, geopolitical, and policy shocks.

Consistent with our Commodity Control Cycle framework, concentrated supply increases disruption risk because dominant producers can use exports as a source of leverage. High disruption risk, in turn, encourages countries to insulate themselves from external shocks through protectionist policies (Exhibit 2). While these policies may improve domestic resilience, they also fragment global markets, reduce traded liquidity, and may increase price volatility.

Exhibit 2: Consistent With Our Commodity Control Cycle Framework, Concentrated Supply Increases Disruption Risk

![](images/58ae4f581fb5e3ae423bcf5b60b192ea481424eba4f3f7435e2885dc940dd4dd.jpg)  
Source: GS Global Investment Research

Since 2022, protectionist measures have increased across commodity markets, likely reflecting lessons from the pandemic, China's 2021 power shortages, and the disruptions to food and energy markets following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The rise in protectionist measures has been most pronounced in agriculture, where food's essential nature makes supply security likely a political priority (Exhibit 3).

Exhibit 3: The Rise in Protectionist Measures Is Most Pronounced in Agriculture

Source: Global Trade Alert, GS Global Investment Research

India (\~40% of global rice exports), for example, imposed a precautionary ban on non-basmati rice exports in 2023 ahead of El Niño concerns, despite domestic production ultimately proving resilient, helping drive a sharp increase in Asian rice prices (Exhibit 4). $^{1}$ More recently, India has introduced sugar export restrictions amid concerns over this year's potential "super" El Niño $^{2}$ . As a result, even modest supply shocks can remove disproportionate volumes from global trade and generate price moves far larger than the underlying physical disruption would otherwise warrant.

Energy security concerns can also tighten food markets via biofuel policies. Higher biofuel blending mandates may reduce exportable crop supply by diverting soybeans, sugar, corn, rapeseed and palm oil into domestic fuel production. Between 2016 and 2026, Indonesia (\~50% of globally tradeable palm oil) raised its domestic biofuel blending mandates from 20% to 40-50%, while its crude palm oil production grew only moderately due to land constraints and stagnant yields (Exhibit 5). The sharp increase in biofuel consumption directly reduced the country's exportable palm oil surplus, tightening global vegetable oil markets and lifting the price floor across the vegetable oil complex as consumers substituted away from what had historically been a low-cost vegetable oil (Exhibit 6).

Import-dependent countries may in turn respond to rising disruption risk by insulating their supply chains. In the near term, this may take the form of stockpiling. Exhibit 7 shows that China accounts for a disproportionately large share of global commodity stockpiles relative to its share of consumption, likely reflecting a deliberate strategy to build domestic supply buffers. Over the longer term, it could involve policies aimed at boosting domestic production and reducing reliance on imports, even at higher production costs.

Exhibit 7: China Accounts for a Large Share of Global Crop Inventories

Source: USDA, GS Global Investment Research

In China, President Xi has repeatedly framed food security as a core national interest, emphasizing that “the Chinese people’s rice bowl should be firmly held in their own hands.” $^{3}$ Consistent with this objective, our equity analysts expect China’s food self-sufficiency rising to the mid-90% range by 2035, up from today’s low-60% levels—implying that nearly all food consumed domestically would be produced domestically (Exhibit 8). Achieving this self-sufficiency requires support for domestic production through minimum prices, subsidies, land protection policies, and periodic restrictions on fertilizer exports to prioritize domestic agriculture. Much of China’s grain production occurs on relatively high-cost land relative to imports from Brazil and the US, implying a willingness to accept higher production costs in exchange for greater food security. India follows a similar approach of food security through minimum support prices for grains farmers, national fertilizer procurement programs, and strategic stockpiles.

While risk-management policies — including tariffs on commodity imports, export controls, forms of state-supported domestic production, and government inventory building — are ultimately aimed at improving domestic resilience, they likely also fragment global markets, reduce traded liquidity and raise price volatility. $^{4}$ We estimate that the price impact of an external disruption (e.g. climate change shock in agriculture markets) depends on its size relative to the market that absorbs it; if a regional bloc is half the size of the global market, the same shock delivers twice the price impact compared to a global market (Exhibit 9). $^{5}$ Measures that lead to fragmentation can therefore generate outcomes that differ from their intended objective of managing disruption risk.

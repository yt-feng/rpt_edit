## WORLD CUP DISRUPTS SEASONAL ENGAGEMENT PATTERN

Total 1H26 engagement is tracking below 96B hours, landing between last year's 1H and 2H. Netflix's global hours watched are projected to come in just under 96B for 1H26, roughly -0.5% versus 2H25 and +0.5% versus 1H25. This places 1H26 between a strong 2H25, which benefited from a robust content slate, and a weaker 1H25, which suffered from a thinner slate — suggesting total 1H26 engagement hours did not meaningfully grow despite some subscriber growth (Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2).

Weekly hours watched declined steadily through the first half, with a sharp drop into June due to the FIFA World Cup. Engagement typically eases through the spring months following a strong holiday viewing season, but 2026 saw declines extend further and steepen heading into June, breaking from the normal seasonal pattern. Summer is usually a seasonal pickup period (as children are out of school etc.) but this year's June instead saw engagement decline further rather than recover. This steeper-than-normal June decline can be directly attributed to the start of the FIFA World Cup in North America. This year's tournament expanded to over 45 participating countries (up from 32 in 2022) with significantly more matches than prior editions (40 more games), creating direct competition for viewing time. Looking at engagement across individual countries, the majority of World Cup participating countries saw Netflix engagement decline versus both May 2026 and June 2025 (Exhibit 3).

This engagement pressure reinforces the growing strategic importance of sports and live programming. Netflix has already been increasing investment in this area (NFL, MLB, WWE, Boxing etc.), as data supports that sports programming has historically delivered more stable, resilient engagement than scripted content over time. This isn't isolated to Netflix — other major media and long-form platforms are increasingly recognizing the value of live sports rights – CNBC reports that Netflix, Disney & YouTube are exploring rights packages for the 2030 or 2034 World Cup, underscoring how seriously streaming and digital platforms now view live sports as core to retention and engagement strategy

## SUBSCRIBER NET ADDS UNDER PRESSURE FROM WORLD CUP, WITH LIMITED 2H CATALYSTS FOR REACCELERATION

World Cup-related engagement pressure is translating into soft 1H26 subscriber growth, with net adds tracking to just roughly 3-4M for the first half. LATAM and EMEA net adds forecasts deteriorated around the same time as the World Cup's kickoff — as viewers redirected attention away from Netflix. Even so, churn has remained notably low even in softer markets, suggesting existing subscribers still view Netflix as a "primary service" they'll return to.

This soft start creates risk to reaching the high end of Netflix's 2026 revenue guide. As we noted in our NFLX 2Q26 preview, the top end of the 12-14% revenue growth guidance implicitly assumes roughly 7% subscriber growth for the year, or net adds in the high-teens-to-\~22M range, alongside the March price increases and an expected \~\$3B in advertising revenue. With only 3-4M net adds tracked through 1H, Netflix would need a meaningful reacceleration in 2H to still hit the full year target — a bar that looks increasingly difficult given current trends.

The 2H26 content slate doesn't offer notable catalyst to drive that reacceleration. Comparing projected first-90-day views for 2H26's headline titles — Lupin S4, Emily in Paris S5, and Avatar: The Last Airbender S2 — against last year's Wednesday and Stranger Things, or Squid Game S2 the year before, the upcoming slate screens weaker apples-to-apples. The prior-year hits were instrumental in driving strong Q3/Q4 net adds, and this year's 2H slate is unlikely to fully replicate 2025's 2H engagement lift.

Live sports and advertising could still provide upside, but visibility remains limited. The addition of NFL games to Netflix's live-event slate, along with the possibility of advertising revenue outperformance, represent two swing factors that could help offset softer subscription-driven growth in the first half of the year. However, without a new structural catalyst — such as the password-sharing crackdown in prior years — it's not reasonable to assume Netflix can outrun the World Cup-related headwind through organic acceleration alone.

## FLAT TO DECLINING PER SUBSCRIBER ENGAGEMENT

Engagement per subscriber has been roughly flat to slightly declining over the past several years (a topic we've discussed in depth), pushing Netflix to continue increasing content investment to hold the line (Exhibit 6). Although Netflix still leads the industry on highest engagement—averaging \~51 hours per subscriber per month versus Disney+'s \~13 hours — the multi-year trend shows engagement intensity per user isn't meaningfully growing, meaning incremental subscriber growth increasingly has to come from adding more hours of content rather than users simply watching more.

Library content now drives the majority of hours, but its decay curve is steepening. Library titles have grown from roughly half of total hours in 1Q23 to about $63\%$ by 2Q26, yet successive additions of library content are decaying faster than prior years' cohorts once titles clear the initial release window. Because per-title engagement is shrinking over time, Netflix has to keep expanding the volume of both owned and licensed content available on the platform just to support total library hours (Exhibit 7).

Returning seasons underperform their prior installment, but their predictability still makes them a core retention tool — one that's getting steadily more expensive. Data across recent franchise sequels (Squid Game, You, The Night Agent, Bridgerton) consistently shows new seasons landing below their predecessor's first-90-day engagement, reflecting natural franchise fatigue rather than anything Netflix-specific — the same pattern has long existed in broadcast and cable television. Despite this fatigue, returning seasons still offer more reliable, lower-risk engagement than new originals, and supports Netflix's own upfront commentary indicating that the majority of its 2026 TV slate would be returning seasons. The trade-off is cost: each season tends to get more expensive than the last, and Netflix is compelled to keep developing new franchises in parallel to avoid becoming overly reliant on aging IP (Exhibit 8).

International content is playing a larger role in sustaining library engagement, but it's also becoming more expensive as competition for premium licenses intensifies. Netflix has meaningfully expanded its licensing footprint in recent quarters — adding more non-English titles— and this expanded international library content is a real contributor to the library-hours growth discussed above. But as this content increasingly proves it can travel and drive engagement, licensing costs for these titles are rising as well, meaning the "cheaper" international content strategy is becoming less of a cost offset than it once was.

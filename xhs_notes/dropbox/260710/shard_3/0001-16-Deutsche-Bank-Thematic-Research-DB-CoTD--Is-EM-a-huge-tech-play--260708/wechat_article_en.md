# The Emerging Market ETF You Think You Know Has Become a Concentrated Tech Bet — And That Changes Everything

For most of the past two decades, owning equities outside the United States has felt like a lesson in patience without payoff. The iShares MSCI Emerging Markets ETF, at the start of 2025, was essentially flat compared to its level in June 2007. Over that same period, the S&P 500 nearly quadrupled. Investors who allocated to emerging markets for diversification, for value, or for exposure to the global growth story were left wondering whether their thesis had any merit at all. Then something shifted. In 2025, the rest of the world began to show signs of change, and the narrative around ex-US equities started to evolve. But the reason for this turnaround is not what most investors assume.

The conventional explanation points to a rotation from US mega-cap tech into undervalued sectors of the global economy. The argument goes that geopolitical tailwinds, fiscal activism, and the physical infrastructure demands of artificial intelligence are finally lifting the financials, energy, materials, and industrials that dominate ex-US and emerging market indices. European banks, after nearly 15 years of underperformance, have re-rated sharply. Germany's fiscal stimulus is redirecting capital. Mexico is pouring investment into rail. The thesis seems coherent: the old economy is back, and the rest of the world is its primary beneficiary.

That story is partially true — and dangerously incomplete. A closer examination of the composition of the most widely held emerging market ETF reveals a striking and underappreciated reality: buying an EM ETF today is effectively a large tech play. This is not the diversified, broad-based exposure to developing-world growth that most investors believe they are buying. It is a concentrated bet on a handful of Asian technology manufacturers. The implications for portfolio construction, risk management, and strategic allocation are profound. If you own an EM ETF, you need to understand what you actually own — and what that means when the tech cycle turns.

## The Rotation Into Old Economy Sectors Is Real, But It Is Not What Is Driving EM Returns

The argument that geopolitical and fiscal tailwinds are reviving traditional sectors is well supported by the data. European banks have seen a dramatic re-rating on improved profitability. Defence spending and industrial self-sufficiency are driving demand for capital goods. AI-related capital expenditure is spilling over into power generation, hardware, and materials. These are precisely the sectors where ex-US and EM indices have historically been positioned relative to the S&P 500. The logic appears sound: if the world is rearming, rebuilding, and rewiring, the companies that make things should benefit.

Yet when we look at the actual performance of the EM ETF, the story is different. The index's returns have been increasingly driven by its technology holdings, not by a broad-based recovery in traditional sectors. The three largest names — TSMC, Samsung, and SK hynix — are not old economy plays. They are global leaders in semiconductor manufacturing, memory chips, and consumer electronics. Their fortunes are tied to the global technology cycle, not to domestic infrastructure spending or defence budgets in emerging markets.

This creates a paradox. The narrative that justifies EM investment today is about the return of the old economy. But the actual composition of the index means that performance is dominated by the new economy. An investor who buys an EM ETF believing they are gaining exposure to a diversified basket of developing-world growth is, in reality, making a concentrated bet on the same technology megatrends that have driven US markets. The diversification benefit is largely illusory.

The degree of concentration in the EM ETF is striking and, for many investors, likely unexpected. The top 10 holdings now account for nearly 40 percent of the fund. This level of concentration carries specific risks. First, it means that the performance of the EM ETF is highly dependent on the fortunes of a small number of companies. If TSMC faces a cyclical downturn, trade restrictions, or geopolitical disruption, the impact on the EM index will be disproportionate. Second, it means that the index is effectively a sector bet on technology hardware, not a broad-based EM allocation. An investor who already holds significant US tech exposure may be inadvertently doubling down on the same risk factors. Third, concentration at the index level reduces the diversification benefit that EM investing is supposed to provide. If the top holdings are globally integrated technology firms whose performance is correlated with US markets and global trade cycles, the index may not offer the low-correlation exposure that portfolio theory recommends.

The concentration trend also raises questions about index construction and passive investing. The EM index was never designed to be a technology sector fund, yet that is what it has become. Passive investors who assume they are buying broad market exposure are making an implicit bet on the continued dominance of a few Asian tech giants. That bet may pay off, but it should be a conscious decision, not an accidental consequence of ETF ownership.

## The Dollar's Bull Run May Be Over, But Currency Risk Remains a Double-Edged Sword

One of the persistent headwinds for US investors in overseas equities over the past 15 years has been the strength of the US dollar. From 2010 to 2024, the dollar appreciated by approximately 40 percent against a broad basket of currencies. For US-based investors who did not hedge their currency exposure, that appreciation erased a significant portion of local-currency returns from ex-US and EM equities. In 2025, the dollar fell by over 9 percent, its worst year since 2017, providing a tailwind for unhedged overseas positions.

The question is whether this represents a structural shift or a temporary reversal. The dollar's strength was supported by a combination of higher US interest rates, strong economic growth, and the dollar's role as a safe-haven currency. As the Federal Reserve cuts rates and as fiscal stimulus in Europe and elsewhere supports growth outside the US, the case for a weaker dollar becomes more plausible. For EM investors, a weaker dollar would be a significant positive, as it would boost local-currency returns when translated back into dollars.

However, currency risk cuts both ways. A weaker dollar also means that the local-currency returns of EM assets need to be strong enough to compensate for any potential dollar rebound. Moreover, many EM economies are themselves sensitive to dollar strength, particularly those with high levels of dollar-denominated debt. A sustained dollar decline would be supportive for EM equities, but the relationship is not mechanical. Investors should not assume that a weaker dollar automatically translates into strong EM performance, particularly when the index itself is so heavily concentrated in technology stocks whose revenues are globally diversified.

If the old-economy sectors finally deliver the returns that investors have been waiting for, the composition could rebalance. Any escalation in restrictions, tariffs, or sanctions could have outsized effects on the EM index. Investors who are comfortable with the technology thesis should also consider whether they are comfortable with the geopolitical tail risk that comes with it.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Reconsidering Their EM Allocation

Given the findings of the report, investors should approach their EM allocation with a clear framework. The first question is intentionality: What is the purpose of your EM exposure? If the goal is diversification across countries, sectors, and stages of development, the current composition of the EM ETF does not serve that purpose well. You are making a concentrated bet on Asian tech manufacturing. If that is your intent, then the EM ETF is a reasonable vehicle, but you should be aware that you are not getting the broad-based EM exposure that the label suggests.

The second question is overlap. Many investors already have significant exposure to global technology through US large-cap holdings. Adding an EM ETF that is heavily weighted toward the same sector increases concentration rather than reducing it. Before allocating to EM, map your total portfolio exposure to technology hardware and semiconductors. If the combined weight is higher than you intend, consider whether a different EM vehicle — such as a fundamentally weighted index, a sector-neutral ETF, or an active manager — might better achieve your diversification goals.

The third question is risk tolerance. The concentration in the EM index means that a single company-specific shock — a trade restriction, a technology shift, a governance failure — could have a disproportionate impact on your returns. If your portfolio is already exposed to idiosyncratic risk through individual stock holdings, adding a concentrated EM ETF may increase your vulnerability. Conversely, if you are comfortable with that risk and believe in the long-term growth of the Asian tech ecosystem, the EM ETF may be an efficient way to express that view.

The fourth question is currency. If you are a US-based investor, the dollar outlook matters. A weaker dollar supports unhedged EM returns, but it also introduces volatility. Consider whether you want to hedge your currency exposure or whether you are willing to accept the additional risk in exchange for the potential return boost.

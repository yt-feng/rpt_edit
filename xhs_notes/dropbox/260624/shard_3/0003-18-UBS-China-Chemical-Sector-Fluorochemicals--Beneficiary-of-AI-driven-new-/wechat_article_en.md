# AI Is Rewriting the Economic Equation for Fluorochemicals, and the Market Has Not Caught Up

The chemical sector is not typically associated with the excitement of artificial intelligence. When investors think of AI beneficiaries, they usually focus on semiconductor designers, cloud infrastructure providers, or power management firms. Yet beneath the surface of this narrative, a less visible but equally structural shift is underway. Since early May, the fluorochemical index has outperformed the broader chemical index by roughly 9%. This is not a random statistical blip. It reflects two converging forces: a regulatory delay in the phase-out of certain refrigerants, and, more importantly, a growing recognition that fluorochemical materials are becoming essential to the physical infrastructure that makes AI possible.

The argument is straightforward but not yet fully priced into valuations. Fluorochemical suppliers currently trade at a notable discount to electronic chemical material firms, despite the fact that their products are increasingly critical for semiconductor manufacturing, data center cooling, and next-generation printed circuit boards. The market sees fluorochemicals as a mature, commodity-like business. It is wrong. The AI buildout is creating demand for materials that can withstand higher thermal loads, maintain signal integrity at higher frequencies, and operate reliably in chemically aggressive environments. Fluorochemicals meet these requirements in ways that conventional materials cannot.

This article argues that the AI-driven re-rating of fluorochemicals is in its early stages, that the opportunity spans multiple downstream applications, and that investors who treat this as a single-product story will miss the more important structural shift. The analysis draws on a detailed report from a global investment bank, but extends the logic into strategic implications for decision-makers.

## The Semiconductor Cycle Is Not the Whole Story; the Material Science Shift Is

The conventional lens for looking at fluorochemicals in electronics is cyclical. Semiconductor chemical material manufacturers benefit when chip demand recovers, and they suffer when it contracts. That framing is incomplete. The current opportunity is not just about volume recovery; it is about a change in the material requirements of advanced chipmaking.

Consider electronic-grade hydrofluoric acid (EG-HF), which is used in cleaning and etching processes during wafer fabrication. The highest grade, G5, is required for 12-inch wafer manufacturing at 55nm and below. As AI chips push toward smaller nodes and higher transistor densities, the purity requirements become more stringent. The technical barriers for G5 production are high, and the top three producers exert disproportionate influence over supply. In China, the concentration is even more extreme: the CR5 for domestic G5-grade EG-HF supply stands at 98%. This is not a fragmented market. It is an oligopoly that benefits from both demand growth and import substitution.

But the more important point is structural, not cyclical. The Middle East conflict has tightened global sulfur supply since March, pushing up prices for anhydrous hydrofluoric acid, the key raw material. This creates upward pressure on EG-HF pricing at a time when demand from memory chip makers is already robust. Chinese producers, who are the world's largest suppliers of anhydrous hydrofluoric acid, are in a unique position to benefit from stable raw material access while global competitors face cost volatility. The combination of rising demand, concentrated supply, and raw material advantage creates a pricing environment that the market has not fully discounted.

The key question is not whether semiconductor demand will recover. It is whether the market has correctly assessed how much pricing power G5-grade EG-HF producers will have over the next two to three years. The evidence suggests the answer is more than current valuations imply.

## The 3M Exit Has Created a Structural Supply Gap That Chinese Suppliers Can Fill

One of the most underappreciated developments in the fluorochemical space is the exit of 3M from PFAS production at the end of 2025. PFAS, or per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, include a wide range of fluorinated fluids that are indispensable in semiconductor manufacturing. They are used for cleaning, cooling, and temperature control in processes where chemical inertness, electrical insulation, and low surface tension are non-negotiable. 3M was the world's largest supplier of these materials. Its exit was driven by increasingly stringent regulations in the US and Europe, where regulators have shifted from single-product restrictions to broader category controls.

The result is a potential supply gap. Overseas regulators are considering longer exemption periods for critical applications such as electronics and semiconductors, but the fundamental trajectory is clear: PFAS production is being phased out in Western markets, and there are no mature non-PFAS substitutes for many of these applications. This creates a substitution opportunity for Chinese producers who are not subject to the same regulatory constraints and who have already built capacity in hydrofluoroether (HFE) and perfluoropolyether (PFPE).

Among domestic players, Capchem appears best positioned. It has built 3ktpa of HFE capacity and 2.5ktpa of PFPE capacity, with a 30ktpa high-end fluorinated fine chemicals project in the pipeline. It has established stable partnerships with major domestic fabs covering both logic and memory, as well as with OEMs and cooling equipment manufacturers. In semiconductor cooling fluid, it ranks first among domestic players by market share. The verification cycles for these products are long, and the technical barriers are high. First-mover advantage matters.

The strategic implication is clear: the 3M exit is not a one-time event. It is a structural shift that will reshape the global supply chain for fluorinated fluids. Chinese suppliers are not just filling a temporary gap. They are building relationships and capacity that will outlast the current regulatory cycle. The market has not yet priced in the duration or the magnitude of this opportunity.

## Data Center Cooling Is the Next Frontier, and Fluorochemicals Are the Leading Candidate

The AI narrative has focused heavily on compute power, but the physical constraints of cooling are becoming a bottleneck. AI servers generate significantly more heat than traditional servers, and the thermal management requirements are pushing existing cooling solutions to their limits. Two technologies are emerging as potential solutions: polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) for next-generation printed circuit boards, and fluorinated fluids for liquid cooling.

PTFE has a low dielectric constant and low dielectric loss, which makes it a strong candidate for high-frequency PCB applications. As AI servers demand higher signal integrity and lower power loss, the material properties of PTFE become increasingly valuable. The leading PTFE producers with advanced technologies are likely to benefit, but the timing and scale of adoption remain uncertain. The question is not whether PTFE will be used, but how quickly the PCB industry can transition from existing materials to PTFE-based solutions.

For liquid cooling, the picture is more complex. Two main approaches are being explored: cold-plate liquid cooling and immersion cooling. Cold-plate systems use refrigerants such as R134a or R1234yf to remove heat from server components. Immersion cooling, which submerges servers in dielectric fluids, is more radical but potentially more effective for high-density deployments. Fluorinated fluids are the leading candidate for immersion cooling, but the mainstream solution has not yet been standardized.

This is where the investment thesis becomes more nuanced. The opportunity is real, but the path to adoption is unclear. Which cooling architecture will dominate? How quickly will data center operators transition from air cooling to liquid cooling? What are the regulatory risks for fluorinated fluids in data center applications? These questions do not have definitive answers yet, and that uncertainty is precisely why the market has not fully priced in the opportunity. Investors who wait for clarity will pay a premium. Those who accept the uncertainty and position early will capture the upside, but they must also accept the risk that alternative cooling technologies could emerge.

## The Report Raises More Questions Than It Answers, and That Is the Point

A careful reading of the underlying report reveals several analytical gaps that are worth highlighting. First, the earnings estimates for the companies mentioned are heavily dependent on assumptions about AI-related downstream exposure. For EM Technology, the report estimates that 60-65% of 2028E earnings will come from AI-related downstream applications. For Guanggang, the figure is above 50%. For Capchem, it is 15-20%. For Dongyue, it is only 3-5%. These are very different profiles, and the valuation multiples reflect that dispersion. EM Technology trades at 96x 2026E PE, while Dongyue trades at 13x. The market is already differentiating. But are the assumptions about AI exposure accurate? That is an open question.

Second, the report does not fully address the regulatory risk for fluorochemicals in AI applications. PFAS regulations are tightening globally, and while Chinese producers are currently exempt from the most stringent restrictions, that could change. If China adopts similar PFAS regulations, the cost structure for domestic producers would shift dramatically. The report acknowledges this risk implicitly by highlighting the importance of non-PFAS alternatives, but it does not model the probability or impact of regulatory changes in China.

Third, the competitive dynamics in fluorinated fluids are not static. Other Chinese producers, such as Zhejiang Juhua and Yongtai Technology, have also established capacity. The report argues that Capchem is better positioned due to its customer relationships and application service capabilities, but it does not provide a rigorous comparison of capacity, technology, or cost structures across these players. The market share data for domestic players in semiconductor cooling fluid is cited, but the methodology and sample size are not disclosed.

These gaps are not weaknesses in the report. They are the natural result of analyzing an emerging trend where the data is incomplete and the future is uncertain. For readers, the implication is clear: the investment thesis for fluorochemicals is compelling, but it requires active monitoring and a willingness to update assumptions as new information becomes available.

## A Decision Framework for Investors Evaluating Fluorochemical Exposure

For investors who want to act on this thesis, the challenge is not identifying the opportunity but sizing the position and selecting the right exposure. The following framework is designed to help decision-makers evaluate their options.

First, assess the type of AI exposure. Not all fluorochemical companies are created equal. Some, like Dongyue, have limited direct AI exposure but benefit from broader chemical cycle recovery. Others, like EM Technology and Guanggang, have very high AI exposure but trade at correspondingly high multiples. The decision depends on whether an investor wants a pure play on AI materials or a diversified bet on the fluorochemical cycle.

Second, evaluate the competitive moat. The fluorochemical value chain is not equally defensible across all segments. G5-grade EG-HF has high technical barriers and concentrated supply. Fluorinated fluids have long customer verification cycles and high switching costs. PTFE has strong intellectual property protection. Companies that operate in segments with high barriers to entry are better positioned to sustain pricing power and margin expansion.

Third, consider the regulatory timeline. The 3M exit creates a near-term opportunity, but the long-term regulatory trajectory is uncertain. Investors should monitor PFAS regulations in China and the EU, as well as the development of non-PFAS alternatives. If alternative cooling technologies or substitute materials become commercially viable, the valuation premium for fluorochemicals could erode.

Fourth, think about geographic exposure. Chinese producers benefit from stable raw material access and a supportive policy environment, but they also face geopolitical risk. If trade tensions escalate, export markets for Chinese fluorochemicals could be disrupted. Investors should consider whether their thesis depends on continued access to global semiconductor and data center supply chains.

Finally, be disciplined about valuation. The dispersion in multiples across the fluorochemical space is wide, and some of the higher-multiple names already reflect optimistic assumptions about AI adoption. The opportunity is real, but it is not free. Investors who pay too much for growth will underperform, even if the thesis plays out.

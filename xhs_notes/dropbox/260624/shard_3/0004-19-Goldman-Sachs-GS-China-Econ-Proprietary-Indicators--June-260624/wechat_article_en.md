# China’s Economy Is Not Recovering Symmetrically: The Manufacturing Engine Masks a Weakening Domestic Core

The latest proprietary economic indicators from a global investment bank’s China economics team present a picture that is far more complex than the headline numbers suggest. In May, the China Current Activity Indicator (CAI) edged up to +4.6% month-on-month annualized, a notable improvement from April’s +2.9%. On its surface, this appears to be a constructive signal—a modest acceleration in activity that could reassure markets concerned about a prolonged slowdown. But this is precisely where the danger of surface-level analysis lies.

The improvement in the CAI was overwhelmingly driven by the manufacturing sector. This is not a broad-based recovery. It is a narrow, sector-specific acceleration that coexists with persistent weakness in domestic demand, a tightening of financial conditions, and a macro policy stance that remains surprisingly restrained. The data tells a story of an economy running on two separate tracks: one powered by industrial output and exports, the other dragging under the weight of weak consumption, a struggling property sector, and constrained fiscal firepower.

For investors and strategists, the critical question is not whether China’s economy is recovering. It is whether the recovery is sustainable and self-reinforcing. The evidence from these proprietary indicators suggests it is not. The manufacturing-led momentum may be creating a false sense of security, masking structural fragilities that could reassert themselves in the second half of the year. Understanding the composition of this recovery—not just its aggregate pace—is the only way to position for what comes next.

## The Manufacturing Uptick Is Real, But It Is Also a Potential Distraction

The decomposition of the CAI reveals a stark divergence. Manufacturing sectors led the improvement in May, while consumption and the “others” category—which includes housing and labor markets—remained subdued. This is not a trivial detail. A recovery that relies disproportionately on manufacturing is inherently fragile for several reasons.

First, manufacturing output in China is heavily influenced by external demand and global inventory cycles. The bank’s own outside-in export tracker outperformed official export data in April, suggesting that trade flows may be stronger than customs figures indicate. But this is a double-edged sword. If global demand softens or trade tensions escalate, the manufacturing engine loses its fuel quickly. Domestic demand, by contrast, has not yet stepped in to fill the gap.

Second, manufacturing-driven growth tends to have lower employment multipliers than services or consumption. A factory producing more goods does not necessarily translate into higher household incomes or stronger consumer spending. This explains why the consumption component of the CAI remains weak even as overall activity picks up. The manufacturing sector is generating output, but it is not generating the virtuous cycle of income and spending that would make the recovery self-sustaining.

Third, the manufacturing proxy itself—based on median year-on-year growth in production of metal cutting machines, autos, power generating equipment, and microcomputers—may be capturing a restocking phenomenon rather than genuine end-demand strength. The bank’s inventory tracker suggests that inventory levels may stay roughly flat in Q2 2026, and the boost from inventory changes to sequential GDP growth is likely to decline. If manufacturers are producing to rebuild inventories rather than to meet final demand, the current momentum is borrowing from the future.

For investors, the implication is clear: do not extrapolate the May CAI improvement linearly. Manufacturing can sustain the headline for another quarter, but without a pickup in domestic demand, the recovery will hit a ceiling.

## Domestic Demand Is the Weak Link, and the Proprietary Indicators Are Flashing Red

The bank’s import-implied domestic demand proxy is one of the most revealing indicators in the entire report. It suggests weak domestic demand growth in Q2. This is not a forecast based on survey soft data; it is derived from actual import flows assigned to final demand using China’s input-output tables. When China’s domestic economy is strong, it pulls in more raw materials and intermediate goods. When it is weak, import volumes stagnate or decline. The data is unambiguous: domestic demand is not accelerating.

This finding is corroborated by the MAP surprise index, which shows that recent macro data have come in below market expectations. The market has been repeatedly disappointed not because the data is catastrophically bad, but because expectations were set too high by the manufacturing narrative. The disconnect between what manufacturing signals and what the broader economy delivers is now measurable.

Why does this matter? Because China’s economic model has been shifting toward domestic consumption as a growth driver for years. If domestic demand remains weak even as manufacturing picks up, it suggests that the transition is stalling. The property sector, which has historically been a key channel for transmitting income and wealth to households, remains in a prolonged downturn. The bank’s city-level property relative tightness index shows continued easing, but easing does not equal recovery. It simply means policy is becoming less restrictive; it does not mean demand is returning.

The strategic implication is that any investment thesis predicated on a Chinese consumer recovery in the second half of the year is not supported by the proprietary data. The import-implied demand proxy and the CAI’s consumption sub-component both argue for caution.

## Financial Conditions Are Tightening, and Credit Impulse Is Turning Negative

One of the most underappreciated developments in the latest data is the slight tightening of the China Financial Conditions Index (FCI) in May. This tightening was driven by two factors: FX appreciation against the trade-weighted basket and weaker equity market performance. On its own, a modest tightening is not alarming. But when combined with the bank’s estimate that credit impulse may stay negative in H2 due to weak credit growth, the picture becomes more concerning.

Credit impulse is a leading indicator for economic activity. When credit impulse turns negative, it typically precedes a slowdown in GDP growth by two to three quarters. The fact that the bank expects negative credit impulse through the second half of 2026 implies that the current manufacturing-led momentum may face a headwind from the financial side later this year.

The mechanism is straightforward. Weak credit growth means less funding for investment, working capital, and consumption. Even if manufacturing output is strong today, if companies cannot access affordable credit to sustain production or expand capacity, the recovery will lose steam. The bank’s FCI includes both price-based measures (yields, exchange rates) and quantity-based measures (M2, TSF flows). The tightening reflects a genuine constraint, not just noise.

For investors, this argues for a defensive posture in sectors that are sensitive to credit availability. Real estate, small and medium enterprises, and consumer durables are likely to face headwinds. Conversely, sectors with strong cash flows and low leverage may be relatively insulated.

## The Policy Stance Is Surprisingly Restrained, and the Fiscal Story Is More Complex Than It Appears

The bank’s China domestic macro policy proxy tightened slightly in May, driven by tighter fiscal policy and slower credit growth. This is a critical finding because it contradicts the narrative that Chinese policymakers are aggressively stimulating the economy. The augmented fiscal deficit (AFD) metric narrowed further in May, indicating that the overall fiscal impulse is contracting, not expanding.

To be sure, the fiscal “spend-through” ratio rose slightly, suggesting that the government is deploying the funds it has raised. But the denominator—total government revenue and bond issuance—is not growing fast enough to generate a strong stimulus. Government bond net issuance is set to accelerate in the coming months, but this is a mechanical projection based on annual quotas and seasonal patterns. It does not necessarily mean that fiscal policy will become expansionary in real terms.

The nuance here is important. There is a difference between fiscal easing and fiscal normalization. What the data shows is that fiscal policy is normalizing from a period of extreme restraint, not actively stimulating. The AFD narrowing indicates that off-budget spending channels—local government special bonds, land sales revenue, LGFV bonds, policy bank support—are contracting. This is the legacy of the property sector downturn and the ongoing restructuring of local government finances.

For investors, the key takeaway is that policy support is unlikely to be the catalyst for a sustained recovery. The bank’s indicators suggest that the policy stance is still in a tightening phase, even if the pace of tightening is slow. Expecting a major fiscal or monetary stimulus in the near term is not supported by the proprietary data.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Missing Transmission Mechanism

For all its analytical rigor, the report leaves one critical question unanswered: why is the manufacturing recovery not transmitting to the broader economy? The CAI decomposition shows that manufacturing is strong, but consumption and housing are weak. The import-implied demand proxy shows weak domestic demand. The FCI shows tightening. But the report does not provide a causal framework for why these pieces are disconnected.

Several hypotheses are worth considering. One is that the manufacturing recovery is export-led, meaning the income generated accrues to corporate profits and capital rather than to household wages. Another is that the property sector downturn has destroyed household wealth to such an extent that the wealth effect from manufacturing cannot compensate. A third is that consumer confidence remains structurally impaired, preventing the normal multiplier effects from taking hold.

The report’s indicators can describe the symptoms, but they cannot diagnose the disease without additional analysis. This is where the reader must engage critically. The data tells you what is happening; it does not tell you why. Understanding the transmission mechanism—or the lack thereof—is essential for forming an investment view.

For example, if the manufacturing recovery is not generating household income growth, then consumption will remain weak regardless of how strong industrial production becomes. In that case, the recovery is inherently fragile and will reverse when the external demand cycle turns. If, on the other hand, the transmission mechanism is merely delayed, then the recovery could broaden in Q3 or Q4. The proprietary indicators cannot distinguish between these two scenarios, which is precisely why they must be interpreted within a broader strategic framework.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: How to Read the Proprietary Indicators

Given the complexity of the data, investors need a systematic approach to interpreting these indicators. The following framework is designed to translate the bank’s proprietary data into actionable signals.

First, treat the CAI headline as a lagging indicator of manufacturing strength, not a leading indicator of overall recovery. The CAI is useful for confirming trends, but it should not be used to predict inflection points. When the CAI improves but the consumption sub-component does not, it is a warning sign, not a confirmation.

Second, use the import-implied domestic demand proxy as the primary gauge of underlying economic health. This indicator is less noisy than the CAI and more directly tied to the domestic economy. If it remains weak for two consecutive quarters, the probability of a broad-based recovery declines significantly.

Third, monitor the FCI and credit impulse together. A tightening FCI combined with a negative credit impulse is a reliable leading indicator of a growth deceleration. If both conditions are present, reduce exposure to cyclical and credit-sensitive sectors.

Fourth, track the fiscal spend-through ratio alongside the augmented fiscal deficit. A rising spend-through ratio is positive, but only if the AFD is also expanding. If the AFD is contracting while the spend-through ratio is rising, it means the government is simply spending its existing resources faster, not adding new stimulus. This is a neutral signal at best.

Fifth, use the property relative tightness index as a sentiment gauge, not a demand gauge. Easing property policies can stabilize expectations, but they do not guarantee a recovery in housing starts or property investment. The index is useful for understanding the policy direction, but it should be cross-referenced with actual transaction data and developer financials.

Finally, treat the outside-in trade measures as a cross-check for official data. If the outside-in export tracker consistently outperforms official figures, it may indicate that official data is understating trade flows. Conversely, if the outside-in import tracker underperforms, it suggests that domestic demand is even weaker than official import data implies. This divergence is itself a signal worth monitoring.

## The Open Questions That Demand a Deeper Dive

The proprietary indicators in this report raise more questions than they answer, and that is precisely their value. For the serious investor, the data is not an endpoint; it is a starting point for further analysis.

Why is the manufacturing recovery not lifting consumption? Is it a structural issue related to income distribution, or a cyclical issue related to consumer confidence? The answer determines whether the current weakness is temporary or permanent.

Why is credit impulse turning negative despite relatively accommodative monetary conditions? Is it because banks are reluctant to lend, or because borrowers are reluctant to borrow? The distinction matters for forecasting the credit cycle.

Why is fiscal policy tightening even as the economy shows signs of weakness? Is this a deliberate policy choice, or a constraint imposed by local government balance sheets and land revenue shortfalls? The answer has implications for the trajectory of fiscal support in 2027.

These are not questions that can be answered by a single data release. They require a sustained analytical effort, cross-referencing multiple data sources, and engaging with the underlying economic dynamics. The bank’s proprietary indicators provide the raw material, but the synthesis is the investor’s responsibility.

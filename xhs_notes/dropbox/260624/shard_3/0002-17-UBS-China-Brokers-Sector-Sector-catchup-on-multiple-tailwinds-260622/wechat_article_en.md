# China Brokerages Are Not Just a Cyclical Play: The Rally Reflects a Structural Shift in Market Architecture

The 6.1% surge in China brokerage H-shares on June 22, set against a 0.65% decline in the Hang Seng Index, was more than a short-term bounce. It marked the moment the sector flipped into year-to-date outperformance of approximately 7 percentage points relative to the broader market. For investors who have watched Chinese financials trade at a persistent discount, this move demands a serious re-examination of the thesis.

The conventional view holds that Chinese brokerages are high-beta proxies for equity market sentiment—stocks to buy when the market is hot and sell when it cools. That framing is no longer sufficient. What is unfolding is a confluence of cyclical earnings momentum and structural policy reforms that together suggest a re-rating cycle may be underway. The catalyst was strong second-quarter results, but the sustaining force is a deliberate regulatory agenda to reshape the industry's business model.

Investors who dismiss this as another tactical rally risk missing a strategic entry point. The sector's valuation remains undemanding, with covered brokers trading at an average of 1.2 times 2026 estimated price-to-book for H-shares and 0.8 times for A-shares. These multiples do not yet reflect the improving earnings trajectory or the long-term growth drivers now embedded in policy. The question is not whether the sector is cheap—it is. The question is whether the catalysts are durable enough to close the valuation gap.

Three forces are converging. First, the operating environment for Q2 is shaping up to be materially stronger than consensus expects. Second, the policy agenda emerging from the Lujiazui Forum and subsequent regulatory actions is more coherent and far-reaching than the market has priced. Third, the competitive dynamics within the industry are shifting in favor of leading brokers with diversified franchises, creating a winner-take-most dynamic that rewards selectivity.

This article unpacks each of these forces, identifies what the available analysis does not yet answer, and provides a decision framework for investors navigating this opportunity.

## Strong Second-Quarter Operating Metrics Signal a Profit Inflection That Is Not Yet Priced In

The most immediate catalyst for the June rally was mounting evidence that listed brokers are on track for a robust Q2 earnings season. First-quarter recurring net profit after tax had already grown 39% year-on-year, but the momentum has accelerated into the current quarter. A-share stock average daily turnover jumped to RMB 2.8 trillion in Q2-to-date, up 1.2 times year-on-year and 9% quarter-on-quarter. This is not merely a recovery from a low base; it represents a sustained expansion in market activity that directly feeds brokerage commissions and margin income.

Margin financing and securities lending balances rose to RMB 3.0 trillion, up 62% year-on-year and 14% quarter-on-quarter. This growth signals that retail and institutional investors are increasing leverage, a behavior pattern that typically correlates with bullish sentiment and higher turnover. The combination of higher volumes and higher leverage creates a compounding effect on broker revenues, since margin interest income carries high incremental margins.

The underwriting pipeline is equally telling. A-share IPO fundraising surged 65% year-on-year and 91% quarter-on-quarter. This is not a broad-based boom; it reflects a targeted policy push to channel capital into technology and deep-tech sectors via the STAR Market and ChiNext. Brokers with strong investment banking franchises in these segments are capturing disproportionate share. The refinancing market, by contrast, has contracted 45% year-on-year, but this is a deliberate policy choice to prioritize primary issuance over secondary fundraising, and it benefits the same leading underwriters.

Retail sentiment remains buoyant. New hybrid fund issuance reached RMB 96 billion, up 1.8 times year-on-year, while new trading account openings hit 5.3 million in April and May combined, up 51% year-on-year. This is not speculative froth; it reflects a structural shift in household asset allocation toward financial markets as property investment loses its relative appeal. The CSI 300 index has rebounded 13.7% in Q2-to-date, reversing a 3.9% decline in Q1, providing a tailwind for brokers' proprietary investment portfolios.

The so-what is clear: the earnings trajectory for Q2 is likely to surprise to the upside, and the market has not fully adjusted its expectations. Consensus estimates, which are backward-looking, do not yet incorporate the sustained nature of this activity. When the actual results are released, the earnings revisions alone could drive further re-rating.

## The Lujiazui Forum and Subsequent Policy Actions Signal a Coherent Agenda to Deepen Capital Markets

The June rally was not solely about earnings. It was triggered by a series of policy signals that collectively represent the most coherent capital market reform agenda in years. The Lujiazui Forum set the tone with a clear emphasis on market stabilization, long-term capital attraction, and the broadening of financial instruments. This is not empty rhetoric; it is backed by specific regulatory actions.

The Action Plan for Stabilizing and Optimizing Foreign Investment Attraction, jointly issued by the Ministry of Commerce, the National Development and Reform Commission, and the Ministry of Finance, is a landmark document. It extends derivative market access to foreign institutions, including treasury futures, and grants them fund advisory licenses. These moves are designed to accelerate the industry's pivot toward a buy-side advisory model, where brokers earn recurring fees rather than transactional commissions.

This shift is critical. The traditional brokerage model in China has been heavily reliant on retail trading commissions, which are structurally declining due to competition and regulatory pressure. A buy-side advisory model, by contrast, generates more stable, asset-under-management-linked revenues. Foreign institutions have long lobbied for this access, and their entry will raise the competitive bar, forcing domestic brokers to upgrade their advisory capabilities rather than compete on price.

The China Securities Regulatory Commission has also been active. In May, it rolled out the first departmental rules governing derivatives, building toward long-term upside for the sector. In April, it unveiled a new round of ChiNext reforms, introducing a fourth set of listing criteria that broadens the pipeline for technology and growth companies. These are not isolated measures; they form a coherent strategy to deepen the capital market ecosystem.

The implications for brokers are multi-layered. Wealth management units benefit from a broader product shelf and higher fee income. Brokerage divisions benefit from sustained trading volumes. Institutional businesses benefit from new hedging and execution opportunities. Leading brokers with integrated platforms are best positioned to capture these flows, while smaller players risk being marginalized.

## Leading Brokers Are Disproportionately Positioned to Benefit from Industry Consolidation and Regulatory Favor

Not all brokers will benefit equally from this environment. The analysis identifies a clear preference for leading players: CICC, CITICS, HTSC, and GTHT. This is not a blanket sector call; it is a selective bet on firms with specific structural advantages.

CICC benefits from its dominant position in investment banking and its exposure to the STAR Market, where its integrated research, underwriting, and investment capabilities create a virtuous cycle. CITICS combines scale with a diversified franchise that spans brokerage, asset management, and proprietary trading, giving it pricing power and earnings stability. HTSC has built a strong retail and wealth management platform that is well-suited to the advisory model shift. GTHT, formed from the merger of GTJA and Haitong, is a consolidation play that could unlock significant cost synergies and market share gains.

The industry is at an inflection point where regulatory support, broader non-brokerage franchises, higher pricing power, and accelerating consolidation are all favoring the largest players. Smaller brokers face margin compression from commission rate declines and regulatory penalties, while also lacking the scale to invest in technology and advisory capabilities.

The valuation case is compelling. Covered brokers trade at an average of 1.2 times 2026 estimated price-to-book for H-shares and 0.8 times for A-shares. These multiples are undemanding relative to historical averages, especially given the improving earnings outlook and structural growth drivers. Even after the June rally, the H-share broker index was down 0.4% year-to-date, while the A-share broker index was down 6.8%. The market is still pricing in skepticism that the policy tailwinds will translate into sustained earnings growth.

The so-what for investors is that selectivity matters. A passive approach to the sector is likely to underperform an active selection of leading brokers with clear competitive moats. The consolidation theme is still in its early stages, and the winners will emerge with stronger market positions and higher returns on equity.

## What the Analysis Does Not Yet Answer: The Sustainability of Market Activity and the Regulatory Implementation Risk

For all its strengths, the available analysis leaves several critical questions unanswered. The most important is the sustainability of the current market activity. The Q2 metrics are impressive, but they reflect a period of elevated sentiment and policy stimulus. Can A-share average daily turnover sustain at or above RMB 2.8 trillion? Can IPO fundraising continue at this pace? The answers depend on the broader macroeconomic environment, which remains uncertain.

The analysis does not address the potential for a market downturn. The risk statement in the original report lists this as the primary risk, but the bullish thesis implicitly assumes that current conditions persist. If the CSI 300 were to reverse its Q2 gains, the impact on broker earnings would be immediate and severe, particularly for proprietary trading books and margin lending portfolios.

A second unanswered question concerns the pace and effectiveness of regulatory implementation. The policy signals from the Lujiazui Forum and the Action Plan are encouraging, but the track record of Chinese financial reform is mixed. Rules can be announced quickly but implemented slowly, and foreign institutions may be cautious about committing capital until they see concrete progress on market access and operational flexibility.

Third, the competitive dynamics of the advisory model shift are not fully explored. Foreign institutions entering the derivative and advisory markets will compete for the same high-net-worth and institutional clients that domestic brokers are targeting. The assumption that domestic brokers will benefit from this opening may be too optimistic if foreign players capture a disproportionate share of the most profitable segments.

Finally, the analysis does not quantify the potential impact of commission rate compression. The risk statement acknowledges this, but the bullish case does not incorporate it into the earnings projections. If commission rates decline faster than volume growth, the net effect on brokerage revenues could be neutral or negative, even in a rising market.

These open questions do not invalidate the bullish thesis, but they do argue for a disciplined approach to position sizing and risk management. The opportunity is real, but it is not without significant caveats.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Determine Your Exposure

For investors considering whether to increase exposure to Chinese brokerages, the following framework can help clarify the decision.

First, what is your view on the sustainability of A-share market activity? If you believe that the current turnover and IPO pipeline are sustainable for the next 12 to 18 months, then the cyclical earnings case is compelling. If you expect a slowdown, then the sector is likely to underperform, and selective exposure to leading brokers may offer some downside protection but not immunity.

Second, do you trust the regulatory agenda to deliver structural change? If you believe that the Lujiazui Forum and the Action Plan represent a genuine shift toward deeper, more open capital markets, then the long-term growth story is intact. If you view these as temporary stimulus measures, then the sector is a tactical trade rather than a strategic investment.

Third, are you willing to be selective? A passive investment in a broker index fund will capture the sector beta but also the downside from weaker players. Active selection of leading brokers with diversified franchises, strong investment banking pipelines, and exposure to the advisory model shift offers a better risk-reward profile. The analysis identifies CICC, CITICS, HTSC, and GTHT as preferred names, but investors should conduct their own due diligence on valuation and execution risk.

The framework leads to a clear conclusion for those with a constructive view on Chinese capital markets: the sector offers a re-rating opportunity that is not yet fully priced. The combination of strong Q2 earnings, supportive policy, and undemanding valuations creates a favorable entry point. But the conviction must come from a belief in the structural story, not just the cyclical tailwind.

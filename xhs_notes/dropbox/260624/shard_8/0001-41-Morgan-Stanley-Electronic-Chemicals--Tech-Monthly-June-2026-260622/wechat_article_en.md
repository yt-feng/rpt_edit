# The Electronic Chemicals Cycle Is Not Peaking. The Market Is Underpricing the Structural Shift in Advanced Packaging.

The semiconductor industry has spent the last eighteen months fixated on the demand cycle for logic and memory, but the real inflection is happening in the materials that enable the chips to function at all. A global investment bank’s June 2026 report on Japanese electronic chemicals makes a quiet but powerful argument: the current cycle is not about volume recovery. It is about a structural change in how chips are built, and the materials companies that serve advanced packaging are entering a period of sustained pricing power and capacity discipline that most investors have not yet priced in.

The report, published on June 22, 2026, maintains an In-Line industry view on both Electronic Chemicals and Fine Chemicals, but the stock-level ratings tell a more nuanced story. Resonac Holdings is rated Overweight with a price target of JPY 22,400, implying 21.8% upside. Sumitomo Bakelite is also Overweight at JPY 8,000. Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, by contrast, is rated Equal-Weight with a target of JPY 9,400, implying a 15.5% downside from its current price of JPY 11,125. This divergence is not random. It reflects a deliberate analytical bet on which companies are positioned for the next leg of the advanced packaging cycle and which are being carried by momentum that may not persist.

The critical insight is this: the market is treating the strong performance in FC-BGA CCLs (flip-chip ball grid array copper-clad laminates) and semiconductor sealants as cyclical tailwinds. The report suggests these are structural. The demand for high-performance substrates is not a temporary inventory rebuild. It is a permanent shift driven by the disaggregation of chips into chiplets, the rise of heterogeneous integration, and the insatiable need for bandwidth in AI and data center applications. The companies that control the materials for these substrates are not commodity suppliers. They are bottleneck providers in a supply chain that is becoming more, not less, complex.

This article unpacks the report’s argument, extends its logic into second-order implications, and provides a decision framework for investors who want to separate the structural winners from the cyclical noise. ## The Materials Bottleneck Is Moving from Silicon to Substrates, and That Changes the Valuation Logic

The conventional semiconductor investment framework focuses on wafer fab equipment and foundry utilization rates. The report implicitly challenges this by focusing on a different part of the value chain: the materials that go into advanced packaging. FC-BGA CCLs are the unsung heroes of the AI era. Every high-performance chip, whether from NVIDIA, AMD, or the custom ASICs being built for hyperscalers, needs a substrate that can handle extreme density, thermal management, and signal integrity. The report notes that FC-BGA CCLs are showing "very strong performance," a phrase that in analyst-speak means demand is exceeding supply and pricing is favorable.

The structural shift here is that substrate complexity is rising faster than capacity. A standard FC-BGA substrate ten years ago had four to six layers. Today, high-end substrates for AI accelerators can have twenty layers or more. Each additional layer requires more CCL material, more precision, and more quality control. The companies that make these materials, such as Resonac, are not just selling a commodity. They are selling a solution that becomes more valuable as the substrate stack grows.

The second-order implication is that the pricing cycle for these materials is decoupling from the broader semiconductor cycle. In the past, material prices fell when chip demand softened because customers could switch suppliers or use lower-grade materials. That is no longer true for advanced substrates. The qualification process for a new material supplier can take eighteen months or more, and once a material is qualified for a specific substrate design, switching costs are prohibitive. This creates a moat that the report captures through its preference for Resonac over Tokyo Ohka Kogyo. Resonac is likely to embark on capacity additions and further price hikes for CCLs. That is not a cyclical call. It is a structural pricing power call.

## Sumitomo Bakelite Is Building a Dual-Moat Strategy in Encapsulants, but the Real Test Is Yet to Come

The report highlights Sumitomo Bakelite as a standout within the coverage universe, and the rationale is instructive. The company is seeing increasing sales of granular encapsulants to Intel, and sample supplies of liquid encapsulants to TSMC and OSATs (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test companies) are starting up. This is not a minor development. Encapsulants are the protective materials that surround semiconductor packages, and the shift from granular to liquid encapsulants is a technological upgrade that improves reliability and enables thinner, more complex packages.

The strategic significance is that Sumitomo Bakelite is effectively building a dual-moat position. In granular encapsulants, it has an entrenched relationship with Intel, which is one of the largest consumers of advanced packaging materials in the world. In liquid encapsulants, it is positioning itself to serve TSMC and the broader OSAT ecosystem in Taiwan. The report notes that semiconductor sealant materials are "doing well in the Taiwan market," which is the epicenter of advanced packaging for AI chips.

The open question that the report does not fully answer is whether Sumitomo Bakelite can maintain its pricing power as volume scales. The company’s business profit for fiscal year 2025 is estimated at JPY 34.5 billion, with consensus expecting JPY 38.0 billion for fiscal year 2026. That implies a growth rate of roughly 10%, which is respectable but not explosive. The risk is that as liquid encapsulants become more standardized, competition from other Japanese chemical companies or from Korean and Chinese entrants could compress margins. The report’s Overweight rating suggests confidence that the dual-moat strategy will hold, but the margin trajectory over the next two years will be the real test.

## The China Photoresist Story Is Real, but It Is Not What Most Investors Think

One of the most counterintuitive findings in the report is that "the China market is driving photoresists." For years, the narrative around Chinese semiconductor materials has been one of substitution and self-sufficiency. Chinese companies are trying to develop their own photoresists to reduce dependence on Japanese suppliers like Tokyo Ohka Kogyo and JSR. The report does not dispute that trend. Instead, it suggests that the current demand surge in China is coming from a different source: the rapid expansion of mature-node capacity by Chinese foundries.

The Chinese government’s push to build self-sufficient semiconductor capacity has led to a boom in construction of 28nm and 40nm fabs. These fabs need photoresists, and Japanese suppliers are still the dominant providers for these nodes. The report notes that Tokyo Ohka Kogyo’s lower growth rate compared to JSR may reflect lower MOR (metal-oxide resist) capacity, which is the high-end resist used for EUV lithography. The implication is that Tokyo Ohka Kogyo is being penalized for not having enough exposure to the most advanced node, while its strength in mature-node photoresists is being overlooked.

The second-order implication is that the China photoresist story has a shelf life. As Chinese domestic suppliers improve their capabilities, they will gradually capture more of the mature-node market. Japanese suppliers will need to move up the technology curve to maintain their margins. The report’s Equal-Weight rating on Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, combined with a price target below the current price, suggests that the market has already started to discount this risk. The question for investors is whether the China-driven demand surge is strong enough to offset the long-term substitution risk, or whether Tokyo Ohka Kogyo is a value trap disguised as a growth stock.

## What the Report Does Not Fully Answer: The Capacity Cycle for CCLs and the Risk of Overinvestment

No analyst report can answer every question, and the most valuable reports are the ones that leave meaningful open questions. This report’s strongest argument is that Resonac is likely to embark on capacity additions for CCLs. The logic is sound: demand is strong, pricing is favorable, and the barriers to entry are high. But the report does not address the risk that multiple suppliers could announce capacity expansions at the same time, creating a glut that destroys pricing power.

The history of the chemical industry is littered with examples of companies that invested heavily during a boom and then suffered during the subsequent bust. The current cycle for FC-BGA CCLs is driven by AI and data center demand, which has proven resilient so far. But if the AI investment cycle slows, or if substrate technology shifts to a new material that renders current CCL formulations obsolete, the capacity additions that look prudent today could become millstones.

The report also does not fully explore the competitive dynamics in the Chinese market for advanced substrates. Chinese companies are investing heavily in substrate manufacturing, and they will eventually need materials suppliers. If Japanese companies like Resonac are too slow to partner with Chinese substrate makers, or if they are excluded due to geopolitical tensions, the demand growth that the report projects may not materialize. These are not flaws in the analysis. They are the natural boundaries of a report that focuses on the next twelve to eighteen months. Investors who want the full picture need to consider these risks alongside the report’s bullish thesis.

## A Decision Framework for Investors: Three Questions to Separate Structural Winners from Cyclical Plays

The report provides enough data to build a simple but powerful decision framework. Any investor evaluating Japanese electronic chemicals should ask three questions.

First, is the company’s revenue growth driven by volume or by pricing? Companies like Resonac, which are raising prices for CCLs, have a structural advantage. Companies that are growing primarily because their customers are buying more units are more exposed to a demand slowdown. The report’s preference for Resonac over Tokyo Ohka Kogyo is consistent with this framework.

Second, does the company have a moat in its specific material category? Sumitomo Bakelite’s dual position in granular and liquid encapsulants gives it a moat that is difficult to replicate. Tokyo Ohka Kogyo’s position in mature-node photoresists is more vulnerable to Chinese competition. The moat is not about the size of the company. It is about the switching costs for the customer and the technological complexity of the product.

Third, is the company investing in capacity that will create value or destroy it? The report suggests that Resonac’s capacity additions are likely to be value-creating because demand is outstripping supply. But investors need to monitor the capacity announcements of competitors. If multiple companies announce expansions, the pricing power that the report projects could evaporate. This is the most difficult question to answer with public data, and it is the one that most differentiates the best analysts from the rest.

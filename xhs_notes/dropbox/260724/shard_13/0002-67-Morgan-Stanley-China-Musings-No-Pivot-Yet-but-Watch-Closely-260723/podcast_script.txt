ZH_A: 最近有一份MS的中国研报，标题叫“还没转向，但需紧盯”。这个标题本身就很引人好奇，它到底在说什么？

ZH_B: 简单说，这份报告的核心判断是：别指望2026年会重演2024年9月那种政策大转向。现在的情况和两年前完全不同，北京暂时不需要，也不会拿出“火箭筒”式的刺激。

ZH_A: 等等，2024年9月的转向到底是什么？为什么当时那么关键？

ZH_B: 2024年9月政治局会议是个分水岭，标志着政策从风险控制全面转向稳增长。当时压力是叠加的：地方政府融资几乎冻结，社会情绪指标逼近临界点，通缩持续了三年，房价加速下跌，GDP增速也滑出了目标区间。所以那次回应是全面的，包括降准降息、债务置换、消费补贴等等。

ZH_A: 那现在呢？你说条件完全不同了，具体好在哪里？

ZH_B: 每个压力点都从底部回升了。地方政府债务置换在推进，税收收入占比企稳了。通缩最严重的阶段过去了，PPI已经转正。房价虽然整体还在下行，但一线城市和高端住宅出现企稳迹象。最重要的是，上半年GDP增长4.7%，仍在官方4.5%到5%的目标区间内。

ZH_A: 那北京为什么能“等得起”？经济不是还有内需偏弱的问题吗？

ZH_B: 关键在于出口。结构性的出口增长成了经济的一个“压舱石”。这里有两条线：一条是AI硬件出口，中国是全球AI基建的关键供应商，这种需求是持久的，不是昙花一现。另一条是亚洲正在展开的工业投资超级周期，中国作为区域制造中心，能双重受益。

ZH_A: 但出口强劲，好像没怎么传导到就业和消费上？

ZH_B: 对，这正是报告的提醒。出口增长是资本密集型的，不是劳动密集型的，所以对就业和消费的溢出效应很弱。而且，AI出口这个利好本身也是最大的风险——如果全球AI投资周期逆转，中国会首当其冲。

ZH_A: 那投资这块呢？房地产和基建都还在拖累吧？

ZH_B: 报告指出，北京想稳住投资，但能做的更多是“缓冲”而不是“逆转”。算力和电力网络投资是新的方向，但这些体量太小，电力投资不到固定资产投资的2%，没法对冲房地产、传统基建和制造业的持续疲弱。

ZH_A: 还有一个很有意思的点，报告专门分析了中国的原油进口。为什么现在进口量这么低？

ZH_B: 因为中国的增长结构变了。上半年建筑业增加值下降了4.5%，而GDP名义增长了5.4%。增长更偏向AI、出口和高端制造，这些不是耗油大户。中国此前积累了大量的商业库存，现在在消耗库存，而不是增加进口。所以进口低迷不是需求崩溃，而是一个主动的调整。

ZH_A: 那接下来呢？政策会怎么走？

ZH_B: 报告预期七月政治局会议会释放更谨慎的信号，强调加快现有政策落地。如果七八月的数据和落实效果还不行，九月才可能加码。但注意，报告留下了一个悬念：如果真要重启基建投资，钱从哪来？中央财政和政策性银行会扮演什么角色？这个在完整报告里还有更详细的讨论。

ZH_A: 看来这份报告的核心逻辑是：不转向，但紧盯窗口。要理解完整的政策推演，还是得去读原文。

ZH_B: 没错，我们只聊了最核心的判断。报告里还有关于债务置换节奏、社会动态指标的详细拆解，以及油价影响的情景分析。建议你去看看完整版，特别是那个悬念——基建融资到底怎么破局。

EN_A: So there's this new China research report from a major global investment bank with a title that really catches your attention: "No Pivot Yet — But Watch Closely." What's the big idea here?

EN_B: The core message is straightforward. Don't expect a policy pivot like the one we saw in September 2024. The conditions today are fundamentally different. Beijing doesn't need to launch a bazooka-style stimulus right now.

EN_A: Let's step back. What made the September 2024 pivot so special?

EN_B: That was a real turning point. The Politburo meeting shifted from risk control to full-on growth support. Multiple pressures had reached a breaking point. Local government financing was freezing up. Social sentiment indicators were approaching dangerous thresholds. Deflation had been going on for three years. Home prices were crashing. And GDP growth was slipping below the annual target. So the response was broad — rate cuts, mortgage relief, debt swaps, consumer subsidies, you name it.

EN_A: And today? You're saying all those pressures have eased?

EN_B: Each one has either bottomed out or improved. The debt swap program is on track. Tax revenue as a share of GDP has stabilized. Deflation is no longer entrenched — PPI has turned positive. Housing is still weak overall, but top-tier cities are showing signs of stabilization. Most importantly, first-half GDP grew 4.7 percent, which is still inside the official target range of 4.5 to 5 percent.

EN_A: So what's allowing Beijing to hold back? Domestic demand is still sluggish, isn't it?

EN_B: The key is exports. They've become a structural bedrock for growth. Two forces are at play. First, AI-related hardware exports are booming — China is a critical supplier in the global AI infrastructure buildout, and that demand looks durable, not temporary. Second, Asia is entering a capex super-cycle, and China, as the region's manufacturing hub, captures a double benefit.

EN_A: But strong exports don't seem to be translating into better jobs or consumption.

EN_B: Exactly, and the report flags that. Export growth is capital-intensive, not labor-intensive. So the spillover to employment and household income is weak. And here's the irony — the same AI export channel that's a tailwind today is also the biggest downside risk. If the global AI investment cycle turns, China will feel it first.

EN_A: What about investment? Property and infrastructure are still dragging.

EN_B: The report's conclusion is that Beijing can cushion the downturn, but it can't reverse it. The new priorities are computing and power grid investment. But these sectors are too small. Power grid capex is less than 2 percent of total fixed asset investment. They can't offset the persistent weakness in property, traditional infrastructure, and broad manufacturing.

EN_A: One section that really stood out to me was about China's crude oil imports. Why are they staying so low?

EN_B: It's because the growth mix has changed. In the first half of this year, construction value-added contracted by 4.5 percent while nominal GDP grew 5.4 percent. The economy is shifting toward AI, exports, and high-end manufacturing — sectors that aren't oil-intensive. Plus, China had built up huge commercial inventories before the Strait of Hormuz conflict. Now it's drawing them down instead of buying more. So the low imports are an adjustment, not a collapse in demand.

EN_A: So what's next? What should we expect from the policy side?

EN_B: The report expects the upcoming Politburo meeting to sound more cautious and push for faster implementation of existing measures. If July and August data disappoint, then September becomes a decision window for additional easing. But here's the cliffhanger the report leaves us with — if Beijing does decide to ramp up infrastructure investment, where will the money come from? The role of central government financing and policy banks is still an open question.

EN_A: So the big takeaway is: no pivot for now, but keep watching the window. To get the full picture, you need to read the complete report.

EN_B: Exactly. We've covered the core logic. But the full report goes deeper into the debt swap timeline, the social dynamics indicators, and the oil market scenarios. It's worth a read, especially to understand how that infrastructure financing puzzle might be solved.

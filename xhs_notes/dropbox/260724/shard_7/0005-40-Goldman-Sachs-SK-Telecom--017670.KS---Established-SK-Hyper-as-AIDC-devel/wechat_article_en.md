## The Subsidiary Structure Improves Project Governance but Does Not Eliminate Funding Risk

SK Hyper sits between SKT and individual project companies, each of which is expected to be established as a separate special-purpose vehicle. This structure is standard for large infrastructure projects and offers several governance advantages: it ring-fences liabilities, facilitates financial investor participation at the project level, and allows for distinct capital structures tailored to each site’s risk profile. The decision to seek FI capital at the SPV level rather than funding the entire build-out directly is prudent and consistent with industry practice for hyperscale data-center development.

However, the absence of disclosed investors, financing terms, or equity-sharing arrangements means the structure remains theoretical. The 750 billion won commitment covers the development platform, but the construction cost for even the initial 2-gigawatt pipeline could run into multiple trillions of won, depending on power infrastructure requirements, cooling technology, and real estate costs. Without confirmed FI participation, SKT bears the risk that it will need to fund a larger share of construction than currently planned, potentially straining free cash flow and constraining capital-allocation flexibility, including shareholder returns.

The critical metric to watch is not the size of the development commitment but the ratio of SKT equity to total project cost at each SPV. If SKT’s ultimate equity contribution exceeds the 750 billion won ceiling, the market will need to reassess the earnings drag from development spending without corresponding revenue, which could persist for several years given the 2029 timeline for first-phase openings.

## Capital Is Being Deployed Before Tenant Commitments, Creating a Sequence Risk That Demands a Premium on Execution

The initial 330 billion won will primarily fund land acquisition, power-grid access, permitting, and substation infrastructure for the first roughly 2-gigawatt pipeline. These are necessary investments to improve project readiness, but they are being made before final tenant commitments are secured. In the current AI infrastructure cycle, hyperscalers and large enterprise tenants are evaluating multiple potential sites across regions, and competition for anchor tenants is intense. SKT is effectively placing a bet that the development spend will attract tenants, rather than responding to confirmed demand.

This sequence risk is not unusual in greenfield infrastructure development, but it carries greater weight here because of the scale involved. A 5-gigawatt pipeline represents a significant fraction of South Korea’s total data-center capacity, and the market may not absorb that supply as quickly as SKT’s timeline assumes. Extended development periods, lower-than-expected utilization, or the need for additional funding to bridge the gap between development spend and tenant revenue are all plausible outcomes that would affect project performance.

Stakeholders should compare this approach to that of established global data-center developers, which typically secure anchor leases or pre-leasing commitments before committing significant development capital. SKT’s decision to proceed without such commitments does not make the strategy invalid, but it elevates the importance of execution quality and tenant acquisition speed. Any delay in securing anchor tenants will compound the cash-flow pressure from development spending and delay the point at which the AIDC business contributes meaningfully to earnings.

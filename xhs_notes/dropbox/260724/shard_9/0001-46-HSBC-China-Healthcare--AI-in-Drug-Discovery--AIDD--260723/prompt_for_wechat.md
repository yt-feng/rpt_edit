## A transformative sector, but still no consensus

Using AI to discover new drugs is rapidly becoming one of China's largest and most misunderstood investment themes. Billions of dollars are flowing into the sector, partnerships with global pharma names are accelerating, and valuations are increasingly pricing in the potential of a company's platform, rather than current earnings. Yet despite the excitement, investors still face one critical problem: there is no consensus on what these AI drug discovery (AIDD) companies are – software, biotech, contract research organisations (CROs) that conduct research and development for drug companies, or future platform monopolies?

## Key investor debates: Our core findings

1. Where is AIDD monetising today? Which business models are durable vs at risk? Current monetisation is real but still concentrated in lower-value layers like software as a service (SaaS) offerings, drug discovery fees, and CRO services. We see long-term value shifting downstream towards asset ownership, proprietary data, and improving platform economics. Pure SaaS providers face rising commoditisation risk.

2. What moves AIDD stocks – and why does leadership change so quickly? AIDD trades on three drivers: narratives, event catalysts, and fundamentals. In bull markets, the AI narrative typically dominates. In risk-off periods, cash runways and monetisation matter a lot more. Timing when these factors rotate and become more important is critical.

3. What is the correct valuation framework? We believe traditional DCF frameworks are insufficient as AIDD companies increasingly trade as “stacks of options” – combining platform capability, talent scarcity, proprietary data, and future ecosystem value. Markets are pricing their future potential trajectory, not their current cash flow.

4. What becomes the long-term moat? We expect most AI advantages to become commoditised. Instead, durable moats will emerge from proprietary data generation, workflow integration, regulatory positioning, and wet-lab infrastructure – not simply better algorithms.

5. What lessons can investors learn from the internet and CRO industries? Like the early internet and CRO leaders, AIDD winners may evolve from fragmented tools into integrated platforms. Historical winners captured ecosystems, embedded workflows, and long-term switching costs – not feature-level innovation alone.

6. What becomes commoditised vs defensible in AI drug discovery? Generalised modeling, protein folding, and molecular design capabilities are rapidly being standardised. Defensible value increasingly sits in proprietary biological data, patient access, experimental feedback loops, and scalable asset-generation infrastructure.

# Five key investor debates

## 1. Where is AIDD monetising today? Which business models are durable vs at risk?

## A. Cash flow visible, but not yet at scale or predictable

AIDD is generating revenue today, but still not at levels where investors would like it. Despite major progress having been made, most of current monetisation sits in the low- to mid-value layers: SaaS tools, discovery collaborations, and fee-for-service modeling. These produce visible but unstable revenue, and are often tied to early-stage research.

Investor pain point remains highly speculative as the industry is still in its early development phase: is this software (high-margin, scalable) or biotech (high-risk, capital-heavy), or CRO (platform services)? There's no clear consensus from the street in terms of which valuation to use or what business model to classify AIDDs as given these companies are also still navigating themselves the fast-changing world of AI. Until companies prove repeatable SaaS scale or clinical asset realisation, we believe multiples could remain unstable.

Future pivot: From selling picks to owning gold – services & SaaS are entry points, not endpoints. The revenue durability question hinges on what gets commoditised vs what compounds. General AI capabilities like modeling, protein folding, and molecular design are rapidly being standardised. As Google DeepMind-type foundational players push performance boundaries, pure-play SaaS AIDD is becoming price-taker infrastructure, not a value owner.

However, we identify where durability is emerging to differentiate the potential “winners” in the vertical applications of AI in healthcare:

\- Closed-loop platforms and infrastructure (AI + wet lab + proprietary data): Harder to replicate, improving with scale.

Exclusive data moats/patient-level data access: Structural moat in a regulated environment (e.g. US FDA constraints).

\- Downstream economics participation/pipeline ownership: Milestones, royalties, equity in assets.

## B. Market trend – booming deal activities: Commanding valuation reflects future platform potential instead of current revenue

![](images/e12ffb508a0036cbafecbb71c68837f0efba9fb03acbf7be5ddc0ab33fcb17fb.jpg)  
Source: Pharmcube, Company reports, HSBC Qianhai Securities

2026 y-t-d deal flow shows a clear pattern: Capital is chasing capability, not cash flow. Most AIDD platforms remain pre-profit, yet command premium valuations. Valuations are based on future platform output, not current revenue.

Overall, we find the following to be highly valued in primary markets:

People: Founders, ex-pharma leaders, top AI researchers.

Data advantage: Exclusive partnerships, proprietary clinical, and longitudinal datasets.

Platform throughput: How many drug candidates can be generated per year.

Early validation: Investigational new drug (IND) filings, pharma partnerships.

Though there is no standard metric, investors triangulate on implied value per pipeline asset, platform replacement cost (data + compute + talent), and strategic premium from pharma optionality.

The key market takeaway? The premium valuations are a function of future pipeline optionality, as in the platform's ability to generate future assets, not present earnings, which means investors risk paying high multiples without proof the platforms will perform well. However, investors are speeding up their entry into this space to gain the “optionality” of potentially significant upside, given biology AI is much harder than scaling general large language models (LLMs) and has much higher barriers to entry.

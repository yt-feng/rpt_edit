EN_A: Today we are looking at a Japanese industrial automation giant, Yaskawa Electric. It recently ran into serious trouble after switching its ERP system, causing production to plummet. Yet a global investment bank just gave it a price target implying almost 70 percent upside. What is going on here?
EN_B: It is a fascinating case. Yaskawa switched its ERP system during the Golden Week holiday in May. The transition itself went smoothly, but then problems emerged in execution. The key insight from the report is that this is seen as a timing issue, not a permanent loss.
EN_A: So the core question we need to answer is this: why is a self-inflicted wound being treated as a buying opportunity?
EN_B: Exactly. The demand picture is extremely strong, especially from AI-related end markets like semiconductors and data centers. If Yaskawa can normalize production quickly, the suppressed orders can be recognized in a concentrated way from the second quarter onward.
EN_A: How bad was the production hit in May?
EN_B: For AC servos, utilization dropped from 91 percent in March to just 51 percent in May. For robots, it fell from 68 to 34 percent. But the good news is that June already showed recovery, and early July data points to continued improvement.
EN_A: So what is the recovery timeline?
EN_B: The company expects AC servos to return to 100 percent single-shift utilization by August. Robots will take a bit longer, until around September. The bottleneck is wafer-handling robots for semiconductors, which involve many manual processes and are still running at only about 60 percent.
EN_A: That means the robot recovery is slower, but the trend is still positive.
EN_B: Right. And crucially, orders are not being canceled. Because AC servos and robots are highly customized, customers cannot easily switch suppliers. For inverters, which are more standardized, the recovery was the fastest, so the risk never materialized.
EN_A: What about the financial damage from the ERP issue?
EN_B: First-quarter operating profit came in at 8.5 billion yen, well below the company's internal target of 12 billion. But management believes a meaningful portion of that shortfall is just a timing difference in revenue recognition. It still targets 16 billion yen per quarter from the second quarter onward.
EN_A: That sounds ambitious. What gives them that confidence?
EN_B: The order momentum is incredibly strong. AC servo orders for semiconductor equipment rose 16 percent quarter-on-quarter and more than tripled year-on-year. The company even said more than half of the incremental demand may be AI-related.
EN_A: So the problem is not a lack of orders, but a lack of capacity to fulfill them?
EN_B: Exactly. Yaskawa plans to increase production capacity for AC servos and wafer-handling robots to 1.3 to 1.4 times current levels from the second half. Inverters have already moved to a 1.5-shift system. If production normalizes, the profits should follow.
EN_A: What are the risks that could break this thesis?
EN_B: The report highlights a few. A slowdown in semiconductor and AI capex, cost optimization that falls short, or a disappointing medium-term plan. But there is one more subtle risk that the report does not fully address.
EN_A: What is that?
EN_B: The long-term operational impact of the ERP change. The company says the issues are temporary, but work processes and data entry methods have been fundamentally altered. How much friction will that create in the quarters ahead? That remains to be seen. The full report goes deeper into this.
EN_A: So the key to Yaskawa's recovery story is really about execution over the next few months.
EN_B: Exactly. Watch the robot business normalization by September and the capacity expansion plans. If you want to see the full risk analysis and valuation model, you should read the complete report.

# Leadership Transitions

As markets move further towards pricing relief in spot commodity markets, we continue to think longer-dated oil prices will be key for more durable disinflation to anchor yields. With the US cyclically outperforming, the signaling at next week's FOMC will determine near-term pricing, with the dots and framing of risks around an on-hold baseline key to curve dynamics. We see multiple routes to a steeper US curve and think that longer term real rates have value on a beta versus nominals. In contrast, European macroeconomic data look less strong. Although 1y1y rates have become less sensitive to spot oil prices, the combination of a hawkish central bank, weak data and a shift in the distribution of energy prices skews risks to the downside for EUR 1y1y and 2y2y rates, and we continue to recommend long 5y5y real rates. We see more compelling prospects for front-end relief in UK rates through next week's BoE; pricing remains highly sensitive to oil prices and reflects too much hike premium versus the fundamentals. While data volatility may make for a gradual process, we think CAD front-end hike pricing can moderate further. Conversely, while a hold is likely next week, the RBA's more proactive tightening approach argues for outperformance further out the curve. In Japan, the prospects for relief are limited given that the economy remains shielded from the inflationary effect of higher energy prices. Instead, a durable US-Iran deal should attenuate both growth and fiscal risks and can create a clearer path for underperformance to migrate in on the curve.

## United States and Canada

Navigating shifting risks and a new Chair. Peak rate pricing in the US front-end has moderated and the curve has steepened from the recent lows as the market has returned to optimism about the US-Iran conflict amid the steady decline in oil prices. Even with those shifts, US rates pricing remains squarely towards hikes over the next year heading into the first meeting of Chair Warsh's term, with this week's inflation data a mixed bag that resulted in a firmer-than-expected core PCE translation. The 30-35bp of hike risk through mid-27 implies that a significant probability of multiple hikes is already priced. Given we continue to think a lengthy hold is more likely than hikes, and that the medium-term cutting tail should not be written off, we see risks firmly skewed towards lower 1y1y yields over time, with reds/greens positioned to benefit most from a US-Iran deal. Room for relative real rate relief. US rate relief has been primarily inflation-led, with longer term real yields only modestly below the post-February highs. While energy has been the common factor driving broader rates pricing and the May core CPI print was below consensus, the composition of this week's inflation data was firmer. Our economists' medium-term outlook for core inflation remains benign, but so long as a cyclical optimism remains in place, we think there should be some floor on inflation forwards which are back to pre-conflict levels. Conversely, additional optimism on disinflation should start to pull real yields lower, and we think real yields can fall meaningfully on renewed concerns about the labor market and/or activity picture. The power of communication. Another point of interest beyond the immediate discussion of risks around the policy path will be how Chair Warsh communicates. In the past, he has argued against the Fed providing too much guidance to the market, noting the risk that by providing forecasts Fed officials might risk “becoming prisoner of their own words.” While we have found that the overall evolution of Fed communication has helped reduce rate volatility and improved the accuracy of markets’ pricing of policy outcomes, the results are somewhat nuanced. Our work suggests that early shifts away from deliberate vagueness towards greater transparency in the mid-2000s have had a more significant effect on volatility than the later additions of the dot plot and post meeting press conference, whose effects are more ambiguous. Focus is also likely going to be on how Chair Warsh intends to conduct the press conferences. Under Chair Powell, press conferences became a more important—and more vol-inducing—instrument of policy transmission than under his predecessors. We overall see some initial upside risks to rates volatility around FOMC meetings as a new communication standard is set and markets adjust.

On-hold BoC should see hike premium decay, but no catalyst. The BoC remained on hold this week, characterizing the risks to the outlook as two-sided but maintaining a baseline of looking through the energy shock. The tradeoff between the hawkish implications of any broadening of inflation risk (which so far remains limited) and the potential impact of further trade tensions with the US (which were characterized as unambiguously dovish in the statement and press conference) remains the main source of tension, with the contributions from growth and policy over the past month roughly balanced (Exhibit 2). We think as time goes by without either of those risks dominating, current hike premium can continue to fade, but it is harder to identify a discrete near-term catalyst, with risks of a bounce back in growth data following the government spending induced pullback in Q1 GDP or renewed geopolitical pressure exerting hawkish pressures.

## Europe

Inflation persistence driving the EUR front-end. The ECB became the first major central bank to hike in the aftermath of the war in Iran, motivated by a persistent inflation overshoot in its latest projections. President Lagarde refrained from providing explicit guidance on the upcoming July decision, but relief headlines on a potential US-Iran deal have taken July pricing towards 6bp as of time of writing. This looks low to us, and is likely only to fall further if a conclusive deal is able to restart commodity flows through the Strait of Hormuz – as our oil analysts have argued, while demand reduction and supply diversion have helped keep oil prices rangebound, relief for longer-dated oil prices is likely to be limited. This matters because the hawkish aspects of the ECB’s inflation projections come from the persistence of inflation, rather than the magnitude of the near-term oil spike. This is also evident in the market – while front-end rates have repriced sharply on the rise in oil prices, we have seen little yield decline despite the fall in front-contract oil. The better guide in recent weeks has been z6 oil, which has remained flat and as a result reflects more persistent inflationary risk. This suggests to us there is more room to price sharper relief in the UK front-end (given the gap to our policy rate views), but also that the ECB’s front-footed approach to policy tightening vs G4 peers should see the EUR curve — beyond 1y rates — outperform on selloffs. We maintain our long in 5y5y real OIS on the potential for both nominal rate relief and inflation curve steepening on any concrete prospects for lower energy prices.

UK front-end remains an inflation relief trade. With no change in rates expected at next week's BoE meeting, focus will be on the Governor's message. So far, Governor Bailey has emphasised that the shift away from cuts already constitutes a tightening, which has served to neutralise some hawkish messaging from other MPC members. With hike premium elevated, we think that UK front-end longs represent reasonable risk reward given the UK activity picture remains soft and the distribution for energy prices has shifted lower from both oil market rebalancing and the prospects for a US-Iran deal. Exhibit 3 shows that even in volatility-adjusted terms, UK rates have exhibited the highest sensitivity to oil prices vs other curves. At the same time, ongoing debates around defence spending are a reminder of the government's limited fiscal room, which we think is unlikely to resolve without some macro relief on inflation and rates. As a result, we continue to expect Gilt risk premium to remain sticky and for UK yields to move lower as a function of inflation relief — stay in GBP forward 2s10s steepeners.

Deregulatory tailwinds to Gilt spreads. Our banks team have flagged the potential for the BoE to adjust the implementation of the leverage ratio for UK banks at its next financial stability report, released 7 July, with up to £250bn of balance sheet capacity freed as a result. As we have shown, bank ownership of Gilts has been increasing in recent years (see here for more detail, Exhibit 4), and an increase in balance sheet capacity could add further support for Gilts. The direct support to bank Gilt purchases is likely to be relatively modest — if Gilts remain constant as a share of bank assets, this would suggest a modest £4bn of additional Gilt purchases, skewed towards the 0-5y sector. However, as we have argued for the US, by improving intermediation capacity at the systemwide level, increased balance sheet capacity could anchor funding spreads and give other institutional investors confidence in market liquidity. Although there are no signs of obvious strains in UK funding markets, borrowing at the BoE continues to trend up, especially through the Short-Term Repo facility (STR), and the Gilt market is increasingly reliant on

leveraged buyers. This suggests that these upcoming changes should offer a tailwind for Gilts on asset swaps, reaffirming that the most pressing risk for Gilts is not supply, but macro risks from growth, inflation and monetary policy.

NOK vs CHF curve divergence justified. Next week, our economists expect both Norges Bank and the SNB to leave rates unchanged, but divergence in policy outlook is driving a steeper CHF vs NOK curve (Exhibit 5). In Norway, after restarting its hiking cycle at the last meeting, a hold next week likely represents a pause rather than the end of the cycle: wage growth remains close to $5\%$ , domestic price pressures remain firm, and our economists continue to see one more hike in September to $4.5\%$ . In contrast, the SNB looks comfortably on hold: inflation has picked up only modestly due to higher energy prices, and labour market conditions are soft. In volatility-adjusted terms, the CHF curve remains sensitive to oil prices, but via the term premium; the limited inflation impact should leave CHF front-end less reactive than peers to any oil reversal in basis point terms (Exhibit 3). If energy stress fades or the Strait of Hormuz re-opens, CHF forwards should lag the rally in higher-beta front-ends, while on a renewed oil shock they should outperform markets where front-end repricing is more tightly linked to energy-sensitive policy expectations. This points to further steepening in CHF vs NOK rates.

## Japan

BoJ Hike Unlikely to Stem JGB Risk Premium. Next week's BoJ meeting is unlikely to turn the tide on JGB risk premia. Our economists expect a hike, in line with consensus and market pricing, and for the BoJ to maintain its roughly semi-annual pace of hikes thereafter. Governor Ueda has signaled the need to secure market confidence that the BoJ will properly control inflation, and a hike is a step in that direction. However, markets continue to imply that a gradual approach to a $1.5\%$ terminal rate may be insufficient towards achieving that goal, while the perception of political constraints on the potential pace of tightening further complicates the market's confidence in the Bank's ability to tame inflation risks. As a result, belly yields have continued to move higher in tandem with traded inflation (Exhibit 6), and will likely continue to do so unless the data turns. Continued JGB underperformance is likely, especially in the belly, without a more convincing hawkish signal, such as faster hikes, from the BoJ. Next week's meeting will also include an update on QT, where our economists expect the BoJ to continue with its current JGB purchase plan through March 2027 and keep purchases steady at ¥2tn from April 2027 onward. The progress of QT has so far had little adverse impact on funding costs. Meanwhile, although long-end JGBs have tended to underperform swaps in sharp selloffs, spreads have retraced more quickly than rate levels, suggesting appetite to buy on signs of consolidation.

## Australia and New Zealand

The hold before the final RBA hike. Following three hikes so far this year, combined with the oil shock, activity data has begun turning in Australia, with a surprise rise in the unemployment rate and weaker-than-expected activity data, as measured by our MAP indices. This has stabilized the forwards, with the curve pricing a hump-shaped front-end that implies about 15bp of hikes to the year-end peak. Our economists expect the RBA to hold next week but still expect one final hike in August to dampen the broadening-out risks from the supply shock, to a terminal rate of $4.6\%$ . In our view, that leaves some room for further front-end cheapening, and given the expected subsequent policy normalization, we also expect greater 1s3s inversion as the policy tightening begins to feed through the economy.

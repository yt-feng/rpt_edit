## Korea Renaissance
# Is the Household Asset Move to Equities Sustainable? Possibly

Korean household balance sheets are undergoing a major shift. Equity holdings are surging, as a long-standing bias towards cash fades. With property accumulation facing policy headwinds and the stock market set for positive catalysts, we see retail investors' participation in equities continuing.

## Key Takeaways

Household asset composition in Korea is undergoing a meaningful structural shift into risk assets, with equity holdings extending their sharp rise since 2025.
Housing unaffordability and tight credit policy contrasted by stock-market reform measures are behind the transition to an alternative wealth-building vehicle.
Both positive and negative economic implications are at play: wealth effect to help consumption, while high exposure to volatility risks the reverse effect.
The tendencies of Korean retail investors toward equity investing appear to be evolving in a positive way, but leverage needs to be managed.
We expect retail investor engagement to be sustained, but investors' expanded scope could be a double-edged sword. We raise our KOSPI target to 9,000.

## Executive Summary

## Korean household assets have grown 91% since 2000 – but equities have not been prominent in the mix

Korea has shown the fastest expansion of household assets among major economies over the past quarter-century. However, the distinctive feature of Korea's wealth accumulation has been that the rise in real assets contributed the most to this surge, in contrast to the growth seen in DMs, which has been led by financial assets. With a heavy bias toward real estate and nearly half of financial assets held in cash and deposits, we see significant room for a rotation toward equity holdings this time.

## Korean households have recently shown a major shift in asset allocation — the proportion of equities has risen with a stronger bourse

After decades of accumulating wealth primarily through real estate, with a persistent bias toward cash and deposits, household equity holdings surged 48%Y in 2025, against a prior decade-long average increase of just 7.4%. This marks a striking inflection in Korean households' appetite for risk assets. We view this as directionally positive for long-term financial asset management, coupled with potential near-term risk factors.

## The tendencies of Korean retail investors toward equity investing appear to be evolving in a positive way

In our deep analysis published in July 2020 (link), we found retail investors to be seekers of volatility and chasers of themes with rather short-term investment horizons. However, we believe that overall retail investors have become smarter thanks to:

1. Improved access to higher quality information.
2. AI providing a more level playing field through improved information access and analysis.
3. Accumulated experience investing overseas.

## Critically, the equity wealth effect is likely more amplified and now more structurally embedded than in prior cycles

We believe the positive spillover from equity wealth gains to the real economy is likely to be both wider in breadth and more intense in magnitude compared to the pre-2020 period, given:

- the number of retail investors has increased to 14.5mn, 50% of total adult population, vs. 6.2mn, 21% of adult population, in 2019;
- a notable jump in the size of equity balance at W662tn in 2025, up $58\%$ Y from W419tn in 2024; this is a 13.8x increase from W17.6tn in 2019; and
- younger investor groups in their 20s and 30s have entered the investor base, who likely to have a high level of propensity to consume.

## We identify additional positive factors that are needed to sustain retail investor engagement over the medium/long term

- Enhanced confidence in local markets amid structural stories leading to higher sustainable ROE levels for the KOSPI.
- Extended conditions of copious market liquidity.
- Continued capital markets reform improving corporate governance and investor payouts.
- Tax incentives including dividend tax reform and upgrades to the ISA program beginning June 2026.

Simultaneously, the government's real estate policy – which actively disincentivizes traditional real estate acquisition – is expected to persist for the next 3-4 years, providing a structural tailwind for the rotation into equities. Newfound FOMO (fear of missing out) for stocks instead of only real estate should lead to sustained participation by retail investors in the equity market.

## We're more bullish on the KOSPI

Raising our 12-month target to 9,000 from 8,500: Retail stickiness should provide support and also continue to act as ballast, assuming that the intertwining of super-cycles with reform remains intact.

Our bull case is raised to 10,500 from 10,000: In this scenario, AI diffusion charges forward and geopolitical risks normalize.

Our bear case is raised to 6,500 from 6,000: The key risk is extended geopolitical uncertainties with oil supply and prices becoming a drag.

## How to be positioned on the money move and wealth effect story?

- We believe retail investors should stick to the winners in Tech and Industrials...
- ...but we see room for rising interest in Healthcare, which has tended to be an area of retail interest, yet stock prices have not moved despite improving fundamentals.
- We also see department stores having more legs as a key beneficiary of the positive wealth effect.
- We continue to like the securities business...
- ...and also banks leveraged to both securities and credit card businesses – prefer SFG and KFG.

## Near-term risks exist around a new and acute balance sheet vulnerability to equity markets

Korean households are entering a period of elevated risk asset exposure. We flag:

1. Record-high equity holdings that could exceed 80% of GDP by 2027;
2. A pre-existing household debt burden that leaves little room to absorb shocks; and
3. A new BoK rate hiking cycle, which we currently expect to begin in July 2026 and run through 2027, implying a sustained period of tighter financial conditions.

Market volatility of a meaningful magnitude could trigger a consumption slowdown and amplified debt service stress across the household sector.

## We see Korea's transition as faster and larger in scale than Japan's retail wealth shift into equities

Both Japan and Korea are experiencing a structural, policy-driven shift in household asset allocation – away from cash and deposits (in Japan's case) and real estate and cash (in Korea's case) – toward equity investments. We observe a more proactive and higher-risk appetite among Korean investors vs. a gradual, albeit strong, increase in Japan. This is reflected in the rapid expansion in financial assets in the past decade in Korea – up 94% vs. +30% in Japan.

# Korean Households' Asset Reallocation Has Been an Ongoing Trend

## Household asset growth has been fastest in Korea vs. DMs

Korean households have recorded the fastest growth in wealth vs. major advanced economies: In the past decade, the Korean household sector's wealth took a major jump up. In nominal terms, it nearly doubled to W15,455tn in 2024 (approximately US\$11tn) from W8,880tn in 2015. The sheer size has increased 80% during this time, with a CAGR of 6.3%, according to the BoK national balance sheet.

Compared to other economies, Korea's wealth accumulation stands out. Since 2000, households in the major economies have shown modest growth in their assets. In local currency terms, Korea's assets surged 91% – vs. 40\$ for the US, 28% for Germany, and 12% for Japan. In USD terms too, Korea's household asset growth jumped the fastest: 83% vs. <40% seen in rest of the major economies. The Korean equity market outperformed globally in 2025, with an outsized return of 75.63% for the KOSPI. Thus, we expect Korean households' total asset growth to accelerate this year vs. other markets.

## What drove this surge in household wealth, however, differs from other markets'

drivers: In terms of composition, Koreans historically have shown a bias to real assets vs. other types, with larger ownership in the housing market. For decades, households were heavily biased towards land, houses, or buildings. Over this period, the increase in real assets such as land and properties contributed 66% while financial assets (including cash, deposits, and financial products) contributed 34%. In the case of the US, roughly the opposite is true – 70% of household assets sit in financial assets.

In 2025, Koreans held a majority – over 75% – of their wealth in real assets. According to the BoK survey on household financial conditions, their average assets reached W566.7mn (approximately US\$400k) with financial assets composing 24.2% vs. real assets 75.8%. This leaves their equity holdings at just 9% of total assets. Not only that, within financial assets, cash and deposits consistently composed the largest share: 46%, vs. investments (25%) and pension and insurance (30%). This captures the conventional asset composition with a heavy bias toward assets perceived as "safer" over risk assets.

## Heavy bias toward real assets coming from lack of retirement safety net

One important aspect of the Korean household balance sheet is the pronounced bias toward real assets relative to an low share in retirement savings: While the share of real assets has remained consistently above 70% of total household assets, it is not merely primary residences driving this concentration – the share of additional (non-primary) residential property ownership in Korea has also exceeded trends observed in peer economies. Meanwhile, Korea's holdings of pension and insurance remain comparatively low. Historically, insurance and pension assets have stood at around 9-12% of total household assets (including both financial and non-financial assets), a stark contrast to 24.7% in Japan, 34% in the US, and 29.6% in Germany.

The US presents a particularly instructive contrast: While equities dominate the broader US household asset mix, retirement assets also constitute a substantial share of the balance sheet, with total retirement assets reaching US\$49.1tn, or roughly 34% of all financial assets as of end-2025. This dual concentration in both equities and retirement vehicles reflects a far more diversified financial asset mix within US households, and points to a considerably more solid structural foundation for post-retirement income security. In Korea, by contrast, the dominance of real assets, at 75.8% of total household assets crowds out the accumulation of diversified financial assets, leaving households with limited funded retirement buffers.

What this pattern reveals is that Korean households have concentrated their wealth accumulation not merely around the purchase of a primary residence, but more deliberately around real assets as investable channels – vehicles through which to generate returns or sustain cash flow, particularly in anticipation of retirement. The relatively early statutory retirement age of 60 in Korea compared to 67 and the US, Germany, Australia and 65 in Japan combined with a structurally less developed post-retirement income system have pushed many households to look toward monthly rental income (monthly rent or jeonse yield) as a primary source of living, which in turn has further reinforced the structural concentration in real assets.

## Real assets a de facto retirement savings instrument

In this sense, real estate in Korea has functioned as a de facto retirement savings instrument. It supplemented the income cliff and substituted for a funded pension system. As a result weak institutional retirement infrastructure ended up encouraging real asset accumulation, which in turn reduced the incentive to build diversified financial portfolios, leaving households with a balance sheet that is concentrated in real assets. This has significantly exposed household sector activities, including consumption, in the real economy to housing price cycles, while being illiquid in nature. In the meantime, rising property prices have widened the asset gap between income groups in the household sector resulting in a wider income inequality gap. For the wealthiest Koreans (top 1%), real estate comprised an even larger share (82.9%) of their wealth in 2025.

## Wealthier households are shifting faster from their concentrated holdings of real assets

One particular feature – this pronounced tilt toward real assets was not merely a broad structural feature of Korean household balance sheets...

...it was systematically more acute among higher-income cohorts: Disaggregating the Ministry of Data and Statistic's 2025 Survey of Household Finances and Living Conditions by income quintile reveals a striking feature in real asset concentration. The top 20% income group held 76.7% of their total assets in real assets, compared to just 26.6% for the bottom 20%, while middle-income households (the third quintile) held approximately 62% in real assets vs. 38% in financial assets.

Equally telling is the composition within real asset holdings across income groups: As household income rises, higher income groups clearly demonstrated a faster rise in the share of real estate held for investment purposes, rather than as a primary residence, relative to lower-income groups. Among the top 20%, investment real estate accounted for 37.3% of total assets, a share that narrows to roughly 20% for the top 40% and falls below 15% for mid-to lower-income households. This pattern is reinforced by data on the top 1% of Korean households, while primary residences accounted for only 25% as opposed to the pattern observed among lower-income groups, where the family home remains the dominant real asset.

Yet this long-entrenched bias toward real assets among wealthier households has shown signs of rotation in recent years: The pace of that rotation toward equity investment has been fastest at the top of the income distribution. Critically, the gains from the surge of equities in total financial assets in 2025 have been highly concentrated among wealthier households. The top income quintile derived 15.2% of total household income from returns on assets in 2025, compared to an all-household average of 8.3%. The income gap from asset returns widened considerably in 2025, with the share of return on assets in total household income climbing to 8.3%, up from below 6% previously, while labor and business income shares declined. This concentration is further underscored by the fact that the top 20% of households hold approximately 87% of all equities.

## Surge in financial asset accumulation

## The overall trend hints that there could be room for a shift towards equity assets:

Since hitting the peak in 2024, the share of real assets has turned downward, to 75% from 78% in 2022. Meanwhile, the share of financial assets has climbed from the bottom of 22% to over 24% in 2024. During this period, households across income groups have seen a gain of around 12% in financial assets; the top 20% income quintile showed the most visible rise at 20%.

## In the past decade, the marginal growth in total assets came mostly from real assets:

Except for the year 2023, when the housing market faced a downturn, gains in household assets came mainly from the acquisition of land and properties. Since 2015 through 2025, the marginal increase in financial assets came to 28% of total gain in household assets. This long-term pattern in favoring real assets over financial assets indicates the pattern of not just purchasing a main residence but using properties as investment assets, acquiring second and other residences over financial products.

Hence, a rotation out of real assets into financial assets may not be as likely vs. rotation from safe financial assets in cash, deposits, and insurance and pension products.

## What came as a surprise is the trend change in financial assets over the past year:

During 2024-25, Korean households showed a significant jump in financial assets, with risk assets accounting for a dominant addition to total assets. The marginal annual increase of equities assets came to W535tn, to a total of W1,644tn. This far exceeded the combined increase in equity assets of W474tn during the previous decade (2015-24). It also marked the first year ever that the contribution from equity assets exceeded the rise in cash holdings within financial assets.

This led the share of equity holdings to reach the largest share within financial assets. It jumped to 26% in 2025 from 20% in 2024. At the same time, holdings of cash came down to 43.2% from 46.6%; pension and insurance holdings also came down to 26% from 28% during the same time. This is the largest incremental drop in the share of these two safe assets in a decade as opposed to the big 6ppt jump in equity assets.

## Ample room for asset reallocation into equities

Korean households' liquidity moving to equity investments: Retail products – including cash management accounts, money market funds, and money market deposit accounts – have shown an increase in growth, especially from 2022. They are at an historically high level, which could signify an initial stage of a shift in household liquidity to financial markets. Moreover, there has been a significant rise in customers' deposits and other short-term stand-by products which can quickly turn into liquidity for financial markets.

There is increasing evidence of Korean household liquidity moving toward equity-linked and short-term financial market instruments. Retail products including money market funds (MMF), money market deposit accounts (MMDA), and other short-term liquid vehicles have shown notable growth, particularly from 2022 onward.

The BoK's flow of funds data showed that MMDA balances have exhibited significant monthly swings. Monthly MMF trends show that MMF average balances reached historically elevated levels through 2025-26, with YoY changes exceeding 50-70%. In 1Q26, net assets of money market funds increased W41.2tn to W186.3tn. recording the largest quarterly increase since 2009.

Customer deposits and other short-term standby products have also shown a significant rise. Core deposits grew substantially through 2024, with transferable deposits showing large positive swings in multiple months.

This accumulation of liquid, near-market assets can be interpreted as an initial stage of household liquidity positioning for eventual deployment into financial markets.

# Can Strong Retail Participation in Equities Be Sustained?

# Leaning to Yes

## Some similarities to 2020\~2021 cycle; fundamentals were weaker and retail investors less mature during that period

There have been several cases of KOSPI bull markets driven by retail investors, but the most relevant should be the 2020\~2021 rally, which was driven by the following factors:

## (1) Tech upcycle

The Tech sector, led by the chipmakers, rose strongly during this period thanks to a combination of a global semiconductor up-cycle, pandemic-driven demand for electronics, and improving memory chip fundamentals. There were expectations of longer-term demand growth fueled by the rise in hyperscale cloud providers, adoption of 5G smartphones, AI-related computing demand, and rising memory content per device. Memory prices recovered significantly from the downturn seen in 2019, boosting earnings expectations.

## (2) Copious liquidity

Liquid money supply growth had been in the mid-teens in preceding months, in response to global and domestic monetary easing. Historically, the money supply growth cycle from the start point to the end point usually lasts around 1-1.5 years and during this period retail participation has moved quite similarly. This implies that, for around 6-12 months, liquidity conditions should have supported retail participation.

## (3) Real estate market

Housing market showed strong price and transaction trends during 1Q20, but the transaction trend had weakened in preceding months with continued policy tightening taking place. Expectation of rate hikes together with a tightening property market policy led volume to decline in 2021.

## (4) Financial products

There were fewer alternative financial asset options owing to the spate of problems related to debt-linked funds (DLF), debt-linked securities (DLS), equity-linked securities (ELS) and privately placed funds. As complex investment products went out of favor, retail investors leaned more to vanilla investments, such as equities. We even saw affluent retail investors become more interested in stocks, mainly large-cap stocks.

## (5) Safety nets

Safety nets, including the short-selling ban, and the stock market stabilization fund and National Pension, provided comfort to retail investors, allowing them to be more aggressive investing in equities.

## Have retail investors changed? Yes, they are evolving but so are related risks

## What we saw in the 2020\~2021 cycle and in previous ones

In July 2020, we carried out a deep analysis of Korean retail investors and came to the conclusion that retail investors should be able to drive up the market – but more importantly, that retail investors' tendencies imply higher market volatility.

\- South Korea Strategy: Raising KOSPI Target to 2,300 on Retail Participation (12 Jul 2020)

Our key findings in this analysis were that retail investors tended to be seekers of volatility and chasers of themes.

## (1) We found that retail investors tend to be volatility seekers with a short-term trading tendency – that's still the case but overall holding periods appear longer:

History pointed to retail investors tending to be more active when beta trade opportunities appear, because they tend to seek out volatility. Combined with learnings from previous down-cycles, the conditions were ripe for a retail push. We believe retail investors are attracted to volatility because of the following traits: (1) higher sensitivity to news headlines, (2) bandwagon effect, and (3) confirmation bias.

- Our analysis showed a positive relationship between volatility and retail activity. Hence, small cap, healthcare and IT are key areas of activity.
- During down-cycles, large-cap stocks show a steeper rise in volatility, leading to increased retail activity in large-cap stocks early on. The learning effect also comes into play here – recollection of previous cycles shows that beta trade

opportunities emerge during down-cycles.

What we are seeing now: While we do find that retail investors tend to be more active after volatility events and the preference for leveraged positions is on the increase – leveraged ETFs are getting more attention. We also find that the trading patterns are somewhat disparate. Older age groups (40 and above) tend to have longer holding periods; younger age groups (20s to 30s) have shorter holding periods.

(2) Retail investors have a higher tendency to chase themes over fundamentals: In view of retail investors' higher sensitivity to positive newsflow, we believe they are more interested in thematic trades, especially when volatility starts to come down. During the 2020\~2021 cycle, the key thematic trades were (1) healthcare, as a result of COVID-19 and recent listings, (2) fintech and Internet, in response to the rise in the 'untact' (contactless) economy, and (3) clean environment-related stocks, reflecting new deal policies.

What we are seeing now: The bandwagon effect remains but the focus across the board is on the AI story with less dispersion of interest compared to 2020\~2021 cycle. Trading patterns were more emotional with certain sectors/stocks being held without consideration of a stop-loss mechanism even as share prices fell sharply in previous cycles. Rather than emotional attachment, we believe FOMO (fear of missing out) and also proliferation of market information are influencing retail investor proclivity.

## Why we believe tendencies have changed

We still believe that many retail investors remain volatility seekers and theme chasers, but we believe that in general retail investors have become astute, less risk-averse, and also more sensitive to FOMO.

(1) Improved access to quality information – fairer playing field: A key change is that the sheer number of individuals investing in the domestic equity market has grown significantly – more than 15mn people now have brokerage accounts and equity investing is now mainstream. With the increasing number of individual investors, the quality of brokerage platforms has improved, non-traditional information providers have proliferated, and AI has also made the playing field more equal between institutional and retail investors in terms of information accessibility and analytical capacity.

## (2) Experience investing overseas has provided better understanding of markets:

According to Korea Securities Depository and brokerage industry statistics, around 8\~9mn retail investors have experience investing in overseas equities, with the US being the main destination. More knowledge of global markets and trends combined with experience in investing in quality stocks appears to have given retail investors more confidence.

(3) FOMO spread to equities and not just real estate: FOMO was a trend that was more associated with the real estate market because in Korea, real estate was the key investment instrument to provide strong returns. Not owning stocks is being seen as more of a risk by individuals in the perspective of wealth accumulation and keeping up with social trends. We believe FOMO should be a trend that comes and goes depending on the performance of the market – but for now this will likely be an important reason to be investing in stocks.

The main investors in the stock market are individuals in their 50s and 60s, which makes sense because wealth levels should be higher for these households. According to reports (Edaily, June 3, 2026), a Korea Securities Depository report released in March on the shareholder status of listed companies with fiscal year-end in December 2025 showed that individuals in their 50s accounted for the largest proportion of retail shareholders – 23.1% as of the end of last year, followed by those in their 40s at 21.8%. In terms of the number of shares owned, investors in their 50s held 34.4% of all shares and those in their 60s held 26.6%, while investors in their 40s accounted for only 18.6%.

## How to look at increased leverage by retail investors – increased liquidity but also volatility

Households have an additional layer of liquidity through leverage: Margin lending by Korean retail investors has reached historically elevated levels in 2026. As of May 28, 2026, the balance had surpassed W37tn for the first time ever, growing nearly W10tn in 2026 alone from approximately W27tn at end-2025. This represents a steep acceleration from the start of the year: margin lending balances had already surged from W17.1tn at end-2025 to W32.8tn as of March 6, marking a 92% increase from end-2025 levels in just over two months.

On an average daily basis, the margin loan balance across all Korean securities firms reached W31.01tn in 1Q26, the first time it surpassed the W30tn threshold on an average basis, up 80% from two years ago and 20% from end-2025. This surge in margin lending is attributed to a debt-fueled investing frenzy by retail investors, driven by a booming local stock market.

However, margin lending risk is at a manageable level, though it does increase volatility – considering that as a percentage of total market cap, margin loans are actually lower than historical peak levels. Furthermore, the financial authorities continue to monitor the situation closely and there have been cases of brokers paring down margin lending to manage risk.

Proliferation of ETFs – especially leveraged ETFs – is a double-edged sword. The impact of leveraged ETF proliferation on the KOSPI is a mix of higher liquidity, stronger momentum, and potentially much higher volatility, especially because the Korean equity market is unusually concentrated in a handful of stocks – namely, chipmakers.

- Note that leveraged ETFs mechanically increase exposure to the underlying index or stocks. When money flows into a 2x KOSPI ETF, the manager must buy futures, swaps, or constituent stocks to create the leverage.
• Daily rebalancing is carried out for leveraged ETF targeting 2x exposure every day.

• If the market rises, it must buy more exposure near the close.
- If the market falls, it must sell exposure near the close. Hence, this rebalancing need can amplify market moves.

ETFs are providing retail investors with more choice and also the possibility of higher returns through the investment in leveraged ETFs, but risks are also higher for individuals. ## Economic Implications of Household Asset Shift

## Notable implications for consumption, financial stability, inflation and monetary policy

With the structural shift toward equity investments that we believe is underway, we see this transition carrying a wide range of positive and negative implications for the real economy. There are two layers to the household asset shift into equity holdings. First, a more observable reallocation of idle cash and standby real estate funds into equities, and second, over the longer term by a broader structural migration from real to financial assets.

Both transitions carry notable implications for consumption, financial stability, inflation as well as monetary policy, in our view. With the broadening of the investor base and scale-up in total equity holdings newly featured in the past 4-5 years that did not exist to the same degree pre-2020, we think the overall economic implications are likely more consequential for the wider macroeconomic landscape.

## Positive implications: (1) Amplified positive wealth effect

The shift toward financial assets could amplify the positive wealth effect. We believe the positive spillover from equity wealth gains to the real economy is likely to be both wider in breadth and more intense in magnitude compared to the pre-2020 period, given:

- the number of retail investors increased to 14.5mn in 2025, 50% of total adult population, vs. 6.2mn, 21% of adult population, in 2019;
- a notable jump in the size of equity balance at W662tn in 2025, up 58%Y from W419tn in 2024; this is a 13.8x increase from W17.6tn in 2019; and
- younger investor groups in their 20s and 30s have entered the investor base, who are likely to have a high level of propensity to consume.


By consumption category, the correlation between overall domestic household consumption growth and KOSPI gains has been relatively weak (R-squared: 0.20), while the correlation with durable goods consumption has been more stable (R-squared: 0.41). Durable goods account for just 10% of total domestic consumption, compared to 21.7% for non-durables and 59.4% for services.

We also find the historical trend past 20 years has been that its transmission to broader consumption uneven and concentrated at the top of the income distribution (see: South Korea Economics/Consumer: Increasing Wealth, Concentrated Effect). Luxury consumption appears more responsive to equity market gains than mass-market spending, as the primary beneficiaries of the wealth effect are affluent shareholders. This also resulted in services consumption responding more responsively to KOSPI moves in recent quarters.

At the same time, the intensity of the wealth effect looks to have deepened. Equity market deposits surpassed W100tn for the first time and total household equity holdings climbed to an all-time high of W1,644tn as of 2025, marking a 48.2%Y increase in 2025. With a larger share of household wealth now directly exposed to equity market performance, the transmission of market gains into consumption and income is structurally more meaningful than it was before the retail investor surge of post-2020.

## Plus, we see the younger retail investor cohorts making up more than 40% of the total investors whose groups tend to have a higher marginal propensity to consume.

According to Korea Securities Depository data as of 2025, those retail investors by age group show that 40s or younger groups make up 43% of total investors. Whilst those in their 50s make up the largest group with 22% by 10-year breakdown, we see that the younger 20s and 30s groups are increasingly entering the stock market, suggesting that future wealth effects could expand beyond the affluent top quintile that has historically captured the bulk of asset returns.

## (2) Reduced systemic leverage and asset skewness to real estate; appeasing concerns on financial imbalance

Asset skewness in Korea has been a ongoing issue for financial stability and economic growth. Not just real estate comprising 75% of the household sector balance sheet, but on a wider economy-level across Korea's three major economic sectors, households, corporates and the public sector, there is a stark contrast in real estate concentration. Using the share of non-financial assets vs financial assets as a percentage of total assets comparison, the household sector is the most severely skewed toward real estate of any sector. The public and the corporate sector have a more balanced financial assets base with 72% and 82% of their book in financial assets.

This household-corporate divergence remains a key constraining factor to household consumption capacity as well as national efficiency of resources. Not only has private consumption remained constrained by elevated household debt levels, it also has a negative spillover leading to potential systemic implications as the heavy concentration of household wealth in illiquid real estate limits capital flows into productive corporate investment, constrains household liquidity, and amplifies the sensitivity of the economy to housing price cycles. By encouraging household asset transfer from real assets to equity investments, we expect household liquidity conditions to improve. In April 2026, local authorities formally set a target to reach the 80% level by 2030, which the government believes is crucial for restoring growth momentum and ensuring financial stability.

## (3) Diversifying household income channels beyond labor and real asset returns

The shift to financial assets can also change the household income structure, which is highly labor income-dependent. Historically, Korea's household income structure has been dominated by labor income. Labor income accounted for 64% of total household income in 2025, while business income fell from 20% to 17.5% over the same period, highlighting the dominant role of labor activity and labor market as the primary income source.

We think this heavy dependence on a single income channel, labor, creates structural vulnerabilities that equity-based asset accumulation can help address. Securing diversified channels of household asset accumulation could lead to more stable income earning channels against the structural backdrop of an aging population that erodes the labor income base, a pension system with low replacement ratios, and a real estate-heavy balance sheet that constrains liquidity and amplifies leverage. This eventually could lead to stronger economic growth via a consumption recovery, which is secured by a broad-based and sustained income channels and outlook.

In particular, dividend income could offer a sustained income channel on top of pure return on investment. With the government's corporate reform on tackling on Korea's historically lower dividend payout ratios compared to advanced markets, and an average of around 25%, dividend income growth will likely provide a stable, long-term source of income to the household sector.

In fiscal 2025, 566 Korean corporates' total cash dividends reached a record W35.1tn, up 15.5%Y, extending the expansion trend for the 4th consecutive year. The average payout ratio for these companies rose to 39.8%, with the average dividend yield rising to 2.6%. As a result, household dividend income grew to W38.5tn, up from W33tn in 2023, which was prior to the government's reform on dividend payouts. At the same time, the size remains small at 2.55% of gross national disposable income, with more room to grow compared to other economies, such as in Japan (3-5%) and the US (6-9%).

## Negative implications: (1) Heightened exposure to market volatility

As Korean households already carry a large debt burden, a period of elevated equity market exposure could stretch liquidity, slow consumption, and increase debt-service stress. While we view the structural shift in Korean household balance sheets towards equities as a long-term positive, it may also raise risks that were less prominent before 2020, when household equity investment was smaller in scale and scope. The record-high level of equity holdings warrants caution as the size of households' equity share could become disproportionate. The scale of equity asset accumulation within Korean household balance sheets reached a record high, W1,644tn as of 2025, or 61% of nominal GDP, a notable jump from the previous five-year trend of 45%. By 2027, we estimate that this is likely to grow to over 81% of nominal GDP. This would heighten household sensitivity to equity market volatility, which has been more elevated of late.

## (2) Risks around asymmetry in reverse wealth effect

We flag that higher market volatility likely suppresses household consumption more than the equivalent gains that stimulate it. Historically, Korea's equity market-driven wealth effect has been limited. Given the small share of active retail investors pre-2020, the wealth effect from equity gains was structurally weak in absolute terms. At the same time, historically households have held a strong bias towards assets viewed as less risky, which results in a lack of experience of in managing volatility.

In 2005-25, the correlation between overall consumption growth and KOSPI gains was relatively weak (R-squared: 0.20), but the correlation with durable goods consumption was more stable (0.41). However, historically, we calculate the wealth effect from equities has a coefficient of only 0.17 on consumption, compared to 0.81 for labor income. This is because in the past, households often perceived the gains from stocks as temporary windfalls rather than permanent income due to market volatility, which dampened the impact on consumption. We note that during periods of higher volatility, potential losses could be perceived as permanent shocks.

Households' sensitivity to rising interest rates not only adds pressure to their already high leverage but also new loans for equity investments. # Implications for CPI Inflation and Monetary Policy

## Amplified wealth effect to accelerate inflation build-up

The faster and more liquid nature of equity returns relative to real estate suggests a more direct pass-through to household income, with broader implications for inflation. As household equity wealth rises, we expect the first-round effect to appear among upper-income households through increased spending, particularly on durable goods and luxury items, adding demand-side inflationary pressure. KOSPI gains show a high correlation with durable goods consumption growth over the past 20 years, with durable goods consumption tracking KOSPI gains most closely among goods categories. The top 20% of Korean households hold approximately 87% of equities, which means the initial consumption impulse is concentrated at the upper end of the income distribution.

Critically, we also identify a second-round channel through services inflation. Personal services inflation, in particular, has shown a strong correlation with KOSPI movements over the past five years, with a lag of approximately 15-16 months. Compared with the broader 25-year historical analysis, the more recent five-year period, during which retail investor participation in the equity market increased significantly, shows a stronger and more durable correlation between equity market performance and services inflation.

As argued above, the structure of the wealth effect has evolved with the surge in retail investor participation, suggesting that equity-related wealth gains likely generate a more pronounced and immediate positive effect on consumption. This contrasts with wealth from real estate, where gains remain largely unrealized and require refinancing, linked to the household debt burden, or require a sale to convert them into consumption capacity.

## Effects on inflation near-term; financial stability long-term

These dynamics carry a two-layer implication for monetary policy, in our view. In the near term, the more immediate concern runs through the inflationary channel: an amplified wealth effect, rising household equity wealth, and the likely upward shift in household inflation expectations potentially intensifying the BoK's focus on price stability. We see that strong exports, coupled with a booming local equity market lifting domestic demand across investment and consumption, could see services inflation move beyond base case expectations. This is consistent with the BoK MPC's acknowledgment at its May 2026 meeting that the positive spillover from wealth effect has become stronger, one of the factors behind the pivot toward a tightening cycle, with the first rate hike now expected in July 2026.

Over the longer term, however, the household asset shift from real assets to financial assets carries a potentially stabilizing effect on financial imbalances. By redirecting household assets away from leveraged real estate holdings toward equity holdings, the shift could slow the pace of household debt accumulation. Even as the shift generates demand-pull inflationary pressure in the near term, it simultaneously reduces the systemic risks associated with the household debt, offering the BoK room to focus on its price stability mandate without the competing constraint of financial stability risks that at times have complicated its policy decisions.

## Long-term sustainability depends more on policy path

Taking a reference from Japan's recent reform case and the pace of recent household fund flow patterns, we think Korean households will be incentivized to increase their equity holdings. In all of our scenarios, we have the proportion of equity holdings in total financial assets increasing, but the rate of change varies.

The more important question, however, is not whether a cyclical rally can boost brokerage earnings in the short term. It is whether this rally can become the starting point for a broader redistribution of household assets from property and deposits into longer-duration financial products. A durable expansion in domestic retail liquidity cannot rely solely on explosive capital gains from a narrow group of cyclical stocks. For retail participation to become structurally stickier, Korea needs a broader re-rating of quality companies, dividend stocks, and stable yield-oriented equities. In this sense, capital market reform, governance improvement, shareholder return policies, and tax incentives are not separate from the wealth effect. Rather, they are the conditions required to convert trading gains into long-term equity ownership.

We believe these are prerequisites for long-term sustainability of retail investors' participation in the equity market.

(1) The current rally likely needs to be sustained without a bear market (20% or greater correction) in order to build up retail investors' confidence in the domestic market. This means that the structural stories driving the market need to continue; also, more quality companies need to be available for investors.

(2) Capital market and governance reform needs to continue, with companies responding positively to the changes, albeit at a gradual rate. Minority shareholders are treated equally and companies are more friendly towards the market. At the end of the day, we would need to see total shareholder returns (dividend payout ratio + buyback ratio) continuing to narrow the gap with global markets.

\- Policy direction appears increasingly aligned with this objective. The Corporate Value-up framework was designed to encourage listed companies to improve valuations, strengthen communication with shareholders, and enhance shareholder returns. The Ministry of Economy and Finance's 2025 tax revisions also included separate taxation for dividend income from high-dividend companies, while the government has also discussed expanded ISA benefits to promote long-term domestic stock investment.

\- These measures matter because Korea's retail investor base has historically been more capital-gain-oriented and volatility-seeking than yield-oriented. Lower tax friction on dividends, stronger incentives for long holding periods, and broader use of ISA, pension savings, IRP, and retirement pension products could gradually shift retail behavior from short-term trading towards portfolio-based investing.

## Should we buy into Korean financials? Need to be selective

## A structural change for the financial sector; IB and WM key to sustainable ROE boost

Korea's recent equity market rally has revived debate around the “wealth effect” in a market where household balance sheets have historically been dominated by real estate rather than financial assets, which has led to financial companies being more focused on real-estate-linked businesses. We have been flagging (refer here) that Korean banks will have to wean themselves off real estate businesses and become more active in the capital markets space to be able to boost sustainable ROE.

For financial institutions, the implication from the money movement story is potentially meaningful. The first-order benefit of the equity rally is obvious: higher market turnover drives brokerage commission income, margin-loan interest, and platform traffic. But the more valuable bull case scenario is a second-order effect, where increased retail familiarity with equities and ETFs raises demand for broader WM services.

In that scenario, securities companies would benefit not only from execution fees but also from fund and ETF sales, wrap accounts, model portfolios, pension products, advisory fees, asset-based management fees, and potentially subscription-like digital services.

The earnings mix would therefore become less dependent on one-time upfront sales commissions and more exposed to recurring revenue linked to assets under management, assets under administration, and client retention. This is particularly important for Korean securities firms, whose earnings have historically been highly sensitive to market turnover, brokerage commission cycles, and episodic IB activity. A larger and more persistent WM revenue pool could help reduce this cyclicality and support a more stable fee-income base over the medium to long term.

The retirement pension channel could become one of the clearest areas of differentiation. As DC (Defined Contribution) and IRP (Individual Retirement Pension) assets grow relative to DB (Defined Benefit) and as more assets move from principal-guaranteed products into performance-linked products, the ability to provide credible long-term portfolios will matter more. Securities companies with strong ETF platforms, global asset allocation products, pension advisory tools, and digital onboarding should be better positioned to capture flows. Banks and insurers will also participate, but we think their advantages will differ. Banks have deposit relationships and broad retail distribution, while insurers have natural links to retirement, annuity, and protection products. The redistribution of household assets into financial products, therefore, has the potential to deepen competition not only among brokerages but across the entire financial holding-company structure.

## Japan's Reform as Reference: Quicker Move amid Active Risk-Seeking in Korea

## Korean households are moving faster vs. Japan

Both Japan and Korea are experiencing a structural, policy-driven shift in household asset allocation – away from cash and deposits (in Japan's case) and real estate and cash (in Korea's case) – toward equity investments. While the two share a common reform philosophy, the specific reform designs, timelines, and household starting points differ meaningfully. For Japan, various market reforms that could affect households' asset allocation, including corporate governance, stewardship, and Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) reforms starting as early as 2014, along with the new NISA reform in 2024. For Korea, starting with the comprehensive capital markets reform in 2022, the Corporate Value-up (2024) and the latest KOSPI5000 initiatives have been the major market reform drivers.

- Speed: Korean household equity holdings surged 29.2% in 2025, pushing the equity share from 20.3% in 2024 to 26.5% of financial assets. At the same time, Japan's equity share has risen from 14.5% in 2015 to 21.6% in 2026.
- Breadth: Nearly 50% of Korea's adult population now holds equities, 14.5mn, up from 15% a decade ago. In Japan, retail investors account for 25% of the total trading value on the TSE, up from roughly 18% to 20% in 2010. In terms of actual stock ownership, individuals hold 17.3% of the total value.
- Policy intensity: Korea deployed aggressive, time-limited tax incentives, known as Returning Investment Accounts (RIA), which give capital gains tax exemptions for funds repatriated from overseas that are invested in local markets to accelerate the shift in equity investment. However, Japan's Nippon Individual Savings Account program (NISA), while successful, is a longer-term, more gradual initiative.
- Market characterization: Korea's market has surged rapidly this year, largely driven by retail investors, particularly in the AI and semiconductor sectors. Japan's retail investor investment appears a more gradual process focused on lower-risk, value assets.

While the two economies share similar household asset structures, the differences also set them apart in terms of speed and scale of the asset shifts into equities.

## Similarities

- Cash holdings accounts make up the largest share of households financial assets.
- Both have seen a 7ppt increase in their equities holdings in the past decade, including the investment trust portion for Japan.
- Both have reduced their weighting in less volatile financial products, such as insurance and pensions since market reforms started.

## Differences

- At the same time, in 2025 Korean households' holdings of equity assets remain more elevated compared to Japan at 26.5% vs. 21.6% in 2025 (including investment trusts).
- Japan's bias toward cash and deposits still appears stronger than in Korea – with the cash share at 48.5% of total financial assets in Japan vs. 43.2% in Korea.
- While Japan's households showed a decline in cash weighting (from 52% in 2015 to 48.5%), Korean households have shown little change (42.8% to 43.2%).

Korea and Japan are more conservative on investing in risk assets compared to the US; we see a closer DM-style move in Korea vs. Japan: Korean and Japanese households have conservative asset dynamics compared to other major economies. For example, compared to the US, where cash and deposit share comes to around 11% of total financial assets vs. 41% in equities and 26% in insurance and pension funds, as of 1Q25, both Korea and Japan hold notably more conservative portfolios with Japan at 48.5% cash holdings in 2025 followed by Korea at 43.2%.

However, in our base case for Korea, we see cash holdings falling below 40% in the next two years as equity holdings rise to 35% from 26.5% in 2025. We think this will set a trend for Korean households to move towards a DM-style asset composition where the proportion of higher-risk assets increases.

## Successful expansion of retail participation in NISA

Even with Japanese households' high cash holdings, we believe market reforms have been successful: Our analysts for Japan believe the nation's market reforms have led Japanese individuals to actively diversify their assets into stocks, bonds and alternative investments (see: Global Financials: Japan's Wealth Transition). Japan attracted flows via tax exemptions on financial returns via individual investment accounts, as well as creating policy-led funds for retail investors to participate. The key tool was via upgrading the previous NISA program into new NISA accounts, led by government incentives.

Upgraded in January 2024, NISA represented a significant shift in Japan's approach to encouraging household investment and long-term asset building. Under its Doubling Asset Income Plan, the new NISA system became permanent in 2024 with unlimited tax-free periods and increased investment limits, and it became structurally important as a distribution channel for investment trusts. NISA's share rose to around 25% of total subscriptions for publicly offered equity investment trusts in 2024. We assess that it promoted a major shift from Japan's traditional savings culture towards investments via means of individuals directly participating in financial investments via iDeCo (individual DCs) and corporate DCs (Defined Contribution).

## Appendix 1: Product Outline

## (1) Expansion of ISA

The Korean government has been pursuing significant expansion of the Individual Savings Account (ISA) program since 2024 as part of its broader capital market reform agenda. These measures aim to incentivize retail investors to invest in the domestic equity market and ensure longer-term investment, particularly in response to the Korea discount and demographic challenges. These government policies aim to encourage long-term investment in the domestic stock market, facilitate asset accumulation for the public, and strengthen the role of capital markets in the Korean economy. The reforms are part of the government's broader domestic liquidity-linked measures to incentivize retail investors to invest in the domestic equity market and ensure longer-term investment.

Korea's ISA reform was inspired by Japan's new NISA reform, which was introduced to support the overall growth of financial assets held by households. Japan's NISA, after rule changes, led to a sharp increase in fund inflows and investment in domestic equities, which raised market hopes that Korea's ISA rule changes could have a similar effect.

Prior to 2024, the ISA maintained a conservative structure: an annual contribution limit of W20mn with a total limit of W100mn over five years. The current tax-exempt limit for investment gains is W2mn, assuming stocks are held for three years, with a separate tax rate of 9% applied to gains above the exempt limit.

However, a number of proposed reforms have gone through in 2024-26:

\- Increased contribution limits: The government has proposed raising the annual contribution limit from W20mn to W40mn, with the total contribution limit over five years rising from W100mn to W200mn. Another bill introduced in the National Assembly in April 2026 proposes an even higher annual limit of W60mn and a lifetime cap of W300mn.

\- Expanded tax-exempt limits: The government is likely to review the ISA tax-exempt limit from the current W2mn, based on proposed revisions that outline increasing the tax-exempt limit for interest and dividend income from W2mn to W5mn for the general accounts. Another bill in April 2026 proposed raising these limits to W6mn for general accounts and W12mn for low-income earners.

\- Progressive tax-exempt increases: A ruling party lawmaker, Lee Soyoung, has proposed an amendment to the Special Taxation Restriction Act that would add W1mn annually to the ISA tax-exempt limit for each year beyond the mandatory three-year holding period. Under this bill, investors holding an ISA for five years would see their tax-exempt limit rise to W4mn (W2mn existing + W2mn).

\- Lower tax rates: Another proposed bill suggests higher investment limits for ISAs and lowering tax rates from the current 9% to 5%, to incentivize longer-term investment into the domestic equity market.

\- Expanded eligibility: The government has proposed allowing individuals, subject to comprehensive financial income taxation, who were previously ineligible for existing ISAs, to join ISAs. However, they would not receive tax-free benefits, and a 14% separate taxation benefit would apply.

\- Multiple account allowance: The regulation limiting individuals to one ISA across all financial sectors is set to be abolished, allowing for multiple ISA accounts.

## New "Productive Finance ISA" or "Domestic Investment-Type ISA"

The Korean government introduced a new ISA variant in 2026, also referred to as "Domestic Investment-Type ISA" or "National Growth ISA," specifically designed to channel capital into domestic markets. This new ISA is designed with eligible investments limited to domestic stocks, domestic stock funds, the National Growth Fund, and Business Development Companies (BDCs), with investments in overseas Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) excluded. The new domestic-only ISA has an annual investment limit set at W40mn (total limit of W200mn) with a tax exemption limit of W10mn. It offers significantly expanded tax benefits compared to existing ISAs, providing dual benefits: an income tax deduction on the contribution amount and reduced taxes on interest and dividend income. There have also been discussions about potentially removing the tax-exempt limit entirely for the National Growth ISA.

## Eligible Investment Instruments Expansion

The Korean government is reviewing a proposal to include the National Growth Fund and Business Development Company (BDC) Fund as eligible investment instruments under the ISA scheme. The government is also considering granting enhanced tax incentives for investments in policy-driven funds such as the National Growth Fund and BDC Fund compared to conventional equity-type funds. Under the proposal, tax benefits would apply to both contributions and dividend income, with dividends from these funds potentially subject to separate taxation at a lower rate.

## Implementation Status and Challenges

Various measures to upgrade the ISA have been proposed by the Korean government since 2024, including raising the limit and increased tax benefits, together with allowing affluent retail consumers to have ISAs. However, the bill was not passed in 2024 as the opposition party was against lowering taxes for the affluent. The ISA upgrade measures requiring an Income Tax Act revision passed the National Assembly and the key date for Korea's ISA upgrade is June 22, 2026, when the new "Productive Finance ISA" is scheduled for launch. This follows years of legislative efforts that failed in 2024 and were revived as part of the government's 2026 economic growth strategy announced in January 2026.

## (2) Advancing IRP


Policymakers have also continued to emphasize retirement asset accumulation through tax-deferred investment growth within IRP accounts and by encouraging investors to transfer matured ISA assets into pension vehicles. Recent discussions have focused on further strengthening retirement adequacy rather than materially altering the structure.

## (3) Retirement pension system reform: transformation into investment funds

Korea's individual retirement pension system is undergoing a major transformation as part of the government's broader pension reform agenda. The Special Committee on Pension Reform, under the Lee administration is expected to continue revamping the national pension system (NPS) and restructure the current individual retirement pension to an investable fund.

The size of the fund reached W431tn as of end-2024, as large as nearly 30% of the NPS's total investment portfolio. The government aims to ramp up its capital markets reform by following up on the latest national pension reforms and proposing new retirement pension changes that can increase the income replacement ratio for retirees and liquidity for local financial markets.

Given the meaningful size of the retirement pension savings, if the funds are unlocked for investment, the asset allocation decision could have an impact on Korea's currency. Korea's corporate reforms could bring \~US\$15-30bn of foreign equity inflows, while NPS foreign investments may rise to \~US\$50bn in 2026. Combined, the two reforms could be positive for KOSPI but have a modest impact on Korea FX and rates (see Korea Reform

Renaissance: Asset Implications of Pension and Corporate Reforms).

## Shift from Defined Benefit to Defined Contribution

Ruling party lawmakers in Korea and the MoFE have been proposing to transform the mostly Defined Benefits-based individual retirement pension into a Defined Contribution plan. The government is looking to turn the pension into a plan based on contributions and investment performance, similar to the current system for the NPS.

## First, the government introduced a "Default Option" scheme for DC and IRP

retirement pensions in 2022: The Default Option scheme is a system in which employees pre-designate certain investment options so that their pensions can be invested in certain types of assets without detailed instructions. The scheme aims to improve the overall return of pension funds by encouraging investment in various types of assets in more convenient ways, as more than 80% of pension reserves have been invested in low-return assets with guarantees on principal.

This system automatically enrolls individuals in a pre-designated investment product if they fail to provide investment direction within six weeks after a product's maturity, and employees can change their default option at any time. Employers were required to establish these default fund options by July 11, 2023. While historically, a large portion of DC assets were in low-interest, principal-guaranteed products, the new system aims to diversify investments.

These default options are pre-approved by the Ministry of Employment and Labor and can include various products, including target date funds (TDFs) and principal-guaranteed options. Retirement pension providers are offering various default options, including those with allocations to stocks, bonds, and alternative assets like gold.

In 2025-26, Korea is moving towards a fund-type retirement pension system: We see efforts around making retirement pension plans mandatory for all workplaces, with the mandate being phased in based on company size, and with larger companies potentially being mandated first. Employers will be required to deposit severance benefits into externally managed retirement pension plans. This involves abolishing the existing severance pay system and unifying it under a retirement pension system managed by external financial institutions. The retirement pension system will also transition to a "fund-type" system, where pensions are pooled into a fund managed by a separate legal entity. The goal is to introduce competition among private entities managing these funds to boost investment returns.

## Poor Historical Performance and Reform Rationale

Given that only 5% of the retirement pensions are being managed, with the rest deposited in savings accounts or other guaranteed principal and interest products, the average return on assets for an individual fund was 2.91% pa between 2020-24, far below the NPS's 7.82% in the same period, or 2% vs. 5.5% over the past 10 years, respectively.

More recently, the NPS's return on investment has surged on the back of equity market outperformance. It further widened the gap with other funds and accelerated the call to transform other pensions into investable funds. In 2024, the NPS posted a record 15% investment return, generating W160tn in profits. The fund's assets surged to W1,213tn by end-2024, driven by a 34.3% return on overseas stocks, which offset a 6.9% loss in domestic equities.

In 2025, it achieved a record-breaking return of 18.82% (provisional, money-weighted return), generating W231.6tn in earnings. This was the best performance since the fund's establishment in 1988. Domestic equities were the highest-performing asset class, with an 82.4% return, driven by technology stocks (AI and semiconductors) and government policies. Overseas equities also performed strongly at +19.7%.

## Progress and Timeline

On October 13, 2025, at the parliamentary audit session, Finance Minister Koo Yun-Cheol indicated a willingness to lead individual retirement pensions' transformation into investable funds. There are six active submissions of amendment bills to current retirement legislation, supported by the government and ruling party lawmakers. The goal is to improve financial performance of the retirement pension by turning it into an investable fund. If achieved, it would supplement the NPS's low-income replacement ratio of 43% by providing an additional source of retirement pension funds, eventually enhancing household incomes post-retirement.

In 4Q25, the government created a task force to push for retirement pension reform. As a part of the KOSPI 5000 agenda, there are ongoing talks around a newly investable asset under the retirement fund that could also be used to help the domestic equity market via increasing asset allocation into local equities and/or bonds.

## (4) National Growth Fund

The National Growth Fund (officially known as the National Participation Growth Fund) launched for sales on May 22, 2026: It is designed to channel capital into hightech industries while also stimulating domestic equity market participation. It is part of the government's broader domestic liquidity-linked measures to incentivize retail investors to invest in the domestic equity market and ensure longer-term investment. The fund is backed by the government and offers retail investors tax breaks and a loss-protection buffer for investments in high-tech strategic sectors like AI, semiconductors, and biotech. Demand was high: its initial W600 billion allocation completely sold out, and the fund is officially scheduled to be established on June 12, 2026, with actual investments commencing on June 15, 2026.

Encouraging households to move from seeking interest-earning deposit-style asset management to shift toward investing in productive sectors, the government sees a new role of household funds as a strategic resource allocation. The fund operates through multiple mechanisms that directly and indirectly boost equity market investments. The goal is to redirect the flow of funds toward innovative sectors and strategic industries, and renewable energy sectors for the next five years including: AI, semiconductors, biotechnology, vaccines, robotics, hydrogen, secondary batteries, displays, future vehicles and defense (press release).

The fund complements other market reform measures, including the Corporate Value-Up Program, which was launched in February 2024 to address the Korea discount through voluntary corporate disclosures, governance improvements, and enhanced shareholder returns. The Korea Value-Up Index was launched in September 2024, composed of companies with strong profitability, shareholder returns, and valuation metrics (PBR, ROE), serving as a benchmark for institutional investors with associated ETFs.

## Fund Structure and Scale

The National Growth Fund plans to invest W150tn (approximately US\$110bn) over five years. The fund is composed of W75tn from a high-tech industry fund backed by the state-run Korea Development Bank (KDB) and another W7tn raised from financial services companies, pension funds, and individual investors. For 2026, the first year of its full operation, the National Growth Fund plans to invest over W30tn, with significant allocations including W6tn for AI, W4.2tn for semiconductors, and W3.1tn for mobility.

## Methods of financial support:

\- Direct equity investment: Participating as an equity investor in new corporate entities, plant construction projects, or providing funding for mergers and acquisitions (M&A) of tech companies; W15tn allocation.

\- Indirect equity investment: The high-tech strategic industry fund and private sector investment funds jointly create large-scale funds for equity investments, with W35tn allocated.

\- Infrastructure investment: Financing for projects like power grids, power generation, water supply facilities, and AI data centers that support advanced industries, with W50tn allocated.

\- Low-interest lending: Ultra-low-interest loans, typically in the 2% range, for large-scale facility investments and R&D projects, with W50tn allocated.

## Appendix 2: Property Market Measures

## Major real estate tightening measures in 2025-26:

- March 2025: The government announced Housing Market Stabilization Measures on March 19, designating major Seoul districts as Real-Estate Transaction Restriction Zones starting March 24, requiring pre-approval for apartment transactions.
- June 2025: On June 27, the government rolled out strict lending policies that took effect on June 28, including a cap on mortgage loans at W600mn for Seoul and metropolitan areas, tighter monitoring on lending, and loan growth target caps for lenders set at 50%.
- September 2025: On September 7, the government announced comprehensive real estate market measures focusing on supply expansion and demand control - plans to supply 270,000 housing units per year over the next five years, totaling 1.35mn units in the Seoul Metro area.
- Demand-side measures included improving mortgage LTV caps in regulated areas from 50% to 40% and restricting mortgages for multi-home owners.
- October 2025: The government unveiled the third and most comprehensive set of housing market measures on October 15. All 25 districts of Seoul and 12 additional areas in Gyeonggi Province were designated as speculative and land transaction permit zones.
- Mortgage caps were tightened on a sliding scale: loans for homes priced between W1.5bn and W2.5bn were capped at W400mn, while those above W2.5bn were limited to W200mn.
- February 2026: The focus shifted to revamping the tax framework for real estate, including not extending the grace period for punitive capital gains tax on multi-home owners and possibly raising home ownership taxes.
- May 2026: The reinstatement of punitive capital gains tax surcharges for multi-homeowners in regulated zones, ended its temporary suspension on 9 May, which had been in effect for about four years.
- 2H26 (possibly July): Beyond the capital gains tax restoration, the government is actively reviewing a broader overhaul of holding taxes (property tax and comprehensive real estate tax). The tax reforms are expected to be finalized and announced around July 2026.

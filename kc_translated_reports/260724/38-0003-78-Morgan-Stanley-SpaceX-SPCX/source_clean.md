# Anomalies and Rapid Unscheduled Disassemblies

Investors should have realistic expectations about anomalies that can potentially delay SpaceX Connectivity and AI milestones, contributing to earnings and stock price volatility. Despite the challenges, we note SpaceX has demonstrated $>99\%$ success rate with its launches – significantly above peers.

Longtime space geeks will tell you, 'space is hard.' They are not wrong. Others will say: "When it comes to space... failure is not an option." That is wrong. In space, failure is not just an option... it's a vital part of the testing and improvement process. If the engineers in the Apollo program were not allowed to fail, we would have never put men on the moon. Anomalies are, in fact, an unavoidable part of pushing the scientific boundaries of space. Investors should have realistic expectations about the physical realities of space. An investment in SpaceX is an exercise in 'the art of the possible,' grounded in physical reality. It may be tempting to say "it's not rocket science"... but in this case it actually it is.

## SpaceX Anomalies (2023–Mid-2026)

## Starship Program (primary source of issues):

\- Flight 1 (Apr 2023): Vehicle lost minutes after launch due to multiple booster engine failures and fires.

\- Flight 2 (Nov 2023): Booster failed during boostback burn; ship lost due to fire after propellant venting.

\- Flight 3 (Mar 2024): Reached higher altitude but both stages lost during reentry and landing attempts.

\- Flights 4–6 (2024): Major improvement. Successful splashdowns; Flight 5 achieved first booster tower catch.

\- Flight 7 (Jan 2025): Booster caught, but ship lost from propellant leak and cascading engine shutdowns.

• Flight 8 (Mar 2025): Ship lost mid-flight after engine failure.

• Flight 9 (May 2025): Anomalies on both booster and ship stages.

\- Ground test (Jun 2025): Ship 36 exploded during static fire prep (damaged pressure vessel).

\- Flight 10 (Aug 2025): Booster soft-splashed in the Gulf; ship reached space, deployed payload, and survived reentry. First full version 2 success.

\- Flight 11 (Oct 2025): Both stages nailed pinpoint splashdowns; a clean finale for the Version 2 design.

\- Flight 12 (May 2026): Booster failed landing burn and crashed into Gulf of America. First Version 3 flight.

\- Flight 13 (July 2026, Initial attempt): Four engines failed to ignite at liftoff triggering an automatic abort.

## Falcon 9 performance since 2023:

• Extremely reliable overall.

\- July 2024: The one true mission failure. An upper-stage engine failed on its second burn. stranding 20 Starlink satellites in a decaying orbit. All lost.

\- Booster losses (Aug 2024, Mar 2025): Two first-stage boosters were lost on landing. One tipped over and exploded on the droneship (ending a 267-

landing streak), the other caught fire after touchdown. In both cases the payloads reached orbit fine; only reusability was affected.

\- SpaceX has shown rapid iteration and learning from each event, with engine reliability, propellant management, and reentry/landing stresses as recurring challenges.

## Crewed missions (100% success rate)

\- SpaceX has flown 20 crewed missions carrying 78 crew members from 20 countries.

\- These are all orbital crewed flights on Falcon 9/Crew Dragon. No crewed Starship missions have flown yet (though plans exist for future ones).

Excluding SpaceX's Falcon launches, the rest of the launch industry attempted 285 orbital launches in 2024 and 2025, recording 15 outright failures and 2 partial failures. Against that benchmark, Falcon 9's $>99\%$ operational full-mission success rate ranks among the industry's best, despite an initial recovery-development campaign in which only 6 of the 16 initial controlled-descent test flights achieved both a soft landing and booster recovery. Space is hard for everyone: anomalies, delays and losses of critical hardware are an unfortunate reality of the industry. Repeated failures can erode investor conviction and may require repeated successes to restore. What has historically set SpaceX apart is its willingness to test aggressively, fail and learn quickly, and eventually turn repeated setbacks into reliable operational capability.

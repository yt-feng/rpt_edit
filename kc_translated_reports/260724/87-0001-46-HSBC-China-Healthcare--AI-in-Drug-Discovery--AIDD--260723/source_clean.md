## A transformative sector, but still no consensus

Using AI to discover new drugs is rapidly becoming one of China's largest and most misunderstood investment themes. Billions of dollars are flowing into the sector, partnerships with global pharma names are accelerating, and valuations are increasingly pricing in the potential of a company's platform, rather than current earnings. Yet despite the excitement, investors still face one critical problem: there is no consensus on what these AI drug discovery (AIDD) companies are – software, biotech, contract research organisations (CROs) that conduct research and development for drug companies, or future platform monopolies?

We highlight that on one side, AI is clearly transforming drug discovery workflows, compressing timelines, reducing experimental costs, and reshaping how pharma allocates R&D. On the other, monetisation remains early, business models are still evolving, and many public names are trading more on narrative, optionality, and AI scarcity premium than predictable cash flows.

Given the potential upside but also significant mispricing risk, we address the five biggest investor debates shaping the AIDD sector today:

1. Where is monetisation emerging? Which business models are structurally durable vs vulnerable to commoditisation?

2. What actually moves these stocks?

3. How should investors value platform optionality?

4. What are long-term moats in a world where AI models can rapidly be commoditised?

5. Which parts of the value chain capture economics – and which get compressed?

Our differentiated approach combines five elements:

US vs China strategic comparisons and competitive positioning.

Lessons from the early internet platform evolution and CRO industry consolidation.

A framework for identifying where future pricing power and data moats emerge.

Analysis of where valuation methodologies are likely wrong – particularly around platform scalability, talent value, and downstream asset ownership.

A roadmap for navigating one of the market's most narrative-driven and volatile sectors.

Our key takeaway? The winners in AIDD may not be the companies with the best AI models today, but the platforms that can keep learning from their own data, fit deeply into pharma workflows, and repeatedly create valuable drug assets at scale.

This sector could become one of the defining healthcare platform transitions over the next three-to-five years. But separating durable platform winners from temporary AI hype will require a fundamentally different investment framework, which is the subject of this report.

## Key investor debates: Our core findings

1. Where is AIDD monetising today? Which business models are durable vs at risk? Current monetisation is real but still concentrated in lower-value layers like software as a service (SaaS) offerings, drug discovery fees, and CRO services. We see long-term value shifting downstream towards asset ownership, proprietary data, and improving platform economics. Pure SaaS providers face rising commoditisation risk.

2. What moves AIDD stocks – and why does leadership change so quickly? AIDD trades on three drivers: narratives, event catalysts, and fundamentals. In bull markets, the AI narrative typically dominates. In risk-off periods, cash runways and monetisation matter a lot more. Timing when these factors rotate and become more important is critical.

3. What is the correct valuation framework? We believe traditional DCF frameworks are insufficient as AIDD companies increasingly trade as “stacks of options” – combining platform capability, talent scarcity, proprietary data, and future ecosystem value. Markets are pricing their future potential trajectory, not their current cash flow.

4. What becomes the long-term moat? We expect most AI advantages to become commoditised. Instead, durable moats will emerge from proprietary data generation, workflow integration, regulatory positioning, and wet-lab infrastructure – not simply better algorithms.

5. What lessons can investors learn from the internet and CRO industries? Like the early internet and CRO leaders, AIDD winners may evolve from fragmented tools into integrated platforms. Historical winners captured ecosystems, embedded workflows, and long-term switching costs – not feature-level innovation alone.

6. What becomes commoditised vs defensible in AI drug discovery? Generalised modeling, protein folding, and molecular design capabilities are rapidly being standardised. Defensible value increasingly sits in proprietary biological data, patient access, experimental feedback loops, and scalable asset-generation infrastructure.

# Five key investor debates

## 1. Where is AIDD monetising today? Which business models are durable vs at risk?

## A. Cash flow visible, but not yet at scale or predictable

AIDD is generating revenue today, but still not at levels where investors would like it. Despite major progress having been made, most of current monetisation sits in the low- to mid-value layers: SaaS tools, discovery collaborations, and fee-for-service modeling. These produce visible but unstable revenue, and are often tied to early-stage research.

Investor pain point remains highly speculative as the industry is still in its early development phase: is this software (high-margin, scalable) or biotech (high-risk, capital-heavy), or CRO (platform services)? There's no clear consensus from the street in terms of which valuation to use or what business model to classify AIDDs as given these companies are also still navigating themselves the fast-changing world of AI. Until companies prove repeatable SaaS scale or clinical asset realisation, we believe multiples could remain unstable.

Future pivot: From selling picks to owning gold – services & SaaS are entry points, not endpoints. The revenue durability question hinges on what gets commoditised vs what compounds. General AI capabilities like modeling, protein folding, and molecular design are rapidly being standardised. As Google DeepMind-type foundational players push performance boundaries, pure-play SaaS AIDD is becoming price-taker infrastructure, not a value owner.

However, we identify where durability is emerging to differentiate the potential “winners” in the vertical applications of AI in healthcare:

\- Closed-loop platforms and infrastructure (AI + wet lab + proprietary data): Harder to replicate, improving with scale.

Exclusive data moats/patient-level data access: Structural moat in a regulated environment (e.g. US FDA constraints).

\- Downstream economics participation/pipeline ownership: Milestones, royalties, equity in assets.

## C. Business model evolution: What's the tension?

AIDD business models are converging but not all paths will lead to value, in our view. We identify three monetisation layers that exist today:

(1) Entry layer: Tools/SaaS/Discovery fees: This is the fastest to revenue, has the lowest barriers and usually used in preclinical stages. The core problem is limited scale, a high commoditisation risk, and weaker pricing power.

(2) Mid layer: CRO/Platform services: This combines AI + lab execution and generates real, recurring revenue. The tradeoff is its operationally heavy and caps margins.

(3) Value layer: Pipeline ownership/Licensing: Revenue via BD, milestones, royalties: We see this with the highest upside but is highly volatile and back-loaded.

We see that companies are shifting decisively towards asset-centric models, as tools don't capture the drug value like assets do. But this also introduces a fundamental tension:

Moving downstream = higher margins, higher risk, higher capital needs.

Staying upstream = lower risk, lower returns, commoditisation pressure.

Ultimately, we see the winners emerging as hybrid operators, using services revenue to fund internal pipelines, while building data engines and scale into multi-asset portfolios with diversified revenue streams. The winners are likely to evolve as platforms that can consistently originate and monetise assets (instead of pure tools and services).

## 2. What moves stocks (and why do the drivers change rapidly)?

The mistake most investors make is treating AI healthcare as a single-factor growth story. We don't believe it is. Instead, we view it as a three-factor trade with unstable leadership, where returns rotate between fundamentals, events, and the narrative – and they can rotate faster than most portfolios can adjust. At any given time, one of these factors dominates more. The edge comes with timing the rotation, or inflection points.

## The three drivers in the AI healthcare trade:

## 1. Fundamentals set the floor:

In theory, recurring platform revenue and pharma partnerships should anchor valuations. In practice, most “revenue” in AI healthcare is milestone-based, back-ended, and only loosely predictive of future cash flow. Partnership backlogs provide validation, but they are not the same as visibility.

This is why fundamentals can sometimes be largely ignored in rising markets. They only become relevant when liquidity tightens and investors are forced to ask a much simpler question: who survives? At that point, the cash runway and deal coverage abruptly replace TAM narratives.

## 2. Events create the spikes and stock differentiation:

AI does not remove the uncertainty from clinical trials, but accelerates exposure to it. Clinical milestones, IND filings, and pharma deals still drive step-function moves, often with classic biotech asymmetry risk-reward: sharp rallies on positive data and deep drawdowns on failure. What AI changes is it could amplify the frequency and perception of these events. As a result, events generate alpha, but they are not a stable driver of sector-wide performance.

This also matters most in the middle of the cycle, when the narrative weakens and investors start discriminating.

## 3. Narrative beta and platform capability drive most upside and are least modeled:

In bull markets, AI healthcare stocks trade less like biotech names and more like long-duration tech stocks. Multiples expand with the broader AI trade, capital rotates into “next wave” themes, and a scarcity premium emerges around pure-play names.

This creates a powerful dynamic – stocks can move without company-specific news. Correlations shift away from clinical timelines and toward AI sentiment, tech multiples, and liquidity conditions.

This is why the biggest rallies in the space often occur before revenue or clinical validation. And it's also why those gains can unwind just as quickly when the AI trade softens.

## What dominates and when?

In risk-on environments, narratives can drive most of the returns. Investors are effectively paying for exposure to AI rather than healthcare outcomes. Valuations expand, dispersion compresses, and even early-stage names re-rate aggressively. Fundamentals are acknowledged but not priced.

As markets transition, the narrative loses momentum, dispersion rises, and investors begin to differentiate between companies with real partnerships or pipelines and those relying more on a story. This is where event-driven performance becomes more important.

In risk-off regimes, the hierarchy of drivers flips. Fundamentals take control, the narrative loses momentum, and capital retreats to names with balance sheet strength and visible deal flow.

## Implications for investors: Timing the rotation

What makes this sector difficult and tradable is not just the existence of these three drivers, but the speed at which the shift from narrative to fundamentals happens, driven by flows and macro conditions.

In bull markets, investors are effectively long AI beta with biotech optionality. In drawdowns, investors risk holding pre-revenue biotech with compressed tech multiples. The underlying companies have not changed, but the driver of their valuation has.

## 3. What is the correct valuation framework when the investor base is gaining optionality, not cash flows?

Healthcare is becoming a primary vertical battleground with sizeable TAM, high-value data, and unsolved problems in drug discovery. However, investors are speeding up their entry into this space to gain the “optionality” of potentially significant upside, given biology AI is much harder than scaling general large language models (LLMs) and has much higher barriers to entry.

## Trading optionality, not cash flows:

AI healthcare companies are not single assets, but more like a stack of options. As we believe the traditional DCF approach is structurally insufficient, we breakdown valuations into three layers – what part of the valuation is real (i.e. based on tangible results), what part is probable (i.e. expected), and what part is story driven – and which one is currently mispriced?

Case study: Anthropic acquisition of Coefficient Bio (cUSD400m, c0.1% of Anthropic equity). This transaction (<10 employees, <1 year old, stealth/no revenue or pipeline) reveals an important implication that valuation methodologies do not always follow a traditional framework. The market assigns material value to “future platform capability and trajectory”, well before monetisation exists.

## What is actually being valued?

\- Talent scarcity: Top-tier AI + biology talent = scarce asset (cUSD40-50m per researcher).

Time-to-market advantage: Pre-built trajectory with a team already aligned on biological modeling architectures and experimental design workflows.

Strategic entry into biology: Proprietary biological data and domain-specific expertise becomes a key moat, while general LLMs are commoditised.

\- Platform integration: Anthropic can integrate Claude models with biology-specific vertical integration, creating a full-stack scientific AI platform.

## What justifies a significant valuation and what is the valuation logic?

Replacement cost/time saved framework: How much does it cost and time consumed to build internally?

Venture-style “power law” optionality: If successful, a platform could unlock multi-billion dollar upside via a drug discovery platform and pharma partnerships (significant TAM vertical).

Data and moat premium: Biological datasets are scarce, proprietary and cumulative – owning a team that can generate novel biological data loops is extremely valuable.

Data assets create future monetisation value: e.g.: electronic health record (EHR) platforms and genomics datasets.

\- Platform multiple or potential: Long-term platform option.

\- Talent and strategic option value: Access to an elite team and entry into biotech AI.

## Where the market could be wrong?

## Mispricing 1: Platform optionality and ecosystem expansion potential

Investors could underestimate repeatable asset-factory monetisation and partnership generation capability, particularly when it evolves faster than originally perceived.

## Mispricing 2: AI scalability could be under-modeled

The market could model AI drug discovery as a linear biotech, while in reality it ignores scalable platform infrastructure economics.

## Mispricing 3: Talent is not capitalised properly

The market still expenses R&D, while frontier deals imply talent should also be treated as a balance sheet asset.

## 5. What is defensible and most valued in AI drug discovery, and what will likely be commoditised?

AIDD is a discipline that integrates machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and advanced statistical modelling to automate, optimise, and de-risk every stage of the preclinical and clinical drug development lifecycle. It represents a paradigm shift from legacy computer aided drug design (CADD) – which relies on rule-based computational chemistry and static molecular modelling – to a data-driven, iterative learning system that continuously improves predictive performance via closed-loop data integration.

Technically, AIDD has evolved through three peer-validated, distinct generations:

First-generation (1970s-2000s): Rule-based quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models and linear regression frameworks, limited to single-parameter prediction of molecular properties with small, structured datasets.

Second-generation (2000s-2019): Classical ML models – a machine learning algorithm that combines the predictions of multiple decision trees (random forests) to generate a single, more accurate result, support vector machines, gradient boosting) and shallow neural networks, enabling multi-parameter optimisation of molecular properties and virtual screening of large compound libraries.

Third-generation (2020-present): Deep learning architectures, generative AI, multimodal large language models (MLLMs), and physics-informed neural networks, enabling end-to-end drug discovery from target identification to clinical trial design, with cross-modality data integration and closed-loop iterative learning.

To accurately value pure-play AIDD companies, investors must look beneath the umbrella term of “AI”. The prevailing market misconception is that AI merely accelerates traditional workflows. In reality, the true valuation premium is entirely dependent on the underlying algorithmic architecture – specifically, the transition from rigid-body screening to equivariant generative models and quantum-approximating neural networks.

## Tier 1: The commoditisation of rigid-body docking (low defensibility)

The base layer of computational chemistry relies on structure-based virtual screening (SBVS). Historically, this involved connecting massive libraries (e.g., ZINC, Enamine REAL) against static protein structures. The open-sourcing of DeepMind's AlphaFold has effectively commoditised target structural data (source: DeepMind).

However, proteins are highly dynamic entities. Standard molecular docking algorithms rely on heuristic scoring functions that fundamentally fail to account for “induced-fit” mechanisms – the conformational changes a protein undergoes upon ligand binding. Companies that merely deploy basic convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to screen open-source structural databases are engaged in a low-margin computational arms race with Big Tech. Their predictive validity in complex human biology is inherently weak (source: Nature Computational Science).

## Tier 2: Geometric deep learning and equivariant diffusion (the De Novo moat)

The first true algorithmic moat lies in generative architecture. Instead of screening existing libraries, advanced players use generative AI to synthesise entirely novel chemical matter.

The cutting-edge standard has shifted from simple generative adversarial networks (GANs) to equivariant graph neural networks (EGNNs) and 3D diffusion models. Equivariance is mathematically crucial: it ensures the neural network understands that a molecule's properties remain identical regardless of its 3D rotation or translation in space (source: ICLR).

## Relevant players:

Insilico (3696 HK): Its proprietary Chemistry42 platform is a prime example of this moat. It uses over 30 generative engines, including structure-based diffusion models, to achieve multi-parameter optimisation (balancing affinity, ADME, and novelty simultaneously, source: Company reports).

Generate Biomedicines (GEMB US): Operates on a similar cutting-edge generative thesis but focuses on large molecules, using its Chroma platform – a generative diffusion model capable of programming programmable protein therapeutics based on structural constraints (source: Company reports).

## Tier 3: Neural network potentials and hybrid physics-ML (the thermodynamic moat)

Once a molecule is generated, its binding affinity must be validated physically. Traditional quantum mechanics (e.g., density functional theory (DFT)) is highly accurate but computationally prohibitive at scale. Conversely, classical molecular dynamics (MD) is fast but lacks electronic-level accuracy.

The breakthrough moat here is the development of machine learning potentials (MLPs) or neural network potentials. These algorithms are trained on vast quantum mechanical datasets to approximate DFT-level accuracy at the computational cost of classical MD (source: Nature Chemistry).

## Relevant players:

Schrödinger (SDGR US): The legacy pioneer of free energy perturbation (FEP+). Its moat remains defensible because its force fields (OPLS) have been refined over decades – a data barrier difficult for purely tech-native startups to cross.

XtalPi (2228 HK): Integrates advanced quantum physics calculations with an automated robotics wet-lab. XtalPi's moat is highly defensible because it creates a closed-loop "Tech-CXO" model; its algorithms predict crystalline structures, and its proprietary robotic arrays synthesise and validate them, generating high-fidelity, non-public data.

\- Iambic Therapeutics (US Private – acquired by Takeda in February 2026): Uses its NeuralPLexer technology, a state-of-the-art physics-informed neural network that predicts structural ensembles of protein-ligand complexes, capturing dynamic flexibility that standard AlphaFold predictions miss.

## Tier 4: Phenomics and multimodal clinical prediction (The de-risking moat)

For equity investors, mitigating the Phase II to Phase III clinical failure rate (the efficacy proof-of-concept) dictates valuation. Biological complexity cannot be solved by chemical structures alone. The ultimate moat involves mapping the drug to human systems biology.

Recursion Pharmaceuticals (RXRX US) pioneers the phenomics approach. Instead of focusing solely on target binding, Recursion uses automated “cell painting” to capture high-dimensional morphological changes in cells upon drug exposure. Their deep learning models map a massive biological dataset (over 50 petabytes) to identify novel biological relationships (source: Cell Systems).

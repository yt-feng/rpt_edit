# All the Data Center Demand in the World, Still Not Enough Supply

Demand for data centers continues to outpace supply, with hyperscaler capex accelerating and chip volume forecasts implying GWs of capacity ahead of feasible data center delivery. Demand is not the issue, but the supply chain is. Our DC Capacity data pulls show only 8.9 GW was lit in '25, while data center demand was nearly 21.1 GW, a \~12 GW deficit, highlighting a tight DC market, and supporting a favorable backdrop for DC REITs and AI DC Developers.

Demand Signals Are Unambiguous Across Every Metric: Hyperscaler capex is on pace for \$770B in CY26E, up 74% YoY and nearly 5x the \$156B deployed in CY23. Combined cloud backlog surged to \$2T in 1Q26, growing 3x faster than capex since CY23. Bottom-up accelerator sales volumes from our semiconductor team imply \~30 GW of global AI power demand in 2026E from GPU/XPU shipments alone, with 19 GW going to North America, nearly 2x the 10.3 GW of new capacity we expect to come online.

Six Physical Supply Constraints Set the Ceiling on Deliverable Capacity: Our proprietary DC Capacity Delivery Model calibrates supply ceilings across six constraint categories. We estimate EPC/Labor as the binding constraint in 2026E, with a ceiling of 10.4 GW. Cooling is nearly co-binding at 11.4 GW and becomes the binding constraint from 2028E onward.

Structural Undersupply Benefits Operators, Suppliers, and New Entrants Across the DC Ecosystem: With vacancy rates at 1-3% across primary markets and wholesale rents accelerating, the demand/supply imbalance continues to benefit operators, suppliers, and new entrants alike. DC REITs (DLR, EQIX, IRM) have realized meaningful growth in leasing volumes and pricing over the past several years, with rents and bookings accelerating alongside AI-driven demand. Supply chain tightness is equally favorable for equipment suppliers, where order growth continues to outpace revenue delivery across cooling, electrical, and power transformer manufacturers, with backlogs expanding at 2-4x the rate of revenue. The demand and supply imbalance is also supporting the emergence of new entrants, including those from the Bitcoin mining industry. CORZ, CIFR, HUT, RIOT, and WULF are converting existing infrastructure to serve AI/HPC workloads, leveraging energized sites and established grid connections to deliver capacity on compressed timelines that other developers, constrained by the multi-year interconnection queue and equipment lead times, struggle to match. See our initiation, AI Data Center Developers: Fresh Faces, Same Math, for more detail on the BTC-to-HPC thesis.

Our Data Validates the Trend; With the Data Center Industry Exiting 2025 With a 12 GW Deficit: According to Aterio's comprehensive site database, only 3.4 GW of lit data center capacity came online that were not hyperscaler self-builds in North America in 2025, against 15.6 GW of leases signed (per Data Center hawk), a 12.2 GW deficit. This gap has been growing significantly, from 2.9 GW in 2023 to 4.3 GW in 2024 and nearly tripling to 12.2 GW in 2025. From 2021 to 2025, the cumulative deficit has reached 20.4 GW of undelivered data center capacity, a trend we expect to worsen as demand continues to outpace the supply chain's ability to deliver. We expect hyperscalers to continue to commit to capacity further into the future. Currently, they tend to commit to capacity deliverable within 12-18 months, but we could see that widen to 24+ months given supply constraints.

# Our Data Center Capacity Model

North American data center capacity increased by 8.9 GW in 2025A to 44.7 GW, and we estimate that another 10.3 GW will be lit in 2026E, reaching a total installed base of 55.0 GW. This marks a significant acceleration from the 1.4 to 3.5 GW per year delivered from 2017 to 2022. Looking further out, we model capacity additions growing from 12.1 GW in 2027E to 15.1 GW by 2030E, bringing total North American installed capacity to 109.3 GW, implying a \~20% CAGR off the 2025A base.

The mix between hyperscaler self-builds and leased capacity is shifting. Hyperscalers accounted for 62% of the 8.9 GW delivered in 2025A, and we expect their share of new builds to increase to 65% in 2026E before moderating to 45% by 2030E as third-party colocation developers accelerate delivery. On an installed capacity basis, hyperscaler self-builds accounted for \~55% of total capacity in 2025A and are expected to peak near 57% in 2026E to 2027E, then decline to \~54% by 2030E as colocation supply catches up. The leased share of new capacity additions dips from 38% in 2025A to 35% in 2026E, then recovers to 55% by 2030E, reflecting a structural rebalancing as developers convert contracted MW into commissioned capacity. On the installed base, leased capacity stabilizes near 43% through 2027E, then increases to \~46% by 2030E. We expect the leased data center market to outgrow owner-occupied builds through the forecast period, supported by hyperscaler demand for third-party capacity as a capital-efficient complement to self-builds.

# Chip Power Forecasts Highlight Overwhelming Need for Infrastructure

Our semiconductor team publishes a bottom-up analysis of total accelerator sales volumes across NVDA, AVGO, MRVL, AMD, and MediaTek to estimate the aggregate power required to run the servers that host these chips. The results validate the structural undersupply of North American data center capacity relative to the pace of AI chip deployment. Total accelerator volumes across the five vendors were 9.4M units in 2025, growing to 16.0M in 2026E, 23.6M in 2027E, and 26.0M in 2028E, implying global total AI data center power demand of 12.5 GW in 2025, 30.0 GW in 2026E, 63.4 GW in 2027E, and 105.5 GW in 2028E.

Applying our estimates for the share of accelerator sales going to North America (declining from 66% in 2025 to 60% by 2028E as international AI buildouts accelerate), we estimate North American AI power demand from chip volumes alone reaches 8.2 GW in 2025, 19.2 GW in 2026E, 39.3 GW in 2027E, and 63.3 GW in 2028E. Compared against our forecast for 10.3 GW of incremental North American capacity additions in 2026E, AI chip-implied demand exceeds new supply by 1.9x, growing to 3.2x in 2027E and 4.8x in 2028E. On a cumulative basis, unmet AI data center power demand versus annual North American supply additions reaches an estimated 8.9 GW in 2026E, 27.1 GW in 2027E, and 50.2 GW in 2028E, underscoring that the constraint is not demand visibility but the physical ability to deliver capacity fast enough to absorb it. AI power demand from accelerator chip sales volumes represented approximately 18.4% of total North American installed capacity in 2025, and we estimate this rises to 34.9% in 2026E and 78.9% by 2028E, reinforcing that AI infrastructure is the dominant driver of incremental data center demand globally.

# Supply Chain Constraints: What are They and Which Companies?

Data center development relies on a small set of physical systems that must be delivered and installed before capacity can be energized. Each system has different lead times, supplier concentration, and degrees of flexibility. Our model calibrates supply ceilings for each constraint annually from 2022A through 2030E. EPC/Labor is the binding constraint in 2026E at 10.4 GW, with cooling nearly co-binding at 11.4 GW. The two converge in 2027E (EPC/Labor at 12.1 GW, Cooling at 12.2 GW), after which cooling becomes the binding constraint from 2028E onward.

# EPC / Construction Labor

# 2026E Supply Ceiling: 10.4 GW

Key Suppliers: Quanta Services (PWR), MasTec (MTZ), Primoris (PRIM), MYR Group (MYRG), Everus Construction Group (ECG), Centuri Holdings (CTRI), Dycom Industries (DY), SOLV Energy (MWH), Argan (AGX), IES Holdings (IESC), Sterling Infrastructure (STRL), EMCOR Group (EME), Comfort Systems (FIX), Legence (LGN)

What is it? Skilled labor is required to physically construct, wire, commission, and energize data centers. This includes licensed electricians, EPC general contractors, and specialized trades.

Why is it required? Physical construction and electrical installation cannot be automated or meaningfully outsourced beyond current limits.

How constrained is it today? EPC/Labor is the binding constraint in 2026E. Headcounts across major EPCs stand at roughly 264K employees, growing at approximately 5% annually, while our model's ratio of workers required per GW delivered declines by roughly 10% annually as productivity improves. The disconnect between surging demand and constrained labor supply is visible in contractor financials: PWR reported record Q1 2026 revenue of \$7.87B (+26% YoY) with record backlog of \$48.5B, while MTZ posted record Q1 revenue of \$3.83B (+34% YoY) with record 18-month backlog of \$20.3B, up \$4.4B YoY, led by 65% growth in Clean Energy and Infrastructure. Both raised full-year guidance, yet the rate of backlog accumulation continues to outpace revenue conversion, reinforcing that labor availability rather than contract visibility is the bottleneck. The constraint is geographically concentrated in primary data center markets, including Northern Virginia, Phoenix, and Dallas, where competition with grid buildout, renewables, and broader infrastructure projects for the same pool of licensed electricians and EPC trades is most acute.

Can it be excluded or deferred? No. Construction labor is required for every project. Delays in labor availability translate directly into slippage in commissioned capacity.

JEF Analyst View (Julien Dumoulin-Smith, Paul Zimbardo): We see craft-skilled EPC labor and self-perform execution capacity as the largest constraint within the broader data center power infrastructure buildout, with the bottleneck shifting from demand visibility to who can actually execute transmission, substation, generation and electrical scope at scale. Our preferred way to play DC-related E&C upside is PRIM on value, as we see shares still discounting prior execution concerns while the growth narrative increasingly shifts toward gas generation, power delivery and data center-adjacent electrical work. We also think Street is underappreciating PWR's data center opportunity, particularly given its customer relationships, labor scale and ability to capture grid-facing work tied to utility large load growth. MTZ a compelling E&C with scale across power delivery, comms, pipeline, clean energy & infrastructure (CE&I) all converging into a cohesive turnkey solution for data centers.

# Cooling Systems

# 2026E Supply Ceiling: 11.4 GW

Key Suppliers: Vertiv (VRT), Johnson Controls (JCI), Carrier (CARR), Trane Technologies (TT), Schneider Electric (SU-FR).

What is it? Cooling systems remove heat generated by IT equipment and include air cooling, liquid cooling, chillers, rear-door heat exchangers, and broader thermal management infrastructure. AI-optimized facilities require substantially more sophisticated and dense cooling configurations than traditional cloud or enterprise data centers.

Why is it required? Data centers cannot operate without cooling. AI workloads generate heat densities that legacy air-based systems are increasingly struggling to address, driving a structural shift toward liquid- and hybrid-cooling architectures that are more capital-intensive and harder to source quickly.


Can it be excluded or deferred? No. Cooling is non-negotiable for any operational data center. Design flexibility exists at the margin such as with air versus liquid configurations or cooling towers vs chillers, but does not eliminate the constraint. AI inference and training workloads, in particular, cannot be served without purpose-built thermal management.

JEF Analyst View (Stephen Volkmann): We see chiller capacity as the largest constraint within the broader cooling universe, given the more commoditized nature of other components such as AHUs, CDUs, etc. We estimate global chiller capacity can likely grow at a maximum \~15-25% CAGR over time given supply chain, labor, and machining constraints. Overall, our preferred way to play DC cooling is through CARR (our top HVAC pick) given its relative valuations vs competitor TT despite similar DC mix and margin profile.

# Power Availability

# 2026E Supply Ceiling: 11.6 GW

Key Suppliers / Proxies: EIA capacity additions, FERC interconnection queue, GE Vernova (GEV) gas turbines, behind-the-meter solutions (WMB, KGS, etc.), and key regulated utilities (ETR, NI, LNT).

What is it? Power availability reflects the total grid-connected and behind-the-meter power capacity that can feasibly be allocated to new data center builds in a given year, drawing on EIA capacity additions, FERC interconnection approvals, and BTM generation sources.

Why is it required? Every data center MW requires a corresponding MW of reliable, deliverable power. Without secured interconnection or on-site generation, capacity cannot be commissioned, regardless of how complete the physical build is.

How constrained is it today? Our 2026E power ceiling of 11.6 GW sits above the binding constraints at 10.4 GW for EPC/Labor and 11.4 GW for cooling, meaning that while power is tight, the supply chain will hit labor and cooling bottlenecks before it hits power limitations. That said, the fundamental challenge is the disconnect between queue size and deliverable power: the US interconnection queue stands at 2,061 GW per LBNL Queued Up 2026, but with a median queue-to-COD of five years nationally and eight years in PJM, the vast majority of that capacity will not be available on a timeline relevant to current data center demand. Natural gas in the queue surged 86% YoY to 253 GW, reflecting data center-driven demand for dispatchable baseload power. BTM power solutions are increasingly viewed as a potential structural workaround to the queue bottleneck, enabling developers to energize sites without waiting for grid interconnection. DC-available new power grows from 8.9 GW in 2025A to 11.6 GW in 2026E, and at our 16% CAGR assumption, reaches 25.1 GW by 2030E, well above the cooling ceiling of 15.0 GW, suggesting power is unlikely to become a binding constraint over the forecast period absent a significant downward revision to grid addition assumptions

Can it be excluded or deferred? No. Power is the foundational input for all data center capacity. However, BTM solutions and miner-to-AI conversions at energized sites provide a meaningful structural workaround to grid queue constraints.

JEF Analyst View (Julien Dumoulin-Smith / Paul Zimbardo): The outlook in 2024 and most of 2025 was universally positive for the independent power producers (IPPs) as a constraint. The investment thesis was that existing merchant IPPs were best positioned to contract their existing generation at premium prices with significant speed-to-market advantages. That thesis has quickly faded as the affordability challenges have grown and residential customers in PJM have been adversely impacted by data center demand projections. With affordability a critical issue, the two key trends that have emerged are (1) increasing preference for regulated utility markets; and (2) behind-the-meter constructs [BTM]. Data centers are willing to pay material premiums for power (\$130+/MWh and \$160/MWh not uncommon) under BTM frameworks which do not adversely impact customers. The regulated utilities are the preferred way to invest in the generation build-out via a low-risk model.

# Electrical Equipment

# 2026E Supply Ceiling: 12.4 GW

Key Suppliers: Eaton (ETN), Schneider Electric (SU-FR), Vertiv (VRT), Prysmian (PRY-IT), Amphenol (APH), ABB (ABBN-CH), Siemens (SIE-DE), Forgent Power Solutions (FPS).

What is it? Electrical equipment encompasses switchgear, busways, breakers, power distribution units, cabling, and connectivity infrastructure that moves electricity from transformers to IT equipment within the facility.

Why is it required? Even with power available at the site boundary, electricity must be safely stepped down and distributed to every rack. This equipment scales directly with power density and redundancy design, meaning AI data centers, which run at meaningfully higher densities than legacy facilities, require disproportionately more electrical infrastructure per MW. Additionally, UPS devices and power management systems ensure maximum uptime and reliability.

How constrained is it today? Switchgear has been in short supply, with lead times for high-voltage switchgear exceeding 12 months. The demand backdrop for this equipment is robust, as order growth across the major suppliers continues to significantly outpace revenue delivery: ETN reported record Q1 2026 sales of \$7.5B (+17% YoY, +10% organic), but Electrical America's rolling 12-month orders grew 42% and sector backlog expanded 48% YoY, prompting ETN to raise FY2026 organic growth guidance to 10% from 8%. APH posted record Q1 sales of \$7.6B (+58% YoY, +33% organically), record orders of \$9.4B, and a book-to-bill of 1.24x, driven by exceptional IT datacom demand, indicating that customer procurement is running well ahead of the supply chain's capacity. Schneider Electric's Energy Management backlog exceeded €25B (+18% YoY), with FY2026 organic growth guided at 7-10%, led by data centers. The pattern is consistent: orders and backlog are growing at 2-4x the rate of revenue, confirming that the constraint is manufacturing throughput and lead times, not demand.

Can it be excluded or deferred? No. Power distribution is essential. Sourcing is more flexible than transformers, and suppliers can add capacity faster, but lead times for switchgear specifically have extended to 52–65 weeks in some configurations, making near-term deferral impractical for projects targeting 2026 delivery.

JEF Analyst View (Stephen Volkmann): ETN continues to aggressively ramp capacity for low/medium voltage transformers, switchgear, UPS, and other critical electrical components. The company is targeting a total of 24 specific capacity expansion projects as part of a \$1.5bn investment plan. Currently, 12 projects are complete with an add'l 6 coming online by the end of 2026, and the rest ramping in 2027+. Going forward, 800VDC architecture likely presents further upside, though adoption will likely be limited. Our preferred way to play electrical is with ETN given margin upside to come.

# Power Transformers

# 2026E Supply Ceiling: 13.2 GW

Key suppliers: GE Vernova (GEV), Siemens Energy (ENR-DE), Hitachi Energy (6501-JP), Hyundai Electric (267260-KS), Forgent Power Solutions (FPS), Toshiba (6502-JP), TBEA (600089-CN)

What is it? Transformers step voltage up or down between the utility grid and the data center facility, enabling high-voltage electricity to be safely received and distributed on-site. Large power transformers are custom-engineered, capital-intensive components with limited interchangeability.

Why is it required? A data center cannot be energized without transformers. They are mandatory regardless of design, tenant type, or geography. There is no workaround, no substitute, and no ability to phase their inclusion into a build.

How constrained is it today? Lead times for large power transformers routinely exceed 24 to 36 months, and data center demand competes directly with grid expansion and renewables for available units, making this the constraint most exposed to cross-sector competition for capacity. Both major Western suppliers are seeing demand accelerate well beyond their ability to deliver: ENR reported record Q2 FY2026 orders of €17.7B with total backlog reaching €154B and book-to-bill of 1.72x, with Grid Technologies orders up 42% YoY. ENR raised FY2026 comparable revenue growth guidance to 14-16% (from 11-13%) and targets a low-teens revenue CAGR through FY2028, yet even these upgraded targets imply production growth materially below order intake. GEV reported Q1 2026 orders of \$18.3B (+71% organically) with total backlog reaching \$163B (+\$13B sequentially), and its Electrification segment booked \$2.4B in data center equipment orders in Q1 alone, exceeding all of 2025 combined. GEV now expects combined gas turbine backlog and slot reservation agreements to reach at least 110 GW by year-end 2026, up from 100 GW at the end of Q1.

Can it be excluded or deferred? No. Transformers are mandatory and cannot be excluded or substituted. Projects without secured transformer delivery commitments face direct commissioning risk.

JEF Analyst View (Julien Dumoulin-Smith): Seeing significant acceleration in order activity across the sector, many suppliers saw 100%+ YoY order growth in 1Q26 as transformer equipment supply has emerged as a key area of project constraint. GE Vernova (GEV) recently supplemented its portfolio with consolidation of the Prolec JV and is only beginning to see an acceleration in data center-related orders (\$2.4bn in 1Q26 orders, more than all of FY25); likely to enhance domestic supply market share organically through FY26+. Forgent Power Solutions (FPS) is in the early stages of ramping electrical equipment supply (therein transformers) from roughly \$1.5Bn run-rate currently to \$5Bn by the end of the decade, with volume leverage offering surplus incremental margins relative to peers. Significant brick and mortar capital expenditures for FPS conclude in mid-2026, with further ability to ramp to be enabled by growing headcount. FPS presents higher beta pure-play exposure while GEV's standard product market share gain opportunity still is not fully reflected in forward estimates.

# Backup Generators

# 2026E Supply Ceiling: 15.2 GW

Key Suppliers: Cummins (CMI), Caterpillar (CAT), Generac (GNRC), Rolls-Royce Power Systems (RR-GB)

What is it? Backup generators provide on-site power during grid outages and must meet facility uptime and redundancy standards. AI data centers often require larger, N+1 or 2N redundant generator configurations that consume more equipment per MW than legacy deployments.

Why is it required? Data centers require near-continuous power availability. Generators ensure resilience against grid interruptions and are mandatory for hyperscalers, colocation providers, and AI inference workloads that cannot tolerate unplanned downtime.

How constrained is it today? Backup generators are the least binding constraint in our model, with a 2026E ceiling of 15.2 GW. Unlike most other constraints, backup generators are more fungible, as in certain use cases, they can either be substituted with other solutions or ignored entirely. However, Tier III data center capacity requires uptime levels that can only be guaranteed with backup generators, and lead times have meaningfully extended from pre-AI norms as data center demand increasingly competes with hospitals, defense, and broader infrastructure for the same equipment. Supplier results confirm the demand acceleration: CMI's Power Systems segment posted record Q1 2026 revenue of \$1.96B (+19% YoY), led by data center backup power demand, and raised FY2026 revenue growth guidance to 8-11% (from 3-8%). CAT reported record backlog of \$63B (+79% YoY), with Power Generation sales up 48% driven by large gensets and turbines for data center applications. Large reciprocating engine backlog is up more than 3.5x since January 2024, and CAT is expanding large engine capacity to nearly 3x 2024 levels to meet demand. GNRC is the emerging entrant in the category, with C&I segment sales of \$510M (+28% YoY) and data center backlog exceeding \$700M, more than doubling YoY. GNRC received a non-binding \$600M notice to proceed from a hyperscale customer for 2027 deliveries and is in final stages of vendor approval with two hyperscale accounts.

Can it be excluded or deferred? In limited edge cases, backup generators have been excluded from early-stage AI training buildouts with less strict uptime requirements. However, any facility supporting cloud, inference, or colocation workloads requires full redundancy. Deferral is operationally viable only for pure training campuses.

JEF Analyst View (Stephen Volkmann): Backup generator capacity continues to grow through newer entrants such as Generac (via Weichai) as well as legacy incumbents like CAT/CMI are also rapidly expanding capacity. Generac recently secured its first hyperscaler contract in early June, a key milestone, although previewed by management. CAT recently announced an additional \~15GW capacity increase for large reciprocating engines, effectively tripling capacity vs 2024, while CMI announced a doubling of large generator production capacity. While there is certainly the risk for overcapacity LT, given the high demand for reciprocating engines for both prime and backup use cases, we still see demand exceeding supply through 2030. Our preferred way to play backup generators is through CAT, our top machinery pick.

# What About the NIMBYs?

Data center opposition is accelerating (see here & here). Opposition to AI data centers now exceeds that to nuclear plants: 71% of Americans oppose local data center construction vs. 53% for nuclear according to Gallup's March '26 survey. The Data Center Watch report found opposition affected 48 projects valued \~\$156bn in H2 of 2025–\$66bn blocked and \$90bn delayed—pushing the cumulative total since 2023 past \$220bn. Over 300 bills were filed across 30+ states in the first six weeks of 2026 (vs. 267 in all of 2025), including rate protections shielding residents from incremental electricity costs (AL, FL, OK) according to MultiState. Fifteen state-level data center moratorium bills have been introduced; though largely symbolic, they signal deteriorating sentiment.

# Opposition is driven by five factors, with the figures referring to the percentage who mentioned the specific concerns (Gallup, April 2026):

- Resource strain (50%) is the dominant concern. Water usage and energy/grid constraints are each cited by 18% of opponents.
- Quality-of-life concerns (22%) center on the community footprint of data centers, including housing and property value impacts (9%), land-use conflicts with residents who prefer alternative development (9%), and increased traffic and construction disruption (5%).
- Cost concerns (20%) are anchored by fears of higher utility bills. A PowerLines study finds that utilities are seeking \~\$31bn in rate hikes to fund grid expansion, and Virginia's \$1.6bn/yr DC tax exemption has become the central fault line in a budget standoff. Although there is limited long term evidence linking DCs to power price increases (see here), YoY increases in '25 have highlighted to policymakers and consumers the potential impact of poorly managed load growth.
- Pollution (16%) is driven primarily by noise complaints (9%), with persistent industrial noise that residents report hearing over half a mile away.
- AI-related concerns (14%) round out the list, with 12% specifically citing fears of AI replacing human workers. More broadly, 80% of Americans express concern about AI (see here), making this the smallest discrete driver but a powerful amplifier of opposition sentiment.

In the near-term, we anticipate political backlash will accelerate around and beyond November 2026. National support for data center construction collapsed from 65% to 36% in just thirteen months (Change Research, April 2026). As we highlighted here, some candidates are running on anti-data center platforms: Michigan's Abdul El-Sayed released a "Terms of Engagement" platform which sets eight conditions for developers and utilities (e.g., zero rate hikes, energy reliability); Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez introduced a federal moratorium act; Hawley proposed parallel legislation. In 2025, candidates in Virginia, New Jersey, and Georgia ran on platforms that explicitly targeted data center growth and promised lower power bills and won. As opposition becomes a midterm wedge issue, investor attention may shift to supply-side bottlenecks in AI deployment, with market noise possible as headline risk intensifies through the fall (see here & here).

In the medium-term, companies and policymakers are likely to adjust, stabilizing the regulatory environment. Renewable deployment in the U.S. offers a lens on how NIMBYism may evolve. Wind, and to a lesser extent solar, has faced delays and some cancellations, but growth has not stalled. In the near term, more efficient data center buildouts (e.g., better grid utilization and demand flexibility), alongside redesigned utility tariffs, are likely key solutions (see here). Some have also pointed to orbital data centers (see here). On policy, the Trump administration's Ratepayer Protection Pledge, signed by seven major tech firms, commits them to "build, bring, or buy" their own power, though it is nonbinding. Community Benefit Agreements are increasingly required, and states like Illinois could mandate that hyperscale facilities fund renewable energy. Together, these steps suggest a regulatory equilibrium is forming that may slow buildouts but improve clarity for long-term capital deployment.

# Australia (Roger Samuel)

As power constraints in the US become increasingly acute, Australia is emerging as a compelling alternative for data centre capacity, underpinned by its stable geopolitical standing, abundant land, and access to renewable energy. In recent months, major operators have signed deals in the hundreds of MWs (CDC 555 MW, NextDC 250 MW), a scale that was virtually unheard of in the Australian market just 12 months ago. Anthropic, for example, has recently flagged requirements for 300–500 MW. Former bitcoin miners are also seeking to expand their data centre operations, drawn to states such as Western Australia and South Australia where land availability and renewable energy resources are particularly favourable.

# Asia (Edison Lee)

AI-driven demand surge signals an inflection point in China's IDC D-S dynamics. VNET's mgt witnessed an unprecedented wave of GW-scale orders in 1H26 from two leading Chinese CSPs, vs the historical norm of 100-300MW per order. With 2GW+ of IDC orders have been issued YTD, mgt estimates the 2026 total wholesale demand in China at \~4GW and VNET's share likely to reach 15-20% (vs 10% last year). With surge demand and supply constrained by gov efforts, ASPs are stabilizing and project economics are recovering after several years of glut in China.

Ulanqab (Inner Mongolia) has emerged as a highly attractive location for Chinese hyperscaler AI workloads, given 1) it is a key data center hub in China's "East Data West Compute" node plan, 2) its proximity to Beijing and Hebei enables low latency services to the Greater Beijing area for both inference and other latency-sensitive cloud services, 3) Ulanqab is accessible by high-speed rail in one hour from Beijing, which is important for AI players as they need to send engineers to the IDCs regularly to do adjustments, and 4) Ulanqab has plenty of low-cost and green power (it has 50% of China's wind resources). VNET still has 700MW of power reserves in Ulanqab, and its excellent gov relationship there would likely allow it to win more resources there in the next 12 months. Only a very few IDC peers could compete with VNET in Ulanqab at this magnitude.

## Chemicals | North America
# China PE Net Imports Likely Critical for US Contract Prices

We view the shape of China's net imports of polyethylene as a critical KPI to the trajectory of US PE contract prices. The starting point for this view is the reduction of China's net imports during April (as the Straight of Hormuz remained closed), which added 9% to ex-China global supply, partially offsetting the 15% reduction to global supply caused by the closure of the SOH. In particular, China reduced its net import position from \~1.1m MT per month pre-conflict to \~180K MT in April. It did this by: i) reducing imports by \~500K mt (approximately \~40% less from the US in particular); and ii) increasing exports by \~400K MT (most of which we believe went to Southeast Asia to offset the lost exports from the Middle East). The overall \~900kMT reduction of China's net import position drove China's net import reliance to \~5% in April vs. \~31% in 2025 (see Exhibit 1).

Naturally, this unanticipated behavior by China made the export market less tight than feared in April (with a carry-over impact into May likely, in our view) but there remains debate about how long such behavior can persist. In particular, we believe that China likely held far more excess inventory (pre-conflict) than understood. Discussions with consultants lead us to believe that \~1mm MT of excess inventory was built by Chinese traders in the 6-12 months prior to the Iran conflict, increasing the country's excess inventory position to \~2.3-3.5mm MT, which is equivalent to \~30 days of domestic demand. We expect to learn China's net imports for May in late June/early July.

Our scenario analysis suggests that the excess inventory may thin out by June/July. Should China then revert to its pre-conflict net import purchasing, we believe that this could lead to incremental demand for US exports of \~250K mt per month (12% of US annual supply). Such an outcome would likely help move US contract prices higher, all else equal, while the lack thereof would likely accelerate US contract price reversion to a lower mean. Notably, Chinese polyolefin inventory data from both Sinopec and CNPC demonstrates our expected draw down trend, with inventory now \~19% below recent highs and back to levels comparable with 2024/2025. Note that the inventory buildup through April is also atypical seasonally (with April 2024/2025 highs remaining -13%/-19% below February 2024/2025 levels), though 2026 YTD polyolefin inventories have fallen to comparable levels to 2024/2025 (-1% below the average) following the 19% (170kMT) draw down since April's peak.

## As it stands right now, the US market is largely playing out as we expected (see:

Chemicals: Polyethylene S&D Update (21 Apr 2026)) with spot prices in a downward trend following substantial March and April contract increases (+10cpp/+30cpp) and a flat May settlement. Looking ahead to June's contract negotiations, Dow and LyondellBasell have nominated 20cpp and 10cpp increases, respectively. At present, we model another flat settlement. Importantly, market commentary indicates that exports have lagged expectations, driving an expected producer inventory increase to \~46 days in May from 42.5 days in April and 44.0 days in February.

Our estimate for 250K mt of potential incremental US exports is derived by dividing Chinese imports ratably by import share (excluding historical Middle Eastern volumes, which are assumed to still be constrained). This uplift would represent \~21% upside to US export volumes, likely maximizing US export volumes (consultants have estimated a \~17% import increase over 2025 levels is the maximum increase given current infrastructure limitations). Considering effective operating rates are already near-maximum levels

(\~98.8% effective operating rates estimated for May), we would expect US inventories to draw down up to \~8 days through August, potentially driving the monthly contract settlement higher.

On the other hand, US export demand fell short of expectations in both April and May, which - if continued – could pull forward expected PE contract declines (e.g., a -5cpp settlement in June or incremental downside to the -5cpp we already expect for July).

Chinese polyethylene price increases have significantly lagged other regions since the onset of the Iran conflict. Current Chinese prices are 39pp below North American prices (versus \~14cpp pre-conflict and 13.9cpp on average over the past three years) which has naturally hurt demand for higher priced US exports. We note that US spot price trends implied \~6cpp of downside in May to the US contract price but the contract settled flat contract nonetheless.

We believe that at the onset of the Iran conflict, Chinese producers and traders had \~2.3-3.5mn MT of excess inventories. On the low end, 2.3mn MT represents \~15 excess days of total Chinese producer inventories and \~1000kMT excess inventories in the hands of traders (which consultants note had built up inventories in the 6-12 months ahead of the conflict due to low market prices). On the high end, 3.5mn MT represents 30 excess days of total Chinese producer inventories and the same \~1000kMt excess trader inventories. The low end assumes that producer inventories start drawing down in May (with the April net-import reduction largely having been absorbed by trader inventories), and that the low net import position is only sustainable through early June.

- Should the leaner inventory scenario play out and China increase its imports (see Exhibit 8), we would expect the North American market to tighten during the second half of June as global trade flows adjust to facilitate China's ramp back towards a \~1.1mn MT/month net import position.
- Should the fuller inventory scenario play out and China's imports remain restrained (Exhibit 9), we believe the North American market would not tighten until late July/early August as global trade flows adjust.
- In either scenario, we would expect China's \~5% net imports in April to revert to the \~mid-20%s for the full year and back >30% in 2027 (Exhibit 10).

We do not believe that freight rates will be the primary driver of Chinese import normalization as the recent ramp up of East-to-West freight rates remains fairly moderate in context (i.e., up 4.7cpp on average in May versus February – see Exhibit 11). Logistical issues - including container scarcity and port congestion - may have

hampered Chinese PE exports somewhat, driving demand upside to the North American market in the process. That said, despite the 4.7cpp increase in freight rates, rates are still well below recent peaks (i.e., \~7.9cpp below summer of 2024).

The aforementioned inventory draw down in China likely limits the probability that downside price risk will materialize, though we note risks remain from (i) recovery of upstream capacities, (ii) demand destruction and (iii) market perception of the direction of crude (and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz). We address the former two, as we see incremental 2026 downside from these factors (relative to our forecasts) as somewhat limited:

Sooner-than-expected recovery of upstream capacities. Thus far, Gail in India, CSPC in China, Formosa in Taiwan and PT Chandra Asri in Indonesia have restarted PE capacity that was under conflict-related force majeure. These restarts likely represent at least 3.5mn MT of conflict-related outages that have come back to market (\~2.2% of global capacity). While we see risk from supply restarts, we note two factors that may draw out associated impact including, (a) upstream inventories purchased pre-conflict were still reportedly being used around the end of April and were expected to wind down as of mid-May. The draw down of upstream inventories leaves open the possibility that further operating rate reductions, constraints and outages remain ahead of us; and (b) earlier comments indicated that logistical issues in energy markets post conflict may take 3-4 quarters to clear up after the Strait of Hormuz reopens. Said another way, the restart of capacities from force majeure may not equate to a prompt resumption of normal operating conditions, as utilization rates may remain low well into 2027 even in the scenario that the conflict resolves quickly.

Demand deferral may be sufficient for China not to resume greater net imports near-term. We do not see this as likely as (a) the net import reduction is well in excess of demand that is likely to be deferred (i.e., would imply up to 23% demand loss) and (b) consultants indicate that the risk within the Chinese economy is to re-exported finished goods, which can be made up for through production of similar goods elsewhere (equating to the same global PE demand and negating any potential effect to utilization rates).

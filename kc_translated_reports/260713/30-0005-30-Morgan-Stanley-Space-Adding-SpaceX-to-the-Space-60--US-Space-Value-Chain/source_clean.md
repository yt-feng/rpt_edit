## Raw Materials & Mining

## Overview

All space hardware begins in the ground. A single satellite can use dozens of specialty metals across structural, power, thermal, and communication systems. While demand is modest in the context of total global metal demand, spacecraft rely on highly specialized materials and alloys engineered to withstand extreme heat, stress, and radiation - often at costs reaching tens of thousands of dollars per tonne or more. For many of these metals, production is concentrated in a single country, operationally complex, environmentally or toxicologically challenging, and/or fundamentally constrained by natural scarcity.

US import reliance on critical minerals remains near a 30-year high, with China alone accounting for roughly \~60% in the case of rare earth mine production to over 80-90% or more in the case of minerals such as tungsten, gallium, and germanium (according to USGS). Disruptions in these inputs can delay manufacturing timelines across the value chain.

## Specialty Materials & Alloys

## Overview

Spacecraft require materials that can tolerate the extreme temperature swings of deep space and high mechanical stress. Rocket engines also require extremely tight tolerances, often measured in microns, to manage combustion environments with temperatures above 3,000°C. Many of these applications use highly engineered superalloys (defined as alloys that can operate at a high % of their melting point, often 70% or more) which are typically nickel or cobalt-based and alloyed with various refractory metals (or metals with extremely high heat resistance and hardness) such as titanium, tungsten, molybdenum, niobium, or rhenium. Notable superalloys used in space applications include:

\- Inconel: A nickel-based superalloy that typically contains \~50–70% nickel, 17–21% chromium, and smaller amounts of iron, niobium, molybdenum, titanium, and aluminum. Commonly used grades such as Inconel 718 maintain strength and creep resistance at temperatures up to about 700°C and resist oxidation in high-pressure environments. Rocket manufacturers use it in turbopumps, combustion chambers, and structural engine components.

\- Waspaloy: A nickel-based superalloy that includes chromium, cobalt, molybdenum, aluminum, and titanium, with nickel as the base element. It is designed for high creep resistance and maintains strength at temperatures up to about 870°C to 980°C. Aerospace suppliers use it in turbine disks and other high-stress rotating components in propulsion systems.

\- C-103: A niobium-based refractory alloy composed of roughly 89% niobium, 10% hafnium, and 1% titanium. It retains strength at temperatures above 1,300°C and can operate at even higher temperatures in vacuum or with protective coatings. Engineers use it in rocket engine nozzles, thrusters, and high-temperature structures.

Not all space materials are metals. Engineers also heavily use fiber-reinforced composites, commonly carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP), to reduce weight while maintaining strength and stiffness. These materials combine carbon fibers with polymer resins such as epoxy, while ceramic matrix composites support higher temperature applications such as thermal protection and propulsion components. Aerospace manufacturers use composites in satellite structures, payload supports, and launch vehicle components, where weight reduction can improve payload capacity and reduce launch costs. Numerous other composites are used on spacecraft for thermal insulation, radiation shielding, and micrometeoroid and debris protection.

# Propulsion & Fuels

## Overview

Both launch and in-orbit movement rely on engineered propellants and industrial gases. A single launch can require hundreds of tons of propellant with propellant often representing 85-90%+ of total launch mass. Launch vehicles have historically used a mix of refined kerosene ('RP-1') as fuel combined with cryogenically cooled liquid oxygen (to enable combustion in the vacuum of space). However, multiple next-gen reusable launch vehicles are now transitioning from kerosene to methane given its cleaner combustion (less soot) which simplifies maintenance between flights. Notable propellant gases used in space include:

## - Launch Propellant:

\- Refined Kerosene (RP-1): A highly refined hydrocarbon fuel with high energy density, launch providers have traditionally used RP-1 as the primary propellant in first-stage engines. It is not a cryogenic fuel and remains liquid at ambient temperatures, making it relatively easier to handle and requiring smaller tank sizes compared to alternatives.

\- Liquid Hydrogen (LH2): A cryogenic fuel stored near -253°C, LH2 offers high specific impulse (thrust produced per unit of propellant consumed) of \~450 seconds in vacuum, which supports upper-stage efficiency and deep space missions.

\- Liquid Methane (LCH4): A cryogenic fuel stored near -162°C, methane burns cleaner and more efficient than RP-1 (specific impulse of \~360-370 seconds vs. \~300-350 for RP-1) and can reduce carbon buildup (soot) in engines, which may simplify refurbishment in reusable systems.

## - Oxidizer:

• Liquid Oxygen (LOX): A cryogenic oxidizer stored at about $-183^{\circ}$ C, LOX supplies oxygen for combustion and pairs with fuels such as RP-1, liquid hydrogen, and methane across many launch systems. Engines that use RP-1 + LOX are referred to as 'kerolox' engines, 'hydrolox' for liquid hydrogen + LOX, and 'methalox' for liquid methane + LOX.

## • In-Space Maneuvering:

\- Xenon (Xe): An inert noble gas used in electric propulsion, xenon enables high-efficiency thrust with specific impulse often above 1,500-3,000 seconds in ion and Hall-effect (using an electric field to accelerate ionized propellant at high exhaust velocities) thrusters for station-keeping and orbit adjustments.

• Krypton (Kr): A noble gas used as an alternative to xenon in electric propulsion, krypton offers lower cost but delivers lower efficiency and thrust, which can require longer maneuver times.

\- Argon (Ar): A widely available noble gas, argon has been tested in electric propulsion systems due to its low cost, though lower atomic mass reduces efficiency compared to xenon and krypton.

• Hydrazine (N2H4): A storable monopropellant that decomposes over a catalyst bed to produce thrust, hydrazine is widely used in satellite thrusters and reaction control systems despite handling and toxicity constraints.

# Electronics & Semiconductors

## Overview

Satellites rely on radiation-hardened chips and sensors to operate in space. Standard commercial chips can fail quickly due to radiation exposure, while rad-hard components can operate for years in orbit depending on mission profile and shielding. These systems traditionally manage communication, navigation, and onboard processing. Engineers use several radiation hardening methods, including silicon-on-insulator designs, physical shielding, error detection and correction, and redundancy at the circuit and system level. Materials also play a role, with wide bandgap semiconductors such as gallium nitride (GaN) offering higher breakdown voltage and improved power efficiency compared to silicon, along with strong resistance to certain radiation effects. GaN devices are widely used in RF power amplifiers and high-frequency communication payloads on satellites.

\- Why is there so much radiation in space? Space contains higher radiation levels because while Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field block most charged particles, satellites operate outside much of that protection. Earth's magnetic field traps charged particles from the Sun and cosmic sources in two main regions called the Van Allen radiation belts, with an inner belt at about 1,000 to 12,000 kilometers dominated by high-energy protons and an outer belt extending to roughly 60,000 kilometers dominated by high-energy electrons. Alpha radiation consists of heavy helium nuclei that stop quickly, beta radiation consists of fast electrons that travel further, and gamma radiation consists of high-energy photons that can penetrate most materials and require dense shielding.

Satellite communications rely primarily on radio frequency (RF) systems for both space-to-ground links and inter-satellite links. These systems operate across spectrum bands such as L, S, X, Ku, and Ka. RF links are reliable and resilient to weather, which supports consistent communication with ground stations. However, spectrum is limited and data rates decline with distance, as signal strength decreases with the square of the distance due to free-space path loss (see: inverse square law & space loss).

\- For space-to-ground communication, RF signals must pass through the atmosphere, which introduces attenuation, particularly at higher frequencies such as Ku- or Ka-band. Higher frequency systems can support higher data rates but require more precise pointing and experience greater atmospheric loss. Lower frequency bands such as L and S are more robust to atmospheric effects but support lower data throughput. These lower bands are commonly used for direct-to-device and mobile satellite services due to better signal penetration and link reliability. Higher frequency bands such as Ku- and Ka-band are typically used for broadband satellite applications, where larger antennas and higher power enable greater data throughput. Larger antennas can improve link performance but add mass and complexity.

\- For inter-satellite links, RF enables direct crosslinks between spacecraft without atmospheric interference. These links support data relay and networked constellations but remain constrained by available bandwidth and onboard power.

Optical, or laser, communications are emerging for both inter-satellite and space-to-ground links. In space, laser inter-satellite links can achieve high data rates with relatively low power and no atmospheric interference. NASA states that optical systems can deliver data rates about 10 to 100 times higher than current RF systems. For space-to-ground communication, laser systems aim to increase throughput but face additional challenges. Signals can be disrupted by clouds and atmospheric turbulence, and systems require precise pointing to maintain alignment. As a result, operators are testing hybrid architectures that use RF for reliable ground communication and optical links for high-capacity data transfer.

# Components & Subsystems

## Overview

Space systems depend on thousands of individual parts such as connectors, valves, bearings, actuators, and wiring harnesses. Each component must meet strict reliability and safety standards, often with qualification cycles that can last several years and include vibration, thermal vacuum, and radiation testing. Failure of a single component can compromise an entire mission, particularly in systems with limited redundancy.

Suppliers in this category focus on precision manufacturing and materials performance, with tolerances often measured in microns for fluid and mechanical systems. Many components must operate across temperature ranges from below -100°C to over 150°C and withstand launch loads exceeding several g-forces during ascent. Long qualification timelines and high switching costs can create durable supplier relationships with aerospace and defense customers.

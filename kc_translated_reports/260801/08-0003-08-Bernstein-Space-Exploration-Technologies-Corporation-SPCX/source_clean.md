# SpaceX: The debates - Investor views and controversies as we head toward the first earnings report

Since SpaceX's IPO on 12 June 2026, we have been on the road speaking with investors about the stock's long-term opportunity, following our recent initiation, "Space Exploration Technologies Corporation: Doing Time in the Universal Mind - Initiate at Outperform; Target Price \$239". At the same time, we have seen high volatility in the stock, with a sharp decline since its peak after the IPO. Here, we discuss the questions raised by investors regarding the fundamentals for the stock - fundamentals centered on the ability of SpaceX to launch thousands of satellites into orbit to form a data center constellation.

There are four topics that we see as central to the success of the SpaceX valuation. Most important is achieving rapid reuse of Starship. The other three are about having sufficient semiconductor capacity, moving through regulatory processes, and that the need for compute capacity to remain high. Three topics that are legitimate areas of debate, but less relevant to the value proposition of SpaceX are China as a competitive threat, attractiveness of global Starlink broadband, and the feasibility of the company's direct to device mobile plan. On this last topic, we also have skepticism regarding the potential of mobile. Lastly, we highlight four topics where we have seen pushback on the technical feasibility of orbital data centers - none of which we see as material issues. Those are about the ability to cool data center satellites, the source of solar energy, avoiding damage from micro-meteorite strikes, and latency related to space-based data centers.

SpaceX will report its first set of results on August 4 $^{th}$ after the market close. We believe the quarterly results should not matter. What will be important is the level of confidence projected by management regarding the company's growth path. Investors should look beyond short term stock movements as we view the case for a multi-trillion dollar valuation is about "if" not "when", for orbital data center plans.

## DETAILS

Following our initiation investors have challenged us on almost every aspect of the story – from the feasibility of launching from multiple sites across the US, to the practicality of cooling orbital data centers. In this note, we distill 10 key insights from those client conversation.

## THE TOPICS THAT MATTER MOST - MAKE OR BREAK ON THE GROWTH MODEL

## 1. Reusability of Starship rockets - The most critical issue - Will the heat shield hold up?

We see the key issue for the SpaceX value proposition as reuse of the Starship rocket. This topic has been an important issue for many investors, as we believe it should be. Our model has roughly 3,600 Starship launches in 2031 (the company's expectations are much higher), which can only be done with a fully reusable rocket (both stages). So far, the company's expectations has landed Starship V2 boosters on the launchpad, but not yet V3 boosters. Launch 13, which was completed on July $20^{\text{th}}$ , was largely successful, although some engines failed to light on the booster ahead of splashdown. Our assumption is that booster landings with reuse will be achieved as it was with the V2 rockets.

The important success on Launch 13 was the successful recovery of the Starship itself (second stage) with the heat shield much more intact than prior launches. We await the company's assessment of the damage to the Starship heat shield. If there is minimal damage, then the company is moving close to reuse. After Launch 13, Elon Musk said that SpaceX may attempt to do a "chopsticks" catch of the second stage on Launch 14. Demonstrating catches of the booster (Super Heavy) and then the second stage (Starship) is the first step toward the critical achievement of rapid reuse. So far, we see the progress as good. But, there is a long way to go. Reaching our launch goal will still require at least daily launches. The frequency of launches will also be tied to the number of launchpads. Today there are two, with the addition of two more coming. The company is in negotiations with state governments to determine where the next five or six will be located.

## 2. Semiconductor availability - Will this limit growth?

The required semiconductor capacity for SpaceX's orbital data center buildout has been another area of investor pushback. We view this as a valid concern, but still see a credible path for SpaceX to secure sufficient semiconductor supply over time.

Under the assumption that a data center satellite will use a similar architecture, we can estimate how much semiconductor manufacturing capacity will be needed by the company. And the numbers are not small. Our model calls for \~20 GW of incremental orbital capacity to be deployed in 2031, which would require close to 170K satellites to be launched assuming \~120 kW per satellite (the current spec of the AI1), requiring \~2.8 million wafers of annual production capacity. At a fab size of \~50,000 wafer starts per month this would translate to \~5 dedicated semiconductor fabs solely serving SpaceX, probably costing somewhere north of \~\$160B to put in place over the next several years (Exhibit 1). This is a large number, though not necessarily impossible (especially as some will still likely be third-party) and compares reasonably favorably with current company Terafab investment plans (\~\$119B forecast as of this writing) though the timeframe to bring this amount of capacity online is ambitious especially as SpaceX has not done this before. If they cannot deliver on time it will put more pressure on them to secure large amounts of incremental capacity from existing foundry and memory players.

## 3. Will regulatory considerations be a problem?

We have heard a number of investors express skepticism about being able to accelerate the SpaceX vision and remain in compliance with regulatory authorities. The FAA must approve the flight path for each Starship launch. So far, the FAA has not cleared SpaceX for an orbital Starship flight. The hope is that Launch 14 may be able to go orbital. But, the FAA performs environmental assessments that consider the impact on airline fight paths, as well as potential environmental damage and

risks to wildlife, including sea life. The current Environmental Assessment has many strict considerations, with August 3 $^{rd}$ as the deadline for public input. But, this week the FAA announced that it would relax environmental restrictions because of the importance of enabling a higher cadence of space launches. The assessment related to air traffic would remain. While changes made should enable more Starship launches, it is not yet clear how the regulatory path will go when thousands of launches are under consideration and a potentially less supportive administration could be in place.

The FCC must approve satellite communications programs. A fast track process was recently approved. But, this will not apply the very large constellations proposed by SpaceX.. Recent filings include proposals for a \~100,000-satellite Starlink V3 constellation and, separately, a potential large-scale AI data center constellation (up to \~1 million units), neither of which has yet received full regulatory approval. Instead, SpaceX is also looking at incremental steps to build up Starlink's capacity.

There are many other regulatory questions that will need to be resolved. Each country will have its own regulatory environment for telecommunications. The issue of data sovereignty is another important topic. Many countries have rules that prevent data from being stored in other countries. But, the argument for orbital data centers is that data stored in space is not resident in any country and consequently has no issues regarding data sovereignty. But, it is not yet clear exactly how space as a location will ultimately be treated.

## 4. Volume of compute needed - Is AI compute a bubble?

The need for orbital data centers starts with the assumption that there is a massive need for compute capability. Elon Musk has suggested that SpaceX would eventually be launching 1 terawatt of compute into space each year. The plans we are working with now are far more modest. But, making the orbital data center program a success still depends on having a huge volume of compute power. To this day, we have never seen the world have excess compute. Possibly, the rush to add capacity to fuel AI models is too rapid. But, even if that is the case, there should still be value in having a lower cost solution for the compute capacity that is needed, relative to terrestrial capacity.

## LEGITIMATE QUESTIONS - ALTHOUGH THEY DO NOT CHANGE THE ULTIMATE VALUE PROPOSITION

## 5. Rising Chinese Competition

In our discussions with SpaceX management, China was identified as the most serious competitor. The country has tremendous resources that it is willing to use for achieving leadership in space. Nevertheless, China is still far behind SpaceX.

On July $10^{\text{th}}$ , China successfully landed the first-stage booster from a Long March 10B rocket launch. Since then, a lot of investors have questioned us around this rising competition and this remake of the Space race. We see China as the lead competitor for SpaceX. This launch and recovery comes roughly six months earlier than we expected. China is moving aggressively to place a research station on the moon and create large LEO satellite constellations. In December, China filed with the ITU to place more than 200,000 satellites in low earth orbit. Despite China's success in landing its Long March 10B, it is far behind SpaceX. This was the first successful landing of a large launch vehicle booster. It has yet to do the reuse of a booster. SpaceX has been relaunching boosters for nearly ten years with its Falcon 9 rocket. 165 launches were done last year, which also required a robust production line for second stages. To reach a high volume of launch, China will need to demonstrate relaunch capabilities and move to high rate production. Most importantly, Long March 10 is only first stage reuse, whereas Starship is intended to be full reuse (not yet demonstrated).

A key part of the Long March program is landing humans on the moon (official target 2030), leading to an eventual lunar research station. SpaceX is a major part of NASA's space program, which has a goal of a manned moon landing by the end of 2028. There is an emerging space race here, which will likely increase U.S. government support for SpaceX (along with others, such as Blue Origin and Boeing).

As discussed in our SpaceX initiation, we see no real competition in the near-term for SpaceX on either launch capability or satellite operations. But, given China's massive resources and long-standing goals, China must be considered a serious competitor over the long-term. It still appears to us that China lags SpaceX by something on the order of ten years, assuming SpaceX is successful in achieving its goals.

## 6. Starlink broadband penetration - Is there an issue here?

We are expecting rapid growth in Starlink's broadband businesses for consumer, enterprise, and government. The main source of pushback here has been among telecom-oriented investors who see terrestrial fiber as a better option for all but rural users. The most powerful application will be with users in locations that are not served by cable or fiber (where the Starlink share will likely be $100\%$ ), But, we expect there will be much broader adoption by suburban users. Even though there is more latency with Starlink (roughly 23ms round trip), this is not a problem even for most gaming applications. Surveys show that Starlink has the highest customer satisfaction rating of any consumer broadband service. In addition, Starlink has the advantage of having an inherently global network so that it can add subscribers in any country with essentially zero additional cost. This gives Starlink

exceptional flexibility in pricing. With its current 9,600 satellite constellation and planned capacity expansion by more than a factor of ten, no other provider is remotely close. Our forecast for global subscribers and ARPU is shown in Exhibit 3.

Starlink also has the advantage of supporting an enterprise and government customer base, which further spreads cost. The U.S. government invests in the defense version of Starlink (Starshield), while many other countries are adopting Starlink for monitor of remote regions, emergency services, and border control (e.g., UK, Australia, Ukraine, Japan, Netherlands, Poland). On the enterprise side, Starlink offers a global reach that other services lack, in addition to mobile applications where no competitor comes close to the performance (e.g., maritime, aviation). For broadband services, we expect high growth from this business that is already highly profitable.

Risks to US telecom providers from Starlink were highlighted by Bernstein's US Telecom team: US Telecom: Adjusting price targets reflecting Starlink risks (which resulted in a material PT cut in response to the Starlink threat).

## 7. Can direct-to-device work for Starlink? The path is not clear

An area where we have heard strong pushback on SpaceX plans is for direct-to-device mobile service. On this one, we agree. Our outlook for the D2D business is far more conservative than what SpaceX has described. In terms of SpaceX as a stock, however, success will not depend on this business. In the end, success will depend primarily on orbital data centers, where the issues at the beginning of this report are most relevant.

SpaceX currently has a relatively small network of V1 Mobile satellites that primarily provide deadzone coverage for mobile network operators (MNOs). The company's plans are to expand this network with its coming V2 satellites. These satellites should each have roughly 16x the capacity of the V1 satellites, with higher power. This new V2 mobile network should begin launches in 2027.

Many telecom-oriented investors see success here as highly unlikely due to the technical limitations of the satellite network and the need for a terrestrial capability to complement the satellite constellation. The company is targeting deals with MNOs to effectively become an MVNO (like Charter, Comcast have done). So far, there is strong resistance in the U.S., although it is very possible that one player (i.e., T-Mobile, AT&T, Verizon) might break and link up with Starlink. But, all this is uncertain. In the absence of a partner, SpaceX has said that it could build its own terrestrial network - something that we find daunting.

In any case, we agree with skeptical investors on this topic. It remains to be seen how this might play out. In the end, however, we believe that success in this business is tangential to the overall case for SpaceX.

## TECHNICAL ISSUES WE BELIEVE ARE RESOLVED - BUT, THESE QUESTIONS KEEP COMING UP

## 8..Cooling datacenters - This is not a problem!

Questions about pace-based data center cooling continue to come up in our investor discussions. First, it would be surprising if this issue had somehow been overlooked by the engineers at SpaceX. Nevertheless, we addressed this directly in our initiation report by explicitly modeling the relevant heat-transfer dynamics. In short, we conclude that cooling data center satellites in orbit is technically 100% viable.

Given that both senior analysts, before spending two decades in the securities industry, were Ph.D. physicists, we decided to do our own analysis. To assess feasibility, we analyzed the thermal environment of a representative data center satellite operating in sun-synchronous orbit, where continuous solar exposure enables stable power generation while dedicated radiators dissipate waste heat. Applying standard heat-transfer principles, we modeled the equilibrium between onboard power generation, absorbed heat, and thermal radiation. In the vacuum of space, heat rejection occurs via radiation rather than convection, making cooling capacity primarily a function of radiator surface area and operating temperature. Our analysis indicates that, with the inclusion of dedicated radiators, satellites supporting in excess of 100 kW of compute load can maintain acceptable thermal conditions while remaining within plausible launch constraints. While meaningful engineering and execution challenges remain, the governing physics do not represent a fundamental barrier. We therefore view in-orbit cooling of high-power AI data center satellites as feasible. A detailed discussion of the heat-transfer physics, assumptions, derivations, and sensitivity analyses is provided in the Appendix.

## 10. Latency of orbital datacenters - Not an issue for most inference applications

The latency surrounding orbital data centers has been put in question by investors multiple times. However, laser communications have already been demonstrated on Starlink, which is now a 9,600 satellite network. We do not expect latency to be an issue, as the delay from a 600-800km high orbit is 2 milliseconds This latency is less than that for users of many terrestrial data centers.

A related question has been about latency within a constellation across many orbital data centers. If one requires a large cohesive data center for training, the terrestrial option is likely better. The intent at SpaceX is to perform most training activity at its terrestrial sites (e.g., MACROHARD) as it does today. Inference would be done with orbital data centers. Because demand over time for compute is expected to move heavily toward inference, the orbital data centers should provide the lowest cost solution for this largest portion of demand. There are applications that may be better suited for collocation operations on earth, such as high-frequency trading. But, such applications are expected to be a small part of total demand.

## 11. Micrometeorite strikes and space debris - A real problem, but we believe it is understood

We have had investor questions about space debris and micrometeorites strikes hitting the large solar panels and radiators on data center satellites. This is a serious consideration for any orbiting spacecraft. It is an issue that satellite operators have dealt with for many years, including with the International Space Station. SpaceX has been addressing this on its Starlink satellites by sensing objects and maneuvering to avoid them. The same technical solution is to be adapted for the data center satellites. Our assumption is that even though the solar panels are much larger than those on Starlink satellites, our assumption is that this issue can effectively addressed, as it has been done on Starlink. The company is (and has been) well aware of this issue in space.

## APPENDIX - TUTORIAL FOR HEAT TRANSFER MATHEMATICS

We are going to try to provide a (reasonably easy-to-follow) tutorial in basic heat transfer for you in order to answer the cooling question.

First, some basics:

\- In basic thermodynamics heat flows from hot to cold.

\- Objects transfer heat through three primary mechanisms:

\- Conduction, where thermal energy is transferred via direct contact of a hot object to a cooler one through molecular collisions, without bulk movement of the materials themselves. Think of a pan heating up when placed directly on an electric stove for example.

\- Convection, where thermal energy is transferred through the bulk movement of fluids, where a cold fluid can remove heat from a hot object. Think of your car's radiator circulating coolant to keep your engine cool.

\- Thermal radiation, where energy is directly emitted from a hot object to its surroundings via electromagnetic waves. Think of putting your hand close to a hot light bulb and feeling the heat. Unlike the other two, radiation requires no medium to travel through, it is just as effective in a vacuum.

\- Objects in the vacuum of space lose energy only through radiation as there is no physical contact with anything and no fluid surrounding the object to remove heat.

\- An object emits energy via radiation proportional to the FOURTH power of its own temperature minus the $4^{\text{th}}$ (known as the Stephen-Boltzmann Law), or

$$
E = \sigma T ^ {4}
$$

which holds for a perfect blackbody.

• The proportionality constant is sigma, the Boltzmann constant, equal to $5.67 \times 10^{-8}$ W/m $^{2}$ /K $^{4}$ .

\- The net energy transfer from a radiating body is proportional to the fourth power of the temperature of the body (in K) minus the $4^{th}$ power of the temperature of the surroundings it is radiating to, or

$$
E _ {n e t} = \sigma \big (T ^ {4} - T _ {s u r r o u n d i n g s} ^ {4} \big)
$$

though in this case given the temperature of deep space is $\sim 3$ kelvin we will assume the temperature of the surroundings is effectively zero, suggesting we can use the Stephen-Boltzmann Law on its own.

\- “Perfect” energy emission is known as “blackbody” radiation, and perfectly follows the Stephen-Boltzmann law. Not all objects emit perfectly however. Objects can have an emissivity (epsilon) between zero and 1, in other words

$$
E = \varepsilon \sigma T ^ {4}
$$

\- An object cooler than its surroundings will experience a rise in temperature (and corresponding increase in emitted energy as temp goes up) until it is emitting as much energy as it is receiving. At this point it is in “equilibrium” and its temperature will stop rising.

\- At equilibrium all energies entering and leaving an object are in balance. We can use this state to calculate what we need to.

We are going to consider the orbital configuration shown in Exhibit 6. We assume a flat plate-like satellite bus for simplicity. In this case the satellite bus is oriented edge-on to the sun's rays; we will for simplicity assume that the area of the bus hence absorbs no solar energy, and that the edge of the bus that is exposed to solar radiation is too thin to capture much (hence we will ignore it). We will assume that the solar panels are oriented directly normal to the sun. This configuration would be representative of what is known as a sun-synchronous dawn/dusk orbit, where the satellite follows the day/night terminator and hence is always in the sun (Exhibit 7).

However, the sun is not the only source of energy impinging on the satellite. The earth itself emits, both via reflected sunlight (often up to a third of the solar energy hitting the planet) as well as through infrared emission. In this configuration there is no reflected sunlight, however the bottom of the satellite bus is exposed to earth-sourced IR radiation. The magnitude of this varies with time of day, seasons, position, and other things. However treating the earth as a perfect blackbody radiating at 15 degrees C would produce a flux of $\sim 390\mathrm{W / m2}$ , which we will use here.

Note that the solar cells do not turn all incident solar energy into power. But, the excess will be radiated away. We will assume the panels are at equilibrium and that this excess heat does not impinge on the satellite body (and hence will not have to be removed from it).

There are many other configurations that the satellite could be in. The sun could be shining directly down on it for example, or it could be positioned at an angle. The below configuration however likely represents a best-case scenario, and we will use there (the math is easier, and we are looking for proof on concept rather than rigorous analysis).

We now set up our energy balance. Remember that the satellite will have to emit all energy it receives, plus all energy produced on board. In other words,

$$
\text { Energy   Emitted } = \text { Energy   Absorbed } + \text { Energy   Produced }
$$

The energy absorbed is the sum of the solar energy hitting the satellite bus (zero in this configuration) plus the energy absorbed from the earth (or I\*A, where I = 390 W/m2, and A is the area of the satellite bus, i.e. what we are trying to solve for).

The energy produced by the panels is P (we will range this from 10 kW to 100 kW).

The energy emitted will come from both the top and the bottom of the satellite bus. Each side will radiate according to the Stephann-Boltzman law as

$$
E = 2 \varepsilon \sigma T ^ {4}
$$

(note the factor of 2 given the satellite can radiate from both sides of the bus). A is the area of the satellite that we are trying to find. Epsilon is the emissivity of the surface (we will take it as 0.85, not quite a perfect black body but close). Sigma is the Boltzman constant. And T is the final cooled temperature of the satellite (we ill range this from 70 degrees C to 100 degrees C, or more appropriately 343 to 373 degrees Kelvin).

Setting up our balance, we find:

$$
2 A \varepsilon \sigma T ^ {4} = P + I A
$$

Solving for A (the area needed for our satellite), we find:

$$
\mathsf {A} = \frac {P}{2 T ^ {4} \varepsilon \sigma - I}
$$

As am example, a 20 kW satellite in this configuration, cooled to 80 degrees C (353 K), would require an area of:

$$
A = \frac {2 0 , 0 0 0 W}{2 (3 5 3 K) ^ {4} (0 . 8 5) \left(5 . 6 7 x 1 0 ^ {- 8 W} / _ {m ^ {2} K ^ {4}}\right) - 3 9 0 W / _ {m ^ {2}}} = 1 8 m ^ {2}
$$

which does not seem unreasonable.

Ranging the math, we find that “launchable” satellites of $\sim$ 30m2 or less could probably be managed with up to $\sim$ 30 kW of onboard compute power supplied by solar cells (Exhibit 8). Note that this is on the order of what the existing Starlink satellites look like in terms of power usage, and they are cooled radiatively just fine without the need for additional on-board radiators.

However, it seems likely that the increased power needs of a datacenter satellite ( $>100$ kW) will not be satisfied by radiative cooling from the bus itself (the satellite would probably have to be too big to launch). But we are not limited by the satellite body itself as an emitter. Rather, we can add a radiator to the satellite to increase the area available to radiate energy away (and hence lower the required size of the satellite itself). In other words, we can fix the size of the satellite (at, say, 30 m2), and calculate

how much energy it could radiate on its own relative to the energy coming in. If it is not enough, we add a radiator, radiating away energy from both sides. The amount of the energy mismatch will tell us how big of a radiator we need. Note that we will orient the radiator perpendicular to the solar flux, and will assume it picks up no radiated IR from the earth (untrue in practice but easier to math).

In fact, this strategy enables us to get much more compute onto the satellite. The math suggests that we could keep a 100 kW satellite at a 30 m2 size by including a radiator 30-50 m2 in size depending on temperature, which seems doable (Exhibit 9).

We note that SpaceX has in fact already outlined the architecture of their first-generation AI1 datacenter satellite, with an average power of 120 kW and a peak power output of 150 kW. At 250 W/m2 for the solar panels this implies a panel area of \~600 m2 (2 wings of \~300m2 each) with a radiator of 110 m2 (Exhibit 10).

Assuming that 100% of the heat removal from this satellite is done via the radiator (i.e. ignoring radiative cooling from the satellite body which is small in comparison) our math suggests a radiator size requirement very consistent with what the company is proposing, giving us confidence that our calculations are at least in the ballpark.

Long story short, we believe cooling 100+ kW datacenter satellites in orbit via radiative cooling is completely viable.

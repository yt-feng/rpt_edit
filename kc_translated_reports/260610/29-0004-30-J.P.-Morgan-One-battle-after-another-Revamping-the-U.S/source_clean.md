## Arsenal at risk

In the early 1990s, it seemed the world had turned a corner. Francis Fukuyama's “End of History” captured the post-Cold War optimism that, with the collapse of the Soviet Union and the rapid spread of globalization, liberal democracy and market capitalism had emerged as the clear victors in the ideological struggle, making large-scale conflicts between major powers seem obsolete.

But history did not end; it just got messier. In the years that followed, the United States no longer confronted a single, easily defined adversary. Instead, it has had to contend with a diverse array of rivals, from advanced militaries equipped with large arsenals, to revisionist states prepared to endure hardship and escalate tensions, to regional players and proxies skilled in causing disruption. Today's competition spans everything from gray-zone tactics and cyber and electronic warfare to precision strikes, often all at the same time.

## Rising conflicts; rising costs

In just the last 20 years, the number of state-based conflicts has increased by 52%, rising from 44 in 2003 at the start of the U.S.-Iraq war to 67 in 2024 (Figure 1). Defense budgets have followed the same trajectory. Global military spending hit a record \$2.7 trillion in 2024, up 34% since 2010 and 16% since 2022, after Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The U.S. accounts for about 36% of global defense spending, while NATO and China make up roughly 18% and 12%, respectively (Figure 2).

The backbone of U.S. military power lies with its defense industrial base (DIB). It is central to national security, alliance leverage, and military innovation, having evolved through geopolitical shocks, war-driven mobilization, economic restructuring, technological revolutions, and policy choices; in today's era of intensified competition, rapid technology churn, and supply-chain risk, this history explains both current vulnerabilities and the urgency of reform.

## From mobilization to consolidation

World War II catalyzed the most ambitious industrial mobilization in American history. With existential threats at the forefront, the U.S. government activated a vast swath of commercial firms, spanning automotive, electronics, chemical, steel, textiles, and heavy manufacturing, for defense production at unparalleled speed. Engaging more than 800,000 firms (most originally civilian) and creating a highly diversified supplier base with strong resilience and substitutable sourcing, it demonstrated extraordinary surge capacity, exemplified by aircraft output rising from about 6,000 in 1939 to roughly 237,000 in 1944.

During the Cold War, with the Soviet Union as the key military and ideological opponent to the U.S., strategic demands drove greater technological specialization and vertical integration, shifting contracting away from broad commercial manufacturers toward specialized prime contractors; while this deepened expertise and advanced capabilities, it narrowed the supplier base and weakened surge capacity. In his 1961 farewell speech, President Eisenhower famously warned that the rise of the “military-industrial complex” would distort policy, entrench special interests, and erode system flexibility.

The end of the Cold War brought fiscal constraints and strategic uncertainty, leading to calls for efficiency and elimination of redundant capacity. In 1993, then-Deputy Secretary of Defense William Perry hosted an infamous gathering known as the “Last Supper,” urging major industry players to pursue mergers and contractions. Over the next decade, the U.S. defense sector experienced massive consolidation: the number of prime contractors fell from over fifty in 1991 to five by 2000 (Lockheed Martin, Boeing, Northrop Grumman, Raytheon, General Dynamics), with these primes controlling more than 60% of contract value and substantial attrition among Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers.

As diversity declined and concentration increased, procurement shifted toward a “flagship-based” doctrine to project overwhelming strength centered on large, technologically integrated platforms (e.g., advanced fighters, carriers, missile systems) that anchor combat capability and alliance interoperability. This approach concentrated resources on marquee programs (such as the F-35 and Ford-class carriers) and deepened integration across weapons, sensors, software, and networks. That architecture produced extraordinary performance in an era when combat advantage could be secured primarily through superior platforms, massed logistics, and uncontested command-and-control.

## New threats; new vulnerabilities

But what worked before may not work now. Today's DIB structure exposes four compounding vulnerabilities:

- Shallow production depth: Thinned ranks of tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers create bottlenecks that can stall or cripple programs.
- Slow acquisition: Major defense programs drag on for eight years or more, lagging behind rapid technological shifts.
- Industry concentration: A handful of dominant primes stifle competition, slow alternative production lines, and weaken system resilience.
- Supply-chain fragility: Global, multi-tiered supply chains are efficient but hard to de-risk—especially with dependencies on China for critical materials and components.

These vulnerabilities are strategically acute because modern warfare magnifies their impact. The U.S. now faces a diversified threat landscape: near-peer rivals like China and Russia, mid-tier states wielding cheap precision weapons, and non-state actors exploiting commercial tech. China’s Anti-Access/Area Denial (A2AD) and Military-Civil Fusion (MCF) strategies have fused commercial and defense sectors, scaled manufacturing, and overcome chokepoints. China’s shipbuilding and missile output dwarfs the U.S., and its supply chain reach—including semiconductors and rare earths—extends globally, even into U.S. defense programs. Other nations (Korea, Sweden, Israel) show the value of tight government-industry integration, supply chain resilience, and rapid adaptation.

## Allure and challenge of “assymetric warfare”

Recent conflicts in Ukraine and Iran have highlighted the need for mass-produced, attritable systems like drones, and the game-changing impact of real-time software and data integration. These developments compress the “kill chain,” enabling faster, more distributed military action and raising tough questions about whether the U.S.—despite its unmatched military power—can both deter and defeat adversaries in a new era of warfare.

Admittedly, these recent wars have increased the allure of asymmetric warfighting, and hence calls for an increased role of large scale, attritable military hardware manufacturing—which the U.S. currently lacks. But a longer term—and just as critical, if not more—lesson is that it has mostly exposed the way in which the digital age has fully permeated into the military realm and the increased role of data technology and software capabilities in shaping the outcomes of future conflicts, or the possibility of avoiding them.

This should not be mistaken for a view that calls for software to replace hardware. It is impossible. Hardware is ultimately always necessary to close what the military refers to as the “kill chain” (Figure 3). Moreover, this should not even be taken as suggesting that attritable hardware should replace the exquisite, powerful, expensive “programs” on which the U.S. has hitherto (and should continue to, in our view) built a good part of its might. The proper way to frame this is that these different layers should complement, enable, and multiply each other’s force capabilities rather than substitute each other.

That said, for these layers to act as complements to each other it is necessary that the U.S. defense industrial base undergoes significant changes that span the institutional, the operational and productive/technological—and success is far from granted. The policy impetus has already been set in motion in past years, and builds on the rapid rise of new defense tech companies, with some of them even developing manufacturing capacity at some scale.

However, the policy push still faces several implementation hurdles, which we address in detail below, and institutional roadblocks remain in place. At the same time, there are doubts regarding the capacity of new defense tech companies to move from innovative solutions to production at scale. Last but not least, there are also questions about how large, incumbent defense companies will adjust to these potential changes, whether resisting them, embracing them and adapting, or slowly fading. So far the results suggest that change is taking place but is being slower than some expected, both in terms of the capacity capabilities of new entrants and signs of change in incumbents.

## Securing the future

Our broader take, however, is that the direction of travel is likely to be shaped by the following factors. First, as mentioned, the nature of war has changed and communication and control capabilities will be fundamental in the digital age. Second, that successful defense industrial bases elsewhere, each in their own way, have largely based their success on the integration of their defense and commercial industrial bases. Third, and related to the two above, that the U.S. possesses a technological edge in the digital space that sits largely in the commercial sector, and that integrating it to the defense ecosystem is crucial for the U.S. to keep its military dominance.

To maintain military dominance, the U.S. DIB must secure enduring advantages by:

- Integration: Forge deeper links between defense and commercial sectors, leveraging shared platforms, open standards, and continuous feedback.
- Scale: Build modular, manufacturable systems for rapid mass production, lower barriers for new entrants, and foster a networked industrial base.

- Innovation: Drive software-led transformation, iterative hardware development, and commercial digital integration. Emerging tech leaders are reshaping autonomy, hypersonics, advanced manufacturing, satellite communications, and mission resilience.
- Policy reforms like NDAA and Modular Open Systems Architecture (MOSA) are promising, but must overcome entrenched acquisition processes, PPBE constraints, and persistent friction over intellectual property and data rights—barriers that can either enable competition and modularity or reinforce vendor lock-in and exclude new entrants.

America's defense industrial base stands at a crossroads. Adapting to persistent competition, rapid technological change, and mass-scale attrition warfare is not optional; it's essential. Enduring advantage will depend on integrating commercial and defense sectors, scaling production, and accelerating innovation, while dismantling deep-seated institutional and structural barriers. Failure to act risks eroding deterrence and long-term military effectiveness, with consequences for national security and global leadership.

## The U.S. defense industrial base

Since the end of the Cold War, the U.S. defense industrial base (DIB) has been acclaimed for its ability to deliver world-class, high-end platforms such as aircraft carriers, stealth aircraft, and advanced missile systems, which underpin its global military dominance (see Appendix: I. U.S. and Chinese Military Capabilities). These achievements, however, rest atop a deeply specialized and concentrated network of contractors dominated by a handful of incumbent “primes.” This underlying architecture has become brittle, exposing the system to several vulnerabilities.

One telling metric that helps sum up many of the issues is the lengthy timelines for developing major defense acquisition programs, averaging eight years, which means that 70% of the electronics procured by the defense forces are obsolete before they even reach the field (Figure 4).

More broadly, there are a handful of overarching issues with the current DIB that have been widely noted.

- First, the production base—especially among tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers—has thinned dramatically. The specialized segments that produce castings, energy materials, advanced electronics, propulsion components, and critical tooling are narrow, often geographically concentrated, and capacity-constrained. This creates hidden chokepoints that can halt or slow even fully funded programs, as a shortage in a single critical component or manufacturing line cascades through the entire system. Such bottlenecks reflect not just the prime’s ability to assemble, but an upstream fragility in the broader industrial ecosystem.
- Second, foreign dependency. Even where final assembly occurs domestically, the U.S. defense supply chain is tightly interwoven with global industrial networks, and in many instances is directly exposed to dependencies linked to China. Raw materials, processed inputs, and specialized manufacturing steps are frequently sourced abroad. These dependencies create acute risks: strategic disruption becomes plausible, either through geopolitical coercion, export controls, market manipulation, or simple supply chain shocks. Substituting these foreign supply sources is not a simple fix; it requires recertification, qualification, and navigating the same slow-moving acquisition logic that created the vulnerabilities to begin with.
- Third, the production throughput among the primes. Strained book-to-bill ratios signal that the existing defense industrial base is consistently operating under strain, which is not the case across the broader commercial industrial base (Figure 5). Limited labor pools, single-point suppliers, and the need for long lead items translate into rising backlogs that indicate demand is outpacing available productive capacity. In a system optimized for reliability and predictability rather than volatility and surge, this is a warning sign: “surge” is not a switch to be flipped, but a capability that must be cultivated and maintained.

Compounding these structural frailties is industry concentration. Decades of consolidation among primes and key suppliers has eroded competitiveness. Barriers to entry remain steep due to capital requirements, compliance burdens, and incumbency advantages in navigating complex military requirements and sustainment. As a result, industry incentives often favor protecting existing program positions and margin structures, rather than cost-driven innovation or manufacturing efficiency. Limited competition also slows the emergence of alternative production lines, depriving the system of vital resilience; a supplier shock rapidly becomes systemic.

## Box 1: A Schematic of the U.S. DIB's Supply Chain

The DIB's supply chain is a multi-tiered, international network comprising a diverse array of contractors, all dedicated to equipping the military with the advanced systems necessary to achieve its strategic objectives.

This network encompasses thousands of companies, ranging from major "prime" (Tier 1) contractors—such as Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, Boeing, and Northrop Grumman—to small and medium-sized enterprises that provide highly specialized components (Figure 6). Importantly, the DIB's scope extends well beyond hardware, incorporating critical domains such as software development, cybersecurity, and logistics services.

A defining characteristic of the DIB is its monopsonistic structure: while the sector is largely driven by private enterprise, the U.S. government—primarily the Department of War—serves as the principal buyer, shaping market demand through the National Defense Strategy (NDS). The Department of War typically awards contracts for complete weapon systems to prime (Tier 1) contractors. For instance, in 2025, Lockheed Martin secured a \$12.5 billion contract to deliver 300 F-35 fighter jets to the U.S. Navy.

Prime contractors, in turn, depend on an extensive network of subcontractors (Tier 2 and beyond) to supply essential subsystems—such as propulsion, weapons, and navigation systems—that are integrated into the final product. In many cases, Tier 2 suppliers, such as those providing weapons systems, further rely on Tier 3 and lower-tier contractors for critical components like fire control systems and warheads. This intricate, layered structure of subcontracting is fundamental to the delivery of defense products, yet it has also posed persistent challenges to the DIB for decades.

## Warfare is outpacing industry

The vulnerabilities afflicting the traditional defense industrial base are not static; they are being compounded by the accelerating evolution of warfare itself. Since the early 2000s, combat has grown more transparent at the tactical level, fueled by advances in sensors, drones, and commercial satellite imagery, as well as distributed reconnaissance and targeting. The Ukraine conflict has massively accelerated these trends, making clear that operational advantage now flows from the ability to connect sensors and shooters, maintain decision superiority under attack, and rapidly regenerate combat power despite attrition.

Additionally, U.S. forces face increasing challenges from adversaries pursuing Anti-Access/Area-Denial (A2/AD) strategies, particularly exemplified by China. These adversaries contest forward bases, threaten concentrated formations, and deny access to critical maritime and air corridors, pushing U.S. doctrine toward distributed operations, longer-range effects, and dynamic force employment. In other words, this is a strategy designed to make it hard for an opponent to get into a region (“anti-access”) and hard for them to operate effectively once they’re there (“area denial”). It’s a playbook for turning a contested zone into a place where enemy forces can’t safely arrive, can’t communicate well, can’t see clearly, and can’t move or fight freely.

It typically combines tools like electronic warfare (jamming), cyber attacks, long-range missiles, and advanced air defenses to disrupt communications and sensing, threaten ships and aircraft from far away, and raise the cost and risk of operating near the contested area. This evolving environment punishes rigid architectures and demands that systems be plug-and-play, reconfigurable, and able to operate under degraded

connectivity.

At the center of the response to this paradigm shift is the U.S. pursuit of Joint All-Domain Command and Control (JADC2), which seeks to connect sensors, decision-makers, and weapons across land, sea, air, space, and cyber domains, with systems such as Palantir's Maven (MSS) and Anduril's Lattice playing a central role $^{12}$ . The DoD uses Uber as an analogy for JADC2, suggesting that instead of a rider being matched to the best nearby driver, the military aims to connect sensors from every service (Army, Navy, Air Force, etc.) into a single network and use software (including AI) to rapidly match an emerging target or threat to the best available shooter or effect—whether a missile, aircraft, cyber tool, or electronic warfare option—based on factors like proximity, timing, and suitability, enabling much faster decisions than today's slower command cycles.

JADC2 is not merely a technical upgrade; it represents a fundamental transformation—enabling a common operational picture that is actionable, resilient, and able to route targeting and tasking decisions through robust pathways even when under attack. Victory in modern contested environments depends on keeping the sense-decide-act loop functioning faster and more reliably than the adversary.

The existing industrial base, optimized for long development cycles and stable sustainment, struggles to meet these requirements. The decisive variables are now the cadence of software updates, integration of new sensors, rapid fielding of mission applications, and replenishment of large numbers of attritable assets, not merely the delivery and maintenance of exquisite platforms. Requirements have shifted from “build once, maintain forever” to “build, iterate, and continuously integrate.”

Taken together, the traditional U.S. defense industrial base is capable of producing excellence, but struggles to generate at scale, rapidly iterate at operational speed, or robustly de-risk its supply chain amid geopolitical disruption. These weaknesses predate current shifts in warfare but are becoming exponentially more dangerous as the operational environment demands speed, dispersion, and constant adaptation.

This new reality demands not just an upgrade to the traditional defense industrial base, but a fundamentally different model: one that mirrors the commercial technology ecosystem in its integration patterns, iteration speed, and ability to scale production rapidly. Integration between the defense and commercial industrial bases is now a precondition for the adaptability and resilience required by modern warfare. As we analyze below, this is a common feature across different successful DIBs elsewhere, regardless of their disparate structures and objectives.

## Lessons from Other DIBs

## China's Military-Civil Fusion

The U.S. Department of War (DoW) identifies China as posing the biggest competition and threat to the security of the U.S. DIB. China's defense industrial base has grown as it has become a global economic force over the last couple of decades. During this time, China has pursued policies that have allowed it to overcome many of the kinds of challenges and chokepoints that the U.S. defense industrial base currently faces. China's primary advantage lies in its Military-Civil Fusion (MCF) strategy. Unlike the U.S., where the commercial and defense sectors are largely distinct with a buyer-vendor relationship, China's DIB is physically and operationally entwined with its global manufacturing base.

China's MCF promotes deep integration between its defense and civilian industries. The MCF ensures that new innovations simultaneously advance economic and military development by removing barriers between China's civilian and commercial sectors and its military and defense industrial sectors. For example, commercially available Chinese DJI drones have been deployed in the Ukraine-Russia war with great effectiveness. The US DoW maintains a list of Chinese companies that contribute to the MCF or may have other connections to the Chinese military; as of 2026, the US DoW has added 13 Chinese drone companies, including DJI. Designation prevents companies from participating in DOD procurement $^{3}$ .

## How China leverages commercial manufacturing

China's expansive and technologically advanced manufacturing sector sits as the bedrock of its DIB, facilitating the rapid development, prototyping, production, and deployment of military systems. Today, China commands approximately $30\%$ of global manufacturing output—nearly double that of the United States. This dominance has been achieved through a suite of policy instruments, including low-interest loans, subsidized tax rates, relaxed environmental regulations, and strategic dumping practices, all designed to bolster industrial growth (Securing rare earth supply: Key is technology).

The sheer scale of China's manufacturing capacity has enabled the swift expansion of its military assets. Notably, China has emerged as the world's largest shipbuilder, with a shipbuilding capacity more than 230 times greater than that of the U.S. (Revitilizing U.S. Shipbuilding: Automation, autonomous, partnerships, and shipping). Individual shipyards, such as Jiangnan, possess more capacity than all U.S. shipyards combined $^{9}$ . ## China's supply chain network

China's defense industry is strengthened by its global dominance in the supply chain for strategic materials and components. As the world's leading producer of rare earth elements, essential for advanced weapons systems, China supplies up to $90\%$ of global rare earths and has leveraged this market position to influence both supply and pricing (Securing rare earth supply: Key is technology). This advantage not only supports China's own defense industrial base, but also extends its reach into the supply chains of other nations, including the U.S. A 2023 analysis by Govini revealed that between 2005 and 2023, the number of Chinese firms embedded in U.S. supply chains nearly tripled to about $43,000^{12}$ . Several factors have contributed to this surge: the proliferation of consumer electronics such as the iPhone and e-commerce, the full integration of Chinese firms following China's accession to the WTO in 2001, and most importantly the widespread offshoring of production to China by Western companies seeking cost efficiencies (FDI: Shifting from outsourcing to securing supply chains).

The implications for U.S. defense are significant. Some estimates suggest that as much as 41% of the Department of War’s weapons systems rely, at least in part, on Chinese semiconductors (Figure 13). Prime contractors for key missile systems—including Tomahawk, LRASM, JDAM, and JASSM, such as General Dynamics, Raytheon, Boeing, Northrop Grumman, Lockheed Martin, depend on hundreds of subcontractors for critical components like propulsion systems, electronics, test equipment, detonators, and data links, who in turn depend on hundreds of suppliers. Govini’s analysis identified as many as 1,587 Chinese firms supplying materials to these subcontractors. This reliance on Chinese suppliers poses a substantial risk to the resilience of the U.S. defense industrial base, particularly in the context of a protracted conflict.

## Other DIBs: Korea, Sweden, Israel

While the U.S. remains the dominant global defense player, particularly in innovation, and China's production scale remains unmatched, a significant shift in cost competitiveness and innovation speed is occurring in the defense industrial bases of other countries, illustrated by Israel, Sweden and Korea. Their defense industrial bases are shaped by their unique geopolitical environments, technological advancements, industrial production, and models of government-industry collaboration. Each of these allied nations has forged a globally competitive defense sector, but their operating models, leading companies, and strategic strengths and weaknesses differ in ways that offer valuable insights for the U.S. DIB.

## Korea - The large exporter

South Korea’s defense budget has increased an average of 53% from \$32B in 2010 to \$49B in 2024, shown in Figure 14 $^{13}$ . At the same time, arms sales have been increasing over the last decade. Total arms and ammunition exports grew an average of 10x from \$245M in 2010 to \$2.7B in 2024 $^{14}$ . South Korea’s rapid innovation cycles and advanced manufacturing capabilities have enabled it to become a major supplier of land systems, naval assets, and missile technology. The industry produces a wide range of weapons, covering conventional weapons and advanced technology-oriented weapon systems across the ground, naval, aerospace, and electronic equipment fields $^{15}$ . The defense industrial base is based on a joint-venture model between government agencies and private companies.

The Ministry of National Defense and the Defense Acquisition Program Administration (DAPA) set strategic priorities and fund research and development, while weapons are produced by 6 main companies: Hanhwa Aerospace, Korean Aerospace Industries, Hyundai Rotem, LIG Nex1, Hyundai Heavy Industry, and Poongsan. In this Public-Private Partnership, the government undertakes the conceptualization, design, experimentation, and testing of a weapon system, while private defense companies undertake mass production to provide complete products for the armed forces and support aftersales maintenance for decades.

New weapons development begins with a government-commissioned R&D project, which is carried out by a government research institute, the Agency for Defense Development (ADD), rather than private companies – which contrasts with the U.S. process in which industry carries out R&D. Korea’s ecosystem, however, has the advantage that it spares enormous R&D expenses and has more financial flexibility to compete in and out of the Korean market $^{16}$ .

The DAPA oversees weapons procurement and is the single point of contact for weapons trade. Finally, the Russia-Ukraine war boosted weapons exports to Europe (Figure 15), e.g., the 2022 sale of K2 Black Panther tanks to Poland, one of the largest defense deals in South Korean history, illustrates both the country's export success and its dependence on foreign markets $^{17}$ .

## Sweden - Reliable supply chains

The Swedish defense industry has experienced remarkable growth and transformation, particularly following Sweden’s accession to NATO in March 2024. The nation’s defense budget has more than doubled over the past decade, rising from \$5.3 billion in 2010 to \$11.7 billion in 2024, with a striking 34% increase between 2023 and 2024 alone (Figure 14). Despite its modest size, Sweden’s defense sector is highly specialized and globally competitive, anchored by prominent firms such as Saab AB and BAE Systems Hägglunds. Sweden has emerged as a significant arms exporter, especially within Europe. Notably, 60-70% of Saab’s profits are derived from exports, as highlighted by CEO Micael Johansson $^{18}$ . The industry’s focus on niche technologies—including electronic warfare, stealth, and advanced sensors—enables Sweden to excel on the international stage. Saab’s development of the A26 submarine, the world’s first 5th generation submarine per Saab, exemplifies the country’s reputation for innovation $^{19}$ .

While Saab and BAE Systems Hägglunds are the industry's major players, Sweden's defense ecosystem is enriched by a multitude of technology companies producing dual-use products for both civilian and military applications. Approximately 400 companies supply the Swedish Defense Materiel Administration (FMV), the government agency responsible for procuring, developing, and supplying equipment and services to the armed forces. The Swedish Security and Defense Industry Association (SOFF) represents industry partners and collaborates closely with FMV to support national defense objectives $^{20}$ . FMV's partnership with industry ensures that procurement and research and development are closely aligned with Sweden's defense needs, similar to the approach seen in the U.S. Department of Defense.

A defining characteristic of the Swedish defense industry, rooted in Cold War ambitions, was the pursuit of independence from foreign suppliers. However, the contemporary landscape reflects a growing need for material cooperation with allies, and Sweden now relies on deliveries of components and systems from abroad $^{21}$ . For example, Bloomberg supply chain data indicates that 70% of Saab's direct suppliers are domiciled in the following countries: U.S. (21%), Sweden (16%), United Kingdom (13%), France (11%), and Germany (9%). Notably, none of the direct suppliers is domiciled in China even though about 4% of all direct supplier facilities are located in China.

Ultimately, Sweden's small domestic market and limited production capacity necessitate a strong reliance on exports and international partnerships. The nation's ability to innovate and specialize has allowed it to carve out a prominent role in the global defense industry and is actively adapting to the changing global conflict environment.

Israel's defense industrial base is a model of adaptability, rapid innovation, and export-driven growth, underpinned by strong government-industry collaboration and integration with the tech sector. With one of the highest defense-to-GDP ratios globally, Israel's defense budget has more than doubled over the past decade, rising from \$16.7B in 2010 to \$45.3B in 2024, with a striking 65% increase between 2023 and 2024 alone following the October 7th, 2023, attacks by Hamas (Figure 16) $^{22}$ .

## The lessons

Across peer and partner defense models, one clear pattern emerges: regardless of political or military differences, defense effectiveness increasingly relies on tight integration between commercial and defense industrial bases. This integration accelerates innovation, production, and operational learning. China's military-civil fusion achieves this at scale, intertwining defense and manufacturing to rapidly convert civilian capacity into military output and move dual-use technologies into operational use.

Supply chain resiliency is another critical lesson. It’s not just a supporting factor—it’s essential for wartime endurance. Sustained production and replenishment depend on minimizing single points of failure, foreign dependencies, and upstream bottlenecks. China pursues resiliency through vertical integration and industrial sovereignty, controlling key production steps and materials. Sweden, by contrast, builds resiliency through trusted alliances and partner supply networks, reducing vulnerability by relying on reliable partners rather than adversaries.

Finally, while technology and innovation are central, their impact depends on the ability to quickly translate operational needs into fielded capabilities. Israel exemplifies this approach, with its defense sector tightly linked to the civilian high-tech ecosystem, startups, and academia, enabling rapid adaptation of dual-use tools and shortening the feedback loop from combat experience to deployment.

## Asymmetric warfare

The war in Ukraine, and, to a degree, Iran, has become a real-time case study in modern conflict, revealing two intertwined lessons for defense industrial strategy.

First, the power of mass and attrition is clear. Drones and other cost-effective, attritable systems have shown how persistence and saturation can impose outsized costs on more expensive enemy assets. These systems aren't just substitutes; they sustain operational tempo, amplify sensing, and force adversaries to waste costly interceptors and reveal valuable positions. The battlefield now rewards rapid regeneration and asymmetric tactics, where affordable hardware can shift the balance.

Second—and arguably more critical—is the transformative impact of technology and software $^{26}$ . Ukraine's forces have leveraged real-time data from distributed sensors, drones, satellites, and commercial sources to create a unified operational picture. Software compresses the kill chain, moving from slow, hierarchical targeting to fast, distributed loops powered by data integration. This shift has dramatically reduced time-to-target, enabling units to coordinate and survive through speed.

Command and control is now pivotal. Without a robust JADC2 infrastructure, even the most advanced platforms lose effectiveness. Stealth aircraft, long-range missiles, and ISR assets only deliver decisive results when connected to resilient data networks that enable coordinated, multi-domain action. Hardware is still essential, but software increasingly determines whether it delivers a decisive effect.

This reality underpins the emerging High-Low strategy: scalable, attritable systems (like UAVs) complement high-end assets (like F-35s), with their synergy driven by command, control, and data. The lesson from Ukraine is clear—modernization isn’t just about hardware, but about integrating software, data, and adaptable manufacturing at speed and scale.

## Box 3: Space-enabled services and JADC2

The war in Ukraine has shown that software-driven command and control, powered by space-enabled services, can be as decisive as any weapon platform $^{27}$ . By compressing the kill chain—from sensing to decision to action—Ukraine leveraged space-based ISR and commercial imagery for timely warnings and sustained battlefield visibility. Intelligence from Western partners and U.S. providers like Maxar and Planet Labs proved critical, echoing the JADC2 vision: connect sensors into a unified network, process data (including with AI), and rapidly recommend the best response, as future decisions may need to happen in seconds.

The conflict also revealed the battlefield as a cycle of disruption and adaptation. On day one, a Russian cyberattack on Viasat knocked out satellite communications, forcing Ukraine to improvise. Starlink quickly became a vital SATCOM enabler, supporting tactical communications, command-and-control, drone operations, and fire support—demonstrating how resilient satellite internet can sustain coordination and tempo under attack. In JADC2 terms, this is the backbone: without robust communications, data can’t flow fast enough to drive effective action.

Precision effects in Ukraine depended on space-provided PNT, but electronic warfare (EW) quickly challenged this advantage. GNSS signals were jammed and spoofed, sparking a constant contest over navigation and targeting. Ukraine, the U.S., and manufacturers adapted systems and tactics in real time, while Russia countered—proving that in an EW-rich environment, precision is a moving target, maintained through continual iteration. This underscores why JADC2 prioritizes rapid, software-enabled decision-making: when navigation, communications, and sensing are contested, the side that integrates data and adapts fastest keeps the kill chain intact.

## The Imperatives: Integration, Scale, and Innovation

The U.S. defense-industrial enterprise now stands at a decisive inflection point. Success in the era ahead will be determined by its ability to deliver on three tightly interlocking imperatives: integrating the defense and commercial industrial bases, unlocking genuine mass-scale production, and unleashing innovation across the entire ecosystem. Together, these are the enabling conditions for a force capable of meeting the relentless operational and technological pressures revealed in Ukraine, exposed by China's fusion model, and only latently present in the U.S. system today.

## Integration: A single, responsive IB

In our view, the key foundational imperative is the deep integration of the defense and commercial industrial bases. This would allow resilience, rapid fielding, and long-term technological superiority, which in turn would mean moving beyond episodic, transactional procurement toward structural alignment in three critical mechanisms:

Leveraging Commercial Manufacturing Platforms: Integration means defense products must be designed from inception for manufacturability on widely available commercial lines, rather than bespoke supplier sets dictated by narrow military-unique requirements. The goal is not simply outsourcing, but genuine modularity—allowing programs like the Barracuda missile (see Appendix: IV. “High and Low” Strategy - The Barracuda Missiles) to be produced across multiple U.S. manufacturing sites, harnessing the scale, process control, and cost discipline of the commercial domain.

Shared Supplier Development and Open Standards: Prime contractors must overhaul relationships with tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers, investing in their capabilities, workforce, and technical tools, potentially mirroring the ASML open innovation model (see Box 4:

ASML's Open Innovation Model below). Establishing common interface standards would reduce barriers for new entrants and enable the commercial supply chain to flow into defense, fostering competition and reducing single points of failure.

Cross-Sector Feedback Loops: Commercial velocity in software, AI, and automation must be continuously imported into the defense design and production cycle—not on a lag, but as the default setting. Feedback from operational use, testing, and adversary adaptation must be rapidly recycled into requirements, integrating lessons learned at every level. This is the mechanism by which the U.S. can maintain its advantage as the tempo of warfare, software, and data integration accelerates.

## Box 4: ASML's Open Innovation Model

Founded in 1984 and headquartered in the Netherlands, ASML is the world's dominant manufacturer of semiconductor lithography equipment. Its product portfolio spans extreme ultraviolet (EUV) and deep ultraviolet (DUV) lithography systems, computational lithography, and metrology and inspection systems, with operations across more than 60 locations on three continents. Despite being a monopoly, holding 100% market share in EUV lithography and approximately 80–89% across all lithography tool categories, ASML has remained at the technological frontier by building a business model around collaborative partnerships, shared risk-reward arrangements, and deep integration with customers, suppliers, and research institutions—an “open innovation” strategy (ASML, 2026)—supplemented by targeted vertical integration.

The most prominent example of ASML's “open innovation” strategy is its Customer Co-Investment Program for Innovation, established in 2012, through which its three largest customers — Intel, TSMC, and Samsung — acquired equity stakes in ASML while simultaneously committing R&D funding and gaining board-level governance participation (ASML, 2012). A comparable logic underpins ASML’s relationship with Carl Zeiss AG, its longest-standing institutional partner and the supplier of the precision optics at the heart of every ASML lithography system.

In 2016, ASML formalized the relationship by acquiring a 24.9% minority stake in Carl Zeiss SMT, while committing €220 million in R&D support and approximately €540 million in capital expenditure and supply chain investments over six years (ASML, 2016). Most recently, ASML extended this co-investment model beyond its traditional hardware ecosystem with a €1.3 billion investment in Mistral AI's Series C funding round, acquiring approximately a 11% stake and a seat on the company's Strategic Committee (ASML, 2025). ASML extends this open innovation logic further through a comprehensive network of public-private partnerships that embed the company within academic and governmental R&D efforts (e.g., Project Beethoven and IMEC partnership).

Complementing this ecosystem-level moat, ASML has systematically integrated critical capabilities through targeted acquisitions. Its first major acquisition, Silicon Valley Group (SVG) in 2001, created the world's leading lithography equipment provider and added photoresist track and thermal product lines to ASML's portfolio (ASML, 2000). The 2013 acquisition of Cymer internalized EUV light source development, eliminating a key external dependency and accelerating ASML's EUV commercialization roadmap (ASML, 2013). In 2016, the acquisition of Hermes Microvision Inc. (HMI) in Taiwan integrated pattern verification and inspection capabilities, enabling ASML to offer holistic lithography solutions combining exposure, metrology, and inspection in a unified product architecture (ASML, 2016).

Finally, the 2020 acquisition of the Berliner Glas Group secured internal manufacturing of specialized optical glass components essential to EUV systems, converting a strategically critical external supply relationship into a controlled internal function (ASML, 2020). Beyond these targeted vertical integrations, ASML maintains a highly diversified supplier base, with $80\%$ of its materials sourced from 5,100 suppliers (ASML Annual Report, 2025). Each acquisition thus follows a disciplined logic of internalizing only those capabilities that represent genuine bottlenecks to ASML's roadmap, while the expansive supplier network ensures the company retains the resilience and flexibility that full vertical integration would undermine.

## Scale: Modularity and scalability

Integration loses much of its purpose without scale. The system must deliver not just exquisite prototypes but repeatable, robust, mass production of attritable systems while also being capable of delivering on high-end systems. The Ukraine War has demonstrated that victory depends on the ability to field thousands of effectors and continuously regenerate lost capability, an industrial challenge for which the traditional U.S. defense base is currently unprepared.

The key to scale lies in two linked reforms:

Modularity and Interoperability: Systems, particularly in the “low” (attributable) tier, must be modular in design, interchangeable in subsystems, and as close as feasible to open technical standards. This approach allows for rapid reconfiguration and taps the full breadth of U.S. manufacturing capacity. Modularity, even at the exquisite tier, matters: next-generation platforms must be upgradable—hardware and software alike—enabling continuous integration rather than serial block upgrades. The standardization and process repetition delivered by modularity is a precondition, as well, for unlocking the full mass gains of advanced production methods such as robotic manufacturing (Revitalizing U.S. shipbuilding: Automation, autonomous, partnerships, and shipping).

Scalable Pathways for New Entrants: Innovation from commercial-origin firms and new defense manufacturers remains at risk unless structural bottlenecks (requirements gold-plating, slow contracting, supply chain credentials) are cleared. Reducing barriers allows these actors to move beyond prototypes to true volume production. Prime contractors must foster—rather than inhibit—these transitions, treating the broader

industrial base as a networked resource, not just vendors of convenience.

Scale in this regard offers both a solution to the attrition needs of the modern battlefield and is also the precondition for fielding enough (and adaptable) exquisite platforms, and modularity plays a large role in enabling this.

Software-Driven Transformation: Software is now a warfighting element in its own right—command and control, sensor fusion, targeting, and electronic warfare all depend on its rapid evolution. ## New names challenge incumbent primes

Some of the drawbacks of industry concentration, outlined in the preceding sections, are already being addressed by an emerging defense tech ecosystem that diverges from the incumbent defense industrial base and offers a potentially complementary production model. Drawing largely on commercial technologies and productive capacities, this ecosystem seeks to fuse the leading edge of software with modern manufacturing processes to deliver a new array of military hardware and software—one designed to meet the autonomy, adaptability, and, potentially, the scale and attrition demands of contemporary warfare. In doing so, it embodies many of the principles highlighted earlier: modularity, iterative production, rapid adaptability, deep software integration, and scalability.

Crucially, it also offers a chance to disrupt the concentration, sluggishness, and limited competition that have come to characterize the exquisite-platform-based infrastructure, fostering a broader and more networked defense base through reproducibility, design simplicity, and lower unit costs—qualities exemplified by programs like the Barracuda missile family (see Appendix: IV. “High and Low” Strategy - The Barracuda Missiles).

Some big players are already emerging within this space—Anduril, Shield AI, Saronic, Forterra, and others have secured billions in capital and increasingly sizeable Pentagon contracts, positioning themselves as central partners in deploying autonomous systems at scale. This rapid ascent introduces a non-negligible risk on two fronts: first, that the new and traditional ecosystems grow along parallel tracks rather than becoming integrated in ways that foster competition and adaptability across the board; and second, that today's primes are simply replaced by a new generation of neo-primes, reproducing the very concentration dynamics this ecosystem promises to disrupt. The new ecosystem must therefore be carefully nurtured, through institutional reform, open standards, and procurement models that preserve openness and competition, if it is to deliver on its promise rather than recreate the limitations of the system it aims to complement.

Defense procurement is shifting from platform-centric programs toward software-defined architectures, spawning a new class of cheaper, scalable hardware with less human involvement. ## Institutional Challenges

Institutional reform is the gatekeeper for industrial transformation. Recognizing the urgent need for structural change, the U.S. government has enacted executive orders, memoranda, and provisions in the FY26 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) to accelerate innovation, streamline requirements, integrate commercial solutions, and align software acquisition with iterative development and rapid deployment.

Against the backdrop of multi-domain conflict, compressed decision cycles, and a battlefield dominated by software, data, and resilient networks, the NDAA's commercial-first approach aims to bridge DoW demand with the agile, scalable capabilities of the broader U.S. industrial sector.

## Commercial First

The DoW has traditionally defaulted to defense contractors and bespoke, defense-unique solutions, even when commercial products are available, because requirements, compliance, and security processes tend to favor established primes and discourage commercial vendors from participating. The FY26 NDAA aims to reverse this trend. It enforces a commercial-first approach by requiring thorough research and formal documentation before opting out of commercial products and services, and it reduces the compliance burden that has historically deterred commercial suppliers by limiting defense-unique clauses in contracts and restricting how far these obligations can be pushed down the supply chain.

The NDAA also expands the use of Commercial Solutions Openings and follow-on production authorities, making commercial adoption feasible at scale rather than just for prototypes. In parallel, the DoD increasingly uses Other Transaction Agreements (OTAs) to engage nontraditional vendors and move quickly outside the rigid FAR/DFARS framework.

To lower barriers that favor incumbents, the NDAA raises thresholds for cost-accounting and pricing regimes, focuses compliance requirements on the largest programs, and updates evaluation practices to better include new entrants—such as recognizing commercial past performance and relying more on demonstrations and testing. It also provides broad exemptions for nontraditional defense contractors from traditional cost-accounting and business-system requirements, aiming to widen the competitive field and allow more commercial companies to participate without disrupting their business models.

For software, the NDAA moves acquisition toward continuous delivery by establishing software-appropriate test and evaluation pathways and enabling commercial-style consumption and subscription models, acknowledging that modern software advantage comes from ongoing iteration rather than one-time deployment.

## IP rights and budget cycles

Procedural reform alone doesn't guarantee operational success. Take MOSA (Modular Open Systems Approach): it's DoW's statutory framework for building modular, upgradeable systems with open standards to reduce vendor lock-in and enable faster refresh cycles. But MOSA's effectiveness hinges on clear, workable approaches to data and intellectual property (IP) rights—specifically, how data must be delivered and what can remain proprietary. Without a robust IP strategy, contracts stall, negotiations drag on, and vendors either price in risk or opt out entirely. These boundaries remain hotly contested, often undermining MOSA's intended benefits.

Even with improved acquisition authorities, the resourcing system, governed by Planning, Programming, Budgeting, and Execution (PPBE), still dictates what gets funded, when, and how flexibly resources can be redirected as technology and threats evolve. PPBE's annual cycles and rigid appropriation categories weren't designed for rapid iteration, continuous software delivery, or quick adoption of commercial tech. While execution flexibility may improve slightly, the fundamental mismatch persists: commercial innovation moves fast, but defense budgeting is locked in early and constrained by “colors of money,” line-item controls, and reprogramming hurdles. Without a budget model that supports the iteration modern warfare demands, this could turn procurement reform into little more than a permission slip.

Ultimately, implementation is where reform succeeds or stalls, and where resistance is strongest. Institutional inertia, compliance culture, and legacy incentives slow progress. Structural barriers like budget categories and documentation requirements, plus the tendency to revert to familiar processes, further impede change. Political concerns about oversight can also limit flexibility. Without real follow-through that transforms day-to-day budgeting and execution, the commercial-first, software-centric agenda risks stalling not in law, but in the practical mechanics of funding, contracting, and acquisition incentives.

## Future-proofing U.S. defense

The strategic necessity facing the U.S. is clear: only through deep integration of commercial and defense sectors can scale and innovation be restored, and only through institutional reform can technological agility be matched to operational need. The lessons drawn from peer and partner ecosystems all point in the same direction. China's military-civil fusion, Israel's tight coupling between defense firms and the civilian high-tech ecosystem, Sweden's reliance on trusted allied supply networks, and Korea's public-private partnership model each demonstrate, in their own way, that defense effectiveness in the modern era flows from the porousness of the boundary between commercial and military industrial bases, not from their separation.

The U.S. retains a decisive advantage in the inputs that matter most for the wars of the future: software talent, digital infrastructure, advanced semiconductor design, AI capabilities, and a deep commercial manufacturing base. The challenge is not the absence of these assets, but the structural friction that prevents them from flowing into the defense ecosystem at the speed and scale that modern warfare demands. Closing that gap is less a technical problem than an institutional and organizational one.

The era ahead will be defined not by any single tier of capability, but by the synergy among them. Attributable systems have proven their worth in Ukraine, Iran, and the Middle East, where mass, persistence, and cost asymmetry have repeatedly shifted operational balances against more expensive adversary platforms. Yet the exquisite tier—stealth aircraft, advanced munitions, nuclear submarines, space systems—remains essential, and will continue to anchor deterrence and high-end warfighting. What unites the two is the connective tissue: command, control, communications, and the software-driven kill chains that allow sensors, decision-makers, and shooters to operate as a single distributed system.

The U.S. is already seeing the rise of a promising new defense tech ecosystem—Anduril, Shield AI, Saronic, Forterra, Epirus, and others—that can complement its globally-leading exquisite tier with attritable, software-defined hardware produced on commercial-style manufacturing lines. But this ecosystem will not deliver on its promise automatically. It must be nurtured and integrated with the incumbent base rather than left to grow on a parallel track, and shaped by procurement and policy choices that preserve openness and competition rather than allowing today’s primes to be replaced by a new generation of neo-primes.

In scale and speed, the U.S. has fallen behind, and the costs of inaction are tangible: thinned tier-2 and tier-3 supplier ranks, lengthy acquisition timelines that field obsolete electronics, fragile supply chains exposed to Chinese chokepoints, and a replicator initiative that produces hundreds rather than thousands of systems. If these gaps are not closed, the U.S. risks both failing to deter adversaries, who increasingly read American industrial constraints as a window of opportunity, and losing future conflicts in which the side that iterates, regenerates, and adapts fastest holds the decisive edge.

Institutional change is building momentum: the FY26 NDAA's commercial-first provisions, expanded use of Other Transaction Agreements, MOSA, and software-appropriate acquisition pathways all point in the right direction. But operational follow-through is far from assured. Procurement reform can be undone by PPBE rigidity, IP and data-rights disputes, compliance culture, and the gravitational pull of legacy incentives. Both the institutional and productive challenges remain tall, and they are deeply intertwined, resolving the former is a precondition for delivering on the latter. The next few years will determine whether America's defense industrial base evolves into the integrated, scalable, software-driven enterprise the moment requires, or whether reform stalls in the practical mechanics of funding, contracting, and execution. The window for getting this right is narrowing.

## II. Drones & Robotics in Modern Warfare

Drones have emerged as a cornerstone of modern warfare, propelling the global defense drone market into a phase of extraordinary expansion, with annual sales surpassing 50 billion and a growth rate of 20% CAGR (JPM Research). This surge is largely driven by the demands of high-intensity attrition warfare, as witnessed in the Russia-Ukraine conflict and the ongoing escalation between the U.S., Israel, and Iran in Operation Fury. The Russia-Ukraine war has firmly established drones as a principal tool of “strategic attrition.” By early 2026, reports indicate that Ukrainian drone strikes have disabled nearly 17% of Russia’s oil refining capacity, demonstrating how high-volume, cost-effective systems can circumvent traditional air defenses and inflict substantial economic and military damage (Link) $^{28}$ . The deployment of drones in the current U.S.-Iran conflict further underscores a “race to the bottom” in U.S./Israeli munitions stockpiles. These drone deployments have illuminated the critical importance of cost asymmetry, innovation, and, above all, supply chain and manufacturing self-sufficiency. For example, the economic imbalance is stark against the U.S.: intercepting a 20,000 Shahed-type drone from Iran with a 2 million Patriot missile is

unsustainable. But drone innovation is evolving from singular, monolithic platforms to adaptive, autonomous swarms: AI-integrated drones capable of real-time coordination without manual piloting to overwhelm advanced air defense systems.

While the U.S. possesses the innovative capability and an evolving policy framework favorable to responding rapidly to the evolving threat of drone warfare, concerns persist regarding manufacturing capacity and supply chain resilience. Critical components such as integrated circuits and batteries remain largely sourced from China, which continues to dominate global electronics production. For instance, in 2024, Skydio, one of the largest U.S. drone manufacturers, reported supply chain strains and rationing of batteries during assembly of their drones due to Chinese sanctions affecting U.S. battery availability.

## Market Landscape

The deployment of drones and robots across commercial and military domains is reshaping the global landscape of defense innovation. While the U.S. defense industry has historically led the way in drone technology, exemplified by the MQ-9 Reaper drone which was first deployed in the Afghanistan war in 2007, the market has since evolved dramatically. Today, consumer and commercial platforms, which are often repurposed for military use, dominate the field, as vividly demonstrated during the Russia-Ukraine war and during the current conflict between U.S./Israeli versus Iran. During the course of the Ukrainian war, First Person View (FPV) drones, Quadcopters such as the DJI Mavic 3, and others built with commercially available components have been widely deployed by both Russia and Ukraine. Despite the U.S.'s early innovation advantage in drone technology, China has emerged as the preeminent producer of low-cost, dual-use drones, leveraging its formidable manufacturing capacity and supply chain.

In response to these shifts, the U.S. government has launched several initiatives to strengthen domestic drone manufacturing. The Drone Dominance Initiative, introduced in June 2025 with a \$1.1 billion investment, aims to bolster the U.S. drone industry. This and similar programs like the Replicator Initiative that aims to field swarms of affordable autonomous attritable drones across air, land, and sea to counter China's military modernization, have accelerated the development of platforms such as SpektreWorks' LUCAS drones, now available for military procurement.

Among other robotics segments, Humanoid Robots have emerged as a technological breakthrough, and are rapidly moving from R&D to real-world deployment. Although initial applications are geared towards research, manufacturing, logistics, retail, and consumer segments, the military applications of these robots are eminent. In China, for example, government procurement and policy initiatives are driving order momentum (Link). Chinese companies such as Unitree, UBtech, and XPeng lead the segment in cost and scale, while U.S. firms such as Boston Dynamics and Tesla lead in setting technical benchmarks. As global competition and the integration of AI into humanoid robots accelerates, both the U.S. and China are beginning to deploy humanoid robots on the battlefield. For example, in the U.S., Foundation Robotics Labs is partnering with the U.S. DoW to develop their Phantom MK-1 robots for the battlefield $^{29}$ . Similar military applications of humanoid robots have been reported in China.

However, the opacity of the industry complicates analysis of the market landscape. Many leading drone & robotics companies, including China's SZ DJI Technologies, are privately held and do not disclose manufacturing capacity, making it difficult to obtain a comprehensive view of the market. To gain more insight into the market, we therefore conducted a market analysis using PitchBook's database and insights from CSET (Source), examining the landscape, major players, private financing, and other market trends. The PitchBook dataset encompasses Robotics and Drones players including manufacturers, assemblers, software providers, and key component suppliers.

## III. The F-35 - A Signature Program

## Historical Background

The F-35 Lightning II (Figure 29), born from the Joint Strike Fighter (JSF) initiative, is among the DoD's most ambitious acquisition programs and a centerpiece of U.S. airpower modernization. Conceived as a fifth-generation aircraft family, it was designed to deliver stealthy, multirole fighters that could replace a wide range of aging platforms across the U.S. services and close allies, while using commonality such as shared parts, software, training, and support, to reduce long-term operating costs.

This strategic logic took shape in the post–Cold War period, when the U.S. and its partners confronted a dual challenge: large, aging tactical fighter inventories and tightening budgets that made separate, bespoke replacement programs for each service increasingly untenable. Rather than building three wholly distinct aircraft for the Air Force, Navy, and Marine Corps, DoD planners pursued a single core design adaptable into multiple variants. The program’s central wager was that scale and standardization, reinforced by broad international participation, could make an advanced stealth platform affordable to procure in meaningful numbers and sustain over decades.

## Contractors and Industrial Base

The JSF competition began in 1993 and culminated in 2001 when Lockheed Martin's X-35 was selected over Boeing's X-32, formally setting the course for the F-35. The aircraft's first flight in 2006 marked the transition from concept demonstration to a

prolonged phase of systems integration, flight testing, and iterative maturation of mission systems and variant-specific performance. In broad terms, it took roughly 13 years from program start to maiden flight, and more than two decades from inception to initial operational service in 2015. While the lengthy timeline is partly a reflection of both the technical ambition and the complexity of delivering three variants from one baseline architecture, it has also come to epitomize the typical long U.S. weapon system development timelines, which in today's evolving warfare are seen as a meaningful challenge to U.S. defense readiness.

Figure 29: The F-35, refueling mid-air

Source: US Air Force

Economically, the program's value chain is anchored by Lockheed Martin as prime contractor and lead integrator. Pratt & Whitney (RTX) holds the propulsion franchise through the F135 engine. Among the major tier-one partners, Northrop Grumman contributes major structures including the center fuselage and participates in mission systems and radar-related elements, while BAE Systems is similarly positioned with major responsibilities often described as including the rear fuselage and key electronics. This structure creates a concentrated value-capture model: although thousands of suppliers participate, a small set of primes and tier-one partners control the subsystems that drive differentiation, integration risk, and much of the lifecycle sustainment economics. Lockheed Martin has cited a global supply chain of 1,900+ companies. While the U.S. remains the principal manufacturer and largest customer, the program has expanded into a broad coalition of buyers—roughly 19 customer countries—spanning partners and foreign military sales customers. More than 1,300+ aircraft have been delivered globally, reflecting the platform's scale and the durability of its multinational demand base.

## Key capabilities and technology

The F-35 is engineered around survivability, flexibility, and information advantage. Its foundation is a low observable (stealth) design, intended to reduce detection and targeting by advanced sensors and integrated air defenses. It is built as a true multirole aircraft, spanning air-to-air missions, precision strike, ISR (intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance) contributions through onboard sensing, and elements of electronic warfare. As a single-engine supersonic fighter, its speed is estimated at Mach 1.6, balancing speed and maneuverability with a design optimized for signatures, sensors, and integrated weapons employment.

What most differentiates the F-35 is the architecture of its mission systems. Its signature capability is sensor fusion, the integration of radar, infrared, and electronic warfare inputs into a single, coherent operational picture intended to reduce pilot workload and accelerate decisions. This is reinforced by 360-degree situational awareness, enabled by distributed sensors (often described as a Distributed Aperture System), allowing detection and tracking across all aspects of the battlefield. The aircraft is also designed for network-centric operations, sharing and receiving near real-time information with other platforms and forces, which extends its role beyond delivering weapons to acting as an airborne node in a joint and coalition kill chain. Finally, internal weapons bays are central to the F-35's survivability concept, enabling weapons carriage while preserving low observability compared to external stores.

## Program Cost

The F-35's performance ambitions have been accompanied by persistent scrutiny over cost growth and schedule pressure. Total lifetime program costs are estimated to exceed \$2.1 trillion through an expected horizon extending to 2088, with a per-aircraft cost cited at approximately \$90 million. The most consequential driver, however, is sustainment – an estimated 75% of total costs accrue from operations, maintenance, and repairs, underscoring that the program's long-run economics are dominated by readiness, supply chain performance, and depot capacity rather than procurement alone. For example, program costs increased from about \$1.1 trillion in 2018 to \$1.58 trillion 2023—a 44% increase—which was attributed to the compounding burden of a complex global supply chain and the logistics required to maintain availability over time.

## Challenges faced in a future war with a peer adversary

Despite its advanced technology and central role in U.S. force planning, the F-35 has clear liabilities in a peer conflict defined by A2/AD threats, long distances, and heavy electronic/cyber contestation—with direct operational and industrial-base implications. First, range and geography are structural constraints. An Indo-Pacific fight would likely force U.S. and allied aircraft to operate from more distant bases due to missile threats to forward airfields, carriers, and tankers. This increases the F-35's dependence on aerial refueling and distributed basing; if tankers are degraded or pushed back, combat reach, persistence, and sortie rates fall. Second, the F-35's edge depends on connectivity. Its core advantage comes from sensor fusion and data sharing, but a peer adversary can attack the enabling networks through EW, cyber, and anti-satellite actions. In a degraded communications environment, the aircraft remains capable, but its differentiating advantage shrinks, weakening the payoff case for its cost.

Third, sustainment and availability can be decisive. High maintenance demands, spare-parts constraints, and depot bottlenecks can translate into a gap between fleet size “on paper” and sorties (recovering, refueling, rearming and launching) generated in practice, especially under wartime stress and disrupted logistics. The platform’s complex, specialized supply chain and reliance on a sophisticated maintenance ecosystem amplify this risk. Fourth, cost limits scale and attrition tolerance. The F-35 delivers high capability per aircraft, but its expense and complexity constrain procurement volumes and make loss replacement and rapid regeneration more difficult than with simpler

platforms—creating a potential quality-versus-quantity mismatch in a prolonged conflict. Finally, counter-stealth improvements (e.g., networked sensors and IR detection) may not negate stealth, but they can erode its margin and raise detection risk in dense air-defense environments. Overall, the F-35 is best viewed as one node in a larger ecosystem, and its peer-conflict performance is contingent on tankers and basing access, resilient networks, robust sustainment/depot capacity, and sufficient force mass to sustain operations.

## IV. "High and Low" Strategy - The Barracuda Missiles

## Background

In 2024, the U.S. Air Force, in collaboration with the Defense Innovation Unit, launched the Enterprise Test Vehicle (ETV) Program—a pioneering initiative aimed at identifying and prototyping commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) components to alleviate supply chain bottlenecks and advance dual-use technology solutions. The program’s central objective is to establish a modular, cost-effective air vehicle architecture that will serve as the foundation for large-scale production of a new generation of missiles.

Participating vendors are integrating COTS components wherever feasible, not only to streamline supply chains and reduce costs, but also to embrace contemporary design-for-manufacturing principles. This approach ensures that air vehicles are purpose-built for their missions, avoids unnecessary complexity and the use of costly materials, and enables rapid, on-demand production—capabilities unattainable with more exquisite, bespoke counterparts such as Tomahawk missiles.

The Defense Innovation Unit selected four companies, including Anduril Industries, to develop prototype solutions for flight demonstration. Remarkably, within just seven months, Anduril refined its Barracuda-500 missile design and successfully conducted a flight test (Figure 30). This achievement underscores how Barracuda's simple, modular architecture accelerates the development, testing, and deployment of disruptive capabilities for warfighters. The Barracuda missile family—including the -100, -250, and -500 variants—embodies air-breathing, autonomous vehicles engineered for high-rate production and modular payload integration, setting a new standard for agility and scalability in defense manufacturing. This makes the Barracuda substantially cheaper (\~10x cheaper) than the Tomahawk missile.

Strategically, the Barracuda is not a replacement for the Tomahawk missile system because it doesn't have the range and payload capacity as the Tomahawk. But it is used to supplement more exquisite systems with cheap munitions used in large scale attrition attacks such as for drone warfare. Barracuda is purpose-built to bring mass to the fight. Designed from the ground-up for hyper-scale manufacturing, its production rates can be doubled on-call to meet short-term surges in demand. Barracuda is designed to be simple to manufacture, both in terms of tooling and talent. This is another example of the implementation of the DoD's high-low acquisition strategy, in which a limited number of high-cost, advanced (exquisite) platforms is paired with a large number of low-cost, disposable assets to enhance military effectiveness while controlling costs. $^{31}$

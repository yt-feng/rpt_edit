# Executive summary

## Ceasefire extended, lower oil, but what about a final deal?

The signing by the US and Iran of a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) to extend the ceasefire and lay a framework for future nuclear negotiations leaves many important questions unanswered. Material risks persist. Even assuming the US-Iran talks on the nuclear programme and other sticking points proceed as planned, it is far from clear what the new normal in the Strait of Hormuz will look like. Already Iran has indicated there will be no return to the pre-war status quo.

## Energy prices have fallen

Nonetheless, the rapid drop in the Brent oil price to around USD80/b in response to the 60-day ceasefire extension and the commitment to re-open the Strait – assuming it is sustainable – would limit the downside risks to growth and limit some of the upside risks to inflation and be in line with our central case scenario for the global economy. We have been assuming that Hormuz traffic and Gulf output would gradually restart in June, and a return to near-normal system-level production and flows would occur by end-Q3 2026.

## Crosscurrents

The pace at which the Strait re-opens will not be the sole influence on the global economy in 2026-27, even if it is the single biggest one. There are also other powerful forces moving in different directions at the very same time, which could have very uneven effects: in particular the AI-related export and investment boom which we discuss on pages 24-32 and the risks to agricultural output and prices from severe El Niño weather conditions which we examine on pages 33-39. Fiscal policy will also have varying impacts on the growth trajectory in 2026-27.

## Consumer spending to soften...

In terms of the economic implications of the supply shock from the Middle East so far, the clearest impact has been an auto-fuel-driven rise in headline inflation and higher input costs, whether from crude, diesel, jet fuel and various gas derivatives for which prices, while off their highs, are still 30% or so higher than pre-conflict levels.

This has all hit confidence and squeezed margins and disposable incomes, though the impact on the limited available consumer data for Q2 suggests no sharp downturn. In the major economies we are so far seeing a familiar pattern of sluggish spending by households in China, weighed down by the ongoing property sector headwinds and limited employment prospects, and ongoing resilience in the US, where the income squeeze has been offset by higher tax rebates and ongoing wealth effects from the surge in tech prices.

Globally, consumer spending appears set to soften further over the next couple of quarters given the squeeze on real wages and, for those reliant on credit, tighter financial conditions even as some areas of investment – particularly AI, but also increasingly energy infrastructure spending – hold up well.

## ...and, El Niño means food prices are still a big risk

Falling pump prices have mostly stabilised consumer and business confidence at a low level, but the risks surrounding food price inflation persist. Diesel costs have already hindered agricultural output in parts of Asia and future harvests in some economies will be affected by lower yields as the planting season was hit by the sharp rise in fertiliser and diesel prices in March and April.

There will still be some lagged effects, but now the potential food inflation story stems from the severe El Niño weather phenomenon, which has the potential to push up inflation materially in H2 2026 and into 2027 in many emerging economies, including the likes of India and Brazil. Even less-exposed advanced economies could be affected by the higher imported food prices as well as higher packaging costs given plastic prices.

## AI surge is potentially huge but uneven...

More positively from a growth perspective, if the energy price shock now fades, as the markets seem to be assuming, the AI boom is set to continue. The relative impacts of the capex, industrial production, export, profits and wealth effects between economies will vary enormously, though there might be scope for usage growth to become more widespread in the coming years.

GDP in Taiwan and the US is set to continue to be lifted the most from the AI boom, but other exporters will too. The enormous investment in the whole AI ecosystem that has continued to drive world trade growth even against a backdrop of tariffs, bilateral trade frictions, and an enormous supply shock. Our forecasts show slower, but still positive, growth in exports for most countries and a clear electronics-driven divide between Asia and the rest of the world. The impact of AI on consumer spending is set to be uneven, though, with big variations across age and income cohorts too.

## ...and poses other cost shocks in the coming year

Currently, the inflation risks of AI stem more from the enormous investment in the whole AI ecosystem that has continued to drive world trade growth even against a backdrop of tariffs, bilateral trade frictions, and an enormous supply shock.

Some of the inflation risks stemming from the AI boom could be more widespread. The sheer scale of AI construction and capex underway means many policymakers perceive that the demand-side effects on inflation might be more immediate than the supply-side effects on productivity and labour markets. The cost of construction from copper wiring to memory chip prices barely paused for breath at even the height of the disruption in April. Apple CEO Tim Cook stated in mid-June that soaring component costs have made price increases “unavoidable $^{1}$ ”.

Even assuming a full re-opening of the Strait by the end of Q3, not all inflation risks will fade as swiftly as the oil price fell in mid-June.

## Pressure on central banks, and maybe governments, could ease

Under our central scenario, the gradual re-opening of the Strait and the fall in crude oil prices should ease some of the demands on fiscal policy in the coming months.

In some economies, domestic demand should still be supported by the pre-war fiscal stimulus that had already been announced or delivered, notably in the US. But in many places, there has been a clear effort to contain gasoline price increases through either price controls or a temporary downside adjustment to fuel taxes, both to reduce the income squeeze from higher energy prices on consumers, and to limit the scope for inflation and interest rates to rise.

Assuming the drop in oil prices is sustained or extends further, the cost of such subsidies should ease somewhat. As every region of the world starts from a position where budget deficits and debts are larger and structural spending demands on defence, age-related spending and the energy transition are set to rise more sharply, any relief governments may get from lower inflation-linked spending and borrowing costs would certainly be welcome.

The different forces discussed in this report will also have an impact on FX. Since global oil and gas supplies started to be disrupted at end-February, the US energy surplus and safe-haven status have supported USD vis-à-vis all energy importers, excluding China. Our FX team forecasts that US growth exceptionalism, and a greater likelihood of a Fed rate rise than a cut this year, means USD should firm further vs most currencies (A new sheriff in town 18 June 2026).

So, whereas the implications of the fall in oil prices for inflation means we now think the ECB will not raise rates again and the BoE will not hike at all. Unlike the market, we think they could both cut in 2027, but we still believe several Asian economies will likely be under pressure to raise rates further in the second half of this year. In Latin America, we forecast an extended pause in Mexico and Brazil.

## Forecasts: the good and the ugly

Pulling all of these forces together, this year's global growth forecast of $2.5\%$ – the weakest pace since the pandemic – is unchanged, as is our 2027 forecast of $2.7\%$ , although that masks sizeable revisions to some economies.

As explained in Running out of gas?, March 2026, there is no winner from the big supply shock that was inflicted on the world by the conflict, but there are always relative winners and relative losers. As with 2026, the most notable upgrade to our 2027 forecast is to the US while the biggest downgrades are to the likes of India and Brazil, reflecting a weaker carryover from H2 2026 when both lagged effects of the energy shock and an anticipated impact from El Niño are set to weigh on the growth outlook.

Even with the drop in the oil price and our assumption that it will average USD75/b by H2 2027, the revisions to our inflation forecasts are upwards for both 2026 and 2027, driven almost entirely by the emerging world, notably across Asia.

In our ‘uglier’ scenario, based on a partial re-opening, whereby less than half of the pre-conflict crude oil passes through the Strait, the oil market would remain in deficit, reserves would continue to run down and prices edge higher over the next six months, potentially to USD140-150/b. Inevitably, the growth numbers would be much lower, particularly in the energy importers, although we would expect investment in alternative energy sources and bypass pipelines to be stepped up and the global AI investment story would not be derailed.

Inflation forecasts would be higher given the enormous array of products that would be affected, and we would expect monetary policy to be tightened more aggressively, pushing some economies into outright recession.

## Deal or no deal?

Progress towards a deal has pulled down oil prices

After more than 100 days, the US-Iran conflict has dragged on longer than most initially anticipated, but the recent signing of a deal sparked optimism in markets and crude oil prices, which had already been trending steadily down since mid-May after earlier spikes of up to USD120/b in April. Prices are around USD80/b as we publish.

The 14-point memorandum of understanding (MoU) signed includes:

\- The lifting of the US naval blockade on Iranian ports

\- The flow of oil through the Strait of Hormuz without payment of tolls to Iran

A waiver for Iran to sell oil for the duration of the 60-day ceasefire extension

\- Iran to reaffirm that it would not procure or develop nuclear weapons

Still lots of uncertainty

The MoU leaves many important questions unanswered and material risks persist, not least because the major political players in this conflict are not all on the same page. Since the MoU was signed, there have been reports of Strait of Hormuz closures $^{2}$ but also of up to 55 ships transiting in a single day $^{3}$ . Importantly, talks between the US and Iran are ongoing.

Even assuming the US and Iran make progress towards a final settlement, the outcome is far from certain. Iran has already indicated there will be no return to the pre-war status quo through the Strait.

So, while the central scenario on which our forecasts are based is unchanged, we also present an alternative more adverse scenario, whereby the Strait does not fully re-open and the oil price grinds higher, peaking around USD150/bl. That seems very unlikely currently, given Brent is trading at around USD80/bl, but we explain later how it could happen. Also, in a historical context, it would not be high in real terms compared with spikes in the 1970s (chart 2).

## Our central case of a sustainable re-opening of the Strait

It may still take time for Gulf oil production and shipments in the Strait to revive

Even assuming the Strait is gradually re-opened on a sustainable basis, things are not going to go back to normal overnight. Not only do we need to see evidence that the terms of the MoU hold, but also mines also need to be cleared, insurance reinstated, ships repositioned, Gulf storage capacity emptied, idled production fields restarted as well as downstream infrastructure across several Gulf producers, and global energy reserves rebuilt. That will take time, as will the negotiations on the nuclear programme, residual sanctions and much more, before any kind of final settlement can be reached (see US-Iran: A deal, still waiting for Hormuz re-opening, 15 June 2026).

Company commentary suggests that re-establishing normal flows from oil fields to markets could take anywhere from a week to three months, varying by area and operator. This supports a normalisation of flows by end-Q3, assuming the deal properly holds beyond the 60 days.

Nonetheless, even if supply normalises (relatively) speedily, demand will remain affected. First, China, with typical imports of 11.5mb/d (alone equal to around 80-90% of the net flows disrupted by the Strait closure) has significantly reduced its oil purchases in recent months (imports in May were 4.5mb/d lower than normal levels) and instead has been drawing down on inventories. This demand must return to global markets at some point.

Second, global inventories have stopped markets from feeling the full force of the disruption. For example, many refiners turned to the US for oil as a replacement for lost Middle East crude. Consequently, as of the week commencing 9 June (latest available EIA data), the US had exported roughly 134m extra barrels (over and above pre-conflict levels) since March. With US crude production essentially stable over that period, almost all of the uplift has come from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) release, and from commercial inventories. This has left US crude inventories at very low levels relative to previous decades.

Significant rebuilds will need to occur both in the US and globally, pushing medium-term demand up. And in the US, at least, this rebuild will be to above pre-conflict inventory levels. This is because the US SPR release does not involve selling barrels of oil, but rather ‘loaning’ them. Under the terms of the agreement, companies have to return the volume of oil ‘borrowed’ as well as interest (in the form of additional barrels) to the SPR at a later date (reportedly anytime between late 2026 and 2029, depending on specific contract terms $^{4}$ ).

## A partial reopening?

In a partial reopening scenario, the oil market would stay in deficit for longer. With oil supply structurally lower, inventories unable to indefinitely cushion the impact, and the prospect of higher demand in global markets as China steps up purchases given an eventual need to rebuild inventories, we could see much higher oil prices than in the base case. The degree of market tightness would depend on the specifics of any new arrangements for passage through the Strait of Hormuz.

Daily traffic in May was about 10-20% of the level seen pre-conflict. If it were to rise to about 50%, this would still leave about 5.5mbd (million barrels per day) of oil missing from markets in the short term (assuming existing pipelines are fully utilised), falling to c3.7mbd once the UAE's new Fujairah pipeline is completed (targeted by 2027). Compared with typical global daily oil consumption of roughly 103mbd, this is not huge – but can still have a significant impact at the margin given the need to rebuild inventories.

Despite some headlines to the contrary, our oil analysts do not subscribe to the idea that inventory ‘tank bottoms’ are ‘just weeks away’ – Q4 seems a more reasonable estimate of when levels would have started to hit operational minimums and it would be later even in a partial reopening scenario. However, if the prospect of a partial reopening of the Strait becomes the most likely scenario, then policymakers and industry may have to start looking at lower supply as the longer-term ‘new normal’.

## Bypass pipelines?

Over the medium term, more bypass pipelines could help. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline is already filling 3-4mbd of the c19mbd supply that would usually flow through the Strait, while the UAE's ADNOC pipeline has been adding an incremental 0.5mbd of flows – but both are essentially at capacity.

In the medium to long term, further capacity should be able to flow through its additional pipeline to Fujairah (on the UAE's south coast, bypassing the Strait), which due to be completed by 2027. Other potential future projects by the UAE as well as cross-border pipelines in the region are also under discussion.

## Or higher non-Middle East production?

The prospect of higher production outside of the Middle East seems limited in the medium term. Even the proposed new US-Canada pipeline is not set for completion until 2029 and the dynamism with which US shale oil producers can increase crude output is lower than it used to be. Uncertainty over long-term oil prices has made both US and Canadian oil producers more hesitant about investing in new rigs.

## Not just the crude oil price that would grind higher

With supply limited under a partial reopening scenario and crude prices grinding higher, to perhaps to USD140-USD150/b, there would likely be a higher degree of demand destruction in many economies. However, as already mentioned, the big reduction in demand for crude in recent months has been the short drop in China's imports of around 4.5mbd compared to pre-conflict levels. Under an extended partial reopening scenario, Chinese buyers would eventually have to return to the market. Coal has been a vital substitute in some areas, but after a significant drawdown inventory rebuilds would, over time, become a necessity even for China.

And of course, under the partial reopening story, it would not just be the crude price that would be affected. We have already seen how the price of refined products such as jet fuel, diesel, naphtha (for petrochemicals), gasoline, marine fuel oil, LPG, and many more are affected by Strait of Hormuz disruption.

## The rise in fuel costs has squeezed real incomes...

In terms of the economic implications of the conflict so far, the clearest impact on countries outside the Middle East region has been an auto fuel-driven rise in headline inflation and higher input costs for companies, whether from crude, diesel, jet fuel and various gas derivatives for which prices, while off their highs, are still 30% or so higher than pre-conflict levels.

## ...and higher energy costs will continue to feed through in CPI

On the inflation side, while the rise in energy prices is feeding through to headline inflation prints and squeezing real incomes, it is too early to expect any evidence of a more widespread impact on core prices.

Core inflation rates have risen, but so far they have mainly been pushed up by services directly related to fuel costs – so airfares, broader transportation costs and food away from home. These trends have further to go and in Europe there will be increases in household energy utility bills coming through from July/August. There are other idiosyncratic country stories where domestic factors have played a role too. Core inflation momentum has picked up in parts of Asia, with the 3m/3m rate higher than the annual rate (chart 14).

Now that the oil price has dropped, the acceleration in inflation should slow from here, peaking in many countries any time between June and the turn of the year, but it may be some time before central banks can be confident that the risk of a broadening of inflation has abated. Food prices tend to lag behind energy prices by 6-9 months and, as we discuss on pages 33-39, agricultural output faces additional risks from severe weather patterns. ## Will companies pass on the cost increases?

It appears that for now, there are many price pressures building in supply chains. In the global PMIs we can see how all sectors have seen a wide breadth of cost pressures. Almost all companies are feeling the squeeze from higher energy prices, but still relatively few appear to be needing or able to pass it on. PMI data often show higher readings for the share of companies seeing input price rises than they do for the share of companies reporting that that they are raising output prices. Nonetheless, the change in the number of companies experiencing higher input prices compared to the January level is much more noteworthy, both in magnitude and in breadth across sectors, than for output prices (charts 15-16).

Output prices for basic materials (where the cost pressures are more acute) and electronics (where there is huge pricing power given surging demand) and transportation are seeing more companies reporting that they are lifting output prices, but in other sectors the share has moved a lot less.

## ...but global trade is set to keep expanding...

Despite the various trade-related uncertainties through 2025 and early 2026, global trade continues to push ahead, with exports from all regions (besides the eurozone) growing robustly (chart 20). Much of this is due to AI-related exports, which we discuss in much more detail on pages 24-32. Based on the PMIs and industrial data, once the disruption in the Strait started, there was a surge in demand for certain products as companies brought forward purchases in anticipation of even bigger supply and price challenges ahead.

...and getting ahead of tariff changes

Some of the trade strength is also the result of renewed frontloading after US tariffs were sharply lowered on imports from some economies with the IEEPA tariffs being deemed unlawful. We now have some clarity on Section 301 (partner) tariffs, with 10-12.5% tariffs aimed at a host of trading partners (see: US tariff announcement: First tranche of investigations completed, 3 June 2026), but there are still a lot of questions about how the overall tariff picture will evolve in 2026 (see: State of US tariffs : What's in effect and what's to come?, 13 May 2026) and, in turn what the impact will be on the nature of global trade flows through the rest of the year, but we still see world trade growth expanding by nearly 5% in 2026.

## If the Strait only partly re-opens, growth would be hit more severely

In our ‘uglier’ scenario of a partial re-opening, the growth numbers are much lower, particularly in the energy importers, although we would expect investment in alternative energy sources and bypass pipelines to be stepped up. Inflation forecasts would be higher given the enormous array of products that would be affected, and we would expect policy to be tightened more aggressively, pushing some economies into outright recession.

Across EM, Thailand would be one of the hardest hit, potentially falling into recession as expansionary fiscal policy in 2026 leaves little space for further support in 2027. Most economies would see additional tightening. Though expectations for a recession are limited, as AI-driven export growth should continue to be a fillip for much of EM Asia, our economists there would expect growth to be 0.5-1pts weaker in most economies, skewed toward 2027. LatAm, driven by oil exporters Brazil and Mexico would be less hard hit, but the impacts would be nonetheless generally net negative. Globally, only Colombia and Russia might see higher growth in such a scenario compared to our base case.

## Could 2027 really be a year of fiscal tightening?

Going into 2027, fiscal factors could play a sizeable role, but in the other direction. The highly expansionary fiscal policies implemented by many countries this year, in many cases to soften the energy price shock, mean many will need to tighten their belts in 2027, even relative to 2025 deficit levels.

Take Thailand, for instance – the Thai cabinet announced a THB400bn (2.1% of GDP) loan decree, half of which will be used to finance consumer subsidies (and the other half to finance Thailand's energy transition). This has been a large factor in the upgrade to our 2026 growth (2.2%, previously 1.6%), and the downgrade to our 2027 forecast (1.7%, previously 2.6%). Malaysia saw its fuel subsidy bill skyrocket from MYR700m per month to MYR7bn (0.3% of GDP) per month – though some of this can be covered by oil revenue-linked additional funding (see: Malaysia in Focus: Decoding the subsidy maths, 22 May 2026).

In the eurozone, meanwhile, we estimate the fiscal stimulus of 0.5% of GDP in 2026, with the biggest contribution in Germany. However, the end of the EU Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF), in combination with the expected unwinding of fuel tax cuts, should mean a neutral to slightly restrictive fiscal stance in 2027.

Our budget balance forecasts suggest that (of the economies covered in this report), more than three-quarters will tighten fiscal policy in 2027, and nearly 60% will narrow their deficits below those of 2025, barring any new global shock.

## The surge continues...

The AI boom has carried on despite the conflict...

The main engine of global growth, which has so far been seemingly unscathed by the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, is the continued surge in investment in the entire AI ecosystem. ## Building it, but need to use it

While the huge pace of change in this transformative technology is set to continue driving capex, it is still far from clear whether usage will increase at a similar rate, resulting in a bigger impact on the broad economy. Usage certainly seems to be broadening out, even in countries where the capex and exports components have had a much smaller impact. ## Usage is still very low...

No matter whether you're an AI bull or bear, the fact is AI usage today, while more widespread in advanced economies than in the emerging world, is still very low. A YouGov survey of British workers in March 2026 found that just 32% of workers use AI tools in their jobs on day-to-day basis $^{6}$ . This fits with Anthropic's observed usage of AI, which shows most sectors are still some way from the frontier in terms of use cases (even if the methodology will overweight how many tasks in each job role could potentially use AI). Looking at it from a corporate survey perspective, smaller firms are currently only using AI in 15-20% of cases, but larger firms are using it more – closer to 30%. This impact looks set to grow for two reasons:

First, the cost of AI tokens continues to fall sharply $^{11}$ and will mean that more small companies can utilise the technology more widely. For many firms, AI spend is rising in aggregate as usage is rising more quickly than prices are falling (due to agents), but for firms yet to try a wide a selection of tools, the competition in the space will continue to push down prices for a given level of AI-related output.

Second, larger firms will work out how to use the tools more effectively – rather than burning through expensive tokens at a seemingly huge cost $^{12}$ . While the exact speed and timing of the growth of AI's impact on the labour market remains unclear, it seems more likely to damp hiring activity by many firms in the coming years even if, as with the internet, it could ultimately lead to some new nimble firms and jobs being created (so-called Jevons paradox) as AI significantly lowers barriers to entry for some new businesses.

When thinking about how this could play out in the coming few quarters, what happens with energy prices and interest rates could have a big role to play in the speed that we see both the investment in AI infrastructure and the spillovers into employment and productivity. The availability of reliable low-cost electricity is set to play a key role in the countries that lead in AI, with the IEA estimating that data centre electricity consumption will rise 15% per year 2024-30 (an increase more than four times the increase of all other sectors).

First, a longer closure of the Strait of Hormuz increases the risk of shortages of helium, which plays an important role in cooling, purging, and plasma control processes within semiconductor fabrication. Given that this is only 2% of costs and firms will be happy to pay higher prices, this could lead to some availability or more extreme pricing challenges within the sector, slowing the AI build out.

## Looming El Niño impact

Even in the event of a near-term re-opening of the Strait of Hormuz, the closure of which had limited supplies of fertilisers and diesel, upside risks to food prices are still rising given the growing prospect of a severe El Niño $^{13}$ weather event.

According to the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) El Niño conditions are now underway in the tropical Pacific, with sea surface temps having risen sharply in recent months, and NOAA estimates point to a north of 60% likelihood of a “very strong” event, known as a ‘super El Niño’, with a possibility that this is the strongest ever recorded.

## Lower income, agriculture-dependent economies will be most affected

While El Niño is a natural phenomenon, climate change has the effect of amplifying El Niño/La Niña cycles. Previously, El Niño events in 1981-82, 1996-97, 2015-16 and 2023-24 have been some of the strongest on record – but US weather service projections suggest that the 2026

cycle could be even more severe, creating risks for food production and prices and hitting growth in lower-income, more agriculture-dependent economies.

## Temperature and rainfall impacts will be important

Precipitation is also affected by El Niño, but the impact is more mixed with Central America and Southeast Asia (Indonesia in particular), as well as India, all expected to see less rain than usual this summer. South America, in contrast, along with northern Vietnam and the Philippines, are expected to see greater rainfall.

Europe, meanwhile, should see little direct impact from El Niño, even if weather forecasters are still pointing to a very hot European summer due to climate change.

Overall, India, Indonesia, Bangladesh, and Nigeria look particularly vulnerable. The expected El Niño event has already driven our India economics team to revise down growth forecasts (see India: Forget the rains, 11 May 2026) despite stronger than expected GDP growth in Q1.

## Possible upside for European and US farmers

Some studies $^{14}$ have suggested that in more temperate regions (Europe, North America), El Niño can actually have a positive impact as agricultural producers there see less impact on their output and can claim some of the demand (and potentially benefit from higher prices) that would typically be filled by El Niño-affected regions. Some crops, such as soybeans (for which the US is the world's second biggest producer after Brazil), could see improved yields in El Niño years. Of course, given the smaller share of agriculture in these economies, this generally has a very marginal effect (especially relative to much bigger economic losses in more affected regions).

## Food inflation impact could vary too

Since the probability of an El Niño event started increasing, there has been a rise in commentary around prospective global food inflation. And while most academic research suggests that an El Niño event will lift global food prices, we need to be wary of some of the nuance.

First, most estimates are based on long time series of historical data, but globalisation, shifting consumption patterns, and advances in agriculture have shifted significantly over time:

More widespread use of irrigation methods has meant that temperature is now the more important El Niño impact when it comes to India's food inflation than rainfall. In response, India has imposed a ban on the export of sugar until 30 September.

\- Globally, increased use of fertilisers, pesticides, and agricultural technology mean that a weaker El Niño event in the 1980s could have greater impacts on food prices than a stronger El Niño event in 2026.

Second, the impact of El Niño on prices varies enormously across and within regions. In India, there is a clear impact on food prices and, given the high share of food in household expenditure, and therefore headline inflation. There are clear impacts in Indonesia, Malaysia, Brazil and others too. However, academic studies $^{15}$ suggest that the inflationary impact of El Niño in the US and Europe is now marginal, if not non-existent.

## 2. Fertilisers

About $12\%$ of the world's globally traded fertilisers come from the Middle East, but that share is higher among phosphate and nitrogen fertilisers, and lower among potassium fertilisers. Most farms and economies use all three types.

This complicates vulnerabilities across countries (see chart 62) – for example, Canada is import-dependent for phosphate fertilisers, while Norway lacks potash production. India, Bangladesh, Thailand, and Sri Lanka all typically source more than 30% of their fertiliser imports from the Middle East. Australia, Türkiye, and South Africa are also high on the list.

But again, another nuance is the timing of planting seasons. In India, fertiliser imports are heavily concentrated in Q3 and Q4, which means supplies for 2026 should have been largely secured ahead of the disruption. For Thailand, though, fertiliser imports are often concentrated in Q2, exposing importers to higher prices.

The rising costs meant that some farmers were better off selling their stockpiled fertilisers rather than using them as part of the growing process $^{17}$ , which could lower yields into 2027. Some farmers are also shifting to more favourable crops; in Australia, higher fertiliser prices have led some farmers to move away from nitrogen-intensive crops, such as wheat, toward less fertiliser-intensive grains, such as barley. In France, it is estimated that maize, a fertiliser-intensive crop, could see the planted area shrink by 10-15% in 2026 $^{18}$ .

## Weathering the storm

Japan's resilience has held up amid the global energy crisis. Despite disruptions to Middle East energy flows since the US-Iran conflict escalated on 28 February, Japan has managed to remain on a stable footing over the past four months by securing Gulf shipments that bypass the Strait of Hormuz, increasing purchases of US energy, and drawing on its sizeable national reserves.

First quarter GDP this year outperformed market consensus, delivering a second consecutive quarter of positive growth. Exports have been a bright spot, underpinned by strong global demand for semiconductors, chip materials, chipmaking equipment, and other AI-related hardware. Yen weakness has also supported external demand by improving the price competitiveness of Japanese exporters. Meanwhile, private investment remains robust, reflecting positive business sentiment. The latest Tankan survey (26 February to 31 March) and the Finance Ministry's quarterly survey show cautious optimism, while corporate profits strengthened further, partly supported by the global AI capex upcycle.

Household confidence, however, is notably more fragile due to rising inflation. Even so, fiscal measures are cushioning household spending, including fuel subsidies introduced on 19 March that cap retail gasoline prices at JPY170/litre, planned summer electricity and gas subsidies of around JPY5,000 per household, and stimulus from the FY2025 supplementary budget, including a one-off cash handout of JPY20,000 per child starting in February 2026, all of which should help private consumption.

That said, downside risks to growth are becoming more visible. Export growth outside of AI-related categories is materially softer, and Japan's automakers, being slower to pivot to electric vehicles, are losing market share in Southeast Asia amid intensifying competition from Chinese brands. Inbound tourism has also softened, reflecting strained ties with China since last November. Private consumption is still positive but remains vulnerable given households' heightened sensitivity to inflation and the impact of Yen weakness on import costs. Although structural labour shortages are driving nominal wages, real wage growth could come under pressure if inflation rises further.

## One year, two shocks

Despite being three months into an energy shock, growth is holding up. We believe the front-loading of manufacturing activity has had an important role to play. Manufacturers are stocking up, leading to a rise in jobs, output, and stocks of finished goods. This is also keeping demand for working capital elevated, showing up in the c18% y-o-y credit growth.

Some of this could reverse in Q3 26. As front-loading activity softens, the true lingering impact of the energy shock could show up - higher cost of inputs and weaker exports. To this, we also need to add the indirect shock to many corporates who are not getting access to industrial feeds like petrochemicals.

Even before the energy crisis is fully resolved, El Niño has set in. Our analysis shows that rising temperatures and heatwaves are far more disruptive for food production and inflation than insufficient rains. The spread of irrigation facilities has helped in the management of drought-like situations. But there is no magic wand for dealing with heatwaves. We find that the temperature spikes in El Niño years are rising with time, and are now crossing important thresholds, where they not just impact perishable crops such as fruits and vegetables, but also hardy ones like rice, wheat, pulses, edible oils, poultry, and dairy.

On the back of the energy shock and its spillover impact on industrial feeds as well as the disruption caused by a likely El Niño, we forecast GDP growth to average 6.3% in FY27, versus 7.7% in FY26. We assume that half of the El Niño shock will show up in FY27, and the other half in FY28.

We also have an eye on India's medium term growth prospects. Here we believe that India's enhanced integration with world trade in an important development for growth. For a country which has dragged its foot on big trade deals, two big ones were finalised in quick succession with the EU and the US in early 2026. The former can have large economic benefits. After all, India and the EU make up $25\%$ of global GDP, but their bilateral trade is only $0.6\%$ of global trade. Enhanced integration can help both sides not just in trade, but also strategic partnerships in energy security, defence and financial services. ## Policy issues

We think the RBI took a split approach in its June meeting, going “all in” on FX with a broad package of measures, while staying more restrained on rates by holding the repo rate at 5.25%.

On the INR, the RBI opened a subsidised window for NRI deposits under which it will bear the full hedging cost for fresh 3–5 year deposits raised by banks. Even if near-term bond inflows are modest, these are positive, market-deepening steps, and could help pave the way for inclusion in new bond indices.

## Market strain, policy pivot

There is much attention on Indonesia these days. Across the major asset classes - equities, bonds and FX - Indonesia has underperformed the broad EM index. A cursory glance suggests that even though markets have not performed too well, the economy is chugging along fine. GDP rose 5.6% in the quarter ending March, higher than the 5.1% growth in 2025. And even though inflation has risen, it remains well within the 2.5-3.5% range.

A deeper review, however, suggests that the economy has started to gradually reflect the impact of the energy shock, and markets may be partly reflecting that. On growth, the latest readings show a fall in retail spending, consumer sentiments and export orders. There has been a significant amount of frontloading in fiscal expenditure, and belts may have to be tightened in subsequent months if the 3% fiscal cap is to be met. We forecast GDP to growth 4.7% y-o-y in 2026 (versus 5.1% in 2025).

PMI input prices have risen quickly, and are gradually pushing up output prices. We forecast inflation to average 3.5% in 2026 (versus 1.9% in 2025). The latest trade data shows a fall in the monthly surplus, with export volumes falling even as import volumes shot up. Meanwhile capital inflows remain sluggish, weighing on the exchange rate. What steps should Indonesia take as the impact of the energy shock shows up more starkly?

Supply shocks such as rising energy costs and adverse weather are always tricky to deal with because they come with trade-off. Generally, inflation rises and growth falls during these shocks. Often, policymakers have to choose between the two. Should they run policies that prop up growth, or policies that cool prices? Eventually it all comes down to country-specific circumstances and objectives. So, what should Indonesia do?

To answer this, we need to narrow down the main issue that needs to be addressed in Indonesia. Some would argue that it's the currency, the IDR. The economic fundamentals behind the fall in the IDR seems to be the balance of payments, which is likely to post its second negative annual reading in 2026. It's tempting to say that Indonesia doesn't have a c/a deficit problem, as it is quite low (-0.1% of GDP in 2025). Rather, the economy has a capital inflows problem, which has been rather weak over the last year (-0.3% of GDP in 2025). The truth is that the two are interlinked. A low c/a deficit reflects weak investment. Indeed, we find that corporates are cash rich but are not investing. And weak investment and growth prospects hurt capital inflows.

## Policy issues

The 11% depreciation in the IDR versus the USD since early 2025 has not incentivised substantial ‘expenditure switching’ (i.e. more exports, lower imports), and cannot be relied on to close the BoP deficit on its own. Will the Thai economy defy the challenges brought by the Middle East conflict?

Private consumption should also slow once the consumer subsidies are wound up, while households continue to face liquidity constraints. ## Policy issues

Like the intricacy of clockwork that tells us time as accurately as possible, monetary and fiscal policy will need to move intricately together to keep growth this year and next smooth and sustainable. That requires reacting not just to what's happening today, but also to what may lie ahead as fiscal policy, for example, may soon take a much-needed break.

Despite the energy shock, we take a contrarian view and expect growth to be above $2\%$ in 2026. This is because of Thailand's aggressive fiscal push and accommodative monetary policy. The administration's THB400bn fiscal stimulus will likely support consumption and investment. However, it will make use of most, if not all, of Thailand's remaining fiscal space; fiscal policymakers in Thailand have a voluntary public debt limit of $70\%$ of GDP. With public debt-to-GDP at $66.6\%$ as of April this year, policymakers will likely narrow Thailand's fiscal deficit in 2027 to comply with the limit they set.

Raising the voluntary public debt limit is another option. However, it has its trade-offs. Increasing bond supply would lead to higher interest rates and debt servicing costs – not the most ideal outcome for an economy that has seen subdued nominal GDP growth.

Hence, as the fiscal deficit has consistently widened over the past decade, we expect fiscal policy to take a step back in 2027 – a fiscally prudent move, but a move that would weigh on growth.

To make up for this, we expect monetary policy to do the heavy lifting. This would entail the Bank of Thailand (BoT) keeping monetary policy accommodative, despite the inflation risks looming over the economy. After all, businesses have found it difficult to pass on the cost of the energy shock to consumers. We expect the BoT to keep its benchmark policy rate unchanged at 1.00% until 2027 at the least. Keeping policy rates low would help soften the burden of tightening fiscal policy.

## Risks

A credit rating downgrade remains a risk to the Thai economy. This is because a downgrade could lead to higher interest rates and debt-servicing costs. The average coupon rate that Thailand pays on its bonds is 2.8% (source: Bloomberg). Any increase in interest payments would make it difficult for Thailand to outgrow its debt, especially with nominal GDP growth averaging only 3.0% since 2023. A credit rating downgrade may, thus, require Thailand to tighten its fiscal policy even more.

## Hanging in there

Stagflation has taken shape, at least in the Philippines. For one, growth continues to sour. In Q1 2026, growth came in at 2.8% y-o-y, stumbling to its slowest pace since 2009, excluding the COVID-19 pandemic. The culprits of the slowdown remain the same: public capital disbursements continue to fall at a significant rate while the uncertainty around public spending has led to households and businesses pulling back on their expenditure. Savings are up, while investment is down.

Understandably so – like a freelance worker, households can weather tough times by putting aside a larger portion of their incomes during good times. The most recent good time was 2025, when wages grew 7% on average while inflation was only 1.7%. However, instead of enjoying the increase in purchasing power by spending more, households chose to save. The share of households reporting that they were able to save before the energy shock in March 2026 was even higher than pre-pandemic levels, with the more vulnerable, low-income households leading the increase.

Unfortunately, this slowdown in demand has already spilled over to the labour market. The unemployment rate in the Philippines has risen above 5%. And soon, households and small businesses may need to dip into any savings they have recently accumulated.

This is because prices continue to rise amid slow growth. Currently at 6.8% y-o-y, headline inflation in the Philippines is the highest in the ASEAN region. The surge in energy prices has already spilled over into core CPI, with core inflation accelerating above the central bank's 2-4% target band. However, the outlook is set to become tougher, with price pressures on food – due to higher fertiliser prices and El Niño – likely to intensify in the coming months.

Hence, the economy is caught between a rock and a hard place. We expect growth to come in well below potential in 2026 and 2027, at 3.4% and 4.8%, respectively.

But the Philippines is hanging in there. Once the energy shock normalises, financial markets in the Philippines are likely to recover quickly. This is because the fiscal response has remained prudent, as officials opted for targeted welfare measures. In addition, a healthy degree of “demand destruction” should eventually come to the fore. Without controls, energy prices in the Philippines have reflected the true scarcity of commodities. Growth may be below potential, but once the dust settles, public debt and the current account will likely remain manageable and resilient.

## Policy issues

The French PMI composite has fallen to 44.9 in May (-5pts since February) versus 48.5 for the eurozone (-3.4pts since February). The sharp deterioration in INSEE consumer confidence (82 in May versus 91 in February, the lowest level since March 2023) has sent another negative message on the consumption outlook. Lastly, hard data have not been more reassuring: consumer spending on goods fell by 0.5% m-o-m in April, dragged down by energy spending (-2.9% m-o-m).

In spite of this bleak picture, we refrain from downgrading too sharply our growth forecasts. Some of the weakness in Q1 was caused by transitory factors (mild weather, a drop in construction ahead of the municipal elections), which may lead to some payback in Q2. Besides, as usual in any economic downturn, France may benefit from automatic stabilisers. In particular, the rise in the minimum wage in June (2.4% due to inflation indexation rules) should limit the hit to consumption.

In the end, we trim down our annual growth forecast for this year to 0.5%, instead of 0.6% in the interim forecast update that we published in May (see Eurozone forecast update, 12 May). Our growth forecast for 2027 remains unchanged at 0.8%.

## Ongoing resilience

The Spanish economy continues to grow strongly, so far appearing to shrug off the headwinds from the Middle East conflict. GDP grew by 0.6% q-o-q in Q1 – a slowdown from 0.8% q-o-q the previous quarter, but 0.1ppt higher than we had expected. Household spending was the main driver, also growing by 0.6% q-o-q. While this is largely looking in the rearview mirror, the indications for activity in Q2 look positive. While the consumer and business surveys have softened, the PMIs did return (just) to growth territory in May. What's more, following what we think was largely a weather-related slowdown at the start of the year, jobs growth as measured by social security registrations picked up in April and May. If recent monthly momentum continues, and the if fairly decent mapping with GDP holds, then growth could hold up at a similar pace in Q2 (left hand chart).

Although activity indicators are holding up, higher inflation is likely to act as a drag on growth. The CPI rate jumped above $3\%$ in March and stood at $3.6\%$ y-o-y in May, mostly on account of higher petrol prices. While recent energy price falls should lessen prospective inflation, pushing in the other direction is the expiry of a VAT cut on utility bills on 1 June and the prospective reversal of a petrol duty cut on 30 June. Against that backdrop, household real income growth is likely to slow. Beyond the squeeze on household incomes, there's a potential challenge for tourism due to pricier travel costs. And 31 August marks the EU deadline for RRF spending, so the recent growth boost from public investment is likely to gradually turn into a drag thereafter.

But there are reasons to think the economy can continue to stand up to these challenges. First, with energy prices well off their peak, and assuming limited second round effects, the inflation squeeze should be temporary. Second, there's perhaps reason to think that labour market strength can persist – much of the recent gains in employment have reflected high levels of immigration. Policy remains open to continued immigration, and a royal decree signed in April to offer temporary residence permits to undocumented migrants might regularise the work status of hundreds of thousands of workers (El País, 15 April). These supportive factors mean that while household income and spending growth is set to soften, the slowdown should be modest. Households may also have scope to ‘smooth through’ the income slowdown – the saving ratio stood at 12% at the end of last year, around double its pre-pandemic level.

## A still-benign inflation backdrop

So far, the conflict in the Middle East has had a relatively limited impact on Swiss inflation. It rose to 0.6% y-o-y in May from 0.1% in April, with the uptick being entirely led by prices of petroleum products. ## Policy issues

The outlook for the Norwegian mainland economy is slightly weaker than our previous forecast because of a higher policy rate and more persistent inflation. Consequently, we reduce our GDP estimate from 1.7% to 1.4%, just below trend. We leave 2027 unchanged at 1.7%.

We continue to expect solid, broad-based economic growth this year, with the strongest momentum coming from investment activity. Our central scenario still assumes a slowdown in 2027, largely reflecting the expected tapering of the Recovery and Resilience-related funding.

A key upside risk highlighted in our previous quarterly has materialised. Poland successfully signed the EU Security Action for Europe (SAFE) loan agreement in time to enable the distribution of two-thirds of the EUR 44bn envelope (around 4.5% of GDP) ahead of the end-May deadline for the so-called single-procurement stage. Looking ahead, the next major hurdle is the end-August deadline linked to Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) funding, which may be more difficult to meet. The government is targeting PLN100bn funding absorption this year and a PLN31bn tranche was disbursed in June. However, implementation bottlenecks in investment projects raise the risk that full absorption targets may not be achieved.

Indeed, Q1 2026 GDP figures were disappointing, with fixed investment rising only 0.3% quarter-on-quarter and slowing to 2.4% year-on-year. That said, more recent indicators offer some encouragement: construction activity has improved meaningfully, with a strong rebound in April and May. The manufacturing PMI remained in contraction for the 13th consecutive month in May, but the output sub-index moved above 50 – the second recent occurrence in the past 13 months (after March), suggesting pockets of stabilisation.

We maintain our view that consumption growth will moderate, primarily due to slowing wage dynamics. We expect real wage growth to ease from around 5% in 2025 to 3% in 2026, and to slow further in 2027. In fact, May real wage growth in the corporate sector fell to 2%, underscoring the downshift already well underway. Our long-standing expectation of wage moderation is supported by contained public-sector wage bill growth and minimum wage increases, both set at 3% in 2026. In June, the government proposed the same 3% levels for 2027.

Overall, we leave our 2026 and 2027 growth forecasts unchanged. Investment remains the key upside risk, meanwhile downside risks relate to private consumption reflecting subdued consumer confidence. Exports continue to hold up well, despite ongoing headwinds from weak eurozone growth.

## Policy issues

Compared with the heightened concerns in May, the recent decline in oil prices has shifted our focus back towards economic resilience, with policymakers more inclined to look through the inflationary impulse rather than respond aggressively to it. Indeed, the Polish central bank (NBP) appears broadly confident, based on June remarks from several Monetary Policy Council members, that it will not have to tighten policy. That stance is supported by two key considerations: moderating wage dynamics and expectations that the recent pickup in core inflation will prove temporary. After consistently undershooting through much of 2025, core inflation rose to 3.1% y-o-y in May, up from 2.5% y-o-y in February. This outcome exceeded the NBP's March forecast for Q2 2026 by 0.4–0.5 percentage points. However, the overshoot has been concentrated in oil-sensitive categories: transport services and packaged holidays, rather than led by a broad-based re-acceleration. That said, the risk that higher oil prices feed through more persistently into core inflation continues and remains a key watchpoint. We still expect headline inflation to test the upper end of the NBP's target range (3.5%) towards the end of 2026 and into early 2027. We are also still mindful of risks to the sustainability of recent optimism around the oil price outlook. Accordingly, the argument for a rate hike this year remains finely balanced. As a result, we pencil in one hike in Q4 2026, to be reversed towards the end of 2027.

Beyond the near-term inflationary risks, we retain a longer-term easing bias, but it is explicitly conditional on fiscal tightening. This is increasingly necessary given Poland's rising debt trajectory and persistently elevated budget deficits.

On our current forecasts, Poland should record the largest budget deficit in the EU in 2027, at over 6% of GDP. In parallel, we expect Poland to see the largest post-pandemic and post-energy-crisis debt accumulation, with public debt rising by around 19% of GDP over four years, through end-2027.

Fiscal policy risks remain unaddressed with policymakers' inaction ahead of what is shaping up to again be a tightly contested parliamentary election in autumn 2027. Positively, so far the public spending focus remains on investment.

## Facing domestic and external shocks

Türkiye's outlook has deteriorated first in the wake of the Iran war, then as domestic political noise started to grow again in May. Q1 GDP data showed that activity slowed sharply, even though the impact of higher oil prices was not yet visible in March and Türkiye suffered no supply shortages. Following the very weak Q1 print, we revised down our full-year 2026 growth forecast from 3.0% to 2.2%. Our 2027 forecast is 3.7%.

Following negative surprises in the recent data, we have also made several upward revisions to inflation forecasts. And even after updating our oil price assumptions in the wake of the US-Iran peace agreement, we continue see headline CPI at 28% and 15% y-o-y by end 2026/27.

The direct impact of higher fuel costs on headline inflation was in part absorbed by a temporary automatic pricing mechanism, whereby the special consumption tax declines to offset up to 75% of the rise in fuel costs. Brent oil prices have increased by 30% in TRY terms over March-May, while the “fuels and lubricants for personal transport” category in the CPI basket has seen a cumulative price increase of 16% over the same time period, adding 0.5pp to headline CPI. Separately, the energy regulator increased residential electricity and gas tariffs by 25% effective 4 April, which added a further 0.6pp to headline CPI. But given the un-anchored nature of inflation expectations in Türkiye, the pass-through from rising energy prices to other prices has been high. Transportation service prices, for example, have risen by more than the increase in fuel costs. 12-month forward-looking inflation expectations of firms and households have also risen at a faster pace than we were expecting, despite currency strength.

We continue to see the current account shortfall at around 3% of GDP this year. Net FDI and portfolio inflows year-to-date have been close to zero. Despite market turbulence, external borrowing by Turkish banks and firms has remained strong, with rollover ratios for long-term loans well over 100% in the first four months of this year. If there is a durable improvement in risk appetite, short-term funding flows could also see a pickup.

## Policy issues

We think a key reason why there has been no rate rise after the outbreak of the Iran war is the absence of FX demand from Turkish residents. We made sharp adjustments to our core macro forecasts for the Kingdom following the onset of the Middle East conflict, premised on a view that the conflict would ease into the second half of the year, allowing activity to steadily normalise. As a result, we for now make no material revisions to projections for the Saudi economy, while also recognising the high level of uncertainty that continues to surround them.

We expect this pick up in momentum to continue to build into next year when we see growth pushing above 5.5%. The forecast is premised on a sharp rise in oil exports as traffic through the Strait of Hormuz normalises and OPEC keeps production quotas loose amid global inventory restocking. But we also look for non-oil activity to firm as confidence recovers and supply-chain disruption fades. Here we are encouraged by the Q1 GDP print which showed non-oil output up 0.3% q-o-q even as the broader economy contracted, as well as high frequency data that suggest sentiment and activity picked-up in April and May after dipping in March as the crisis began. Pre-conflict data also show FDI inflows at a record high.

While renewed geopolitical volatility would set back the growth story, Saudi Arabia's balance sheet strength has allowed it to weather this year's conflict with no sign of market strain. Red Sea access also means the Kingdom's export receipts have been sheltered from the Strait of Hormuz disruption. We expect this to result in a budget deficit of a little under 5% of GDP this year – down 1ppt on 2025 despite the spending pressures of the crisis – falling to under 3% of GDP in 2027 as higher oil production offsets softer prices. After a run of sharp gains, we anticipate this outturn will see public debt settle at around 35% of GDP. We also look for the current account deficit to narrow this year and to return to surplus in 2027, with the overall balance of payments position likely to be strengthened by sizeable debt and equity capital inflows. We are comfortable, too, with prospects for price stability with inflation holding below 2% y-o-y in May despite the supply challenges brought by the crisis, and likely to remain contained as rents decelerate.

## Policy issues

While we are comfortable with the near-term outlook and confident of the Kingdom's capacity to weather any fresh external shocks, two sets of policy challenges complicate the view. Most immediately, 2026 was due to be a year of fiscal consolidation, with the budget targeting a $5.5\%$ drop in spending on last year's outturn to cap gains in public debt. This adjustment always appeared challenging to deliver but now looks more testing still given the sustained increases in military and infrastructure outlays the war is likely to bring – concerns heightened by the $20\%$ y-o-y rise in spending in Q1. Provided oil prices remain firm, the Kingdom has scope to absorb these additional outlays but only at the cost of delaying debt stabilisation to next year, while revenue declines would intensify pressure to accelerate broader fiscal adjustment.

Adding to the challenge, off-budget spending delivered through the Public Investment Fund (PIF) was also due to trend lower this year as the government scaled back more ambitious elements of Vision 2030. In addition to these cyclical challenges, Saudi Arabia must also look to frame the next phase of its structural reform programme. A raft of policy changes to the business environment and social system have encouraged a sharp increase in investment since Vision 2030 was launched in 2016. The task of driving these long-term gains is likely to be further complicated by the Middle East conflict, with the conflict bringing into sharp relief the challenge regional political tensions pose to supply chains and security, creating fresh headwinds to plans for manufacturing and tourism to emerge as long-term growth drivers.

## Risks

Unresolved political tensions remain the key threat to the Kingdom's economic outlook - a fresh round (or even persistent fears of renewed insecurity) would weigh on confidence and recovery. Beyond this, we look closely to see what impact demand losses and a weaker supply discipline have on oil prices, particularly following the UAE's withdrawal from OPEC. While losses are unlikely to be immediate, a price that settled below USD70/b in 2027 would weigh heavily on nominal balances and start to constrain policy choices.

The South African economy had a solid start to 2026, but the impact of the global energy shock is visible in the available Q2 data. However, if these revisions materialize and validate a more challenging outlook, we do not expect Banxico to discuss rate hikes any time soon, as market instruments price-in (i.e. we think markets instruments would need to erase the hikes that are incorporated in the curve). In fact, risks are biased to the downside, as we think Banxico would take advantage of any opportunity to cut more in 2027.

On the fiscal side, risks are deteriorating rapidly. We expect wider deficits which, combined with lower-than-expected GDP growth, could lead to a steep climb of debt-to-GDP in 2026 and 2027. The 2027 Economic Program that will be presented in September will prove key to assess the credibility and plans to prioritise fiscal consolidation. We think lower-than-expected revenues and limited room to the manoeuvre the already-stretched spending will dominate.

## Risks

Key risks relate to the USMCA review process, scheduled to start on 1 July. Business sentiment will follow the progress of these conversations, which could weigh on investment. A deal before the end of 2026 would be ideal, but it is not clear this is the base-case scenario now. Instead, the scenario of annual reviews to extend discussions into 2027 is strengthening.

## Multi-speed economy

The Argentine economy continues to witness a multi-speed economy. On one hand, primary sectors have been doing well, especially the oil & gas and agricultural sectors, thanks to growing local production alongside high global prices. However, the manufacturing and services sectors have been struggling, with an overvalued currency partly to blame. This has also created a divergence in fortunes between urban and rural parts of the economy, and most importantly for markets, affected President Milei's approval rating, which hovers around 40%. As for growth, we see the economy decelerating to 3.5% this year given the aforementioned dynamics, although activity appears to be bottoming out in 1Q and we should see a modest recovery throughout the rest of the year. The government is focusing on accelerating growth, primarily through reform implementation and monetary policy settings. While Argentina has yet to establish a more conventional monetary policy framework, market interest rates have been driven lower lately, which should help boost activity at the margin, while also contributing to our expectation for modest FX depreciation for the remainder of 2027. We see growth rebounding to 3.7% next year, still driven by primary sectors, but more balanced than in 2026. Fortunately, the government has continued to improve Argentina's fiscal outlook by keeping expenditure firmly under control. The government appears on track to achieve a primary surplus of 1.5% of GDP this year, which is stabilising debt and, most importantly, improving inflationary dynamics. While inflation progress started to slow in 2H25 and into 2026, lately we have seen more encouraging signs as we go into Q3 2026. We believe progress in lowering inflation is unlikely to be linear, but fiscal management suggest the direction of travel will ultimately stay positive. We see inflation at 28.25% by end-2026 and 21.50% by end-2027.

One of the more significant risks for inflation is in the FX realm. The exchange rate remains overvalued and is becoming stronger as inflation outpaces the rate of ARS weakness vs the USD. The FX bands now widen at the pace of inflation (2-months prior), but the USD-ARS rate is now comfortably inside the bands (left chart below). More important is that the stable currency relative to inflation is seeing the ARS real exchange rate appreciate to overvalued levels (right chart below). An overvalued peso is affecting the tourism and manufacturing sectors, impacting growth. Some adjustment in the exchange rate ultimately needs to be taken to encourage direct investment and rebalance key accounts, but this may only happen after 2027 elections. Even better, we believe, would be an abandonment of the FX bands altogether, allowing USD-ARS to float freely. This could result in a modest devaluation and a temporary spike in inflation, but we believe that over the longer term this would be net beneficial for the economy.

## Policy issues

Since obtaining more congressional support in last year's mid-term elections, President Milei has been able to move forward with his legislative structural reform agenda, moving beyond the decree-focused approach he pursued until that point. He managed to pass his first budget for 2026, institutionalising the fiscal consolidation effort, and passed a significant labour reform, arguably the most important pending structural reform. The reform makes the labour market more efficient and should help boost overall economic competitiveness. Important pending reforms are pension reform, a tax reform, and broad-based administrative reform.

While the passage of labour reform was a significant accomplishment, it also contributed to a decline in President Milei's approval rating at the start of the year (El Pais, 27 February), and while it has recovered somewhat, it remains below the average since the start of his government. This has resulted in some investors already thinking to general elections in October 2027 and President Milei's chances at securing re-election and thus continuing the reform trajectory. The government's focus remains fixed on boosting growth performance, but managing this, together with the ongoing effort to reduce inflation while maintaining a stable exchange rate will be difficult.

In terms of exchange rate policy, a strong harvest and growing oil and gas exports gave the central bank the ability to boost FX reserve purchases while even allowing for some pockets of ARS strength. We estimate total purchases of USD9.8bn ytd through May. Net reserves now stand at (negative) -USD5.2bn, from -USD11.7bn at end-25. We believe that going forward, as agricultural exports slow post-2Q, it will be difficult to achieve both reserve accumulation and maintain a stable FX rate. We see USD-ARS drifting higher at an average monthly pace of 2.9% for the rest of the year. We believe a more substantial FX liberalisation, including removal of remaining capital controls, is only likely post-elections.

## Risks

Risks to the current economic plan have alleviated somewhat due to the surprising victory of Milei's party in the congressional elections in October. Now the risks are mostly concentrated in the FX sphere, which is a high-stakes balancing act. As detailed above, the FX band is likely not sustainable, and it's probable that the government will either need to make an adjustment to the rate that it widens at or, better yet, drop FX controls all together. This is because net reserves remain low, even though the government has managed to significantly ramp up USD purchases this year.

2026 has been an extremely eventful year for the Colombian economy filled with immense policy uncertainty leading up to the recent presidential election. ## Policy issues

The recent presidential election is a watershed moment for Colombia. Abelardo de la Espriella is due to assume the presidency on 7 August, thus ending the first left-wing administration in Colombia's history. President Gustavo Petro had pursued an ambitious reform agenda, but most importantly from a macro-economic perspective, his government oversaw a significant deterioration in the country's fiscal health and rise in debt-to-GDP levels. He also instituted a ban on oil & gas exploration, which has kept overall investment subdued, and clashed with the central bank, threatening its independence (Semana, 10 April). There were fears that a continuation of his party's rule under Senator Ivan Cepeda would have led to further fiscal and institutional deterioration (Infobae, 2 June).

President-elect Abelardo de la Espriella and his government will be able to quickly achieve some low hanging fruit, such as allowing fresh oil and gas permits and reducing pressure on the central bank. The President will also have to negotiate with centrist parties to achieve his legislative agenda, which seems doable, but will require political capital.

## Risks

The passing of elections reduces the most significant risk facing Colombia's economy. Now the main risk will be President De La Espriella's administration failing to significantly reduce fiscal expenditure and re-anchor fiscal deficits. Failure to deliver on market expectations could lead to disappointment in the FX and rates markets. The other significant risk is El Niño. Colombia tends to see an inflation shock in strong El Nino years given how the reduction in rainfall impacts hydroelectric power generation, resulting in increased use of more expensive alternatives such as coal and gas. It also leads to crop disruption which can push food prices higher, while also impacting the coffee crop, a key export.

## On a solid footing, despite oil prices

The Chilean economy started 2026 on a weak footing. Growth was already subdued going into the start of the Middle East conflict and corresponding oil price shock, which had a large impact on Chile's economy given its position as a significant oil and gas importer. Moreover, in an effort to demonstrate fiscal responsibility, the government passed through nearly the entirety of higher energy prices onto consumers and businesses, resulting in dual growth and inflation shocks.

In terms of growth, activity was already trending lower at the start of the year when oil prices spiked. The government allowed consumer gasoline prices to rise between 30-45%, while diesel prices rose 55-60%. While this had a clear first-round impact on inflation, it also resulted in reduced disposable household income and sparked some demand destruction as well. We see growth falling to 1.4% this year, before rebounding sharply to 2.9% next year. We are optimistic on growth in Chile over the coming years as the newly inaugurated Kast Administration has been squarely focused on boosting activity. The government used considerable political capital to pass an economic reform which lowers corporate taxes and pursues deregulation, with a goal to drive investment growth. With a large pipeline of FDI projects, a large part in mining, we believe this reform can have a tangible impact. The government is also focused on fiscal consolidation, which all else equal will take a toll on growth in the coming years, but should be offset by gains in investment.

In terms of the inflation impact, following a significant first-round effect which derailed the previous inflation trajectory, we have yet to see second-round effects and in fact the latest inflation print surprised on the downside. Meanwhile, 2-year inflation expectations have remained anchored at the target, suggesting reduced risks. With global prices now falling, local inflation risks will also fade into the background. We see inflation returning very close to the 3% target by the end of 2027, thus obviating the need for monetary tightening. We see the monetary policy rate unchanged at 4.50% over the foreseeable future.

## Policy issues

Encouragingly, the government kicked off its legislative agenda with a substantial 40-measure economic reform package designed to spur domestic and foreign investment and boost productivity. Most notably, corporate taxes will be cut from 27% to 23% over time, and the government will provide tax and investment incentives, alongside a deregulation push. We believe this reform should tangibly drive growth higher in the coming years.

In terms of fiscal policy, during his campaign trail, Mr Kast pledged to cut the federal budget by up to USD6bn (1.6% of GDP) within the first 18 months of his presidency (Bloomberg, October 19). Due to the structural nature of spending increases, we always thought such a pace of consolidation was unlikely. Making matters worse, the government revealed a sharper-than-expected deterioration in fiscal accounts in 2025, with a structural deficit of 3.7%, a contravention of the fiscal rule. This has resulted in a slower-than-expected pace of fiscal consolidation this year, but the direction of travel is nonetheless positive.

## Risks

We believe the overall balance of risks for the Chilean economy are skewed to the upside now that the oil price shock appears to be fading. That said, any additional disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz will impact the Chilean economy given its sensitivity to oil and gas imports. Fiscal risks have become more significant following the upward revisions to 2025 fiscal performance, and it will be important for the government to deliver ongoing fiscal consolidation through limiting expenditure growth, as pro-growth measures will not be enough.

# Key debates before the numbers: cyclical air-pocket or a structural reset?

Ahead of luxury results season kicking off this week, we frame the key investor debate facing the sector over the next 12-24 months: is the slowdown of the past two years a cyclical air-pocket that normalises as comps ease and China stabilises, or is the sector undergoing a genuine structural reset in its growth drivers? China remains the single biggest swing factor either way.


Inside this note we unpack the key elements behind this debate – namely why the bulls see this as cyclical (easier comps, US holding up, China stabilisation, AI wealth etc.) and why the bears see this as structural (customer loss, pricing power used up, general luxury fatigue, rise and acceptance of the second-hand market, democratisation of luxury in the West may be reversing, shift towards wellness & longevity, etc).

## What would change our mind

\- Towards the cyclical camp: evidence that recent product/mix repositioning (accessible bags, entry points) is durably restoring volumes rather than just flattering one quarter; creative reinvigoration driving its own demand; a sustained (not one-quarter) re-acceleration in Chinese footfall/spend independent of the equity market wealth effect;

\- Towards the structural camp: further evidence that second-hand growth is cannibalizing (rather than expanding) the primary-market customer funnel; continued store rationalisation in China Tier 2-3 cities alongside flat-to-negative net openings in core Europe; industry commentary continuing to flag pricing/value-perception issues rather than volume recovery; a further delay in China property stabilisation past the 2H 2027 timeline currently being cited by our contacts.

## The bulls vs the bears; China still the swing factor

## Why bulls see this as cyclical

\- Comps are genuinely easier. 2026 laps two years of destocking/demand normalisation in China and a soft US-election-adjacent comp base; bulls argue this alone should flatter growth optically.

\- US demand still holding up, with our channel checks showing no clear slowdown as the region "comps the comp" following the post-election 2024 acceleration.

\- China: signs of a slow inflection, not just easier comps. Equity market strength is starting to offset (though is not yet fully offsetting) the property drag on household wealth; anecdotal evidence of returning shopping queues and firmer second-hand prices since late 2025 supports a bottoming narrative rather than a structural impairment of the Chinese consumer. In fact, year to date, the cumulative change in A share market capitalisation (up RMR +10trn) exceeded the cumulative value destruction in the housing asset value (down RMR -6trn), as the tech heavy Shenzhen index is up c.+9%) – see our note here. In addition, the savings rate in China remains elevated, and the bulls argue that the narrative that Chinese consumers fundamentally like luxury has not changed (they are holding back their purchases in light of the property market downturn, and once sentiment changes they will have savings to spend).

\- Creative reshuffling could generate its own demand. The unusually large wave of new Creative Director appointments across the industry (more in the last few months than at any point in \~30 years) is viewed by our channel checks as a potential supply-side demand catalyst, alongside a pendulum swing back toward a more maximalist aesthetic. Chanel's recent apparent success can be seen as evidence of the former (see our notes Read across from Chanel's 2025 results and Luxury 2026: If Chanel wins, at whose expense?)

\- The aspirational customer hasn't disappeared, but is dormant. Recent product/mix moves (ex. Chanel's sub-€6k "25" bag) suggest that where brands have corrected pricing architecture, volumes have responded, implying the customer base is recoverable rather than permanently lost.

\- AI wealth creation boom should be supportive for luxury spend. Near-term AI-driven wealth creation (newly minted millionaires, elevated bonus pools) should provide a cyclical boost to luxury demand. As per the Knight Frank Wealth Report 2026, between 2021 and 2026, the global population of UHNWIs (those worth more than US\$30m) rose from 551k to 713k. That equates to +162k new UHNWIs in just five years, or 89 people crossing the US\$30m threshold daily. Looking ahead, Knight Frank forecasts +235k more UHNWIs by 2031 (or 129 per day).

\- GLP-1 incremental boost. The rise of GLP-1s is creating a new incremental source of apparel demand, particularly relevant for high-end brands, as adoption today skews disproportionately towards affluent consumers, and weight-loss driven size changes are triggering a higher-than-normal wardrobe refresh cycle. This effect is also amplified by a reward/self-reinvention dynamic, whereby consumers upgrade both fit and style following weight loss (and this reward intention could skew towards luxury).

## Why bears see this as structural


\- General luxury fatigue. In response to slowing volumes, we believe brands intensified marketing and commercial initiatives to defend share. In an interview with Business of Fashion (October 30th 2024), Chanel Watches & Jewellery president Frédéric Grangié observed that "an overly commercial approach to fuelling luxury has weakened the allure brands have held over many years", introducing 'luxury fatigue' – essentially a lack of excitement and exclusivity in the industry. This could put further pressure on demand, if consumers struggle to justify such expensive purchases.


\- China has shifted from a growth engine to a harder-to-underwrite pillar. Even our more bullish industry contacts now talk about +MSD%-to-+HSD% medium-term growth in China, not the consistent double-digit growth that drove \~60% of the industry's expansion pre-2019. The read-through from the property market (\~70% of China's household wealth, still confirmed as declining per the most recent data, with some contacts not expecting recovery before the second half of 2027) is a bigger and more structural swing factor than in past cycles, and the economy looks increasingly K-shaped: HNWI spending (aided by equity market gains) is fine, but the aspirational middle class (historically the volume growth engine) remains constrained by weak job/wage confidence despite near-record savings rates.

\- A shift towards wellness & longevity. As highlighted by Bain at our luxury conference recently, luxury spend is undergoing a structural shift towards “experiential indulgence”, with consumers increasingly reallocating wallet share from products to experiences linked to wellness, longevity and self-reward. However, the impact is highly segment-dependent: aspirational consumers are increasingly forced to switch spend away from goods towards experiences, exacerbating weakness in entry-level luxury, while HNWIs/UHNWIs retain the capacity to spend across both categories, with experiences acting as a complement rather than a substitute, we believe.

## China: still the swing factor

Both sides of the debate ultimately collapse into a China argument. Bulls point to early, tentative green shoots (equity-market-driven wealth effects, some returning mall queues, firming second-hand prices) as evidence that the worst is behind us and that China can again become a (partial) tailwind. Bears counter that:

\- The property market remains the dominant driver of Chinese household wealth and is still declining, with local contacts pushing out the recovery timeline into the second half of 2027.

\- The market is increasingly K-shaped, with the aspirational/middle-income cohort (not HNWIs) being the historical volume driver, and that cohort remains under pressure from job security and AI-related white-collar concerns.

\- The rise of dupes and second-hand supply in China specifically dilutes the pricing power and scarcity value that underpinned brand economics there.

\- Some investors are concerned about potential parallels with Japan's property-led slowdown in the 1990s/2000s, where repeated "false starts" in real estate led to a much more prolonged consumer recovery than initially expected: the key risk being that China's recovery, if and when it comes, is neither quick nor linear.

## Longer term, China optimists would note the market remains structurally

underpenetrated relative to its size (and a bull case scenario could be that China goes in the direction of Korea where luxury spend per capita is among the highest in the world, >\$325/capita vs. China's \$55). And in parallel newer and emerging markets should gradually follow a similar path of luxury adoption as their middle and upper-middle classes expand, supporting the next wave of consumer stratification and premiumisation. At Kering's CMD in April, management noted a "6-pack" of newer markets (Middle East, India, Southeast Asia, Brazil, Mexico, Nigeria) which already represent \~€34bn in luxury demand versus China's \~€42bn, as per Bain Altagamma estimates, and could eventually replicate China's role as a growth engine. But even the most positive framing pushes that diversification

benefit out several years, leaving China as the swing factor for 2027/2028 numbers.

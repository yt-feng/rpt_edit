# European Software and Services: The AI tide won't lift all boats
## Specialist Sales

AI is forcing a structural reset across European software and IT services. This note synthesizes our H1 research, showing AI is already reshaping business models, pricing and margins, not just productivity. Our proprietary frameworks (AI leverage scorecards, disruption mapping, platform positioning) provide a consistent lens on winners vs losers. We reiterate ratings, but incrementally raise conviction in platform-led software and “Services-as-Software” leaders, and bring forward the timeline for meaningful differentiation and dispersion.

AI is shifting software from applications to control planes, driving monetization dispersion. The core shift is from “systems of record” to AI-enabled “systems of action,” where value accrues to platforms embedding AI into workflows. Differentiation is now economic - ARPU uplift, retention and pricing power tied to automation - rather than feature breadth. The bull case is margin expansion through productivity and bundling; the bear case is AI commoditization before pricing catches up. Cloud remains a stabilizer, but AI execution now defines growth and returns.

AI is transforming services from labor arbitrage to “Services-as-Software.” Enterprise behavior is pivoting faster than expected, with c.60% of clients planning to replace people-led delivery by 2028. This drives a shift from time-and-materials to outcome-based, AI-enabled models - expanding TAM while compressing legacy revenue pools. This is structural, not cyclical. Winners combine consulting, AI and proprietary IP to deliver scalable, recurring services; laggards remain exposed to labor-heavy delivery and rising pricing pressure.

AI impact is bifurcated - driven by segment economics, not company labels. Our disruption framework (automatability vs defensibility) shows outcomes depend on revenue mix. High-defensibility platforms (ERP, databases, embedded workflows) remain resilient and monetize AI as a layer. High-automatability segments (analytics, BPS, application development) face structural pricing pressure. AI is rarely existential - it reallocates value across the stack - driving widening dispersion between “AI transformers” and low-moat models.

Stock views reflect this dispersion, reinforcing selective conviction. We remain Outperform on platform-led software (SAP, Dassault Systèmes) and AI-enabled services leaders (Capgemini, Indra), where monetization, demand visibility and margin trajectories are strongest. Indra remains a Best Idea, with AI, defense and space accelerating re-rating potential. Capgemini screens as a high-conviction “AI transformer” despite near-term noise. We stay cautious on labor-led models, where pricing pressure may outpace cost adaptation. Valuations do not yet fully reflect this gap.

Bottom line: AI is a stock-picking regime, not a sector uplift. We reiterate ratings but sharpen our stance: own platforms and IP-led services that capture AI economics through pricing, data control and scalability. Conviction increases in early “AI compounders” and Services-as-Software leaders, while risks rise for low-defensibility models. The timeline for differentiation is compressing - execution on AI monetization is now the key driver of relative performance.

## GLOBAL SOFTWARE: MID-MARKET ERP AT THE AI INFLECTION POINT

AI is redefining mid-market ERP economics, shifting the sector from a cloud-driven upgrade cycle to an execution-driven AI investment debate. Investors should engage as valuation dispersion is set to widen meaningfully, with the market still largely pricing ERP as a steady SaaS compounder rather than an AI execution story. Our incremental analysis introduces a proprietary AI leverage scorecard and vendor-level positioning framework, highlighting where AI is already driving growth, margins, and cash flow versus where benefits remain theoretical. We reiterate ratings (Outperform: Microsoft, Oracle, SAP, Workday; Market-Perform: Sage) and see conviction strengthening around early AI compounders, extending the importance of stock selection over sector exposure.

AI is emerging as the primary driver of differentiation - and dispersion - across ERP. The report's core incremental insight is that ERP is moving from “systems of record” to “systems of action,” with AI-enabled workflows becoming the key value layer. Penetration of AI-enabled ERP is expected to approach c.90% by 2030, but early copilots are already commoditized; differentiation is shifting toward agentic, autonomous workflows that automate finance, supply chain, and HR end-to-end. The implication is a widening gap between vendors that embed AI deeply into workflows (capturing ARPU uplift and retention) and those deploying surface-level features with limited monetization.

AI economics will determine whether margins expand or compress. The central debate is the shape of the AI ROI curve relative to the cost curve. In the bull case, AI lowers cost-to-serve, shortens implementation cycles, and supports premium bundling, driving operating leverage and higher free cash flow conversion. In the bear case, AI becomes table stakes before pricing catches up, forcing vendors to absorb elevated R&D and compute costs with limited revenue upside. Our analysis suggests margin dispersion will widen structurally, as scale players benefit from hyperscaler economics while sub-scale vendors risk margin dilution.

Cloud has shifted from growth catalyst to stabilizer, raising the bar for AI. While mid-market ERP spending remains attractive (c.13% CAGR through 2030), cloud migration no longer provides meaningful upside; remaining workloads are complex and services-heavy. Incrementally, this implies the next leg of growth and margin expansion must come from AI-driven productivity and monetization—not migration volumes. This reframes ERP from a secular SaaS growth story into an execution story on AI deployment, pricing discipline, and time-to-value.

Competitive dynamics are bifurcating between scaled platforms and focused specialists. Our AI scorecard highlights that large platforms (Microsoft, Oracle, SAP, Workday) benefit from unified data, R&D scale, and ecosystem advantages, strengthening cross-sell and lock-in. However, specialists (e.g., Sage, Visma, Infor) retain defensible niches where domain-specific AI delivers faster ROI. The market is likely to reward both ends, while undifferentiated generalists face pressure as AI investment requirements rise.

This is now a stock-picking market with clear leaders and laggards. We see Microsoft and Oracle as “sooner” AI compounders already translating AI into ARPU and margin benefits; SAP and Workday remain execution-dependent; and Sage offers a more defensive AI-enhanced earnings profile. Our overall conclusion is that AI will not lift all boats - returns will accrue to vendors that convert AI capability into sustainable pricing power and operating leverage, reinforcing selective positioning and conviction in AI leaders.

## GLOBAL SOFTWARE: THE COMING AI SUPERCYCLE IN SUPPLY CHAIN SOFTWARE

Supply chain software is entering a structural AI-driven supercycle, and we reiterate Outperform ratings across key beneficiaries (SAP, Oracle, Capgemini, Microsoft) as this report frames why the market is underestimating the durability and scale of growth. Consensus largely sees SCM as a steady modernization theme; we argue it is a full-stack reinvention, with AI acting as a new operating system. Incrementally, we combine proprietary market sizing (\$217bn cumulative 2026-29 opportunity) with adoption curves (AI-enabled spend rising toward c.80% by 2030) to support a stronger, longer investment runway and reinforce conviction in platform leaders.

AI is transforming SCM from decision-support to autonomous execution, materially expanding ROI and spend intensity. The report's core incremental insight is that AI is not a feature but a system-wide shift, embedding across forecasting, inventory, logistics, and procurement. Real-time data ingestion (macro, weather, logistics signals) enables dynamic optimization of supply chains, delivering tangible financial benefits - lower working capital, fewer disruptions, and higher service levels. This drives unusually high ROI (25–30%), positioning SCM as one of the most economically attractive enterprise software categories. Crucially, AI strengthens - not disrupts - incumbent moats due to deep integration and switching costs.

The market opportunity is larger, faster-growing, and more structurally supported than consensus assumes. SCM software is expected to double from \$33bn in 2024 to \$66bn by 2029 (c.15% CAGR), with c.\$217bn cumulative revenue over 2026-29. Growth is supported by multiple structural drivers: geopolitical supply chain redesign, resilience-first strategies, ESG compliance, and rising customer expectations. Beyond this, AI-enabled SCM is projected to grow c.46% annually through 2030, implying a rapid shift in mix rather than incremental upsell. This combination of volume growth and mix uplift is underappreciated in current expectations.

Cloud migration and pricing innovation create a second monetization leg - but introduce near-term volatility. SaaS SCM is growing c.23% annually, driving cloud penetration from 52% (2024) to 74% (2029), representing one of the largest remaining enterprise upgrade cycles. Simultaneously, outcome-based pricing (e.g., linking fees to savings or service improvements) expands the addressable market and accelerates AI monetization. However, this introduces revenue variability and margin pressure during transition, as vendors invest in measurement, customer success, and AI infrastructure. Over time, hybrid pricing models should stabilize revenues while increasing lifetime value and retention.

Market structure favors scaled platforms, with consolidation upside reinforcing leadership. Despite fragmentation (top 3 at c.38% share), the market is moving toward end-to-end platforms, supported by M&A and integration demand. SAP stands out with c.25% share and structural advantages from ERP integration and S/4 migration; Oracle offers underappreciated upside via Fusion integration and data architecture; services players - especially Capgemini - capture a disproportionate share of transformation spend and influence vendor selection.

We reiterate Outperform ratings, with AI-driven SCM strengthening conviction in a multi-year structural growth thesis. The combination of AI adoption, cloud migration, and platform consolidation supports sustained double-digit growth, high ROI, and durable competitive advantages. Near-term risks (margin pressure, adoption variability) are outweighed by long-term monetization potential, extending both visibility and conviction in key sector leaders.

## IT SERVICES: THE IRRESISTIBLE RISE OF SERVICES-AS-SOFTWARE

Services-as-Software is emerging as the next structural disruption in IT services, and this report frames why investors should reassess medium-term growth and margin trajectories across the sector. While consensus still anchors on cyclical recovery and incremental GenAI productivity, our analysis highlights a deeper shift toward outcome-based, AI-delivered services - supported by proprietary survey data showing 60% of enterprises planning to replace people-led services by 2028. We reiterate our ratings, with conviction strengthened on structurally advantaged players (e.g., Capgemini), as the timeline for business model disruption appears earlier and more material than the market expects.

Enterprise behavior is already pivoting toward outcome-based, AI-driven delivery. Industry survey data show c.60% of enterprises plan to replace people-run services with software-led alternatives by 2028, implying a structural - not cyclical - shift in demand. This is reinforced by rising contract renegotiations and GenAI-driven productivity gains, which are exposing the mismatch between traditional billing models (time-and-materials) and actual delivery economics. The implication is clear: pricing frameworks will migrate toward outcomes, compressing legacy services revenue pools while rewarding scalable, software-embedded offerings. This transition is happening faster than consensus anticipates, as clients actively seek to capture AI-driven efficiency gains.

Services-as-Software represents a large and rapidly scaling TAM with meaningful mix implications. Estimates suggest the market could expand from sub-\$100bn today to \$600-700bn by 2028, and up to \$1.5tn longer term. Crucially, this growth will cannibalize both traditional IT services and SaaS budgets, reshaping IT spending allocation: Services-as-Software could reach c.20% of budgets versus c.0% today, while legacy services decline sharply. This supports a re-rating framework for companies successfully pivoting, as revenue streams become more recurring, scalable, and software-like - versus labor-constrained and cyclical.

The financial model reset is material: faster growth and structurally higher margins. Services-as-Software combines software scalability with outcome-based pricing, enabling $>30\%$ growth on a small base and potentially adding 5-10pp to blended growth as adoption scales. Margin upside is equally significant: gross margins could reach 65-70% and operating margins 15-25%, versus 25-30% and 5-15% for traditional services. This reflects lower marginal costs, reduced reliance on headcount, and improved pricing power tied to delivered outcomes. However, this comes alongside upfront investment, execution complexity, and risk of pricing pressure during transition.

The shift will disrupt labor models and accelerate in-sourcing, reinforcing competitive divergence. AI-driven efficiency is triggering workforce restructuring, increasing in-house delivery (“AI-shoring”), and challenging offshore-centric models. At the same time, regulatory developments (e.g., US outsourcing constraints) may further pressure traditional delivery models. Providers must combine consulting, vertical expertise, AI capabilities, and proprietary IP to remain relevant - raising barriers to entry and favoring scaled, well-invested players. Companies unable to adapt risk structural decline, particularly in labor-intensive segments like business process outsourcing.

We maintain our ratings, with stronger conviction on structural winners. The transition to Services-as-Software reinforces our positive stance on companies best positioned to capture outcome-based, AI-driven demand (notably Capgemini), while increasing medium-term risks for laggards. The key investment debate shifts from cyclical recovery to structural positioning - accelerating the timeline for differentiation and supporting a more selective approach across the sector.

## DISRUPTED OR SUPERCHARGED? MAPPING AI RISKS IN SERVICES AND SOFTWARE AND AI DISRUPTION RADAR - WANT TO TRY IT YOURSELF?

AI is unlikely to be an existential disruptor for European software and services - but it will sharply reshape value pools and relative winners. This report updates and operationalizes our AI disruption framework, responding to investor demand for a more transparent, scenario-testable methodology. While consensus increasingly prices in broad disruption risks, our analysis is more nuanced and often counter-consensus at the segment level. Our incremental contribution is a proprietary, revenue-weighted scoring model (automatability vs. defensibility) applied consistently across companies, with granular segment inputs. We reiterate ratings; the framework modestly strengthens conviction in high-moat “AI transformers” and “resilient compounders,” while reinforcing caution on low-defensibility exposure.

The key insight is that AI impact is bifurcated by segment economics - not company labels. Our framework shows disruption risk is driven by two variables: how automatable core work is, and how defensible the product or relationship remains. High-automatability segments such as analytics, application development, and business process services face clear pressure, particularly where switching costs are low. By contrast, systems of record (ERP, databases) and deeply embedded workflows retain strong defensibility, limiting displacement risk. The rationale is structural: proprietary data, regulatory constraints, and integration complexity anchor incumbents, meaning AI is more likely to layer onto existing platforms than replace them outright.

Revenue mix is the critical differentiator at the company level. By mapping segment exposure to company revenues (using Gartner data), we show why firms like SAP, Dassault Systèmes, and Nemetschek screen as relatively resilient: their exposure is concentrated in high-defensibility, lower-risk segments where AI enhances rather than disrupts value. SAP is emblematic - ERP and supply chain form a deeply embedded base, with AI positioned as a monetization lever (workflow automation, analytics) rather than a threat. Conversely, vendors with greater exposure to front-end, easily replicable functionality (e.g., analytics layers or commoditized tools) face higher structural risk, particularly if AI-native competitors bypass legacy interfaces.

In services, AI disrupts delivery models far more than client relationships. Most services segments rank high on automatability, but defensibility varies: application and infrastructure services remain sticky due to contracts, complexity, and embedded knowledge, while business process services are the most exposed given standardization and low switching costs. This creates a clear divide: "AI transformers" such as Capgemini face margin pressure but can offset it by industrializing AI at scale; high-end consulting and engineering firms (e.g., Alten, Reply) benefit from lower automatability and stronger expertise moats. The strategic imperative is rapid transition toward "services-as-software," where value shifts from labor to IP and platforms.


Bottom line: we stay constructive on high-moat platforms and selective services leaders. Ratings are unchanged, but conviction increases in companies positioned as “AI transformers” with defensible cores, where AI is a growth and margin enhancer rather than a disruptor. Conversely, we remain cautious on businesses with high exposure to commoditized, automatable tasks and limited switching costs, where pricing pressure and share loss risks are highest over the medium term.

## FORWARD DEPLOYED ENGINEERING (FDE): WHERE AI SOFTWARE MEETS THE REAL WORLD AND FORWARD DEPLOYED ENGINEERING: FORCE MULTIPLIER - OR FALSE COMFORT - FOR ENTERPRISE AI

Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) is becoming the default execution layer for enterprise AI - but we argue the market is over-interpreting it as proof of scalable software economics. This report matters because rising FDE investment is widely seen as evidence of AI traction, whereas we show it is equally a signal of platform immaturity and execution risk. Drawing on investor discussions and field feedback on deployment costs and failure patterns, we highlight that FDE is a force multiplier - not a silver bullet. We reiterate ratings, but our analysis sharpens differentiation: conviction increases on vendors that demonstrably reduce FDE intensity over time, and weakens where FDE scales linearly with ARR.

FDE growth signals both traction and immaturity - not just demand. The prevailing narrative treats expanding FDE teams as confirmation of strong AI adoption. Our work challenges that view: while embedded engineers accelerate time-to-value and unlock early monetization, they also reveal the lack of plug-and-play scalability in enterprise AI. The key analytical lens is whether FDE effort per customer declines as platforms mature. Bull case: FDE acts as a bootstrapping mechanism, converting bespoke work into reusable product modules, enabling operating leverage. Bear case: headcount scales with revenue, implying a services-heavy model with structurally lower margins. For investors, this dynamic is the single most important underwriting variable.

FDE compresses risk timelines rather than eliminating risk. A critical insight from field work is that FDE does not “solve” deployment risk - it accelerates its manifestation. Strong organizations convert this intensity into faster learning cycles and scalable systems. Weak organizations experience cost inflation, stalled deployments, and dependency. Failure modes are rarely technical (e.g., model accuracy), but structural: mis-scoped use cases, lack of executive ownership, poor data quality, or governance bottlenecks. This reframes AI risk from a technology problem to an organizational one, with direct implications for durability of spend and expansion potential.

Persistent FDE reliance creates hidden customer fragility and long-term churn risk. Beyond margins, we highlight a less-discussed issue: internal capability erosion. Heavy reliance on vendor engineers leads to loss of architectural ownership, weaker internal talent retention, and growing dependency. While this can increase short-term stickiness (bull case), it also raises medium-term risks of spending caps, partner substitution, or re-platforming (bear case). Investors should therefore treat flat or rising FDE intensity in mature accounts as a warning signal of consulting-like dependency rather than deep competitive moat.

FDE economics hinge on productization velocity and governance architecture. The model only scales if learnings are systematically converted into product features, templates, and partner-deliverable playbooks. In parallel, agentic AI increases the importance of governance, auditability, and ownership frameworks - areas where FDE plays a critical enforcement role but cannot compensate for weak organizational design. A credible bull case requires both declining marginal effort and clear separation between reusable “commodity IP” and customer-specific differentiation. Without this, the model drifts toward bespoke delivery and capped operating leverage.

Investment conclusion: favor platforms that use FDE as a transient scaffold. We reiterate our ratings, but sharpen our framework: winners will use FDE to compress learning cycles and then scale via product and ecosystem leverage; laggards will remain trapped in services-like models. Incrementally, our conviction strengthens where management can evidence declining FDE intensity, rising reuse, and partner-led delivery - and weakens where FDE growth is presented as an end-state rather than a transition.

## ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE... SOONER OR LATER: AI IS ALREADY HERE - THE STRUCTURAL RESET OF IT SERVICES & SOFTWARE

AI is already driving a structural reset in IT services and software, but the market is underestimating both the pace and the dispersion of outcomes. This report matters as investors debate “sooner vs later” AI impact; we are firmly in the “sooner” camp, arguing AI is not incremental but re-architecting business models, pricing, and margins today. Our incremental analysis introduces a proprietary AI leverage scorecard and industry feedback on monetization, showing winners will capture productivity through pricing and IP, while laggards face commoditization. We reiterate ratings, but incrementally strengthen conviction in platform-led software and IP-driven services while becoming more selective on labor-centric models.

AI is shifting software from applications to enterprise control planes. The key structural change is the transition from feature-centric applications to AI-driven workflow orchestration embedded in systems of record (ERP, HR, supply chain). Growth increasingly comes from installed-base expansion - via AI agents, premium bundles, and consumption pricing - rather than new logos. The bull case is clear: AI drives ARPU uplift, higher retention, and structurally higher margins once monetization shifts from "AI included" to "AI priced." The bear case centers on execution risk: failure to price AI or control compute costs risks margin dilution and value leakage to hyperscalers.

AI is transforming services from labor economics to “Services-as-Software.” AI does not eliminate services demand; it reprices it. Delivery models evolve toward agent-driven, platform-led, outcome-based offerings, compressing the delivery pyramid while expanding deal scope. The bull case is margin expansion via retained productivity and IP-led differentiation. The bear case is structural commoditization: if productivity gains are fully rebated, services revert to lower-margin, price-competitive models. This creates a widening gap between “AI operators” and traditional integrators, driving valuation dispersion rather than sector-wide uplift.

AI creates a dual revenue dynamic: deflation in effort, expansion in value. Automation reduces hours and compresses pricing in commoditized activities, but this is offset by new, higher-value AI-driven use cases - copilots, workflow automation, and data platforms. Industry feedback suggests productivity gains (c.7–8%) are typically reinvested into broader transformation programs, resulting in “more for the same budget.” The bull case is a sustained multi-year growth cycle driven by transformation demand and cross-sell. The bear case is mix dilution if companies fail to convert this demand into recurring, software-attached revenue.

Margins and cash flows follow an AI-driven J-curve. Near term, margins face pressure from R&D, compute, and reskilling, but over time expand as automation scales and high-margin AI revenues grow. Gross margins benefit first from productivity, followed by operating leverage as SG&A is automated. Cash flow improves structurally as recurring, outcome-based models and consumption pricing increase visibility and reduce working capital intensity. The key execution variable is whether firms retain productivity gains or pass them through to clients.

Investment conclusion: AI increases dispersion - own platforms and IP, avoid commoditized delivery. We reiterate ratings but sharpen our framework: software vendors controlling data and workflow orchestration, and services firms monetizing AI through platforms and outcomes, are structurally advantaged. Conversely, labor-led models face margin pressure and weaker valuation support. Incrementally, our conviction strengthens on AI control-plane software and IP-led services, with a longer-term horizon as monetization and margin expansion fully materialize.

## WHEN ANTHROPIC AND OPENAI MEET IT SERVICES

Enterprise AI has already moved to production scale, but the market is underestimating how decisively model ecosystems - Anthropic vs OpenAI - will reshape IT services economics and competitive positioning. This report matters as consensus still frames AI as productivity uplift; we argue the real shift is control of delivery platforms and value pools. Our incremental analysis introduces a platform-centric lens on services providers' positioning and contrasts "endurance" (Anthropic) vs "dominance" (OpenAI). We reiterate ratings, but incrementally strengthen conviction on platform-led, multi-model operators and become more cautious on players lacking deep ecosystem leverage.

Anthropic vs OpenAI is becoming the defining strategic fault line in enterprise AI. Rather than interchangeable models, the two platforms represent distinct paths to value creation. Anthropic prioritizes “trusted AI” embedded in regulated, mission-critical workflows via services-led distribution, while OpenAI emphasizes scale, platform control, and hyperscaler-driven reach. The bull case is that both ecosystems expand the total AI opportunity; the bear case is that vendor concentration and execution risk increase. Critically, services providers must align their delivery model, client base, and IP around one - or both - strategies, making model choice a determinant of long-term positioning.

Services firms are reorganizing around AI as a delivery platform, not a tool. Across the sector, services providers are embedding Claude and GPT internally to compress delivery costs, then externalizing these capabilities into agentic, outcome-based offerings. Organizationally, this means dedicated AI business groups, large-scale reskilling, and a shift toward agent design and governance roles. The bull case is structural productivity gains and new revenue pools from platformized services; the bear case is near-term disruption, execution complexity, and the risk that productivity gains are competed away rather than monetized.

The business model is shifting from labor to Services-as-Software, with a three-phase financial profile. In the short term, heavy investment in training, platforms, and co-development is margin dilutive. Over the medium term, growth shifts toward higher-value transformation and agent-led services, with early operating leverage. Longer term, successful players generate recurring, outcome-linked revenues tied to proprietary platforms and agents, not headcount. The key debate is whether services providers can cannibalize legacy revenues fast enough to capture this upside before competition compresses returns.

Relative positioning reflects ecosystem choices, creating clear winners and laggards. Multi-model orchestrators (e.g., Accenture) appear best positioned, combining Anthropic depth and OpenAI scale. Platform-led players (e.g., Infosys) offer strong exposure to agentic workflows but face execution risk. OpenAI-anchored integrators (e.g., Capgemini) provide clean exposure to platform scaling but may need deeper “trusted AI” capabilities over time. The bull case is differentiated positioning and pricing power; the bear case is dependency on partners, delayed margin inflection, and uneven monetization.

Investment conclusion: own platform orchestrators and early platformizers; avoid undifferentiated exposure. We reiterate ratings but sharpen our framework: long-term winners will be providers that translate Anthropic/OpenAI partnerships into proprietary platforms, recurring revenues, and outcome pricing. Incrementally, this strengthens conviction in multi-model and platform-led strategies, while increasing selectivity on firms lacking clear ecosystem leverage or Services-as-Software execution.

Accenture and Infosys are not covered by Bernstein. Anthropic and OpenAI are private companies.

## EUROPEAN IT SERVICES: EU TECH SOVEREIGNTY - REGULATION CREATES DEMAND, BUT ONLY THE RIGHT MODELS WIN

EU tech sovereignty is set to reshape European IT services demand - but not evenly, challenging the market's implicit assumption of broad-based upside. This report argues that while investors broadly expect regulation to drive incremental growth, the key insight - supported by our proprietary scoring framework and detailed exposure mapping - is that most of the value creation will be highly selective. Sovereignty creates a multi-year pipeline of non-discretionary projects, but also raises delivery complexity and cost. We reiterate ratings, with conviction shifting toward a stock-picking framework rather than a sector-wide call.

Regulation is creating a structural, non-discretionary demand pool. The EU tech sovereignty package forces public sector and regulated industries to re-platform IT architectures, localize data, and upgrade governance frameworks. This translates into a multi-year pipeline of sovereign cloud migrations, compliance projects, and infrastructure build-outs - core areas for IT services providers. Crucially, this demand is not cyclical but policy-driven, meaning projects are less discretionary and more resilient across macro conditions. The mix also shifts toward higher-value services (consulting, integration, managed services), supporting revenue visibility and reinforcing incumbency with governments and critical sectors.

However, demand converts unevenly due to exposure and capability differences. Our proprietary scoring framework highlights that upside is concentrated among firms with high EU public/regulated exposure, strong cloud/AI/security capabilities, and deep EU delivery footprints. Sopra Steria and Indra emerge as the clearest direct beneficiaries, with high exposure to sovereignty-relevant workloads, while Capgemini, CGI, and Reply offer more diversified, but diluted, leverage. The key incremental insight is that exposure (e.g., 70-80% revenues in regulated sectors for names like Indra/Aubay) and capability alignment - not just scale - determine how much of the demand tailwind converts into growth.

Execution risk is driven by structurally higher delivery complexity and cost inflation. Sovereignty requirements fragment delivery models, constrain hyperscaler usage, and increase reliance on EU-based talent, all of which raise costs. Compliance overhead, multi-vendor architectures, and country-level regulatory divergence further reduce standardization and productivity. While some of these costs can be passed through, competitive public tenders limit pricing power, creating a risk that incremental revenue becomes margin-dilutive. This dynamic is particularly acute for offshore-heavy or hyperscaler-dependent providers, where cost structures may reprice faster than revenue.

The outcome is a clear bull vs bear divergence across business models. The bull case is straightforward: sovereignty drives non-discretionary spend, increases complexity (and therefore value-add), raises barriers to entry, and strengthens strategic positioning for incumbents. The bear case is equally credible: sovereignty becomes compliance-heavy, fragmented, and cost-driven, crowding out higher-value digital initiatives and compressing margins. The key differentiator is execution - firms that can translate complexity into billable value outperform, while others risk being trapped in low-margin compliance work.

Bottom line: a durable growth theme, but fundamentally a stock-selection story. We reiterate ratings, but emphasize that EU tech sovereignty should not be viewed as a sector-wide re-rating. Instead, it acts as a structural filter, amplifying divergence between well-positioned players and structurally disadvantaged models. Investors should focus on exposure, capability alignment, and delivery model resilience as the primary drivers of relative performance.

## SAP: AI POLICY PIVOT - DEFENSIVE MOVE, HIGH STAKES

Investors should read this as consensus still assumes SAP will benefit from AI structurally, but with rising concern that external tools could disintermediate the ERP layer. Our incremental analysis leverages proprietary DSAG survey data showing most production AI use cases today run on non-SAP stacks, reframing the policy as defensive rather than demand-led. We reiterate Outperform, with conviction intact but acknowledging higher execution risk and a more binary AI outcome path.

This effectively creates “Indirect Access 2.0” for the AI era, making all AI-driven transactions observable and billable. The incremental insight is that SAP is not just protecting data integrity, but repositioning ERP as the central orchestrator of AI workflows rather than a passive data layer—supporting long-term pricing power and moat durability.

Customer behavior suggests the move is defensive, not demand-led. DSAG survey data shows that while 43% of customers have implemented AI, production deployments are predominantly built on non-SAP solutions. This implies SAP is reacting to a loss of share of wallet in enterprise AI rather than leading adoption. The implication is twofold: (i) SAP's AI competitiveness today is still in question (innovation speed, developer appeal, TCO), and (ii) policy enforcement is being used to redirect ecosystem activity back toward SAP-native tools, increasing execution and adoption risk in the near term.

The upside case hinges on monetization via Digital Access and ARPU expansion. By routing high-frequency, machine-driven AI interactions through SAP-governed channels, the company can transform AI from a potential disintermediation threat into a usage-based revenue engine. This embeds AI within SAP's ERP flywheel, reinforcing platform stickiness and supporting long-term revenue visibility. Additionally, tighter control over data, semantic models, and system performance reduces the risk of ERP commoditization and strengthens the clean-core transition strategy.

Execution risk is the key swing factor, particularly around product readiness and customer friction. SAP is constraining third-party innovation before its own AI stack is clearly best-in-class, raising the risk of slowed adoption, higher perceived lock-in, and pushback on Digital Access pricing. There is also a risk of increased friction with partners and developers, potentially dampening ecosystem innovation. In this context, the policy raises the bar for SAP to deliver compelling AI capabilities quickly, as control without competitiveness could backfire.

Bottom line: strategically coherent but tactically demanding, with asymmetric outcomes. We reiterate Outperform, as reclaiming control of the ERP layer is critical to long-term value capture in AI. However, the policy introduces a tighter execution window and amplifies near-term risks, shifting the investment case toward a higher-stakes, execution-driven re-rating path.

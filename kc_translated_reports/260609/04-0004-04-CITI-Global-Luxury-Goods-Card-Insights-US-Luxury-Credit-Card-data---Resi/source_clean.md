Total Luxury Brands' credit card spend rose 3% YoY in May, a fifth consecutive month of growth, albeit moderating slightly from +4% in April amid continued geopolitical and macro uncertainty. This was achieved against a \~5ppt tougher comparative, with 2Y-stacked spend turning positive to +2% from -3%. The YoY deceleration was driven by slightly softer albeit robust transaction values (ASP), up mid-teens, and broadly unchanged transacting-customer trends (volumes), down \~10%. Inflationary pressures continued to weigh on consumer sentiment in May. The Conference Board index edged lower as weaker current business and labour conditions offset improved income expectations, while UMichigan survey softened for the third consecutive month. US consumers remain resilient, particularly at the higher-end, supported by wealth effects from equity markets and steady wage growth. That said, discretionary spending could soften if geopolitical tensions and inflationary pressures persist, particularly against a backdrop of negative real income growth and savings erosion.

# Credit Card Insights for US Luxury, Sporting Goods and Beauty Spend

## Demand resilient in May (+3% YoY) as 2Y-stacked spend turns positive

The US economy was the second-best performer among developed markets in 2025, with real GDP growth of $2.2\%$ , behind Spain at $2.9\%$ . Australia ranked third at $1.9\%$ annual growth. Over the same period, the Euro Area expanded by $1.6\%$ , while Japan and the UK grew by $1.2\%$ and $1.4\%$ , respectively. US resilience has persisted despite compounding headwinds: elevated post-pandemic inflation, a prolonged period of restrictive interest rates, and significant trade-policy shifts under President Trump's second term. Over the past six months, the effective tariff rate has fluctuated but currently stands near $10\%$ , up from roughly $2.5\%$ pre-2025, according to our estimates. Following February's decision by the Supreme Court to strike down IEEPA-based tariffs, much of these levies ( $\$ 166$ bn according to a report by Customs and Border Protection) are now expected to be refunded, posing a potential relief to European Luxury companies.

Our outlook for the global economy continues to assume near-trend growth at 2.6% for 2026E, and global headline inflation to 3.5%, reflecting the imprint of higher oil prices and accompanying supply-chain disruptions from the Middle East conflict. The global economy remains in the jaws of a severe supply shock due to the Iran war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Even so, various real-time readings on economic activity point to still-solid performance. These include PMIs, “surprise” indexes, and the performance of global equity markets. The continued momentum reflects in part the accelerating strength of AI spending. But more broadly, the global economy looks to be absorbing the oil shock fairly smoothly.

US consumer sentiment ended 2025 on weak footing and has been volatile in 2026 to date, weighed down by federal government restructuring, tariff volatility, softening labour-market expectations, and renewed geopolitical stresses. Core PCE inflation has been sticky above 3% YoY while core CPI has slowed below 3%, an abnormal divergence as CPI typically runs stronger than PCE. Headline inflation will continue to be strong in the near term as energy prices have remained elevated. We do not expect much passthrough of higher energy prices to core inflation, but with upside risks to our forecasts for components like goods prices. Core PCE inflation is sticky in part to rising prices for AI-related goods like computer software and accessories. The Fed and markets remain focused on medium- and longer-term inflation expectations that have stayed anchored. We expect more benign monthly core inflation readings in the summer and fall.

The US consumer continues to spend at a slower pace than in recent years, yet aggregate consumption shows few signs of faltering. Balance sheets remain generally healthy, though pressures among lower-income households persist. These strains have been building for some time, even as this cohort has benefited in recent years from very low unemployment and a tight labour market. Monthly payroll job growth has been very volatile month-to-month but has picked-up slightly on a 6m moving average. The unemployment rate has increased gradually over the last couple of years but has stayed within a narrow range around 4.3-4.4% in the last several months. We expect labor demand to remain softer than labor supply this year, which would put further upward pressure on the unemployment rate by the summer. The labor market continues to be defined by a low hiring rate but also low layoff rate and is not a source of inflationary pressure as wage growth has slowed. By contrast, spending among higher-income

households has been supported by strong wealth effects from equity and home prices. This dynamic should continue to provide support, as equity markets are thus far tracking gains seen in recent years, although the housing market appears softer, with some measures of home prices declining in recent months.

US credit card spend for Total Luxury Brands card rose 3% YoY in May, marking a fifth consecutive month of growth, although momentum moderated slightly from +4% in April amid continued geopolitical and macro uncertainty. This was achieved against a \~5ppt tougher comparative, with 2Y-stacked spend turning back positive to +2% from -3% in April.

The slight YoY deceleration was driven by softer transaction values (ASP), though still robust, up mid-teens YoY, while transacting-customer trends (volumes) were broadly unchanged, down \~10% YoY. Inflationary pressures linked to the Middle East conflict continued to weigh on consumer sentiment in May. The Conference Board index edged lower as weaker current business and labour conditions offset improvements in short-term expectations for business, labour and income. The UMichigan survey softened for the third consecutive month, with sharper declines among lower-income and non-college consumers. Overall, US consumers remain resilient, particularly at the higher-end, supported by wealth effects from rising equity markets, property gains, potential tax-cut support and steady wage growth. That said, discretionary spending could soften if geopolitical tensions and inflationary pressures persist, particularly against a backdrop of negative real income growth and eroding savings rate (2.6% as of May 2026).

Considering the Total Luxury Market index (i.e., including High-End Department Stores, Online Luxury platforms, and Online Luxury Off-Price and Resale platforms), spend remained broadly stable at +1% YoY in May, with the stronger performance in Online Luxury Off-Price/Resale offsetting deteriorating trends in Online Luxury platforms, while High-End Department Stores remained stable. On a 2-year stacked basis, Total Luxury Market spend improved sequentially to -3% in May from -7% in April.

Finally, Sport brands and retailers remained broadly stable at -4% YoY in May, and Beauty improved to -3% YoY from -7%. On a 2Y-stacked basis, Sport brands and retailers remained stable at -11% in May, together with Beauty, broadly stable at -10%.

Analysis of Citi credit-card data indicates stronger resilience among upper-income demographics across 2023–2025, with transaction-value growth (a proxy for ASP) consistently outperforming transacting-customer growth (a proxy for volumes).

Steep price increases implemented during the pandemic have arguably alienated aspirational buyers—particularly in developed markets and in entry-price categories that typically target these consumers (e.g., small leather goods, small/mini leather bags, canvas bags, backpacks, sneakers, slides)—posing a long-term risk to volume growth, in our view.

After highly differentiated pricing approaches in 2023 (ranging from low single-digit to \~10% increases), pricing became more consistent in 2024, averaging +3–4%, with Hermès the main outlier at +9% in January 2024.

In 2025, most brands implemented low- to mid-single-digit price increases across regions, reflecting brand-specific strategies, input-cost pressures (precious metals, FX, general inflation), and US-focused adjustments to offset higher tariffs: the 10% tariff on EU goods (raised to 15% from 1 August 2025 under the US–EU deal) and the 31% tariff on Swiss luxury goods since 9 April 2025 (raised to 39% from 7 August before being reduced to 15% under the agreement effective 14 November 2025). In Japan, price increases in 2025 were more limited than in prior years, initially reflecting JPY strength against the CNY following the June 2024 trough. However, renewed yen weakness late last year widened the Japan–China retail price gap above historical norms, leaving Japan roughly 20–25% cheaper and continuing to support parallel-market (daigou) activity. The Japanese government plans to curb this through changes to the Tax Free Shopping scheme expected to take effect on 1 November 2026.

In 2026 to date, very low single-digit price increases have been implemented at most soft luxury brands (except for Hermès at +5–6% in January), while hard-luxury brands have raised prices a touch higher, by low- to mid-single-digit percentages, reflecting higher precious-metal costs (watches, jewellery) and CHF strength (watches). While we expect pricing to become more homogeneous across brands in the medium term, we still see multi-year catch-up opportunities at Hermès and at Richemont's jewellery Maisons—Cartier, Van Cleef & Arpels, and Buccellati.

## Appendix 1 – Regressions: Credit card data vs. US quarterly YoY sales growth for key luxury stocks

We back tested the robustness of our credit card data by running regressions with historical quarterly constant currency sales growth. We aggregate monthly credit card data to derive US quarterly spend by product category. We then multiply these datapoints to each company's last publicly available (or estimated) revenue sales mix by category (Leather Goods, RTW, Watches, Jewellery). Finally, we plot on the X-axis the constant-currency quarterly sales growth as reported by each covered company in the US and on the Y-axis the quarterly sales growth implied by Citi's credit card data.

On each regression chart in the following pages:

■ The blue dots denote historical US quarterly sales growth.
■ The red dot denotes the last published quarter.
■ The red star denotes the implied growth for the next quarter to be reported.

While there is some degree of variability on a company-by-company basis, the aggregate credit card data displays an acceptable degree of correlation with US quarterly sales growth as reported by our covered companies (\~0.75 on aggregate) and a very good fit (R-squared >0.8 for most brands).

## Card Insights Disclaimer and Methodology

Card Insights consists of selected data from a subset of Citi's credit card transactions and is not representative of Citi's overall credit cardholder population. The data is highly aggregated and anonymized and has undergone rigorous security protocols to keep all customer information confidential and secure. All Card Insights datasets reflect a degree of selection bias whereby general and proprietary methods are used to aggregate and classify transactions into spending categories. Card Insights data is unadjusted for the number of credit card customers and can be influenced by market share gains/losses from Citi's card business. Card-not-present transactions are those where the card information was not transmitted directly from the card at the time of transaction and is utilized as a proxy for online, telephone, or mail-order spending.

This data should not be considered as indicative or predictive of the past or future financial performance of Citi or its credit card business. Additional information about the methodology used to aggregate the data is available upon request.

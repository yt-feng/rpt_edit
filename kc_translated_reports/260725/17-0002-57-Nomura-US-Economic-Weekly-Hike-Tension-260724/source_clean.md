## We expect the FOMC to remain on hold

We expect the FOMC to leave the policy rate unchanged at $3.625\%$ in July (Fig. 1 & 2), with no substantive changes to the post-meeting statement.

Market pricing for a July hike has risen recently, but we think this largely reflects limited forward guidance and uncertainty around the Fed's reaction function rather than a meaningful change in the economic outlook.

Incoming data also support a hold. June CPI and PPI surprised to the downside, pointing to a sharp moderation in core PCE inflation. While some temporary factors likely amplified the June weakness, the broader trend remains encouraging, with inflation momentum easing (Fig. 3). Most wage-sensitive components have also eased recently (Fig. 4). Residual seasonality, the BEA's methodological revisions, and moderating wage pressures should contribute to further slowdown in core PCE inflation.

Most Fed officials also appear comfortable with a “wait-and-see” approach as they expect sequential disinflation. Speaking before the June CPI report, Governor Waller seemed to


be cautious about inflation risks and mentioned that policy decisions should be based on incoming inflation data. We think the June CPI and PPI reports are likely to convince him to remain on hold at the July meeting.

## The case for a proactive hike

Markets are pricing roughly a one-third probability for a hike in the July meeting. We remain skeptical of the case for a proactive rate hike though. A surprise hike could increase uncertainty around the Fed's reaction function and reinforce expectations of a more hawkish policy path rather than being viewed as substituting for future hikes.

Recent market volatility appear to worsen the case for surprise tightening.

Moreover, longer-term inflation expectations remain well anchored, with 5-10y breakevens around 2.2–2.3% (a level flagged by Governor Waller in April as a reassuring sign). This suggests markets are not questioning the Fed's inflation fighting credibility.

## We expect two hawkish dissents

We expect Cleveland Fed President Hammack and Dallas Fed President Logan to dissent in favor of a rate hike. Both have argued that elevated inflation, resilient growth, and easier financial conditions suggest policy is not restrictive. They have also highlighted upside inflation risks while noting that a stable labor market suggests there is no tension between the Fed's dual mandate.

Another dissent from Minneapolis Fed President Kashkari is possible, but this is not our base case. Kashkari had expected one rate hike later this year as of the June FOMC meeting, which suggests his policy stance was not as hawkish as other uber hawks.

## Inflation data likely to reinforce the case for patience

We expect next week's inflation data to reinforce the case for patience by highlighting easing price pressures.

We expect core PCE inflation to slow sharply to $0.175\%$ m-o-m in June, consistent with an annualized pace near $2\%$ (Fig. 5). On a y-o-y basis, core PCE likely eased to $3.3\%$ from $3.4\%$ . Softer than expected CPI and PPI data point to moderating supercore PCE inflation, while core goods inflation likely rebounded modestly.

Looking ahead, easing tariff pass-through and residual seasonality should keep core PCE on a gradual disinflationary path. We continue to expect y-o-y core PCE inflation to slow to $3.2\%$ by year-end under the current methodology (or around $3.0\%$ after the planned methodological revisions). A $0.2\%$ monthly core PCE print would represent further progress toward the Fed's inflation target and reduce the likelihood of near term hikes.

We expect the Employment Cost Index (ECI), the Fed's preferred wage growth metric, slowed to 0.7% q-o-q from 0.9%. Private sector wages likely remained steady, while state and local government wages slowed. The benefits component also moderated following a strong uptick in Q1. Our forecast for ECI points to easing wage pressures (Fig. 6), in line with alternative wage indicators, suggesting labor-intensive services inflation are likely to continue to moderate.

## New tariff announcements point to continuity

The Trump administration announced new tariffs under Section 301 to replace expiring Section 122 universal $10\%$ tariffs. A transition to Section 301 from Section 122 tariffs will likely increase the average effective tariff rate by about 1pp to $8\%$ , broadly in line with our expectation (Fig. 7).

However, there are mitigating measures in the announcement. The additional impact on imports from some major trading partners will be net out the MFN duties. In addition, the general exemption product list was expanded including some consumer/food products (Fig. 8). Those modifications suggest that the Trump administration did not intend to escalate tariff policy substantially.

We continue to expect that increased focus on affordability issues and elevated inflation will likely prevent US tariff policy from escalating drastically going forward. We maintain our expectation for the terminal average effective tariff rate at the 8-9% range.

Major risks to monitor will be the ongoing USMCA negotiations as well as another Section 301 investigation on excess production capacity.

## Real GDP growth likely accelerated in Q2

We expect real GDP growth accelerated to $2.5\%$ q-o-q ar in Q2 from $2.1\%$ in Q1. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers likely strengthened to $3.4\%$ from $2.2\%$ , driven by a rebound in personal consumption growth and robust equipment investment. While AI-related capex led business investment in early 2026, recent data suggest investment momentum is broadening. We expect residential investment likely grew in Q2 for the first time since Q4 2024, while nonresidential structures investment remained subdued. Volatile components such as government spending, net exports, and inventories were weaker than Q1 and likely weighed on headline GDP growth.

Advance goods trade balance (Tuesday): We expect the advance goods trade deficit narrowed slightly to \$104.5bn in June from \$105.9bn in May. Consumer confidence (Tuesday): We expect the Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index edged lower to 90.5 in July from 91.2 in June. Resumption of hostilities in the Middle East, pickup in energy prices and volatile stock prices likely weighed on consumer sentiment. The labor market differential, a gap between the share of households reporting “jobs are plentiful” and “jobs are hard to get,” likely remained stable after a sharp decline in the two prior months.

FOMC meeting (Wednesday): We expect the July FOMC meeting to be uneventful, with policy remaining on hold and no meaningful forward guidance from Chair Warsh's press conference. There will not be an updated SEP or dot plot at this meeting.

Warsh's rhetoric in the press conference will likely continue to focus on inflation-fighting credibility. However, we expect him to stop short of indicating his threshold for rate hikes or potential actions the Fed should take if inflation remains elevated. We expect Warsh to revisit several dovish talking points, including his belief in the disinflationary impact of AI, skepticism on the quality of official inflation data, and possibly the role of money growth in the inflation outlook.

There will likely be two hawkish dissents in favor of a rate hike, from Dallas president Logan and Cleveland president Hammack. A third dissent from Minneapolis president Kashkari is a risk.

Centrist and dovish officials signaled some possibility of future hikes in the run-up to the July blackout period, but most indicated that incremental disinflation would support the case to remain on hold. Given the slowdown in June inflation data, this suggests a solid majority of voters are comfortable with a “wait-and-see” approach.

Although markets are pricing in a decent probability of a rate hike next week, Warsh's inherent dovish bias as well as a potential risk of excessively tightening financial conditions will likely prevent him from advocating for a rate hike.

Jobless claims (Thursday): Jobless claims declined this week, continuing to highlight resilient labor markets. Note, continuing claims data scheduled for release next week refer to the July NFP survey reference week.

PCE deflator (Thursday): Based on June CPI and PPI data, we expect core PCE slowed sharply in June to 0.175% m-o-m, consistent with an annualized pace of roughly 2%. On a y-o-y basis, core PCE inflation likely decelerated to 3.3% in June from 3.4% in the previous month.

Core CPI inflation surprised to the downside, falling by $0.017\%$ m-o-m in June, much lower than $+0.208\%$ in May, the first monthly decline since May 2020. Details of CPI and PPI data suggest supercore PCE inflation decelerated while PCE core goods inflation rebounded slightly.

Compensation of employees remained strong as private payroll income rose in the month; however, this was more than offset by other components as the one-off increase in farm proprietors' income from USDA disaster relief payments faded in the month.

We expect personal spending growth decelerated to 0.3% m-o-m in June from 0.7% in May. Retail sales slowed in the month as energy-related spending slowed. Services spending growth also likely moderated. Based on our PCE inflation estimates, we expect real spending grew 0.4% m-o-m.

Tensions between the US and Iran escalated during the survey interview period, and energy prices rose. In addition, high frequency data from Morning Consult and SF Fed's Daily News Sentiment Index also point to worsening sentiment.

## US Economic Outlook

We expect the Fed to stay on hold indefinitely (with risks skewed towards hikes) due to persistent price pressures, stable labor markets and waning political pressure.

Economic activity: Growth has remained robust lately, supported by easing tariff uncertainty, fiscal stimulus from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and the lagged impact of Fed rate cuts amid easy financial conditions. Business investment appears to be broadening beyond the AI sector. Despite higher energy prices stemming from the Iran war, consumption has held up, partly due to higher tax refunds and strong payroll income growth. NFPs have been volatile, but an array of indicators point to stabilization and tentative signs of reacceleration in the labor market. While the recent decline in the unemployment rate was due to a one-off factor, we expect the unemployment rate to eventually edge lower to $4.1\%$ by year-end. The US economy remains relatively insulated from the crude oil price shock due to the Iran war, owing to the small share of energy-goods consumption in household spending, as well as domestic energy production.

Inflation: Core inflation remains well above the Fed's $2\%$ target, and risks appear skewed to the upside. We forecast Q4 2026 core PCE inflation of $3.3\%$ y-o-y, but the BEA's planned methodological changes to certain PCE price components could reduce core PCE inflation by about 20bp to $3.1\%$ y-o-y in Q4 2026. Wage growth is moderating and tariff-related price pressures are abating, but new AI-related price pressures from robust demand and supply shortages could push up goods inflation. Overall, we expect core PCE inflation to peak in coming months on lower oil prices, residual seasonality and moderating wage growth. We expect rent disinflation to continue, albeit gradually.

Policy: We expect the Fed to remain on hold indefinitely. Fed Chair Warsh likely remains motivated to ease, but recent elevated inflation readings and hawkish Fedspeak suggest he may not be able to convince the majority to support cuts. In addition, he is likely to encourage the committee to keep policy rates steady until recently announced task forces make recommendations on different aspects of data and monetary policy. While near-term hikes appear unlikely, the Fed's hesitance to respond to tentative signs of inflation pressure raises the risk that it may end up "behind the curve" and need to hike quickly to re-establish credibility. The balance of risks tilts towards tightening. On tariffs, wider Section 122 exemptions, lower tariff rates and a focus on affordability suggest limited further escalation of US trade policy. We don't expect substantial economic stimulus from another reconciliation by Republicans before mid-terms.

Risks: Further escalation of geopolitical risks could lead to a tightening of financial conditions and a worsening of the fiscal outlook. Increased political pressures on FOMC participants could undermine the Fed's credibility and cause sharp market reactions. An AI bust could lead to material corrections in asset valuations and reduced business investment.

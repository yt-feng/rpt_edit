June NFP – noisy, but dovish on net

Job gains slowed to 57k in June (NOM: 70k, Consensus: 113k). There were also downward revisions of 74k to the prior two months, bringing the 3m average pace down to 111k (Fig. 1).

As we expected, the slowdown was largely driven by a reversal of temporary factors that boosted the May print (Fig. 2). Leisure and hospitality employment fell 61k in June, and was revised down by 67k over the prior two months, accounting for nearly all of the negative revisions to headline NFP. In addition, local government employment also retraced lower after an outsized gain in May.

Excluding these volatile components, employment growth was stable and the breadth of job gains remained solid. Hiring in the cyclical sector improved and large services sector were also positive in the month.

The unemployment rate fell to 4.2% (4.189% unrounded) from 4.3% in May.

Broader details of the household survey were weak though, making us skeptical of the headline improvement in unemployment. Labor force participation dropped sharply, driven by the 25-34 age cohort (Fig. 3). This segment is typically seen as more susceptible to AI-risk due to a disparate impact on recent college graduates; however, data from the BLS by education attainment suggested that the decline was broad-based (Fig. 4).

Labor force flows were also negative. The job finding rate ticked down slightly, while measures of layoffs were sideways (Fig. 5). Alternative data for job finding have been mixed – JOLTS job openings remained elevated in May, while the Conference Board labor differential dropped to a multiyear low. Overall, while we are optimistic on the unemployment rate in the medium term, we do not see today's print as strong evidence of a sustained move lower.

## Warsh and Fedspeak point to patience

Chair Warsh leaned dovish at the ECB's Sintra forum, which is consistent with our read of his post-FOMC press conference two weeks ago. He remained optimistic about the medium-term disinflationary impact of AI, arguing that while AI is boosting demand in the near term, supply side gains should follow, even if they are harder to measure. In our view, emphasizing hard-to-observe supply improvements gives policymakers more latitude in the reaction function. Warsh also downplayed government statistics, repeating his “echoes of history” line from the June presser.

Warsh noted that inflation risks and expectations have come down over the past four weeks, likely alluding to developments in the Middle East and lower crude oil prices. His framing appeared closer to dovish policymakers, who have attributed elevated inflation partly to energy prices and tariffs.

Warsh did not give any signal of the near-term policy path. He pushed back against forward guidance and expressed skepticism about the dot plot, suggesting the dots are likely to remain in place for now but could be revisited.

Overall, Warsh's comments reduce the likelihood of a July hike. We think he will favor a wait-and-see approach and avoid any policy shift until the Fed receives recommendations from the task forces.

Broader Fedspeak this week also pointed to patience. Although hawkish officials such as Goolsbee, Barkin, Kashkari and Hammack expressed concern about sticky inflation, they did not commit to a July hike. Lower commodity prices also raised a bar for policy tightening in the near term as SF Fed President Daly, a dove, saw lower crude oil prices as “hope for relief” and assessed the current stance of monetary policy as slightly restrictive. This week’s Fedspeak supports our call for the Fed to remain on hold for the foreseeable future.

## King-sized task

Warsh indicated that some members of the announced task forces are likely to be announced next week.

Reportedly, former BOE Governor Mervyn King is likely to co-chair the Fed's communications task force. King's views appear broadly aligned with Warsh's pushback against forward guidance and apprehension about the dot plot. In a June 2024 NBER paper, King argued that "there is no time-invariant policy reaction function" that can describe a central bank's policy intentions. He noted that the private sector does not share the central bank's outlook and mainly needs to understand the reaction function. In his view, forward guidance conflates the two. King was also critical of the dot plot, arguing that a central bank's attempt to forecast its own policy rate under genuine uncertainty can damage credibility. Consistent with Bernanke's BOE review, King argued that forecast

communication should focus more on risks around the outlook than on central tendencies.

Overall, King's reported role reinforces the idea that the communications task force may recommend a less explicit Fed communication strategy.

## SCOTUS ruling favors Cook, but tail risk remains

The Supreme Court ruled that Fed Governor Lisa Cook can remain on the Board, rejecting the Trump administration's bid to remove her. Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Kavanaugh joined the Court's three liberal justices in a 5-4 decision in Cook's favor.

The ruling appears to rest on procedural grounds. The Court did not decide whether the allegations cited by the administration would satisfy the Federal Reserve Act's "for cause" removal standard. Instead, it noted that the ultimate question depends partly on the underlying facts, which the Court did not address.

This leaves some ambiguity around the scope of “cause.” In principle, the administration could restart the process with stronger procedural safeguards. That said, the decision also underscored the Fed’s unique institutional status, emphasizing that both Fed independence and the “appearance of independence” are key to its design.

Overall, the ruling supports Fed independence, but does not fully eliminate the risk of another removal attempt. Following the verdict, President Trump doubled down, promising to take “appropriate action immediately” to prevent Cook from making important decisions. Repeated threats to Fed independence could increase the likelihood that Powell remains on the Board for longer. Moreover, because the case was decided on procedural grounds and left the definition of “cause” unresolved, the administration could also attempt to restart its probe into Powell with proper procedural safeguards. Note, NEC director Hassett in his Bloomberg interview today expressed concerns about Powell staying at Fed, describing it as “extremely unorthox.”

## The US opts out of a clean USMCA extension

USTR Greer stated on 1 July, that the US is opting for a renewal of the USMCA agreement and is planning to shift to a system of annual reviews. Reportedly, the US could also move to separate bilateral agreements with Canada and Mexico.

The announcement is in line with our expectation. Several trade irritants remain unresolved, but we believe that all the parties have incentives to strike a deal. A shift to annual reviews or narrower exemptions is likely to institutionalize volatility and weigh on growth, but less so as in the case of a complete rupture.

## Survey data offer some relief on price pressures

Survey data released this week suggested manufacturing activity remained resilient, while input price pressures eased following the US-Iran ceasefire. The ISM manufacturing index ticked lower to 53.3 in June, but demand indicators remained solid. Easing supply chain bottlenecks led to a drop in the supplier delivery times (Fig. 7), and a sharp drop in the prices paid index is encouraging (Fig. 8). The balance of risk for goods price inflation is still tilted toward the upside given AI boom-induced price pressures.

## The week ahead

June FOMC minutes are likely to show dispersion across view on policy path ahead

ISM Services (Monday): We expect the ISM Services index declined to 53.3 in June from 54.5 in May. Service sector surveys released thus far suggest business activity and new orders softened in the month. The employment index likely remained in contractionary territory, while the supplier deliveries index likely edged down, reflecting an improvement in supply chain bottlenecks following the US-Iran ceasefire.

Trade balance (Tuesday): We expect the trade deficit widened to \$78.5bn in May from \$55.9bn in April. The advance goods trade report suggested that imports surged in the month, while there was a broad slowdown in goods export growth.

FOMC meeting minutes (Wednesday): As Chair Warsh said there was “a family flight” during the June FOMC meeting, the minutes will likely show intense discussions on key topics. The dispersed distribution of the dot plot suggests that the minutes will likely reveal multiple views on the future path of the federal funds rate. Based on Fedspeak, dovish policymakers tend to link elevated inflation to temporary factors such as high crude oil prices and tariffs, while hawkish policymakers highlight the fact that inflation has been above 2% over years and services price inflation has remained sticky. It is also interesting to watch policymakers’ intentions behind the simplified post-FOMC policy statement, which could signal future developments to the Fed’s communication strategy. On the policy statement, its reference to productivity growth was notable, suggesting debates on the importance of the supply side of the economy at the meeting. On balance sheet policy, the minutes might explain why the policy statement explicitly mentioned that the ample reserves system will be maintained. At his press conference, Warsh provided a rough timeline of the new task forces on five key areas and said it is up to the FOMC whether to accept recommendations from the task forces. The minutes might give further information about the task forces. Lastly, given the simplification of the policy statement, there is a risk of the minutes being shortened substantially, with less disclosure of forward-looking elements.

Jobless claims (Thursday): Both initial and continuing claims remained essentially unchanged this week. Despite a pickup in layoff announcements, claims have remained subdued, pointing to few signs of stress in labor markets.

Existing home sales (Thursday): We expect existing home sales rose to 4.24mn in June from 4.17mn in May. Pending home sales, a leading indicator for existing sales, remained strong from February through May. In addition, waning uncertainty following the US-Iran ceasefire announcement was likely a tailwind for sales.

## US Economic Outlook

We expect the Fed to stay on hold indefinitely due to diminished political pressure, elevated inflation, and hawkish Fedspeak.

Economic activity: Growth momentum has accelerated, supported by easing tariff uncertainty, fiscal stimulus from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and the lagged impact of Fed rate cuts amid easy financial conditions. Business investment appears to be broadening beyond the AI sector. Higher tax refunds and strong payroll income growth have supported consumer spending thus far, despite headwinds from the Iran war. A broad range of indicators point to resilience and tentative signs of reacceleration in the labor market. We expect the unemployment rate will decline, reaching 4.1% by year-end. We think that the US economy remains relatively insulated from the crude oil price shock due to the Iran war given the small share of energy-goods consumption in household spending, as well as domestic energy production.

Inflation: Core inflation remains well above the Fed's $2\%$ target, and we believe risks are skewed to the upside. We forecast Q4 2026 core PCE inflation of $3.3\%$ y-o-y. We expect tariff price pressures will diminish in the coming quarters. However, this will likely be offset by new price pressures driven by supply shortages caused by AI (memory chips, electricity, etc.) along with spillovers from higher energy prices. Wage growth seems to have moderated gradually, but remains higher than the level consistent with the Fed's $2\%$ inflation goal, which is likely to lead to stickiness in supercore service inflation. We expect rent disinflation to continue, but at a very gradual pace.

Policy: We expect the Fed to remain on hold indefinitely. Fed Chair Warsh likely remains motivated to ease policy, but recent elevated inflation readings and hawkish Fedspeak suggest he may not be able to convince the majority of the Fed to support rate cuts. Moreover, support among Fed officials for an easing bias has begun to wane. While hikes appear unlikely in the near term, the Fed's hesitance to respond to tentative signs of inflation pressure raises the risk that they may end up "behind the curve," needing to hike quickly to re-establish credibility. The balance of risks is tilted towards tightening at this moment. On tariffs, wider Section 122 exemptions, lower tariff rates, and a greater focus on affordability suggest limited further escalation in US trade policy.

Risks: Further escalation of geopolitical risks could lead to a tightening of financial conditions and worsening of the fiscal outlook. Increased political pressures on individual FOMC participants could undermine the Fed's credibility, soliciting sharp market reactions. A bust of the AI boom could lead to material corrections in asset valuations. A continued shortage of memory chips and supply chain disruption due to a protracted Iran war could lead to a second round of goods price increases.

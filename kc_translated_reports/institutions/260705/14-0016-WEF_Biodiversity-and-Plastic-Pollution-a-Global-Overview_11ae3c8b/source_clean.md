## Preface

Clémence Schmid
Director, Global Plastic

When plastic waste is unmanaged or improperly discarded – as an estimated 130 million tonnes is every year – it can become a major source of pollution.

As awareness of this challenge has grown, action has expanded at all levels. Significant efforts have been made to tackle the mounting challenge of plastic waste, from negotiations towards a global plastics treaty to a growing range of international, regional and national initiatives aimed at addressing plastic pollution and advancing circular economy solutions.

However, much less attention has been given to understanding how plastic pollution affects biodiversity and the ecosystem services that underpin economies and human well-being.

This report aims to address that gap. It draws on field assessments conducted across nine geographies on three continents – developed by the World Economic Forum's Global Plastic Action Partnership (GPAP), with support from the Government of Canada. Together, this body of work presents one of the most detailed multi-country evidence bases to date on the interconnections between plastic pollution and biodiversity.

The impacts of plastic pollution are particularly severe for sectors such as fisheries, agriculture and tourism that depend directly on healthy natural systems. More broadly, the findings reinforce that biodiversity loss and plastic pollution are not

only environmental challenges, but increasingly economic and resilience challenges as well. More than half of global GDP is moderately or highly dependent on nature. The degradation of ecosystems therefore creates growing risks for communities, industries and economies at a time when they are already under pressure from climate change and geopolitical uncertainty.

Examining plastic pollution and biodiversity loss as a shared economic, environmental and social challenge also reveals important opportunities. It helps make the value of biodiversity more tangible to people who may feel disconnected from nature or unaware of the impacts of plastic pollution. It encourages collaboration across sectors and jurisdictions, reflecting the cross-border and interconnected nature of plastic pollution.

Perhaps most importantly, a joint focus on plastic pollution and biodiversity helps identify where action is needed most, enabling investments to deliver stronger outcomes for both pollution reduction and nature protection. This in turn reframes metrics of success that highlight outcomes for biodiversity, ecosystems and national economies – not tonnes of waste collected.

Above all, this report demonstrates that tackling plastic pollution and biodiversity loss together creates new opportunities for collaboration, investment and impact. The priority now is to translate this evidence into coordinated efforts that deliver measurable benefits for nature, people and economies.

# Executive summary

By tackling plastic pollution and biodiversity loss as an interconnected challenge, we can conserve the critical ecosystems that underpin the viability of human societies and economies.

Rates of extinction among animal and plant species are now tens to hundreds of times higher than the historical average. Plastic pollution is among the top five drivers of this biodiversity loss. Since 1950, annual plastic production has surged from 2 million to nearly 500 million tonnes, yet only 10% of all plastic ever made has been recycled. Every year, 130 million tonnes of plastic waste pollutes the planet's air, land and water. Without urgent action, that figure could more than double by 2040 – equivalent to dumping a lorry-load of plastic on nature every second.

Plastic waste harms biodiversity in three ways. Through physical harm – entanglement and ingestion – it affects over 4,000 species, from zooplankton to whales. It degrades habitats, suffocating mangrove roots, elevating disease risk in coral reefs and contaminating nesting sites for sea turtles. And through chemical pollution, it disrupts reproductive systems and compromises soil fertility and crop yields.

The economic stakes are huge. One peer-reviewed study concluded that the 75-150 million tonnes of plastic in the world's oceans have led to losses of between \$500 billion and \$2.5 trillion in marine ecosystem services every year. Plastic pollution is eroding fisheries, coastal tourism, agricultural productivity and the carbon sequestration capacity of ecosystems such as mangroves, which store four times more carbon per hectare than tropical forests. Plastic's greenhouse gas emissions of 2.7 billion tonnes a year are on course to exceed every country's in the world, bar China and the US, by 2040.

## Mapping the geography of impact broadens stakeholder cooperation

This synthesis report draws on field assessments conducted across nine geographies on three continents – Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Indonesia, Nigeria, Peru, the Philippines, Viet Nam and the Indian state of Maharashtra – commissioned by the World Economic Forum's Global Plastic Action Partnership (GPAP), with support from the Government of Canada.

One important contribution from this research is a map-based approach to pinpoint where plastic pollution and biodiversity loss converge most acutely. Instead of a waste management approach, researchers for GPAP's nine reports developed national risk maps that overlay plastic pollution pathways with biodiversity data – identifying the hotspots where interventions will deliver the greatest returns.

This geographic lens yields powerful insights. In Colombia, plastic generated in highland cities travels 1,000 kilometres down the Magdalena River to damage coral reefs and mangroves on the Caribbean coast – a connection invisible to the separate agencies responsible for urban waste and coastal biodiversity. In Costa Rica, more than 35% of Key Biodiversity Areas display ecological damage related to plastic pollution.

Mapping plastic's journey unites stakeholders who may not normally work together: inland cities and coastal settlements, farmers and fishers, corporates and traditional leaders such as the guardians of Nigeria's Osun-Oshogbo sacred groves. It creates a “source-to-sea” logic for governance and action that cuts across administrative boundaries and focuses effort where it can do most good.

## Businesses face mounting risks – but also opportunities for engagement

Businesses face rapidly escalating risks from their exposure to plastic pollution and its impact on biodiversity. The Forum's Global Risks Report 2026 ranks biodiversity loss and ecosystem collapse as the second most severe global risk over the next decade. Corporate liability cases linked to plastic pollution could reach \$100 billion per year by 2030; mandatory extended producer responsibility (EPR) schemes could add another \$100 billion to that bill. Reputational risks from association with high-polluting supply chains are already tangible. Yet only 12% of companies have set targets to halt biodiversity loss – compared with 78% that have climate targets – and fewer than 1% disclose their biodiversity impacts.

The opportunities, however, are equally significant. Some 91% of plastic waste is currently burned, buried or thrown away – representing \$80-120 billion a year in packaging value lost after a single use. Upstream, companies that spend \$570 billion manufacturing single-use plastic could nearly halve plastic pollution by 2040, through shifting towards reusable and compostable packaging, creating 8.6 million jobs in the process. Downstream, better product design could raise the share of economically recyclable plastic from 22% to 54%.

Companies across the plastics value chain – from producers and packagers to consumer brands that rely on them – are encouraged to embed biodiversity into their core strategies, disclose plastic-related impacts, and include EPR and biodiversity in their risk registers.

## Communities, cities and governments all have a role to play

Communities are on the frontlines of this crisis – and often its most effective responders. Informal networks – such as the women waste pickers of Ambujwadi near Mumbai – collect and sort nearly two-thirds of all recycled plastic globally, yet receive no formal recognition, stable wages or legal protection. Indigenous Peoples and local communities have long served as guardians of biodiversity: in Raja Ampat, Indonesia, they restrict fishing to allow ecosystems to regenerate; in Colombia’s Amazon, they turned waste sorting into an income once they recognised the threat to the rivers and forests on which their livelihoods depend. Incentive programmes, such as cash-for-plastic in the Philippines, have proved more effective at changing behaviour than regulation or penalties.

Cities are critical actors. Urban plastic waste degrades the biodiversity that underpins cities' drinking water, food supply chains and flood protection. Innovative municipal solutions range from motorbike-and-sidecar waste collection in narrow Philippine streets to AI-powered plastic-flow monitoring in Viet Nam.

National governments face a more structural challenge: plastic waste management and biodiversity conservation are governed by different ministries using different metrics. Priorities include breaking policy silos, connecting EPR targets to

ecological outcomes rather than tonnes collected, and ensuring sufficient resources reach local level to back up enforcement.

Global governance is evolving but incomplete. The Kunming-Montréal Global Biodiversity Framework sets ambitious targets for reducing pollution – yet most national biodiversity action plans fail to identify plastic as a primary threat. Meanwhile, negotiations on a global plastics treaty have stalled.

## Joint action on biodiversity and plastic pollution multiplies benefits

One of the most distinctive contributions of this research is the case for tackling plastic pollution and biodiversity loss together as a single, interconnected challenge. There are five compelling reasons:


Makes biodiversity's value tangible: tracing plastic waste's journey from disposal to the ecosystems it damages connects consumers and companies to the natural systems on which they depend.


Engages diverse stakeholders: city dwellers, businesses, ministries, fishers, farmers and communities are more likely to engage when the framing is not just waste management but protection of ecosystems on which shared livelihoods and economies depend.


Maximizes return on investment: mapping plastic waste's impact on biodiversity highlights hotspots where scarce resources will deliver the greatest good.


Breaks governance silos, by connecting upstream and downstream authorities, urban and rural municipalities, environment and economy ministries along shared ecological pathways.


Builds resilience: tackling plastic pollution's impact on biodiversity increases nature's resilience to cope with climate change, habitat degradation and overexploitation.

## Introduction

This report synthesizes and builds on two years of research in nine geographies across three continents, which assessed the impacts of plastic pollution on biodiversity and the most effective ways in which communities, cities and countries can respond.

The nine studies were commissioned by the Global Plastic Action Partnership (GPAP), the World Economic Forum's multi-stakeholder platform tackling plastic pollution, and supported by the Government of Canada (see Box 1).

This research contributes new insights into an important but still underexamined aspect of plastic waste. Conducted in Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Indonesia, Nigeria, Peru, the Philippines, Viet Nam and the Indian state of Maharashtra, this body of work represents the first detailed multi-country baseline of evidence for the biodiversity losses caused by plastic pollution and the consequent damage to ecosystem services and the economic sectors that depend on them.

While the country studies share a common framework, their methodologies, data availability and analytical depth vary across contexts, which may affect comparability. As the research was conducted within the GPAP network, the findings reflect the scope and priorities of these partnerships' geographic specificities. The insights presented should therefore be understood as indicative of emerging patterns and promising practices rather than definitive or universally generalizable conclusions.

This synthesis report captures the insights and best practices from these country studies and presents them in the context of the latest global research on plastic pollution's impact on biodiversity.

# Biodiversity and the impact of plastic pollution

Plastic waste directly degrades biodiversity, while emissions from plastic production fuel climate change which further destroys habitats.


# 1.1 | Scale and scope of the challenge

“Without urgent, ambitious action, plastic waste could more than double to 280 Mt per year by 2040 – equivalent to dumping a garbage truck into the environment every second.

Natural systems that sustain human civilisation are increasingly being disrupted by human pressures. Around one million of an estimated seven million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction – a rate tens to hundreds times higher than the average over the past 10 million years. $^{1}$ Freshwater species populations have declined globally by 85% since 1970; terrestrial populations have fallen by two-thirds and marine populations have more than halved. $^{2}$ Forests continue to be cleared at the rate of almost 11 million hectares a year. $^{3}$ Many scientists refer to the current period as a sixth mass extinction; $^{4}$ but for the first time it is driven by a single species – humans – rather than by asteroid impacts or geophysical forces.

Plastic pollution is one of the key factors driving biodiversity loss, along with destruction of both terrestrial and oceanic habitats, overexploitation of organisms, climate change and disruption through invasive species. $^{5}$ Plastic production exploded from 2 million tonnes (Mt) in 1950 to 460 Mt in 2019. $^{6}$ Just 10% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled – the rest ends up in landfills, rubbish dumps or the natural environment. $^{7}$ Every year, 130 Mt of plastic waste pollutes the planet's land, air and water. Without urgent, ambitious action, plastic waste could more than double to 280 Mt per year by 2040 – equivalent to dumping a garbage truck of plastic waste into the environment every second. This threatens to overwhelm waste management capacity, which is expected to grow at only half the speed of plastic production. $^{8}$

The impact of plastic pollution on biodiversity is serious and accelerating: the International Union for Conservation of Nature has termed it “a major driver of biodiversity loss and ecosystem degradation”, affecting all marine, freshwater and land ecosystems. $^{9}$ Since 1980, marine plastic pollution has increased tenfold, according to the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), “affecting at least 267 species, including 86%

of marine turtles, 44% of seabirds and 43% of marine mammals". $^{10}$

The global plastic value chain is also driving the climate crisis, emitting 2.7 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide-equivalent (GtCO $_{2}$ e) in 2025. Under business as usual, plastic's emissions are projected to rise by nearly 60% to 4.2 GtCO $_{2}$ e by 2040 – equivalent to 1 billion internal combustion engine cars. If plastic were a country, this would make the industry the world's third-largest emitter after China and the United States. $^{11}$

According to analysis by The Pew Charitable Trusts and Systemiq, cumulative emissions from the global plastic system from 2025 to 2040 would equal 42% of the remaining carbon budget required to limit global warming to 1.5°C, making “business as usual” (BAU) for the plastics industry incompatible with the climate goals of the Paris Agreement. $^{12}$

This matters for biodiversity, because climate change is having a significant impact on nature, from genes to ecosystems. Shifting temperature and precipitation patterns, ocean acidification and extreme weather events are altering and destroying habitats, pushing species beyond their tolerance ranges. Climate change has negatively affected almost half of threatened terrestrial mammals and a quarter of threatened birds – “many species are unable to cope locally with the rapid pace of climate change, through either evolutionary or behavioural processes,” according to IPBES. $^{13}$

Meanwhile, biodiverse ecosystems from the Arctic tundra to tropical islands and coastlines are under threat from the compound impacts of plastic pollution and climate-driven rising tides and warmer seas. Coral reefs, for example, are predicted to decline to less than 1% of their former cover at $2^{\circ}$ C of atmospheric warming – and their chances of resisting or recovering are hampered by plastic waste, which can multiply their risk of developing disease by 20 times.

# 1.2 | Global governance – progressing but still patchy

Global governance of biodiversity has evolved significantly in recent decades. The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF), adopted at the UN's Biodiversity Conference (COP15) in December 2022, framed a historic set of global targets for biodiversity conservation and sustainable use (see Box 3). The GBF is ambitious and comprehensive, but its implementation pathways – National Biodiversity Strategies and Action Plans (NBSAPs) – are nationally determined. Many NBSAPs have not been updated since the GBF was launched or omit to address plastic pollution directly as a primary threat to biodiversity.

# 1.3 Pathways for the impact of plastic pollution on biodiversity

Plastic pollution principally impacts biodiversity through three direct pathways:

\- Physical harm: entanglement and ingestion

\- Habitat degradation

\- Chemical contamination

To design interventions that maximize effectiveness and efficiency, it is essential to understand not only how these pathways work, but also the ways in which biodiversity and plastic pollution interact in different urban, rural, riparian, coastal and marine geographies (see Chapter 3).

## Physical harm: entanglement and ingestion

The most visible pathway is direct physical interaction between plastic and wildlife. Globally, over 4,000 species of microbes, plants and animals have been documented interacting with plastic through ingestion or entanglement, mostly in the marine realm but also in estuarine and freshwater ecosystems. $^{24}$

“

## We have seen seagulls trapped in bags and boobies with ropes tangled around their legs.

Fisherman, Ancón, Peru

Abandoned, lost or discarded fishing gear (ALDFG) represents a chronic entanglement risk for marine megafauna, with tens of thousands of square kilometres of fishing nets lost at sea annually. $^{25}$ Meanwhile, the marine aquaculture industry in Indonesia alone dumps over 80,000 tonnes of plastic ropes, buoys, harvest bins and feed sacks into its oceans every year, according to the World Bank. $^{26}$ In Indonesian Borneo, the critically endangered Mahakam Irrawaddy dolphin – a population of approximately 60 individuals – has been regularly reported entangled in fishing nets, while saltwater crocodiles in Sulawesi have been found strangled by discarded tyres. $^{27}$

Plastic disrupts food webs through ingestion by organisms from zooplankton to megafauna. In Indonesian waters, filter-feeders such as manta rays and whale sharks ingest more than 100 plastic particles per hour, resulting in toxic exposure. $^{28}$ In Indonesia's Raja Ampat – an MPA and designated UNESCO Biosphere Reserve – sperm whales and dugong have been found dead with large amounts of plastic debris in their stomachs. $^{29}$ Crabs deliberately ingest waterborne microplastics, mistaking the particles for prey – studies have found that the accumulation of microplastics in crabs clogs digestive tracts, disrupts energy metabolism and increases mortality rates. $^{30}$

Sea turtles suffer from both entanglement and ingestion – bringing socio-economic consequences to local communities who earn a living from the sea (see Box 4).

## Habitat degradation

Plastic pollution modifies habitats in ways that reduce their carrying capacity for native species, often without causing immediately visible mortality. Plastic waste can contaminate sediments and root systems, while floating plastics serve as vectors for invasive species to reach vulnerable habitats. $^{33}$ This mobile “plastisphere” also introduces bacterial pathogens such as Aeromonas caviae, which pose a risk of transmission to humans through contaminated seafood.


## A day of dirty sea is almost a lost day; the nets come out with trash instead of fish.

Fisherman, Ancón, Peru

## Mangroves

Mangrove forests are critical ecosystems that are particularly vulnerable to plastic pollution. Their dense root systems – so important in protecting juvenile marine species such as shrimps, crabs and finfish – are also ideal for trapping plastics and the toxins they contain. Plastic debris clogs waterways, altering hydrodynamics, the physical properties of sediments and aeration of soil. $^{34}$ This in turn affects gas exchange in roots and the habitat structure for associated biota; it also fosters the growth of harmful microbes that disrupt the decomposition of organic matter and key biogeochemical cycles, negatively affecting molluscs and crustaceans living on mangrove roots. $^{35}$

During the monsoon, high tide gets more plastic waste trapped in mangroves.

Respondent, Thane Creek, Maharashtra, India

Research in Colombia, which hosts 7,500 km $^{2}$ of mangroves on both its Pacific and Caribbean coasts, shows that, by altering soil structure, root dynamics and bottom-dwelling fauna, plastics reduce mangrove forests' ability to perform key ecological functions $^{36}$ and recover from disturbances, weakening their role as a natural coastal buffer. $^{37}$

## Coral reefs

Coral reefs face a different but equally serious form of habitat modification. A landmark study in 2018 of 159 reefs across Asia-Pacific estimated that over 11 billion plastic items were entangled on the region's reefs. The study assessed the role of plastic waste in promoting microbial colonization by pathogens and found that the likelihood of disease increased from 4% to 89% when corals were in contact with plastic. This is because plastic surfaces concentrate pathogenic microbes and abrade coral tissue, creating infection entry points. $^{38}$

Structurally complex corals – such as the branching Acropora species that provide the habitat diversity on which reef fish communities depend – are eight times more likely to be impacted than simpler, more robust forms. This in turn has a disproportionate impact on food chains for valuable fisheries.

## Beaches

Plastic pollution is widely recognized as causing significant stress to beach ecosystems. In Viet Nam, plastic waste on beaches has been identified as a factor contributing to declines in sea turtle nesting activity and hatching success, alongside egg collection and coastal infrastructure.

Monitoring by IUCN Viet Nam from 2019 to 2021 assessed marine debris on 33 beaches, including in 10 MPAs, and classified 73.5% of beach segments as polluted or heavily polluted with plastic waste.

## Agricultural systems

Roughly one-third of plastic refuse is estimated to enter soils or inland waters, where natural weather breaks it down into microplastics (fragments of <5mm). Farming across the world makes extensive use of plastic products, such as mulch sheets, drip irrigation pipes, tarpaulins and laminated packaging. In India's Maharashtra state, for example, fertiliser and pesticide packaging (classified as hazardous waste) is commonly dumped in ditches or burned at field corners. As a result, plastic waste frequently becomes embedded in soils.

Sewage sludge – used widely by farmers as fertilizer – is another important and underappreciated pathway by which plastic finds its way into soils and aquatic ecosystems. Treatment plants, which process both sewage and wastewater from drains, are highly efficient at removing microplastics from water. However, rather than eliminating it, this process concentrates microplastics, many of which come from textiles and car tyres. Sewage sludge has been found to contain on average 14,750 particles per kilogram, leading to high concentrations of microplastics in topsoils fertilized with sludge. $^{40}$

Although oceans have attracted far more scientific attention than land-based ecosystems, “mounting evidence shows that agricultural fields and other terrestrial systems now receive enormous microplastic loads”, according to research by scientists in Pakistan published in 2025. $^{41}$ They add that “soils may harbour 4-23 times more microplastic particles than ocean water, and farmland soils could cumulatively store more microplastics than all ocean basins”.

This matters because microplastics impair crop growth and could contribute to a “projected 5-15% decline in crop yields in the coming decades”. In turn, these particles infiltrate the global food web and pose a rising threat both to agricultural

productivity and food security. Farmers have also noticed the quality of their soil declining when plastic waste blocks water flows and increases stagnation, damaging soil structure and fertility. This in turn affects soil biota, water infiltration and crop resilience. $^{42}$

## Chemical contamination

Plastics are not chemically inert; they contain additives – such as plasticizers, flame retardants, stabilizers or pigments – which can interfere with hormone systems in exposed organisms, reducing fertility and hatch success in fish and invertebrates. $^{43}$ In Indonesia’s Benoa Bay, Bali, sea urchins and seagrass have been found contaminated with both heavy metals and microplastic fibres. $^{44}$

Plastic surfaces attract hydrophobic pollutants and metals, often concentrating them above surrounding water levels. In Viet Nam's Ho Chi Minh City, microplastics in surface waters combine with chemical compounds at concentrations posing risks for fish and invertebrates in the Sai Gon River and the downstream Can Gio mangrove estuary. $^{45}$

The pathway from chemical contamination to population-level biodiversity impact is less well understood than physical harm, but accumulating evidence suggests it is real. Field observations in North Sulawesi show that plastic debris deters mud crabs from laying eggs, in line with research finding that microplastics and their chemical additives can disrupt the endocrine system, impairing reproductive success. $^{46}$

## Ecosystem services provided by healthy biodiversity

Healthy, biodiverse ecosystems deliver a vast range of “ecosystem services” on which human societies and economies directly depend – including goods, services and gifts that IPBES has termed “nature’s contributions to people”. $^{47}$ More than half of global GDP – equivalent to 58 trillion – is estimated to be moderately or highly dependent on nature – although more broadly, humanity’s very existence depends on the food and water systems underpinned by biodiverse ecosystems. $^{48}$

These services are often presented under four functional types:

Provisioning: Food, fresh water, raw materials, medicines.

Regulating: Climate regulation, water purification, flood and erosion control, pest control, disease regulation, pollination.

Supporting: Soil formation, photosynthesis, nutrient cycling, water cycle, habitat provision.

Cultural: Recreation, tourism, cultural and spiritual value, aesthetic appreciation, education.

## Marine ecosystems – the value at risk from plastic pollution

Estimates of the value to societies and economies of marine ecosystems go way beyond simply the market value of fisheries or tourism sectors and run into the tens of trillions of dollars. This is an emerging area of research and data aggregated at a global level should be interpreted as indicative of orders of magnitude rather than precise values. The same can be said for estimates of the impact of plastic pollution.

One peer-reviewed and widely cited study published in 2019 concluded that the total stock of marine plastic in the world's oceans in 2011 – estimated at 75-150 million tonnes – had led to a 1-5% decline in delivery of marine ecosystem services, which equated to an annual loss of between \$500 billion and \$2.5 trillion in the value of benefits derived from those services. $^{49}$ The authors added that this estimate “represents a ‘lower bound’ of the full economic costs of marine plastic”.

## Carbon sequestration

Globally, the planet's oceans and land absorb over half of all anthropogenic CO $_2$ emissions. The UN has termed biodiversity “our strongest natural defence against climate change”. Without intact, biodiverse ecosystems, net-zero targets become significantly harder to achieve. $^{55}$

Ecosystems that are functioning and resilient can buffer extreme weather events, act as carbon sinks and better absorb climate-driven feedbacks. However, when biodiversity is degraded, this reduces the resilience of both marine and terrestrial ecosystems and their ability to sequester carbon; this in turn increases greenhouse gas concentrations and accelerates climate change. $^{56}$

Mangroves are critical allies in fighting climate change. They store up to four times more carbon per hectare than most mature tropical forests $^{57}$ and sequester $CO_{2}$ from the atmosphere at a rate 10 times greater than tropical forests. $^{58}$ Yet, as GPAP's report on Indonesia highlighted, they are particularly vulnerable to plastic waste: "Plastic accumulation can block mangrove roots, stunting growth and causing restoration failures. While mangroves may withstand partial root coverage, complete suffocation by plastic debris is fatal. Plastics can persist in sediments for up to a century, posing long-term risks to mangrove survival and the ecological services they provide."

## Coastal risk reduction

Biodiverse, healthy ecosystems also function as the world's most cost-effective risk management. Degrading this “green infrastructure” – whether through plastic pollution, conversion or overexploitation – converts natural buffers into sources of systemic risk for governments, businesses and communities alike. Equally, actions to protect and restore ecosystems reduces exposure to a broad spectrum of hazards.

The UN Environment Programme identifies the degradation of both marine and terrestrial ecosystems as “a major driver of disaster risk”. $^{59}$ Mangroves and coral reefs provide coastlines with protection from climate-driven sea-level rise and storm surges worth hundreds of billions of dollars a year. Re-establishing coral reefs significantly reduces coastal flooding risk, while investment in restoring saltmarshes and dykes can protect coastal communities at a lower cost than engineered alternatives. $^{60}$

This geographic approach supports a key finding of this synthesis report – that joint action to map and tackle the nexus of plastic pollution and biodiversity loss brings multiple advantages that separate approaches to each issue do not (see also Chapter 5). For example, by mapping the geographies where plastic pollution has the highest impact on biodiversity, governments are able to target scarce resources to locations and priorities that will deliver the most cost-effective outcomes.

Equally, mapping plastic waste's impact on biodiversity offers a way to unite, perhaps for the first time, disparate actors along pathways of plastic pollution: from the coastal communities whose fishing nets catch nearly as much waste as food, upriver to the municipalities responsible for ensuring waste stays out of waterways, then upstream further to the companies responsible for designing and manufacturing the plastic packaging that enters economies in the first place. It unites stakeholders from different ecosystems and sectors to act along plastic waste pathways, rather than along political, administrative or social lines.

The same logic applies to breaking government silos. Mapping the nexus reveals how plastic waste often goes on a long journey from where it is dumped to where it has most impact. In Colombia, for example, plastic generated in highland Bogotá and Medellín reaches Caribbean coral reefs and mangroves via the Magdalena River; so waste management in inland cities is a biodiversity protection measure for coastal communities 1,000 kilometres away. However, separate administrative systems for urban waste governance and coastal biodiversity governance make this connection invisible. A mapping-based approach enables governments to adopt an integrated “source-to-sea” approach to the governance of plastic waste, aligning disparate authorities along ecological pathways rather than administrative or political boundaries.

Businesses are exposed to a range of economic, legal and reputational risks due to unmanaged plastic waste and its impacts on biodiversity and ecosystem functions. At the same time, addressing these risks can create strong incentives for innovation and unlock new sources of value, from cost savings to new markets and revenue streams.

The linear model of single-use plastics represents billions of dollars of value literally thrown away after one short lifecycle. $^{84}$ The cost that plastic waste imposes on marine ecosystems alone – estimated at between 500 billion and 2.5 trillion per year – far outweighs the estimated 140 billion per year for effective waste management by 2040. $^{85}$ Meanwhile, the value of a healthy ecosystem in supporting the resilience of economies and societies is almost incalculable.

This level of waste and cost offers valuable opportunities for businesses to engage in finding solutions. There are both upstream and downstream value chain opportunities for companies to reduce plastic waste's impact on biodiversity:

\- Upstream: improved product and packaging design can help optimize plastic use, reduce unnecessary applications, expand reuse models and enhance recyclability, while exploring alternative materials where appropriate.

\- Downstream: opportunities include committing to targets for both recycling and recycled content, cooperating with civil society on more effective waste avoidance and recovery, and voluntary disclosure of plastic pollution-related risks and impacts.

While the business debate on addressing the challenge of plastic waste has to date revolved largely around the cost-benefit analysis of recycling and circularity, the impact of plastic pollution on biodiversity offers a critical new angle for businesses to consider. As this report has made clear, not only are important nature-dependent economic sectors directly impacted by plastic pollution; but in addition, viewing the challenge through a biodiversity lens focuses the awareness of all stakeholders – including businesses – on where efforts to reduce the impacts of plastic pollution can be most cost-effective and valuable.

# 3.3 Downstream opportunities for private sector engagement

While optimizing plastic use is an important lever, plastic remains a critical material for many applications – particularly in low- and middle-income countries where it provides a cost-effective and hygienic way to deliver food and drink. With global production capacity expected to grow significantly by 2040, strengthening downstream waste management and recycling systems will be essential to protect biodiversity and all the ecosystem services that depend on it.

## Recycle

At a global level, as seen in OECD's graphic in Figure 4, although 55 Mt of waste was collected for recycling in 2019, 22 Mt ended up as a residue that needed further disposal; so just 33 Mt or 9% of plastic waste was ultimately recycled.

A key reason is that many plastic items are designed in ways that make recycling uneconomical or impossible in many local waste management systems. Better design of products and packaging for recyclability could expand the share of economically recyclable plastic from \~22% today to 54% by 2040. More efficient recycling – driven by better design for recyclability along with more efficient waste collection – could reduce plastic pollution by a further 20%. $^{104}$ High-tech plastic sorting and recycling have been identified in a recent World Economic Forum report as among 50 investible opportunities for a new nature-positive economy (see Box 11).

## Extended producer responsibility

All nine of the GPAP study countries have some form of mandatory extended producer responsibility (EPR) framework in place or under development, requiring producers to finance the collection and recycling of their packaging.

In Indonesia, GPAP researchers refer to EPR as a mechanism to shift responsibility for waste management upstream to producers, adding that “fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) companies were highlighted as particularly influential actors because of their control over upstream production, strong lobbying capacity, and ability to influence regulatory outcomes”. By contrast, recycling industries operating downstream, often with limited funding, “were perceived as less supportive of stringent policy measures”.

EPR implementation quality varies dramatically, however. The Indonesian government's Waste Reduction Roadmap requires producers to redesign products for circularity, phase out single-use plastics and implement waste take-back mechanisms, but enforcement is incomplete. $^{105}$ The Philippines' EPR Act, passed in 2022, is undermined by non-compliance of plastic producers – driven by a perceived lack of economic benefits – along with weak enforcement, high compliance costs and inadequate recycling infrastructure. $^{106}$ Viet Nam's Law on Environmental Protection (LEP, 2020), among the most advanced frameworks in the region, introduced EPR and a phase-out of single-use plastics in tourism and commerce from 2025 and from the entire economy by 2030. $^{107}$ Yet EPR and economic instruments are only partly operational.

Meanwhile, agricultural plastics remain largely unaddressed by EPR frameworks across all nine countries, which focus mainly on consumer packaging – despite agricultural soils being a primary global sink for plastic pollution. As a result, agricultural plastic waste is routinely burned or dumped in fields and watercourses across Indonesia, Maharashtra and other GPAP study countries.

## √ Recommendation

Governments: improve enforcement of EPR laws and include agricultural plastics in EPR legislation.

## Connect EPR compliance targets to ecological outcomes

One critical EPR reform to emerge from GPAP's country studies is to connect EPR compliance targets to ecological outcomes rather than to volumes of waste recovery. A tonne of PET bottles collected in Hanoi, for example, does not deliver the same biodiversity benefit as a tonne of ghost nets removed from a coral reef in Viet Nam's Con Dao marine protected area.

Directing a greater share of EPR compliance towards areas of high biodiversity value at greatest risk of plastic pollution – rather than exclusively towards urban collection systems where recovery is cheapest – would substantially improve the biodiversity return on EPR investment. Mapping solutions described in Chapter 2, such as Peru's Plastic Biodiversity Risk Index, provide ready-made spatial targeting tools for directing EPR investment towards the ecosystems whose biodiversity is under the greatest pressure from plastic waste.

GPAP's Nigeria report calls for producer responsibility organizations (PROs) – paid by plastic producers and retailers to coordinate their plastic waste management and recycling obligations under EPR – to be engaged through regulation and partnerships to ensure that EPR investments target riverine, mangrove, wetland and coastal hotspots.

## √ Recommendation

Governments: connect EPR compliance targets to ecological outcomes rather than to volumes of waste recovery, using mapping that highlights biodiversity at risk from plastic to maximize the biodiversity value of EPR action by companies and PROs.

There is also a risk to corporate inaction on plastic waste. If governments were to require all companies to cover the costs of their plastic waste management, for example under mandatory EPR schemes, the global corporate price tag could reach \$100 billion per year by 2040, according to the UN. $^{108}$

A common principle in business is that “you can’t manage what you don’t measure”. Corporate environmental disclosure initiatives – such as CDP, the IFRS Foundation’s International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), and the twin taskforces on nature-related and climate-related financial disclosures (TNFD and TCFD) – have transformed how the corporate world measures and discloses its impacts and dependencies on climate and nature. The global plastics industry is no exception: it currently lacks transparency across the value chain, which makes it hard for governments, NGOs or consumers to understand how much plastic is being produced, the chemicals that plastic contains and any negative impacts on environment and health.

Washington, DC – July 7, 2026: On June 29, the Executive Board of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) completed the Article IV Consultation for Solomon Islands. $^{1}$

Economic activity in 2025 exceeded staff expectations. The economy is estimated to have grown by 3.5 percent, following 3.0 percent growth in 2024, supported by strong gold production, agricultural output, and progress in infrastructure projects. Inflation averaged 2.7 percent. Staff estimates that the current account balance surged to a surplus of 5.6 percent of GDP in 2025, driven by gold and agricultural exports, while the fiscal deficit is estimated to have widened to 3.9 percent of GDP, reflecting continued spending pressures and progress in externally financed infrastructure projects.

The Middle East war is expected to weigh on growth and heighten inflationary pressures in 2026. Growth is projected to slow to 2.6 percent, while inflation is expected to rise to an average of 5.4 percent, reflecting higher fuel prices. The current account is projected to return to a deficit of 3.4 percent of GDP, weighed by fuel and food import prices. Staff expects the fiscal deficit to widen to 4.1 percent of GDP, amid significant budget implementation risks arising from depleted cash reserves, revenue uncertainty, and limited investor capacity to absorb government bonds.

Risks to the outlook are firmly tilted to the downside, notably from prolonged geopolitical tensions, domestic political instability, and budget financing constraints. Upside risks include a further expansion of gold mining—if supported by robust resource management and limited fiscal incentives—and additional donor support.

## Executive Board Assessment $^{2}$

In concluding the 2026 Article IV consultation with Solomon Islands, Executive Directors endorsed staff's appraisal, as follows:

Staff projects growth to soften and inflationary pressures to rise in 2026. Growth is expected to remain moderate over the medium term, supported by agriculture, mining, and infrastructure projects, but constrained by declining logging activity, limited diversification, and weak governance. Current account deficits are projected to persist, while strong gold exports and continued aid inflows are expected to support rising reserve coverage. Solomon Islands' external position in 2025 was assessed as broadly in line with fundamentals. Staff expects continued fiscal deficits leading to higher public debt, while the risks of external debt distress and overall debt distress remain low and moderate, respectively. Risks to the outlook are firmly tilted to the downside, notably from prolonged geopolitical tensions, domestic political instability, and budget financing constraints.

Reducing fiscal deficits and rebuilding liquidity buffers is critical at this juncture. Depleted cash balances and rising debt underscore the need for realistic, fully financed budgets to avoid disruptive budget freezes. Staff's advice—capping domestically financed deficits while steadily rebuilding cash balances—therefore remains appropriate. Reallocating resources away from the CDFs and strengthening coordination with donors would enhance spending efficiency. Further delays in introducing a VAT should be avoided, and efforts to strengthen tax enforcement and rationalize exemptions, particularly in extractive industries, should be accelerated. Measures to help mitigate the impact of the war should be strictly time-bound, preserve price signals, be as targeted as possible, and be well coordinated with donors.

Long-standing weaknesses in PFM continue to undermine fiscal transparency and credibility. Persistent delays in fiscal reporting, audits of government financial statements, and the completion of the Pacific Games audit weaken accountability. Immediate priorities include resuming regular inter-agency coordination meetings to facilitate fiscal reporting and completing the FMIS upgrade. Strengthening procurement, audit, and debt management capacity across government remains critical.

Solomon Islands should begin considering a medium-term fiscal framework to properly manage mineral resource revenues. Effective implementation of such a fiscal framework requires strengthening basic PFM functions, including realistic budgeting and timely, reliable fiscal reporting.

The CBSI should remain ready to tighten monetary policy in response to inflationary pressures stemming from the Middle East war, while avoiding ad-hoc exchange rate adjustments. The primary response should be to allow the exchange rate to move in line with the currency basket, as it is calibrated to limit exchange rate volatility and support price stability. Adherence to the fixed exchange rate regime is essential to preserve policy credibility and transparency and to support market development. Adopting a coherent foreign exchange market policy would strengthen policy consistency and communication with market participants.

Staff welcomes the CBSI's commitment to modernizing its monetary policy operations and encourages phased but steady implementation of the reform agenda. Near-term priorities include gradually absorbing excess liquidity in the banking system and introducing standing facilities linked to the policy rate. Establishing effective monetary policy operations would help the CBSI better manage a potential surge in foreign exchange inflows associated with an expansion of mining activity.

While the financial sector remains broadly sound, addressing gaps in financial stability, inclusion, and integrity remains crucial. Priorities include upgrading legal and regulatory frameworks, strengthening risk-based supervision, and establishing a framework for crisis management. Completing the rollout of the outstanding National Payment System components would further support financial inclusion. The authorities should expedite legal reforms to establish a robust AML/CFT system.

Diversifying the economy and addressing governance weaknesses are key to achieving sustainable and inclusive growth. Staff welcomes the authorities' focus on boosting productivity in agriculture, while improving the business environment and addressing infrastructure gaps remain priorities. Efforts to address governance weaknesses should be accelerated, including by advancing legislation in extractive sectors and resuming the implementation of the National Anti-Corruption Strategy.

Slow moving climate change is expected to have incremental but meaningful cumulative effects on the economy and debt trajectory over the long term. Staff's assessment highlights the need for adaptation policies, particularly to mitigate impacts on populations vulnerable to sea-level rise. A critical first step is to build basic PFM capacity to prioritize, execute, and monitor climate-relevant projects.

Strengthening core capacity for sound macroeconomic management remains the near-term IMF CD priority. Data provided by the authorities continues to exhibit some shortcomings that somewhat hamper surveillance, particularly in national accounts and GFS. The IMF continues to support efforts to address these gaps. Other immediate priorities include strengthening PFM, establishing a medium-term fiscal framework for managing resource revenues, improving monetary policy and foreign exchange operations, and enhancing financial sector stability.

It is proposed that the next Article IV consultation takes place on the standard 12-month cycle.

## KEY ISSUES

Context. Economic growth and the external position strengthened in 2025, supported by export-led production in mining and agriculture. Further expansion of gold mining could boost growth and generate sizable fiscal revenues, if it is managed prudently with limited fiscal incentives. However, the country faces heightened risks from the war in the Middle East, while underlying fragilities, including weak state capacity and political instability, persist. Depleted government cash buffers, together with long-standing weaknesses in public financial management (PFM) and governance, continue to constrain effective policy implementation.

## Main Policy Recommendations.

\- Fiscal policy. Reducing fiscal deficits and rebuilding liquidity buffers is critical. This requires realistic, fully financed budgets guided by simple, top-down guidelines. Measures to help mitigate the impact of the war should be strictly time-bound, preserve price signals, be as targeted as possible, and be well coordinated with donors. Further delays in introducing a value-added tax (VAT) should be avoided, and addressing persistent weaknesses in fiscal reporting and PFM is an urgent priority. The authorities should begin considering a medium-term fiscal framework to properly manage mineral resource revenues.

\- Monetary and exchange rate policies. The central bank should remain ready to tighten monetary policy in response to inflationary pressures stemming from the Middle East, while avoiding ad-hoc exchange rate adjustments. Staff encourages phased and steady implementation of the reform agenda to modernize monetary policy operations, starting with the gradual absorption of excess liquidity in the banking system.

\- Financial sector policy. While the sector remains broadly sound, efforts to address gaps in financial stability, integrity, and inclusion need to be further intensified.

\- Structural reforms. Accelerating reforms is needed to raise growth potential, including measures to boost productivity in agriculture and fisheries, improve the business environment, build infrastructure, strengthen governance, and enhance climate resilience. Addressing data gaps is critical for informed policy making.

## CONTEXT

1. Solomon Islands is beginning to see signs of emerging growth opportunities, while facing heightened risks and persistent fragilities. Strong gold exports are significantly improving the external position and could potentially offer fiscal revenue gains, depending on the outcome of revenue sharing negotiations with a mining company. Nonetheless, the outlook is subject to significant risks stemming from the war in the Middle East. In addition, the underlying drivers of fragility—such as limited connectivity, high exposure to climate change, and weak state capacity—persist, as discussed in the updated Country Engagement Strategy (CES, Annex I). Political instability remains elevated, as evidenced by a recent change in government following the third motion of no confidence in the past two years.

## RECENT DEVELOPMENTS

2. Growth has picked up since the last Article IV consultation. $^{1}$ The economy is estimated to have grown by 3.5 percent in 2025, following 3.0 percent growth in 2024, supported by strong gold production, agricultural output, and progress in donor-funded infrastructure projects. Adverse impacts of global trade tensions have so far been limited, partly reflecting Solomon Islands' minimal exports to the US.

3. Inflationary pressures have remained contained so far, amid volatile domestic prices. Headline inflation rose to 3.1 percent year-on-year in March 2026, after averaging 2.7 percent in 2025, mainly driven by volatile movements in betel nut prices. Imported inflation declined to -0.7 percent, while core inflation—excluding volatile items such as betel nuts—remained low at 0.7 percent. $^{2}$ The Central

4. The current account balance is estimated to have surged to a surplus of 5.6 percent of GDP in 2025 from a deficit of 4.2 percent in 2024. This was driven by strong gold and agricultural exports—supported by higher international prices—and relatively flat fuel imports. Services exports were also strong, as Solomon Islands hosted regional and international events. This leaves the government with limited cash reserves, with a broader cash balance of SI\$140 million (0.4 months of total spending), although the 2025 budget was executed without tight expenditure controls, unlike in recent years. Public debt is estimated to have risen to 29.5 percent in 2025, driven by both external and domestic borrowing.

## OUTLOOK AND RISKS

6. Staff expects growth to soften and inflationary pressures to mount in 2026. $^{3}$ Growth is projected to weaken to 2.6 percent in 2026, as the global oil shock will weigh on domestic and external demand. Sectors particularly reliant on electricity and transport, including agriculture, mining, and tourism, are expected to see a slowdown. Inflation is expected to rise to 5.4 percent in 2026, reflecting higher fuel and food prices. Over the medium term, growth is projected at around 3 percent, supported by agriculture, mining, tourism, and donor-funded infrastructure projects, but constrained by declining logging activity and an undiversified economic base with weak governance. $^{4}$ Inflation is forecast to return gradually to around 3 percent over the medium term.

7. The current account is expected to revert to a deficit. Staff projects a current account deficit of 3.4 percent of GDP in 2026: while exports are expected to remain supported by robust gold production, imports are projected to increase markedly, reflecting rising fuel and food prices. Over the medium term, current account deficits of around 3 percent of GDP are expected, reflecting weakening log exports and continued import needs for infrastructure projects. Steady remittances from seasonal workers are expected to help contain these deficits. Robust gold exports, together

with continued aid inflows, are expected to drive sustained accumulation of foreign reserves. As a result, reserves are forecast to rise steadily to 11 months of prospective imports by 2031, well above the adequacy range.

8. Staff expects a wider fiscal deficit in 2026, amid significant budget implementation risks. The 2026 budget formulated by the previous government envisages a significant increase in expenditure, especially in development spending. The budget anticipates financing this spending through a surge in tax revenue—with tax exemptions expiring for a key gold mining company—and sizable budget support from donors (6 percent of GDP). However, mining revenues may be weaker than expected given capacity constraints at revenue authorities, while budget support disbursements depend on the implementation of agreed reforms. Despite assuming some additional borrowing amid investors' limited absorptive capacity, the budget still leaves a financing gap of 0.8 percent of GDP. Accordingly, staff considers the 2026 budget unlikely to be executed as planned, as in previous years, predicting a deficit of 4.1 percent of GDP. $^{5}$

## 9. Fiscal deficits of around 3 percent of GDP are expected over the medium term.

Expenditure needs to boost growth potential, as well as a narrow revenue base on the back of weak governance, are expected to continue driving these deficits. Public debt is projected to continue growing, exceeding the authorities' threshold of 35 percent of GDP in 2029. The Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) indicates that the risk of external debt distress and the overall risk of debt distress remain low and moderate, respectively, as in the previous DSA. However, stress tests point to significant risks—stemming from volatile commodity prices, vulnerability to natural disasters, and an undiversified export base—while debt management capacity remains low. This underscores the importance of prioritizing grant financing where available.

10. Risks to the outlook are firmly tilted to the downside (Annex IV). A prolonged war in the Middle East would pose severe downside risks (Box 1). The formation of the new government following recent political turmoil could complicate policy execution and further delay legislative actions, including those required for budget support disbursements. The risk of a freeze in budget execution due to financing shortfalls remains elevated, which could adversely affect the economy. The country remains particularly vulnerable to natural disasters. $^{6}$ Moreover, further escalation in global trade barriers and policy uncertainty could weaken demand from key trading partners. On the upside, an expansion of gold mining—if supported by robust resource management and revenue administration with limited fiscal incentives—could boost output and allow for additional government expenditure. Additional donor assistance could also support the economy.

## Box 1. Prolonged Middle East War: Impact and Policy

Solomon Islands would be severely affected by persistently high fuel and food prices stemming from a prolonged Middle East war as in the severe scenario in the April 2026 WEO. The country relies on imported fuel for nearly all domestic transport and electricity, and its geographic dispersion makes economic activity—including agriculture, exports, and tourism—highly sensitive to transportation and energy costs. $^{1/}$ Food items account for around one-third of the consumption basket, with roughly half sourced from imports. Second-round effects could be significant, as seen in 2022 when higher global commodity prices temporarily pushed core inflation above 7 percent. Additional spillovers could arise through lower aid flows from donors facing weaker growth and reduced remittances from seasonal workers abroad, while the impact on gold exports would depend on movements in international gold prices.

The previous government introduced short-term cuts in fuel taxes from May 1 to August 1, the cost of which is estimated at 0.4 percent of GDP. While the new government has not made any announcement, it may need to extend the measure beyond 2026 if the war is prolonged, given the absence of a platform for targeted social assistance. This could significantly weaken the fiscal position amid substantial budget implementation risks. Staff therefore advises better targeting—for example, by limiting relief to specific fuel types—and incorporating clear sunset clauses to contain fiscal costs, while maintaining partial price signals. The government should also outline a credible plan to finance these costs through expenditure rationalization, additional revenues, and donor support. Sector-wide support or ad-hoc exchange rate adjustments should be avoided.

1/ The country holds fuel reserves equivalent to about 30–60 days of consumption.

## Authorities' Views

11. The authorities broadly agreed with staff's assessment of the outlook and risks. They acknowledged heightened uncertainty stemming from the ongoing Middle East crisis, noting that a prolonged disruption to oil supplies could have severe economic repercussions through higher living expenses and production costs. On the other hand, the authorities envisage a more optimistic growth trajectory and a stronger external position, assuming an expansion in gold production, while anticipating higher inflation owing to a persistent fuel shock. The authorities are more sanguine about their ability to implement the 2026 budget, citing efforts to expedite budget support disbursements and broaden the investor base for government bonds.

## ECONOMIC POLICIES

## A. Fiscal Policy

12. Reducing fiscal deficits and rebuilding liquidity buffers is critical at this juncture. The 2026 budget projects that the public debt ceiling of 35 percent of GDP—the medium-term fiscal anchor that staff considers still relevant—will be nearly reached within two years, reflecting large anticipated fiscal deficits. However, staff considers the 2026 budget unlikely to be executed as planned (18). Instead, staff assesses that the government faces the risk of reverting to a budget freeze due to a cash crunch, which would make the rebuilding of cash buffers implausible. The government should therefore closely monitor liquidity conditions and prepare contingency plans for potential cash shortfalls. Measures to help mitigate the impact of rising fuel prices should be strictly time-bound, preserve price signals, and be as targeted as possible (Box 1). Such measures should be well coordinated with donors.

13. There is scope to rationalize expenditure without compromising growth through better planning and targeting. Shifting resources away from the Constituency Development Funds (CDFs) $^{7}$ , which account for 1.6 percent of GDP in spending, would enhance public spending efficiency. Staff also notes that compensation of government employees has risen by more than 6 percent of GDP relative to pre-pandemic trends. The government should assess the appropriate size, allocation, and remuneration of its workforce in coordination with donors. Continued efforts to better integrate donor-financed off-budget programs remain important to improve the efficiency of education and health spending.

14. Domestic revenue mobilization is critical to accelerating priority investment. Further delays in introducing a VAT should be avoided, and the authorities should ensure smooth implementation by developing regulations and strengthening public outreach. Given the relatively high income tax rates, broadening the tax base through improved registration, compliance, and rationalization of exemptions is essential. $^{8}$ These efforts will require further administrative reforms by IRD, including greater digitalization and enhanced audit capacity. Special economic zones should be limited to activities with clear externalities and subject to robust cost-benefit analysis. Fiscal regimes for mining companies should be simple, transparent, and avoid excessive exemptions.

15. Significant gaps in fiscal data and reporting persist, as highlighted by the recent Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) assessment (Annex V). Monthly and quarterly budget execution reports have not yet resumed, partly due to prolonged disruptions following the rollout of the new Financial Management Information System (FMIS). The budget tables remain misaligned with international standards, limiting comparability and assessment. The compilation of the Government Finance Statistics (GFS) continues to face challenges (135). Immediate priorities include resuming regular inter-agency coordination meetings and completing the FMIS upgrade.

16. A simple, top-down fiscal guideline remains essential for formulating realistic, fully financed budgets and rebuilding cash buffers. While additional fiscal rules were introduced in the 2026 budget, many lack specificity, and consistency across the rules is unclear. $^{9}$ Staff's advice from the previous Article IV consultation—capping domestically financed deficits while ensuring a steady accumulation of cash balances—remains appropriate. Amid rising external borrowing, stronger government ownership over donor-financed infrastructure projects is needed. Staff emphasizes the importance of rigorous cost-benefit analysis for all potential projects under the Public Investment Management Framework.

17. Solomon Islands should begin considering a medium-term fiscal framework to properly manage mineral resource revenues (Box 2). Such a framework should be guided by core principles, including ensuring fiscal sustainability and intergenerational equity, managing volatility, and mitigating the risk of Dutch disease. It should also be tailored to country-specific circumstances, notably expected fiscal deficits, sizable infrastructure gaps, limited absorptive capacity, and institutional capacity constraints. Effective implementation of such a fiscal framework requires strengthening basic PFM functions, including realistic budgeting and timely, reliable fiscal reporting.

Box 2. Fiscal Framework for Managing Revenues from Natural Resources: Key Considerations

Expanded gold production could generate significant revenues for the government over the medium term. Gold Ridge Mine—a key mining company in Solomon Islands—has announced plans to triple gold manufacturing capacity in the coming years. While heavily dependent on actual production and the agreed fiscal regime, media reports indicate that revenues could exceed 10 percent of GDP once fully operational. The expansion plan is currently being assessed by the authorities, and the associated fiscal regime remains under negotiation.

Solomon Islands should consider a medium-term fiscal framework to harness natural resource revenues. Such a framework should include a strategy articulating the objectives and modalities of resource utilization, including the appropriate balance between consumption, investment, and savings, and be underpinned by fiscal rules and institutions to support implementation. Key principles should include ensuring fiscal sustainability and intergenerational equity, managing volatility associated with natural resource revenues, and mitigating the risk of Dutch disease. While the framework should be simple, operationally feasible, and sufficiently flexible to adapt as circumstances evolve, it should also take into account the country's economic structure, notably significant infrastructure gaps, limited absorptive capacity, and weak governance in extractive industries (130). It should weigh using additional revenues to close deficits and repay high-interest domestic debt.

A dedicated savings vehicle—such as a sovereign wealth fund (SWF)—could be helpful. Three elements should be kept in mind when designing such a vehicle. First, strong governance insulated from short-term political pressures is essential. Second, clear objectives should be determined. National savings vehicles in the Pacific Islands (e.g., Kiribati and Tuvalu) are often aimed at one or more of (i) short-term economic stabilization, (ii) accumulation of intergenerational wealth, and (iii) providing a source of development financing. $^{1/}$ Third, asset allocations need to be aligned with the objectives.

## Authorities' Views

19. The government remains committed to gradually reducing deficits and rebuilding cash buffers over the medium term. The 2026 budget represents a first step, with a smaller deficit and an operational surplus. Key measures include containing the public sector wage bill, introducing a VAT, and reviewing income taxes. The government stands ready to mitigate the impact of global fuel supply disruptions on households and businesses in coordination with stakeholders. Strengthening revenue administration and establishing a SWF—anchored in a robust medium-term fiscal framework—are key priorities to harness the country's natural resource endowment. The government is committed to addressing PFM gaps identified in the PEFA assessment, in coordination with development partners.

## B. Monetary and Exchange Rate Policies


21. Staff welcomes the CBSI's commitment to strengthening monetary policy transmission through the modernization of its monetary policy operations. To address weak monetary policy transmission, an IMF CD mission provided sequenced recommendations in August 2025, including absorbing excess liquidity from the banking system, introducing a policy rate and an interest rate

corridor system, and establishing a liquidity monitoring and forecasting framework (Box 3). $^{13}$ Staff advises the phased but steady implementation of these recommendations with continued IMF support. Establishing the foundations for effective monetary policy operations in line with the recommendations would help the CBSI better manage a potential surge in foreign exchange inflows arising from expansion of mining activity.

Box 3. Capacity Development Support for Enhancing Analytical and Forecasting Capacity for Monetary Policy Formulation

The IMF's Institute for Capacity Development (ICD) supported the CBSI in establishing a Forecasting and Policy Analysis System (FPAS) under a five-year CD program. $^{1/}$ The program aimed to strengthen analytical and forecasting capacity, integrate model-based analysis into monetary policy decision-making, and modernize communication.

The project's success reflected hands-on engagement, careful sequencing, continuity, and strong tailoring to local circumstances. The program initially focused on building core analytical capacity and basic forecasting tools before advancing to the development of a forecasting model and embedding it into policy processes. Over five years, ICD delivered ten missions, including virtual engagements, working closely with CBSI staff to provide hands-on training and jointly develop analytical tools and models tailored to the country's economic structure. To support this, ICD developed a projection model using EViews, adapted to the CBSI's capacity and working environment.

The project markedly enhanced the CBSI's analytical capacity and operations. CBSI staff now undertakes GDP and inflation nowcasting and produces model-based forecasts that are fully embedded in monetary policy deliberations. These advances have strengthened internal decision-making, improved external communication practices, and facilitated deeper policy discussions with the IMF country team. The CBSI and ICD plan to jointly author a working paper documenting the experience and lessons learned. Building on capacity developed under the FPAS program, the CBSI has begun a multi-year reform agenda to modernize its monetary policy operations, with further support from the IMF's Monetary and Capital Markets Department (MCM).

1/ These activities were funded by the government of Japan.

## Authorities' Views

24. The CBSI remains vigilant to developments in the Middle East and stands ready to act as needed. It emphasized that exchange rate adjustments are Board decisions for monetary policy purposes, reflecting the country's high import dependence, while recognizing the need for clearer communication. The CBSI reaffirmed its commitment to transitioning toward market-based monetary operations, including by addressing excess liquidity in the banking system.

## C. Financial Sector Policy

25. The financial sector remains broadly sound. Banks are well capitalized, liquid, and profitable. While non-performing loans are largely collateralized, they are concentrated in certain sectors (e.g., personal and construction), and slow court proceedings and an illiquid real estate market continue to impede their write-off. Rising oil prices due to the Middle East war could potentially affect banks' asset quality by weakening borrowers' loan repayment capacity. However, staff's sensitivity analysis suggests that banks remain resilient under all shock scenarios, including credit risk (Annex VI). Performance of the Solomon Islands National Provident Fund (SINPF) improved, thanks to higher dividend income and valuation gains from offshore investments, alongside ample liquidity. However, high demand for early withdrawals by members and the concentration of the investment portfolio in unlisted domestic shares continue to pose risks to financial stability, given the funds' dominant role in the financial system. $^{15}$

26. The authorities should accelerate reforms to safeguard financial stability and promote financial inclusion. The Financial Sector Stability Review (FSSR) made sequenced recommendations aimed at enhancing the resilience of the sector. These included measures to upgrade prudential standards, deepen risk-based banking supervision, and introduce a framework for crisis management. The CBSI should work on these reforms, supported by a multi-year CD program with the IMF. Completing the rollout of the outstanding National Payment System components $^{16}$ and its regulations, as well as the implementation of the credit reporting framework, will help enhance financial inclusion. Expediting legal reforms to strengthen governance and the regulatory frameworks for financial institutions, such as the Financial Institutions Act and the SINPF Act, remains crucial.

27. The authorities should continue to address key deficiencies in the AML/CFT system, including those identified by the 2019 Asia-Pacific Group Mutual Evaluation. This remains critical to safeguard correspondent banking relationships. Priority should be given to enacting the Money Laundering and Proceeds of Crime Act and enhancing risk-based AML/CFT supervision. The authorities are also reviewing the Companies Act and working to develop a targeted financial sanctions framework. Strengthening the capacity and resources of supervisory agencies is critical.

## Authorities Views

28. Enhancing financial stability, integrity, and inclusion remains a priority for the authorities. The CBSI is committed to addressing the gaps identified in the FSSR to strengthen the resilience of the financial system along with the CD roadmap. The authorities also recognize the importance of expediting legislation to strengthen the AML/CFT framework. They further highlighted ongoing efforts to promote financial inclusion, including financial literacy programs, the establishment of the credit reporting regime, and the development of an inclusive green finance roadmap.

## D. Structural Reforms

29. Limited diversification and weak private sector participation continue to hamper growth prospects. The economy continues to rely on mining and logging, while agriculture and fisheries suffer from low productivity due to predominance of semi-subsistence production with minimal mechanization, isolation from markets, and inadequate market infrastructure such as storage and cold-chain facilities. Addressing these challenges through targeted interventions would significantly contribute to job creation, higher incomes, and improved food security. $^{17}$ Moreover, promoting private sector participation, including by improving the business environment, addressing infrastructure gaps, and enhancing the quality of education and training, would alleviate the bottlenecks to enhancing growth prospects.

30. Progress in tackling governance weaknesses and combating corruption has been slow, constraining growth. Strengthening governance and regulation in the logging and mining sectors through legislative reforms remains an urgent priority for the authorities. The ministries continue to face resource constraints that limit effective monitoring, compliance, and enforcement in these sectors. While the Solomon Islands Independent Commission Against Corruption (SIICAC) was granted dedicated funding in the 2026 budget, the Commission continues to face resource shortages and delays in recruiting a new Director General. These constraints have stalled the

implementation of the National Anti-Corruption Strategy, including amendments to the Anti-Corruption Act 2018. The framework for implementing the strategy should be revamped upon the arrival of a new Director General, with adequate support from the government and donors.

## Authorities Views

31. The authorities remain committed to advancing reforms to unlock the Solomon Islands' growth potential, including by addressing governance weaknesses. The government is strengthening support for the agriculture sector to enhance productivity, market access, and food security, in partnership with development partners. The Public Investment Management Framework and the National Infrastructure Investment Plan provide a platform to appraise and prioritize infrastructure projects, including to improve transport and digital connectivity. The authorities acknowledged delays in enacting the Mineral Resources Bill and implementing the National Anti-Corruption Strategy, while noting that public consultations on the bill and recruitment of a Director General for SIICAC are ongoing.

## E. Macroeconomic Impact of Climate Change

32. Slow-moving climate change is expected to have incremental but meaningful cumulative effects on Solomon Islands' economy over the long term. $^{18}$ Climate models indicate that annual temperatures could rise by 0.9–1.2°C by 2050. Staff analysis suggests that it could reduce the level of GDP by 1–4 percent by end-century, relative to a continuation of historical trends, depending on the global warming scenario. The impact could be twice as large in the absence of adaptation. Fish stocks in the country's exclusive economic zone are projected to decline by about 11 percent by 2045. On the other hand, commercial timber production could increase by about 13 percent by mid-century, if sustainable forestry practices are widely adopted. In the absence of adaptation, sea-level rise could cause welfare costs estimated at about 1.5 percent of GDP per year over the long term. Scenario analysis in the DSA shows that these forces could have a significant cumulative impact on the public debt trajectory over the long run.

33. Strengthening core PFM functions is an urgent priority for effective climate adaptation. Staff assessment clearly indicates the need for adaptation policies, particularly to mitigate impacts on populations living in areas vulnerable to sea-level rise. These policies would entail significant fiscal and social costs, although a comprehensive set of policies and reliable cost estimates are not yet available. The government will therefore need to allocate scarce resources strategically, by first mapping climate-exposed regions and vulnerable groups, and then by establishing criteria for public intervention. A critical first step is to build basic PFM capacity to prioritize, execute, and monitor climate-relevant projects in line with budget plans and donor support. A robust PFM foundation is also a prerequisite for accessing multilateral climate funds.

## Authorities Views

34. The authorities agreed on the risks posed by climate change and the importance of adaptation policies. They noted that climate change is already having material economic and social impacts, citing extreme weather events such as cyclones. While efforts are underway to strengthen climate resilience, including under the National Infrastructure Investment Plan, the authorities acknowledged resource constraints. They are working to integrate climate considerations into budget processes, including through climate budget tagging, and to gain access to international climate finance.

## Annex I. Country Engagement Strategy: Update

The Country Engagement Strategy for Solomon Islands, formulated in 2023, identified key drivers of fragility, including limited connectivity, high exposure to natural disasters and climate change, and weak state capacity. It also set out policy priorities to address these vulnerabilities, notably maintaining macroeconomic stability and strengthening fiscal governance and transparency. These findings remain relevant and continue to inform IMF engagement. Near-term priorities therefore continue to focus on strengthening the authorities' basic capacity to implement sound macroeconomic policies. In doing so, the Fund should place greater emphasis on ownership by the authorities, and on the sequencing and tailoring of support. Staff will continue to maintain close communication with the authorities to provide support as needed.

## A. Fragility Drivers

1. The 2023 Staff Report for the Article IV Consultation with Solomon Islands included the Country Engagement Strategy (CES). $^{1}$ This strategy outlined the key drivers of fragility in Solomon Islands, identified policy priorities and constraints to address them, and provided guidance for Fund engagement with the country.

2. The CES identified drivers of fragility stemming from the country's geographic characteristics and historical context. These drivers included extremely limited connectivity across the country; high exposure to natural disasters and climate change; tenuous national unity; weak state capacity; and predominance of customary rules rather than explicit legal frameworks. The strategy further argued that these fragility drivers undermined economic outcomes, contributing to an underdeveloped private sector; insufficient and unequal allocation of physical and human capital; high aid dependence; and governance and corruption vulnerabilities.

3. These fragility drivers remain in place. They are largely structural and are deeply rooted in the Solomon Islands. Hence they require sustained efforts to address and long time to resolve. For example, while the successful hosting of the Pacific Games in 2023 seems to have contributed to enhancing social cohesion and confidence, political instability remains a material risk, as evidenced by a recent change in government following the third motion of no confidence in the past two years.

## B. Policy Priorities and Constraints

4. The CES prioritized policies to mitigate these fragility drivers. They included policies aimed at maintaining macroeconomic stability; strengthening fiscal governance and transparency, notably through improvements in public financial management (PFM); expanding access to public services and infrastructure, especially in underserved rural areas; and developing new growth drivers by addressing structural bottlenecks to private sector development. The strategy recognized weak state capacity and governance vulnerabilities as major constraints in advancing these policy priorities.

5. These policy priorities have yet to be fully implemented. The authorities' policy directions are broadly aligned with the priorities, and they have managed the economy without major disruptions amid two landmark events: the Pacific Games in 2023 and the general elections in 2024. Nonetheless, progress on reforms has been slow, reflecting capacity constraints and governance weaknesses. The economy remains susceptible to exogenous shocks, as cash buffers have been depleted and public debt has accumulated. Weaknesses in core PFM functions persist, which is illustrated by repeated cash shortages and delayed fiscal reporting and audits. The government has prioritized investment in rural areas; however, long-standing structural challenges—such as land issues and weak governance in natural resource sectors—remain unresolved and continue to constrain private sector development and economic diversification.

## C. IMF Engagement and Capacity Development Priorities

6. Consistent with the CES, IMF engagement with Solomon Islands has placed particular emphasis on strengthening the authorities' basic capacity to implement sound macroeconomic policies. The IMF has provided advice on overhauling the fiscal framework and tax administration, improving exchange rate and monetary policy operations, and enhancing the quality of statistics. Capacity development (CD) support has followed and is helping with the implementation of these recommendations.

## 7. Some engagements have made remarkable progress, while others have faced

challenges. The Inland Revenue Division has reformed its organizational structure and functions, with support from the Pacific Financial Technical Assistance Centre (PFTAC). The IMF's multi-year program—which comprised total 10 missions over nearly five years—has helped the Central Bank of Solomon Islands (CBSI) strengthen its analytical capacity and decision-making processes. These have brought about visible improvements to their operations. On the other hand, many of the IMF CD mission recommendations aimed at consolidating core PFM functions have been left unattended for years, partly due to political constraints and issues with the financial management information system. CD missions to support compilation of the national accounts and government finance statistics (GFS) have faced challenges due to the shortage and high turnover of staff, as well as weak coordination between agencies.

8. These experiences underscore several important lessons. Firstly, strong ownership by recipients of CD is essential for success. Secondly, proposed reforms must be actionable and carefully sequenced over a sufficient period of time, taking into account recipients' absorptive capacity and potential staff turnover. Lastly, regular and frequent engagement with recipients, including hands-on training, is important in order to maintain momentum.

9. Near-term CD priorities continue to focus on strengthening basic capacity for sound macroeconomic management. The authorities recently underwent two diagnostic CD programs by the IMF: the Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) assessment and the Financial Sector Stability Review (FSSR). The former investigated the strengths and weaknesses in PFM, while the latter assessed capacity to identify, monitor, and manage financial stability risks. These diagnostics will lay the groundwork for subsequent CD programs, which will be designed to address the identified weaknesses and vulnerabilities. In addition, the CBSI is committed to implementing reforms to modernize its monetary policy operations, following steps presented by an IMF CD mission in 2025. Assisting the implementation of these multi-year programs, alongside the continuation of existing support—particularly with regard to statistics—will be the priority for the IMF's CD support in the coming years.

10. The lessons learned should be embraced in working on the priorities. When formulating reform plans, CD providers should hold intensive discussions with recipients to fully convey the necessity and details of the proposed reforms. The reforms should be focused and actionable, with a realistic timeframe for implementation that reflects the views of the recipients. In addition to regular visits to the country, CD providers should maintain frequent contact with recipients to encourage the implementation of reforms and provide assistance as needed.

## D. Coordination with Stakeholders and Risks to Fund Engagement

11. Coordination with development partners remains essential. In the Solomon Islands, donors such as Australia, the World Bank, and the Asian Development Bank, are actively providing support on a wide range of issues, some of which occasionally overlap with support from the Fund. It is therefore important to ensure an appropriate division of labor among donors, including through the Joint Policy Reform Group.

12. Staff should be aware of the risks involved in the Fund's engagement with Solomon Islands. As previously discussed, political instability and staff turnover could hinder the pace of economic reforms. These risks should be factored in when formulating engagement plans.

# Annex III. Assessing Solomon Islands' Growth Drivers and Prospects $^{1}$

This annex examines the factors driving or hindering economic growth in Solomon Islands and forecasts medium- to long-term growth prospects using growth accounting and dynamic time warping methodologies. The analysis shows that rising contributions from physical capital have been more than offset by negative productivity growth over the past decade, underscoring the need to improve the economy's absorptive capacity. Both methodologies suggest growth prospects of around 3 percent for Solomon Islands, consistent with staff baseline projections.

## A. Introduction

1. Understanding growth dynamics and prospects is crucial for the Solomon Islands. Real per capita income growth has stagnated for a quarter century, lagging behind population growth (Figure 1). Investigating the factors behind this weak economic performance is essential for policy makers to design effective measures to boost the country's growth potential. Developing realistic and reliable growth forecasts is particularly challenging for countries prone to exogenous shocks and structural changes, like the Solomon Islands. Nevertheless, such forecasts are a fundamental basis not only for policy makers, but also for investment planning.


[[KC_IMAGE_025]]


2. This annex assesses Solomon Islands' growth drivers and medium-to-long-term growth prospects using growth accounting and dynamic time warping approaches. The growth accounting approach allows one to decompose historical growth into contributions from production factors (i.e., capital, labor, and productivity). It can be also utilized to predict future growth, based on the expected trends of these factors. However, growth accounting requires assumptions about the shape of the production function and its parameters, which may not accurately reflect the country's economic structures. The dynamic time warping approach could be a useful complement in this regard, as it predicts a target country's future growth by identifying similar growth episodes of other countries by pattern recognition, without relying on production functions.

## B. Growth Accounting

3. Several assumptions underpin our growth accounting analysis. Following Che and others (2025), we assume that real GDP $Y = K^{\alpha}(AhL)^{(1-\alpha)}$ , where K, A, h, L, and $\alpha$ represent the capital stock, total factor productivity (TFP), human capital, employment, and the capital share, respectively. This equation can be rewritten in per capita income terms as follows:

$$
{\frac {Y}{P}} = \left({\frac {K}{Y}}\right) ^ {(\alpha / (1 - \alpha))} A h {\frac {L}{P}}\tag{1}
$$

where P stands for population. The capital stock (K) is set at 0.7 times real GDP in the initial year, following a conventional rule of thumb, and is assumed to follow the perpetual inventory equation in subsequent years:

$$
K _ {t} = K _ {t - 1} (1 - \delta) + I _ {t}\tag{2}
$$

where $I_{t}$ and $\delta$ represent gross fixed capital formation at time t and the depreciation rate, respectively. We assume a depreciation rate ( $\delta$ ) of 5 percent, following the cases of other low-income countries (IMF, 2024). $^{2}$

4. We rely on various data sources, including modelled estimates on the labor market. Data on real GDP (Y), gross fixed capital formation (I), and population (P) are sourced from the national accounts database compiled by the United Nations Statistics Division. $^{3}$ This database features wide country coverage, including all the Pacific Island countries (PICs), dating back to 1970. Employment data (L) and the labor income share (1- $\alpha$ ) are taken from the International Labor Organization’s modelled estimates database. $^{4}$ Human capital (h) is measured in the same way as in the Penn World Table, using the mean years of schooling data from the Human Development Index dataset. $^{5}$ For forecasts, we use the medium scenario population projections from the World Population Prospects. All of these data are available for six PICs, including Solomon Islands (i.e., Fiji, Papua New Guinea, Samoa, Solomon Islands, Tonga, and Vanuatu).

5. Growth accounting reveals that increasing capital investment has not translated into higher income of Solomon Islanders. The left-hand panel of Figure 2 shows GDP per capita growth of Solomon Islands and its decomposition, calculated according to equation (1). The chart shows that increasing contributions from physical capital since 2015 have been more than offset by negative productivity growth. The period between 2020 and 2023 was exceptional, characterized by the pandemic and the riot, in addition to infrastructure building in preparation for the Pacific Games. However, it is striking that TFP declined by 4 percent per annum between 2015 and 2019—a sharper decrease than during the early 2000s, when the country experienced civil conflict. This trend of capital investments crowding out other economic activity is also seen in other PICs, as shown in the right-hand panel of Figure 2.

## C. Dynamic Time Warping Forecasts

9. The Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) algorithm employs machine-learning techniques to predict countries' growth prospects in a data-driven, non-parametric manner. It computes distances between the target country's variables in a target period and those of other countries in all possible historical periods. It then identifies the pairs of a country and a period with the shortest distances, which closely mirror the target country's economic conditions during the target period. These identified episodes are used to predict the target country's future growth, by computing the median, simple and weighted averages, and the $25^{\text{th}}$ and $75^{\text{th}}$ percentile values of the subsequent growth rates of the selected growth episodes. $^{6}$ According to Che and others (2025), this approach yields relatively stable medium-term forecasts.

10. We use historical data of more than 200 countries ranging from 1970 to 2023 to forecast Solomon Islands' growth rates from 2024 to 2044. The data are sourced from the national accounts database compiled by the United Nations Statistics Division, the same database as used for the growth accounting exercise. The target period is set at 2017–2023, in order to cover both the pre- and post-pandemic periods. We select the top one percent of historical episodes with the shortest distances from Solomon Islands in the target period, focusing on two variables: real GDP per capita and population. This ensures that the country's small population size and income level are appropriately taken into account and yields relatively good forecast performance.

## D. Conclusion

13. Both the growth accounting and DTW approaches envisage around 3 percent growth for Solomon Islands over the medium to long term, based on its historical performance. Growth accounting shows that increasing contributions from physical capital to GDP per capita growth since 2015 have been more than offset by negative TFP growth in Solomon Islands. The growth accounting exercise suggests that Solomon Islands economy will grow by 2.1–3.5 percent until 2044, which is consistent with forecasts from the DTW approach.

## Annex V. Improving Fiscal Reporting in Solomon Islands $^{1}$

Solomon Islands has taken important steps toward strengthening fiscal data governance since the last Article IV consultation. Significant gaps remain, however, in fiscal reporting, the financial management information system (FMIS), and coordination among stakeholders. The immediate priority is to resume regular inter-agency coordination meetings and finalize the memorandum of understanding to formalize this coordination. The government should coordinate with development partners to complete the upgrade of the FMIS, which is necessary for the government to resume periodic reporting on budget execution. Continued capacity development of staff and institutions, supported by partners, is central to achieving these goals and fostering confidence, accountability, and sustainable fiscal policy in the Solomon Islands.

## A. Background

1. The Solomon Islands' public financial management (PFM) framework, anchored by the Public Financial Management Act (PFMA) 2013, has suffered from weak and inconsistent implementation, resulting in poor fiscal reporting, limited transparency, and diminished accountability. Persistent capacity constraints, reliance on external support, and system challenges including prolonged disruptions following the rollout of the new FMIS have undermined timely, reliable budget execution and financial reporting. Audited financial statements are outdated. The government publishes budget outturns and debt data with long delays, and the reporting formats do not align with international standards such as the Government Finance Statistics Manual 2014 (GFSM 2014) and the International Public Sector Accounting Standards (IPSAS). This is creating inconsistencies across fiscal, debt, and macroeconomic statistics. The authorities, in particular the Central Bank of Solomon Islands (CBSI), have been making efforts to improve data quality. Nevertheless, fragmentation between institutions and nonstandard budget presentations continues to hinder policy analysis, donor confidence, and public oversight.

2. The IMF set out a series of recommendations to address these challenges in the previous Article IV consultation. The recommendations covered a wide range of areas including the legal framework, the FMIS, staff capacity, and coordination among stakeholders, as shown in Table 1 (IMF 2025). The following sections provide updates on the progress in implementing these recommendations and discuss the remaining challenges, focusing on the recommendations relating to fiscal transparency and reporting, the FMIS, and the capacity and coordination of the authorities.

## B. Improving Fiscal Transparency

3. The PFMA 2013 requires regular compilation and dissemination of public accounts, but it is not being fully implemented. The Act requires the submission of monthly financial reports and annual financial statements by the government within certain timeframes. However, the government has long suspended the release of monthly reports on budget execution, while budget outturn reports are published with significant delays. Therefore, the first step towards ensuring fiscal transparency is to fully comply with the Act. In addition, the format of these reports does not align with international standards such as GFSM 2014 and IPSAS, impairing consistency, comparability, and quality of fiscal reporting. Over the medium term, fiscal transparency could be further improved by adopting international best practices, such as the IMF's Fiscal Transparency Framework (FTF), in the PFMA. Given this, IMF (2025) specifically recommended the following:

\- Resume the release of quarterly and monthly budget outturns. [June 2025]

• Comply with PFMA2013: Regular dissemination. [December 2025]

\- Align fiscal reporting templates with IPSAS and/or GFS standards. [January 2026]

\- Amend the PFMA to adopt the Fiscal Transparency Framework. [December 2027]

4. Although the authorities are committed to improving fiscal reporting, delays to the FMIS upgrade are hindering the effort. The Ministry of Finance and Treasury (MoFT) has not resumed dissemination of monthly reports on budget execution. The budget outturn report has not been published on the MoFT's website since 2021, and the most recent audit report on the government's annual financial statements that is available on the Auditor General (AG)'s website is for 2017. Budget tables in the 2026 budget documents are not aligned with the international standards such as IPSAS and GFSM 2014. No discussion has taken place regarding the adoption of the fiscal transparency framework. The government is committed to resuming publication of the periodic fiscal reports once the FMIS upgrade is complete.

5. In addition to implementing these recommendations, developing a formal data revision policy will enhance transparency and improve confidence in fiscal statistics. Solomon Islands' fiscal data is often revised retroactively without explanation. Having a data revision policy—that stipulates procedures for updating previously released fiscal statistics when new information becomes available—enhances users' understanding of how fiscal data evolves and guards against misinterpretation of past reports.

## C. Ensuring Functioning Financial Management Information System

6. The introduction of Microsoft Dynamics 365 (D365) for the FMIS has exacerbated long-standing weaknesses in fiscal reporting and data availability. The system initially failed to generate basic operational and management reports, partly because in-person installation assistance was not possible due to the pandemic. The MoFT was unable to provide the CBSI with

granular data on budget execution, which prevented the CBSI from compiling the Government Finance Statistics (GFS) in a reliable and timely manner. Given this, IMF (2025) specifically recommended the following:

\- Collaborate with PFM experts to address challenges utilizing Microsoft D365 for public finance management. [March 2025]

\- Ensure access for all government agencies. [December 2025]

## 7. The provision of granular data has resumed, although further improvements are

needed. The MoFT has resumed extracting granular data from D365, but the process of sharing the data is still subject to significant delays. This has enabled the CBSI to publish the GFS up to 2024 on a provisional basis. Once Phase 2 of the FMIS upgrade is complete, the government expects financial management reports to be generated automatically. Development partners, including the PFTAC, have supported the upgrade. Work remains in refining the economic classification of stocks and flows and the classification of the functions of government (COFOG) to better align to the GFSM 2014 classifications. Further efforts are needed to reduce the significant statistical discrepancies between above and below the line, as well as to include the government's financial assets associated with on-lending and equity investments in state-owned enterprises. The development of fiscal reports that map FMIS outputs to the government's chart of accounts (COA) has yet to start.

## 8. Officials across all ministries and departments that operate in the financial

management environment have now been granted access to D365. However, further investment in training and upskilling officials on the technicalities of the FMIS is required to ensure that the system is functional throughout the government.

## D. Strengthening Capacity and Collaboration of Stakeholders

9. Weak institutional capacity and fragmented coordination continue to undermine the quality, consistency, and credibility of fiscal data and reporting in the Solomon Islands. Staff responsible for budgeting, accounting, fiscal analysis, and statistics face skills gaps in FMIS use, PFM, and the GFS. The authorities continue to face difficulties in recruiting staff with the appropriate skills and background. In addition, insufficient coordination among the MoFT, the CBSI, line ministries, and development partners hampers data sharing, task alignment, and timely statistical compilation. Given this, IMF (2025) specifically recommended the following:

\- Strengthen the capacity of staff in relevant agencies through targeted training and development programs focused on fiscal data management and analysis. [April 2025]

\- Resume monthly policy implementation meetings between MOFT agencies and the CBSI. [January 2025]

\- Establish a memorandum of understanding to facilitate data sharing and timely submission. [January 2025]

10. While the government is planning to increase training in accounting and auditing, government officials would also benefit from training in fiscal data management and analysis. The government is working to set up a training center for accounting and auditing officers. MoFT officials, particularly those in charge of economic analysis, budget formulation and execution, and treasury, would benefit from training on PFM and the GFS, possibly funded by development partners including the IMF.

11. The monthly policy implementation meeting has not been held since May 2025, while the finalization of the memorandum of understanding (MoU) is pending. Regular monthly meetings support timely discussion of fiscal trends, resolution of data discrepancies, and alignment of forecasts. International experience shows that frequent inter-agency coordination improves the timeliness and accuracy of fiscal indicators and strengthens budget execution. A draft MOU to set up a committee to facilitate data sharing, timely submission, and division of labor is waiting for finalization.

12. Donor grant data provided by donors is being used to improve the coverage and consistency of fiscal data. The CBSI collects data on donor grants through a survey of multilateral and bilateral donors for the compilation of the balance-of-payments (BOP) statistics. The central bank has decided to use the data for the GFS as well, to cover off-budget grants and improve consistency with the BOP statistics. Additional efforts are required to validate and reconcile external borrowing in the BOP statistics with government debt data in the fiscal accounts and public sector debt statistics.

## E. Conclusion

13. Solomon Islands has taken important steps toward strengthening fiscal data governance, but significant gaps remain in fiscal reporting, the FMIS, and coordination among stakeholders. The MoFT has resumed providing granular data extracted from the FMIS, and the government is working to establish a training center for auditing and accounting. The immediate priority is to resume regular coordination meetings across agencies and finalize the MoU to formalize this coordination. The government should coordinate with development partners to complete the FMIS upgrade, which is necessary for the government to resume periodic reporting on budget execution. Continued capacity development, supported by partners such as the IMF, the World Bank, and regional partners, is central to achieving these goals and fostering confidence, accountability, and sustainable fiscal policy in the Solomon Islands.

## Annex VI. Sensitivity Analysis of Banking System in Solomon Islands $^{1}$

Solomon Island's banking system is small and concentrated, dominated by foreign ownership. The sector is generally well capitalized, liquid and profitable. However, areas of vulnerabilities including elevated non-performing loans, and the concentration of large exposures require careful monitoring. The sensitivity analysis indicates that banks remain resilient to different shocks under both mild and severe downside scenarios. Although credit risk shocks have the largest adverse impact on banks' capital positions relative to foreign exchange (FX) and interest rate shocks, all banks' capital ratios remain above the regulatory minimum of 15 percent under all shock scenarios. $^{2}$

## A. Banking System Characteristics

1. Solomon Islands' banking system is small and concentrated, dominated by foreign-owned banks. In 2024, banks held SI\$7.2 billion in assets, equivalent to 61 percent of the total financial system and 0.7 times the size of the economy. $^{3}$ The system comprises four banks, three of which operate as branches of foreign-owned banks, and a domestically incorporated bank. The foreign-owned banks dominate the system, holding 90 percent of total assets, with the largest accounting for 47 percent.

2. The banking sector is centered on traditional lending and exposed to the personal, distribution, and construction sectors (Figure 1). However, in 2024, loans made up just 35 percent of total assets, equivalent to 24 percent of GDP, while cash comprised half of banks' assets


(Figure 2)The modest lending activity reflects structural challenges, including high informality, limited collateral due to communal land ownership, and high costs of providing services in rural and outer islands. On the liability side, banks are primarily funded through deposits, which account for 85 percent of total liabilities.

3. Banks are profitable due to robust interest and non-interest incomes, and are adequately capitalized and liquid. Interest earnings remain the main source of revenue, accounting for over half of banks' gross income. Despite the high non-interest income from fees and FX transactions, elevated operating costs, particularly outsourcing service fees and salaries and wages caused some banks to report negative net non-interest income in 2024. Average return on assets stood at 3.0 percent and return on equity at 22.0 percent in 2024 (Figure 3). The capital adequacy ratio (CAR) was 39.6 percent in 2024, with wide variation (23-65 percent) among banks (Figure 4). Tier 1 capital makes up 84 percent of total capital, underscoring the quality of capital.


4. Non-performing loans (NPLs) remain elevated, and among the highest in the Pacific (Figure 5). In 2024, the NPL ratio stood at 11.6 percent, and concentrated in the personal, distribution, transportation, and construction sectors (Figure 6). These sectors also represent substantial portions of gross loans, and are vulnerable to shocks, including adverse climate events and economic slowdown. Banks' provisioning $^{4}$ for NPLs appear adequate and collateralized; however, challenges related to slow court processes and an illiquid property market hinder loan recovery. Additionally, the concentration of large exposure warrants closer monitoring.

## Exchange Rate Arrangements:

The de jure exchange rate arrangement is a conventional peg arrangement. The Solomon Islands dollar (SBD) is pegged to a basket of currencies consisting of the US dollar, the Australian dollar, and the New Zealand dollar. The basket was revised on August 21, 2024. The Central Bank of Solomon Islands (CBSI) sets the exchange rate vis-à-vis the US dollar as to maintain the value of the basket constant in SBDs given the movements of currencies in the basket relative to each other. The exchange rate is allowed to move along the exchange rate basket and market fundamentals with no fixed band imposed on the basket movement. The de facto exchange rate arrangement is classified as craw-like. Solomon Islands has accepted the obligations of Article VIII, Sections 2, 3, and 4 of the IMF's Articles of Agreement and maintains an exchange system that is free of restrictions on making of payments and transfers for current international transactions and multiple currency practices.

## Last Article IV Consultation:

## Safeguards Assessment:

An update safeguards assessment of the CBSI was completed in July 2022. The assessment found that the external audits and financial reporting framework continue to be broadly aligned with international standards, and the CBSI's organic law provides an adequate legal underpinning for its autonomy. However, substantial effort is required to modernize the internal audit function and to enhance the audit committee's oversight. Since the assessment, the CBSI has advanced the implementation of some recommendations with IMF technical assistance, such as on internal audit and risk management.

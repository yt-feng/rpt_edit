# ABSTRACT/RÉSUMÉ

## Non-compete clauses and productivity: New cross-country firm-level evidence

Restraint clauses that prevent workers from joining or starting a competing firm – commonly known as non-compete clauses – are traditionally justified to protect legitimate business interests. Yet, such clauses may suppress job mobility, business dynamism and competition, with adverse consequences for productivity growth. This paper presents the first harmonised cross-country evidence on the link between non-compete clauses and productivity performance. Merging novel data on industry-country-level prevalence of non-compete clauses with firm-level data from Orbis, we find that a higher non-compete prevalence is associated with weaker productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and slower knowledge diffusion. The estimates translate into meaningful aggregate productivity losses (1.9% for a 10-percentage-point increase in non-compete incidence). While the economic “bite” of non-competes on productivity seems larger in countries where firms have greater freedom to deploy them, it remains negative in countries where non-competes are more tightly regulated. This underscores the idea that non-compete clauses can exert “chilling effects” even when they are unlikely to be upheld by courts, raising questions about whether current regulatory frameworks are fit for purpose.

# Non-compete clauses and productivity: New cross-country firm-level evidence

Dan Andrews, Andrea Garnero, Sara Holttinen $^{1}$

## 1. Introduction

Over the past 20 years, the productivity slowdown has been underpinned at the microeconomic level by lower rates of job reallocation, firm entry and knowledge diffusion (OECD, 2025[1]; Shambaugh, Nunn and Liu, 2018[2]; OECD, 2025[3]). One factor that could partly explain these headwinds is the spread of post-employment restraint clauses, which prevent workers from joining (or starting) a competing firm (non-compete clauses) and disclosing confidential information; or firm-to-firm no-poaching agreements (Andrews and Garnero, 2025[4]). While a growing body of evidence highlights the negative effects that non-compete clauses can have on wages and job mobility, it is unclear whether these effects simply reflect a redistribution of value from workers to firms, or whether non-compete clauses also depress productivity and thus shrink the size of the overall economic pie.

To fill this gap, this paper presents new harmonised cross-country evidence on the link between non-compete and related clauses and productivity performance. To this purpose, novel data on the share of workers bound by non-compete and related clauses at the industry level – sourced from a recent OECD-Bocconi survey of firms in 15 countries – is merged with cross-country firm-level balance sheet data from Orbis. Consistent with existing evidence showing that non-compete clauses can stifle economic dynamism, we first present descriptive evidence from new OECD surveys which shows that: i) non-compete clause prevalence is surprisingly high (OECD, 2026[5]); and ii) a meaningful share of all employees have been stopped from moving jobs or starting a new business due to having a non-compete clause. In turn, we explore the potential for non-compete and related clauses to distort two key structural features of well-functioning economies: productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and knowledge diffusion. While causality is difficult to pin down, we find clear evidence of a robust negative relationship between the prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level and the aforementioned structural drivers of aggregate productivity growth.

We augment the state-of-the-art dynamic reallocation methodology (Foster, Grim and Haltiwanger, 2016[6]; Decker et al., 2020[7]) and find that a higher prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level is associated with a lower propensity for labour to flow to more productive firms, at the expense of less productive firms. The baseline results are robust to a battery of robustness tests, including controlling for labour market tightness, workforce composition, product and labour market regulations and the use of other restraint agreements (see below). On average across countries, a 10-percentage-point increase in the share of the workforce bound by non-compete clauses is associated with an estimated $17\%$ reduction in the strength of productivity-enhancing labour reallocation. While the economic "bite" of non-competes on labour reallocation is somewhat larger in countries where firms have greater freedom to deploy such clauses $(-20\%)$ , it remains negative in countries where regulatory frameworks place more restrictions on firms' use of non-competes $(-14\%)$ .

Drawing on neo-Schumpeterian growth theory – whereby aggregate productivity growth is influenced by knowledge transfer from frontier to laggard firms (Aghion and Howitt, 1998[8]) – we then show that a higher prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level is associated with weaker technological convergence of laggard firms to the global productivity frontier. A 10-percentage-point increase in non-compete prevalence is linked to about a 5% reduction in catch-up growth, implying a 1.8-percentage-point decline in annual firm-level labour productivity growth for the median non-frontier firm. While the estimated economic “bite” of non-competes on knowledge diffusion is also larger in countries where firms have greater freedom to deploy such clauses, these results are less robust to the inclusion of additional institutional controls than the reallocation results.

Taken together, these results suggest that non-compete clauses – by directly impeding the ability of workers to join (or start) a competing firm – stifle the reallocation of talent to productive firms and the spread of leading ideas. To understand what they imply for aggregate productivity performance, we conduct a simulation exercise utilising a well-established aggregate productivity decomposition that distinguishes between within-firm productivity growth and the productivity gains arising from labour reallocation across continuing firms (Foster, Haltiwanger and Krizan, 2001[9]). The results suggest that a 10-percentage-point increase in the prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level is associated with a decline in the level of aggregate (business sector) labour productivity of 1.9%, with smaller impacts (1.5%) in countries where such clauses are more difficult to deploy. While this figure should not be taken too literally given that we have not identified a purely causal relationship, it is nonetheless illustrative of the potential for non-compete clauses to have material consequences for aggregate productivity. Moreover, these figures do not account for other mechanisms through which non-compete clauses may depress aggregate productivity, such as reduced firm entry and competition.

These findings have important policy implications for at least three key reasons.

1. The spread of non-compete clauses could have played a role in the productivity slowdown and the accompanying decline in economic dynamism by amplifying frictions that restrict the mobility of productive resources as well as competition in factor and product markets. The productivity effects associated with a relatively modest change in non-compete prevalence are – as expected – smaller than the productivity impacts of larger-scale reforms, but still material. This suggests that recent regulatory interventions to restrict the use of non-compete clauses in selected OECD countries are a potential productivity-enhancing structural reform. The evidence that non-compete clauses may reduce productivity even in countries where they are more tightly regulated supports previous findings in the literature that such clauses can have a “chilling effect” on job mobility, as workers often perceive non-compete clauses as valid and act accordingly, irrespective of the likelihood that courts would uphold them. Policy approaches that focus only on the formal regulatory framework of non-compete clauses are therefore unlikely to fully address their economic effects.

2. The link between non-disclosure agreements and productivity is much weaker than for non-competes, suggesting that there may be less distortive alternatives to protect firm's business interests than mobility-sapping non-compete clauses.

3. There is some evidence to suggest that in industries where the share of firms aware of no-poaching agreements being deployed is higher, the extent of productivity-enhancing reallocation tends to be weaker, consistent with the adverse effects of no-poaching agreements on job mobility. This underscores the importance and continued relevance of recent enforcement actions by competition authorities with respect to (firm-to-firm) no-poaching agreements, which are illegal under anti-trust laws in most jurisdictions.

## 2. Non-compete clauses and productivity

Previous literature on the economic effects of non-compete clauses has mainly documented their impact on job mobility and wages. Studies exploiting field experiments or natural experiments such as policy reforms or differences across neighbouring jurisdictions consistently find that non-compete clauses are associated with lower job mobility and lower wages (Garmaise, 2009[10]; Marx, Strumsky and Fleming, 2009[11]; US Treasury, 2016[12]; US Treasury, 2022[13]). $^{2}$ Recent evidence (Engbom, Baksy and Caratelli, 2026[14]) shows that the long-run decline in job-to-job mobility – linked to reduced outside offers and weaker employer competition – has materially slowed wage growth in the United States (around 0.7 percentage points per year). A back-of-the-envelope calculation suggests that rising employer concentration and the use of non-compete clauses may account for roughly 60% of the decline in on-the-job search efficiency between the 1980s and 2010s. Importantly, the effects on job mobility and wages are observed not only where non-compete clauses are lightly regulated, but also in jurisdictions where they are partially or fully banned, as workers often forgo job offers because of non-compete clauses which would not be upheld in court. This “chilling effect” implies that analyses based solely on the legal framework may understate the economic impact of non-compete clauses.

At the same time, changes in regulation have been shown to affect outcomes, even with imperfect compliance. Various studies exploiting policy variation across and within US states find that restricting or banning non-compete clauses increases job-to-job mobility and raises wages. For example, reforms targeting low-wage employees in Oregon (Lipsitz and Starr, 2022[15]), high-tech employees in Hawaii (Balasubramanian et al., 2022[16]), and middle-income employees in Washington State (Hiraiwa, Lipsitz and Starr, 2024[17]) all led to higher mobility and wage growth. Studies exploiting the large differences in regulation across US states find that limiting non-compete use increases wages by around $3 - 4\%$ on average and substantially raises employee mobility (Greenwood, Kobayashi and Starr, forthcoming[18]; Johnson, Lavetti and Lipsitz, 2025[19]). While evidence outside the United States is more limited, it generally points in the same direction. Young (2021[20]) finds that Austria's 2006 reform banning non-compete clauses for low-wage employees increased transitions to better-paying jobs. More recently, Bartelsman, Dobbelaere and Zona Mattioli (2024[21]) show that reforms introduced in the Netherlands in 2015 were associated with higher wages and increased mobility, particularly in intangible asset-intensive firms.

An important policy question raised by the evidence on wages and mobility is whether non-compete clauses merely redistribute surplus between firms and workers, or whether they also generate efficiency losses by constraining productivity-enhancing labour reallocation. From a conceptual perspective, non-compete clauses may affect productivity through several, potentially offsetting, channels. By limiting worker mobility, non-compete clauses can reduce productivity growth by impeding the reallocation of labour from less productive to more productive firms, thereby weakening allocative efficiency. This mechanism has been highlighted as a key driver of aggregate productivity differences across countries and over time (Bartelsman, Haltiwanger and Scarpetta, 2013[22]; Andrews and Cingano, 2014[23]). Reduced worker mobility may also slow knowledge diffusion across firms, limiting within-firm productivity growth, particularly for laggard firms for which catch-up to the technological frontier is a key source of aggregate productivity growth. Mobility restrictions may also worsen the quality of job matching if workers are prevented from moving to jobs that better fit their skills and experience. Against this, stronger clauses limiting what employees can do after they leave their job may encourage firms to invest more in training, research and development, or other intangible assets, and facilitate the sharing of proprietary information within firms.

While a growing literature documents the negative effects of non-compete clauses on job mobility and earnings, their implications for productivity remain largely unexplored. To date, there are only two exceptions: Anand et al. (2017[24]) who show that where non-compete clauses are more likely to be upheld in courts, they stifle worker mobility and innovation, ultimately reducing productivity. The second is a recent paper by Chang et al. (2026[25]), which uses newly assembled manufacturing data for the United States and finds that increases in the likelihood of non-compete clauses being upheld in court – creating a legal environment more favourable to firms – are accompanied by reductions in output and productivity. $^{3}$

This paper provides a contribution to fill the gap by presenting new harmonised cross-country evidence on the link between non-compete prevalence, regulation and productivity outcomes. Given existing evidence that non-compete clauses stifle job mobility, we explore the potential for non-compete and related clauses to distort two key structural features of well-functioning economies: productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and knowledge diffusion. While the results are robust to a wide range of controls and specifications, unobserved factors correlated with non-compete prevalence may still play a role. Therefore, the findings should be seen as associations and not causal effects. However, the consistency of the results with established mechanisms in the productivity literature lends support to the findings.

## 3. Data

The analysis combines data on the use of non-compete and related clauses from the OECD–Bocconi employer survey with firm-level balance sheet data from Orbis.

## 3.1. Non-compete survey data

The OECD and Bocconi University jointly developed two surveys, one for employers and one for employees, to study the prevalence and characteristics of non-compete and related clauses in 15 OECD countries, which represent a variety of labour market and social models. The employer survey is used to construct an industry-country level measure of non-compete prevalence due to the higher precision of responses compared to the employee-level survey. Employers were offered graded response options (“definitely yes” / “probably yes” / “probably no” / “definitely no”) alongside a distinct “don’t know” category, allowing respondents to express uncertainty. In addition, firms were asked about their use of related clauses, including non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) $^{4}$ , and were given the same response options to choose from as for the question regarding non-compete clauses. 8. Annex A provides more information about the surveys.

The industry-country level non-compete prevalence is defined as the percentage of workers covered by non-compete clauses. It is derived following the steps below:

\- The firm-level incidence of non-compete clauses is computed as the mid-point of the range of employees reported to be covered by a non-compete clause, for firms that selected “definitely yes” or “probably yes” to using non-compete clauses.

\- An industry-country level estimate of the proportion of employees covered by a non-compete is computed by aggregating the firm-level incidence measures, weighing by firm size and the appropriate survey weights. Industry-country cells with less than 10 firms present in the survey are discarded to improve reliability of the estimated non-compete clause incidence at the industry-country-level. $^{5}$

Non-disclosure agreement (NDA) is a contract or clause that prohibits an employee from sharing or using confidential or proprietary information obtained during the employment relationship. A similar approach is followed to compute the incidence of NDAs than the steps outlined above concerning the incidence of non-compete clauses.

No-poaching agreements (NPAs) are firm-to-firm arrangements in which employers agree not to recruit or hire each other's employees. Unlike non-compete clauses – which are contractual restrictions between individual employers and employees – no-poaching agreements involve collusion among employers to restrict competition for labour and are, in most cases, considered illegal under antitrust law. Therefore, the employer survey did not ask firms whether they engaged in such practices directly. Instead, firms were asked whether they were aware of no-poaching or wage-fixing agreements occurring in their industry. No-poaching agreement awareness is measured as the proportion of firms aware of firm-to-firm no-poaching agreements in their industry.

Table A.2 in 8. Annex A shows summary statistics for the main variables used in the empirical analysis. The industry-country-level prevalence of non-compete clauses is relatively high: on average, 27.6% of workers are covered by a non-compete clause. NDAs are the most commonly used clause, with a mean industry-country-level incidence of 57.5% of workers. Awareness of no-poaching agreements is also high, especially given that such agreements are illegal in most of the survey countries: on average, 28.2% of firms report being aware of no-poaching agreements in their own industry. For an in-depth analysis on the prevalence and characteristics of non-compete and related agreements in OECD countries, see OECD (2026[5]).

Though most of the analysis is based on data from the employer survey, the employee survey was used to gather descriptive evidence on the relationship between non-compete clauses and business entry (Box 1): the employee-level survey asked workers if they have been stopped by their employer from opening a business because of a non-compete clause.

## Box 1. Non-compete clauses and business entry

Non-compete clauses directly prohibit employees from starting competing businesses. Though the practice is often justified by the need for incumbent firms to protect legitimate business interests and trade secrets, it can have a severe knock-on effect on productivity by reducing the number of productive entrants, as well as by limiting the competitive pressure on incumbent firms.

The OECD-Bocconi employee-level survey offers direct evidence of the impact that non-compete clauses have on new business formation and job-to-job mobility. Respondents were asked if a non-compete clause has ever prevented them from starting their own business, or from moving jobs. Figure 1 shows the results. Across the survey countries, over 20% of workers with a non-compete clause had been stopped from moving jobs and over 10% had been stopped from opening a business (Panel A). Given the high prevalence of non-compete clauses, Panel B illustrates that these figures translate into meaningful shares of all private sector workers surveyed. Over 5% of workers report having been stopped from moving jobs due to a non-compete clause at some point in their career, and around 2.7% of all workers surveyed report having been prevented from opening a competing business (2% of workers by a current non-compete and 0.7% by a past one).

These figures are economically meaningful in the context of new business formation statistics. In recent years, the average annual new business density in countries included in the survey has been around 5.7 per 1000 working age people. $^{1}$ Converting the proportion of private sector employees who have been stopped from opening a business by their current non-compete clause into a comparable number – as a proportion of the working age population, per annum – the results suggest that new business formation could have been around 1.2 businesses per 1000 working age people higher, had these employees not been prevented from opening new businesses because of non-compete clauses. $^{2}$ This represents an increase in new business formation of approximately one-fifth.

Though previous evidence on the impact of non-compete clauses on firm entry is limited, existing studies support the view that non-compete clauses reduce entry. Babina (2019[26]) finds that new start-up creation of employees in existing companies subject to adverse financial shocks is significantly reduced in states where non-compete clauses are more easily enforced. ## 3.2. Firm-level data

The industry-country level measure of non-compete incidence is matched with firm-level Orbis data, at the 1-digit NACE Rev. 2 industry classification level. The Orbis database is a firm-level cross-country longitudinal dataset maintained by Bureau van Dijk. The data is processed using the methodology adopted by Gal (2013[29]) and Kalemlî-Özcan et al. (2024[30]). $^{6}$ The 12 OECD countries included in the merged dataset – which combines productivity data from Orbis and non-compete prevalence data from the employer survey – are: Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, Korea, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden and the United Kingdom. $^{7}$

The key firm-level variables for the analysis are labour productivity, number of employees and firm age. Labour productivity is measured as the natural logarithm of value added per employee. Value added is constructed by summing Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortisation (EBITDA) and employee costs, instead of using Bureau van Dijk's measure. This approach offers better coverage and avoids issues arising from Bureau van Dijk treating missing components as zero, which can lead to inaccurate or unreasonable results (Gal, 2013[29]; Andrews, Criscuolo and Gal, 2016[31]).

The rest of the variables used in the empirical analysis are constructed using the key variables listed above. As is customary in the productivity literature, all firm-level variables have been trimmed at the 99th and the 1st percentile to reduce the influence of large outliers on the empirical estimates.


## 3.3. Additional data sources

In addition, the analysis makes use of the following data:

\- Labour market tightness: defined as vacancies over unemployed, at the sectoral level, harmonised across countries. The data is from Dorville, Filippucci and Marcolin (2025[32]), which also provides more detail about the methodology.

\- EPL: the OECD Employment Protection Legislation (EPL) indicator, at the country level. The EPL indicators measure the strictness of regulations governing hiring and firing, including rules on individual and collective dismissals and the use of temporary contracts. They capture the extent to which employment protection may affect labour market flexibility and adjustment. The analysis uses the latest available OECD EPL data (Chapter 6 of OECD (2026[5])).

\- PMR: the OECD Product Market Regulation (PMR) indicator, at the country level. The PMR indicators measure the distortions to competition that can be induced by the regulatory barriers to entry and growth faced by firms. 2023 values are used in the analysis. Source: PMR database.

\- Regulatory index: the OECD index of non-compete regulation (Andrews et al., 2026[33]), which builds on Bishara (2010). The index captures the stringency of non-compete regulation across countries along three dimensions: (1) the regulatory level (statute, case law, or collective bargaining); (2) the object of bargaining (scope of protectable interests, compensation requirements, and timing of introduction); and (3) enforcement (burden of proof, judicial modification, post-dismissal enforceability, and sanctions). The index ranges from 0 to 700, with higher values indicating stronger enforceability of non-competes. See Figure A.1 in 8. Annex A and Andrews et al. (2026[33]) for details.

## 4. Empirical methodology

We provide new cross-country evidence on the potential for non-compete prevalence to shape within-industry productivity performance via two relevant structural mechanisms:

\- Productivity-enhancing labour reallocation: the extent to which more productive firms can attract labour and grow at the expense of less productive firms.

\- Knowledge diffusion: the potential for firms further behind the global productivity frontier to experience higher (catch-up driven) productivity growth, consistent with the larger stock of unexploited technologies that they have at their disposal for adoption.

## 4.1. Channel #1: Productivity-enhancing labour reallocation

To study the link between non-compete clauses and productivity-enhancing labour reallocation, the analysis builds on established approaches in the productivity literature (Decker et al., 2020[7]) and the large body of OECD work using a similar methodology (Andrews and Hansell, 2021[34]; Andrews, Charlton and Moore, 2021[35]; Zona Mattioli and Borowiecki, 2025[36]) to assess whether employment growth responds less strongly to firm-level productivity in environments where non-compete clauses are more prevalent. The baseline specification is given by the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{r} e m p l o y m e n t g r o w t h _ {\{i, j, c, t \}} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} L P _ {i, j, c, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} L P _ {i, j, c, t - 1} * N C C _ {j, c} + c o n t r o l s _ {i, j, c, t} + \mu_ {j, c} + \theta_ {t} \\ + \varepsilon_ {i, j, c, t}. \end{array}
$$

\- Employment growth is the growth in the number of employees at firm i, in industry j and country c, between year t and $t-1^{9}$

\- LP is the logarithm of firm-level labour productivity (value added per employee)

\- NCC is the share of workers bound by non-compete clauses at the 1-digit NACE Rev. 2 industry level, in country c

Controls: firm-level employment in year t-1 and firm age $^{10}$

\- Fixed effects: industry x country ( $\mu_{j,c}$ ) and year ( $\theta_{t}$ ) fixed effects. 2-digit NACE Rev. 2 industries are used to construct the fixed effects in the baseline specification. The fixed effect structure captures country-specific industry-level shocks as well as other omitted variables that are likely to be correlated with firm-level employment growth and industry-wide use of non-compete clauses that may otherwise confound the analysis. For example, industry-country-level R&D expenditure, innovation and intangible investment, as well as labour force characteristics, are absorbed by the fixed effect structure. In addition, the fixed effect structure ensures that the results can be interpreted as capturing the strength of labour reallocation within industry-country cells.

If the estimated coefficient $\beta_{1}$ is positive, then labour reallocation is productivity-enhancing: higher productivity firms are more likely to expand and lower productivity firms are more likely to contract. But if the estimated coefficient on the interaction term $\beta_{2}$ is negative, then a higher prevalence of non-compete clauses at the industry level is associated with weaker productivity-enhancing labour reallocation.

## 4.2. Channel #2: Knowledge diffusion

A second channel through which non-compete clauses may affect productivity is by limiting knowledge diffusion, given the wealth of evidence showing that such clauses stifle job mobility. This may be particularly penalising to firms further away from the technological frontier, for which adopting ideas, best practices and technology developed by frontier firms requires access to skilled employees.

To assess if non-compete prevalence is associated with reduced catch-up growth, this section follows the framework developed by Aghion and Howitt (1998[8]) and Aghion et al. (2005[37]). In neo-Schumpeterian growth models, aggregate productivity growth is influenced by knowledge transfer from leading firms (“the frontier”) to other firms. The frontier is defined as the most productive firms within each industry (top 5% of firms), which increase their productivity through innovation and continuous improvement in production processes. The rest of the firms in the economy can achieve faster growth by adopting technologies and practices implemented by the frontier. The productivity difference between the frontier and any given firm is referred to as the gap-to-the-frontier.

The baseline specification is given by:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} L P g r o w t h _ {i, j, c, t} = & \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} f r o n t i e r g r o w t h _ {j, t} + \beta_ {2} g a p _ {i, j, c, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} g a p _ {i, j, c, t - 1} * N C C _ {j, c} \\ & + c o n t r o l s _ {i, j, c, t} + \mu_ {j, c} + \theta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, j, c, t} \end{array}
$$

\- LP growth is the change in log labour productivity at firm i, in industry j and country c, between year t and t-1

\- Frontier growth is defined as the change in the average log labour productivity of the global productivity frontier (the top 5% of all the firms in the NACE Rev. 2 2-digit industry). $^{11}$

\- Gap is defined as the difference between the labour productivity of each firm and the average labour productivity of frontier firms in the firm's NACE Rev. 2 2-digit industry

\- NCC is non-compete prevalence at the 1-digit NACE Rev. 2 industry level, in country c

Controls: firm employment in year t-1 and firm age $^{12}$

\- Fixed effects: industry x country $(\mu_{j,c,})$ and year $(\theta_t)$ fixed effects. In the baseline, 2-digit NACE Rev. 2 industries are used to construct the fixed effects.

A positive $\beta_{2}$ indicates catch-up growth: firms further from the frontier have the potential to grow faster, given a larger stock of unexploited technologies at their disposal for adoption. However, if $\beta_{3}$ is negative, the results are consistent with the hypothesis that a higher non-compete prevalence is associated with less knowledge diffusion and slower catch-up to the frontier. Similar to the reallocation regressions, this specification can be estimated using interacted industry x country fixed effects, as the non-compete prevalence is interacted with the gap to the frontier (a firm level variable). The catch-up growth regressions are estimated on a sample of firms that excludes the global productivity frontier.

## 5.1.1. Non-disclosure and no-poaching agreements

The links between higher non-compete prevalence, weaker productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and slower catch-up growth remain statistically significant when controlling for interactions with the incidence of non-disclosure agreements (NDAs, Column 3 of Table 1 and Table 2), awareness of no-poaching agreements (NPAs, Column 4) or both (Column 5). While we cannot rule out the possibility that confounding factors remain, these results suggest the baseline findings are unlikely to be driven by unobservable factors related to the need to protect sensitive information or firm investments (which is partially controlled for by the inclusion of the NDA interaction), or other anticompetitive behaviour in the labour market (partly captured by controlling for NPA awareness).

These results also suggest that the reallocation and catch-up effects are stronger for non-compete clauses in comparison to less restrictive NDAs. The estimated coefficients for the interaction terms with NDAs are negative in both specifications, and significant at the 10% level, though much smaller than the estimated associations with non-compete clauses. However, when controlling for no-poaching agreements or additional institutional controls (interactions with employment protection legislation – EPL – and product marker regulation – PMR) in columns 4-6, the coefficients for non-compete clauses remain negative and significant, while the NDA interaction term is less robust, becoming statistically insignificant. This pattern is consistent with the idea that non-compete clauses, by directly restricting job mobility, are particularly distortive for labour reallocation and knowledge diffusion, whereas more targeted tools such as NDAs could have more limited productivity effects.

Chapter 5 in OECD (2026[5]) reports high awareness of no-poaching agreements amongst firms. As they restrict labour mobility, NPAs may also play a role in slowing down productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and knowledge diffusion. Column 4 in Table 1 shows the results when the share of firms that report being aware of NPAs in their industry, interacted with lagged labour productivity, is added to the baseline reallocation regression. The interaction term between no-poaching agreement awareness and lagged labour productivity is negative and significant: no-poaching agreements are associated with less productivity-enhancing labour reallocation. However, it should be noted that the point estimates for non-compete clauses are not directly comparable with those for no-poaching agreements because the measures have different definitions: the former is the share of workers covered by a non-compete clause, while the latter is the share of firms that are aware of no-poaching agreements in their industry. In addition, given the imprecise nature of the measure of no-poaching agreement prevalence, the coefficient estimate for the NPA interaction is less precise and less robust to the inclusion of further controls.

Higher awareness of no-poaching agreements is also associated with slower catch-up to the frontier (Column 4, Table 2). However, this relationship is not statistically significant at conventional levels, which could also reflect the imperfect nature of the measurement of no-poaching agreement prevalence.

## 5.1.2. Additional robustness tests

Further robustness checks are displayed in 8. Annex B. The baseline specifications pool data from the 2023 and 2022 period. $^{14}$ Table B.1 and Table B.4 illustrate that the results hold if only a single time period is included in the analysis. The results are also robust to different fixed effect specifications (1-digit or 2-digit NACE Rev. 2 industry classification; interacted and separate fixed effects, displayed in Table B.1 and Table B.4. One possible reason for the negative associations between non-compete clauses, labour reallocation and catch-up growth is variation in labour market tightness, i.e. how difficult it is for employers to fill open positions. If a tighter labour market reduces the ability of more productive firms to expand, or reduces firm-to-firm hires thus limiting the spread of knowledge, while simultaneously increasing the use of non-compete clauses, this could result in negative associations between non-compete incidence, efficient labour reallocation and catch-up growth. In addition, labour force characteristics are likely to be correlated with non-compete prevalence, and may also be linked to labour reallocation and/or the strength of knowledge diffusion.

Table B.3 shows that the reallocation results are robust to allowing for interactions between lagged productivity, an industry-country-year-level proxy for labour market tightness (described in Section 3), the proportion of highly educated workers as well as workers in high skill occupations, at the industry-country-level. Controlling for labour market tightness slightly reduces the association between non-compete clauses and weaker reallocation, while including labour force characteristics has a smaller effect on the non-compete coefficient. Nevertheless, the estimated coefficients on the non-compete interaction term remain negative and significant at the 5% level. Interestingly, the interaction between labour market tightness and lagged labour productivity is negative and significant: higher labour market tightness is associated with weaker productivity-enhancing labour reallocation. This is consistent with the view that high productivity firms may find it more difficult to expand when the pool of available workers is reduced. $^{15}$

The results are similar for catch-up growth. Table B.6 shows that allowing labour market tightness to influence technological catch-up slightly attenuates the coefficient estimate on the interaction between the gap and non-compete prevalence, whereas controlling for labour market characteristics amplifies the association between weaker catch-up growth and non-compete clauses slightly. Overall, the estimates are robust to including these controls and the interaction term remains significant at the 10% level. The interaction term between labour market tightness and the gap to the frontier is negative, and significant in some specifications. Tight labour markets may therefore slow down the spread of knowledge within industries.

## 6. Aggregate productivity implications

So far, the analysis has documented that higher non-compete prevalence is associated with weaker productivity-enhancing labour reallocation and slower technological catch-up among non-frontier firms. Taken together, these findings point to potentially non-trivial implications for aggregate productivity. This section uses the estimated associations between non-compete prevalence, reallocation and catch-up growth to compute the implied effect of non-compete clauses on the level of aggregate labour productivity.

The scenario considered is a 10-percentage-point increase in industry-level non-compete incidence. $^{18}$ Aggregate productivity effects are computed by applying a well-established productivity decomposition in the literature (Foster, Haltiwanger and Krizan, 2001 $^{[9]}$ ), commonly known as the “FHK decomposition”, which distinguishes between within-firm productivity growth and productivity gains arising from the reallocation of labour across continuing firms. The reallocation regressions allow us to estimate how each firm’s employment share changes when exposed to higher prevalence of non-compete clauses, taking their firm-level labour productivity as given. The catch-up growth regressions allow us to estimate how a given (non-frontier) firm’s labour productivity growth changes when exposed to higher non-compete prevalence, holding their employment share constant. $^{19}$ This allows us to convert the estimated firm-level coefficients into changes in the aggregate productivity level associated with a 10-p.p. increase in the prevalence of non-compete clauses, isolating the reallocation and within-firm effects. $^{20}$ The methodology is outlined in detail in Box 2.

## Box 2. Computation of aggregate productivity effects

To estimate the aggregate productivity effects, changes in aggregate productivity are decomposed building on a well-established approach in the literature (Foster, Haltiwanger and Krizan, 2001[9]), henceforth “FHK decomposition”, which distinguishes between within-firm productivity growth and productivity gains arising from the reallocation of labour across continuing firms. The original FHK decomposition also has terms reflecting the productivity contribution of entry and exit. It should be noted that the current exercise is restricted to continuing firms and thus does not capture any potential effects of non-compete clauses on the entry (or exit) margin. We discuss the potential impact of non-compete clauses on the entry margin in more detail in Box 1.

The change in the aggregate labour productivity index (given by the employment weighted average of firm-level labour productivity) when the incidence of non-compete clauses increases by 10 percentage points, is given by:

$$
P _ {t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} - P _ {t} ^ {\mu} = \sum_ {i} \hat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} \hat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} - \sum_ {i} \hat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} \hat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu}
$$

Where $P_{t}^{\mu}$ denotes the aggregate labour productivity index when the prevalence of non-compete clauses is at their current level, and $P_{t}^{\mu+0.1}$ denotes aggregate labour productivity when non-compete prevalence is 10 percentage points higher. $\hat{\theta}_{it}^{\mu}$ and $\hat{\theta}_{it}^{\mu+0.1}$ are the firm-level employment weights when non-compete clause prevalence is at its current level and when it is 10 percentage points higher, respectively. $\hat{a}_{it}^{\mu}$ and $\hat{a}_{it}^{\mu+0.1}$ denote the logarithm of firm-level labour productivity when non-compete clause incidence is at its current level and 10 percentage points higher, respectively.

The change in the productivity index can be split into the following components, noting that only continuing firms in the data (C) are observed, instead of the sample of firms present in the scenario, resulting in the additional entry and exit terms.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {P} _ {t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} - \boldsymbol {P} _ {t} ^ {\mu} = \sum_ {i \in C} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} \widehat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} + \sum_ {i \in C} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} a _ {i t - 1} - \sum_ {i \in C} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} a _ {i t - 1} + \underbrace {\sum_ {i \in E ^ {\mu + 0 . 1}} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1} \widehat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1}} _ {= E ^ {\mu + 0. 1} (\text {Entry})} \\ \quad - \underbrace {\sum_ {i \in X ^ {\mu + 0 . 1}} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1} \widehat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1}} _ {= X ^ {\mu + 0. 1} (E x i t, \mu + 0. 1 o n l y)} + \underbrace {\sum_ {i \in X} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1} \widehat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1}} _ {= X ^ {a - (\mu + 0. 1)} (E x i t, a c t u a l o n l y)} - \sum_ {i \in C} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} \widehat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} - \sum_ {i \in C} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} a _ {i t - 1} \\ \quad + \sum_ {i \in C} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} a _ {i t - 1} - \underbrace {\sum_ {i \in E} \widehat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} \widehat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu}} _ {= E (E n t r y)} \end{array}
$$

This can be simplified to yield:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \pmb {P} _ {t} ^ {\mu + 0. 1} - \pmb {P} _ {t} ^ {\mu} = \underbrace {\sum_ {i \in C} \big (\hat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1} - \hat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} \big) a _ {i t - 1}} _ {\text {Reallocation}} \\ + \underbrace {\sum_ {i \in C} \theta_ {i t - 1} \big (\Delta \hat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1} - \Delta \hat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} \big)} _ {\text {Within - firm}} + \underbrace {\sum_ {i \in C} \Delta \hat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1} \Delta \hat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu + 0 . 1} - \sum_ {i \in C} \Delta \hat {\theta} _ {i t} ^ {\mu} \Delta \hat {a} _ {i t} ^ {\mu}} _ {\text {Interaction}} + \underbrace {E - X} _ {\text {Entry\ & Exit}} \end{array}
$$

which forms the basis of the computation of aggregate effects. Non-hatted variables refer to observed labour productivity and employment weights for period t-1.

The reallocation component captures how much the change in the employment weights of firms, implied by the empirical model estimates, contributes to the change in aggregate productivity. The computation of this component follows similar approaches in the literature (Decker et al., 2020[7]; Andrews and Hansell, 2021[34]). The employment weights are computed as the weights implied by the empirical model, starting from observed employment and labour productivity in period t-1, for the baseline (where non-compete clause prevalence is at their current level) and the scenario where non-compete incidence increases by 10 percentage points. The empirical estimates used in the computation are displayed in Table 1, column 2, and Table 3, Panel A.

The within-firm component captures the effect of changes in within-firm productivity growth, keeping the employment weights fixed at their observed values. Labour productivity growth at the firm-level is given by the empirical model implied predicted values, when non-compete incidence is at its current level $\left(\Delta\widehat{a}_{it}^{\mu}\right)$ and when non-compete clause prevalence increases by 10 percentage points $\left(\Delta\widehat{a}_{it}^{\mu+0.1}\right)$ . The coefficient estimates used are given by Table 2, Column 2 and Table 3, Panel B. As the empirical specification capturing catch-up growth is only estimated for non-frontier firms, the predicted firm-level productivity growth rates are replaced by the observed growth rates for frontier firms. This is equivalent to assuming that the labour productivity of frontier firms does not change when the non-compete incidence increases, which – for reasons explained below – is a relatively conservative assumption.

The interaction term between changes in weights and firm-level productivity is similar to the cross-term of the FHK decomposition. It is computed combining the terms computed for the reallocation and within-firm terms. It results in a small adjustment to the overall aggregate productivity change. Finally, the analysis abstracts from entry and exit effects, as estimating the association between non-compete clause prevalence, firm entry and exit is beyond the scope of the current analysis, partly because entry and exit are not well captured by the Orbis data.

The reallocation and within-firm productivity effects are computed for each country in the sample, for years 2022 and 2023. To obtain the overall figure, the mean of the country-level effects is computed for each year. The figures displayed reflect the average effect of the two years. It should be noted that the results illustrate the difference in the level of aggregate productivity. Although the catch-up growth regressions capture the association between non-compete prevalence and firm-level productivity growth for laggard firms, these results are used to infer the predicted change in the aggregate productivity level; not changes in its growth rate.

Figure 4 illustrates the economically material productivity losses associated with the spread of non-compete clauses. Across the sample of countries, a 10-percentage-point increase in non-compete incidence is associated with a 1.9-percentage-point decline in aggregate productivity. Most of this effect reflects slower catch-up growth among non-frontier firms (around 1.6 percentage points), while worsening of the efficiency of labour reallocation across firms contributes a further 0.3 percentage points. These results suggest that the productivity costs of non-compete clauses operate primarily through weaker knowledge diffusion and slower convergence, but reduced allocative efficiency also plays a role.

In line with the previous discussion, the aggregate productivity effect of non-compete clauses seems more pronounced in countries where they are less regulated or more likely to be upheld in court. In these countries, the increase in non-compete incidence is associated with a reduction in aggregate productivity of over 2 percentage points. In relative terms, this loss is estimated to be over one-third larger in countries with lenient regulation than in countries with more stringent non-compete regulation, highlighting the potential role of court enforceability in shaping the economic bite of these clauses. Nevertheless, even in countries where regulation already places tighter constraints on non-compete use, the implied productivity costs are sizeable – at 1.5 percentage points – suggesting that further restrictions in the use of non-compete clauses could still yield meaningful efficiency benefits.

The productivity effects associated with a relatively modest change in non-compete prevalence are – as expected – smaller than the productivity impacts of larger scale structural reforms, but still material. For example, large-scale network sector deregulation between 1980 and 2023 is estimated to have boosted labour productivity by around 5 percentage points on average across OECD countries (Andrews et al., 2025[38]). In comparison, the nearly 2-percentage-point gain associated with a 10-percentage-point reduction in non-compete prevalence illustrates the benefits of relatively confined policy action, suggesting that reforms to non-compete regulation may be a low-hanging fruit for boosting productivity.

It should be noted that the analysis presented above is limited to considering the aggregate productivity impact arising from the two channels studied in detail: weaker labour reallocation and slower knowledge diffusion. As discussed in Section 2, there are other potential mechanisms through which non-compete clauses can affect labour productivity. On the one hand, the negative impact on catch-up growth and technological diffusion could be attenuated by increased firm-level innovation, investment into human capital of workers and other intangible assets. On the other hand, lower competitive pressure and entry, as well as higher labour market mismatch could amplify the negative productivity effects quantified in the analysis above. $^{22}$

As discussed in Box 1, suggestive evidence from the employee-level survey shows that non-compete clauses could be placing meaningful constraints on new business formation, which can have knock-on effects on productivity directly by reducing the stock of productive entrants, as well as by reducing the competitive pressure on incumbent firms. While empirical evidence on the impact of non-compete clauses on training and investment is mixed, the balance of evidence does not point to notable productivity-enhancing benefits; on the contrary, new evidence supports the view that a regulatory framework that favours the use of non-compete clauses actually hinders innovation (Johnson, Lipsitz and Pei, 2023[40]; Lipsitz and Tremblay, 2024[41]; Rockall and Reinmuth, forthcoming[42]). The evidence from the literature as well as the results above suggest that the potential benefits are unlikely to outweigh their costs, and that the estimated productivity effects are likely to be a lower bound as other potential negative effects are not accounted for.

## 7. Concluding remarks and policy discussion

The results presented in the previous section have important policy implications. The spread of non-compete clauses could have played a role in the productivity slowdown and the accompanying decline in economic dynamism by amplifying frictions that restrict the mobility of productive resources as well as competition in factor and product markets. This suggests that recent regulatory interventions to restrict the use of non-compete clauses in selected OECD countries are a potential productivity-enhancing structural reform. While outright bans represent one policy extreme, they are far from the only option available. Most OECD countries have instead adopted more tailored approaches, combining restrictions on scope and procedural requirements with safeguards aimed at protecting workers while preserving legitimate business interests – see (Andrews et al., 2026[33]) for an overview of OECD countries and Bishara and Luisetto (2025[43]) for an overview of state initiatives in the United States.

The evidence that non-compete clauses may reduce productivity even in countries where they are more tightly regulated supports previous findings that such clauses can have a “chilling effect” on job mobility, as workers often perceive non-compete clauses as valid and act accordingly, irrespective of the likelihood that courts would uphold them. Policy approaches that focus only on the formal regulatory framework of non-compete clauses are therefore unlikely to fully address their economic effects. Beyond more effective legal enforcement, higher transparency and clarity are essential complements. Where legal frameworks are complex, fragmented or poorly communicated, employees may overestimate the strength and scope of contractual clauses. Conversely, clear rules, accessible information and visible enforcement can reduce uncertainty and weaken the deterrent effect of unenforceable clauses. This suggests that policy effectiveness depends not only on the content of regulation, but also on how rules are communicated, interpreted and enforced.

The link between non-disclosure agreements and productivity is much weaker than for non-competes, suggesting that there may be less distortive alternatives to protect firm's business interests than mobilitysapping non-compete clauses. Field experimental evidence shows that non-compete clauses do not reduce unauthorised information sharing beyond what non-disclosure agreements already prevent (Cowgill, Freiberg and Starr, 2024[44]). Consistent with this, Greenwood, Kobayashi and Starr (forthcoming[18]) find that non-compete bans increase mobility while trade secret litigation remains flat in the short run and declines in the long run. Surveys of legal practitioners indicate that alternative tools are generally viewed as adequate (Hiraiwa, Lipsitz and Starr, 2024[17]).

Finally, there is some evidence to suggest that in industries where the share of firms aware of no-poaching agreements being deployed is higher, the extent of productivity-enhancing reallocation tends to be weaker, consistent with the adverse effects of no-poaching agreements on job mobility. This underscores the importance and continued relevance of recent enforcement actions by competition authorities with respect to (firm-to-firm) no-poaching agreements, which are illegal under antitrust laws in most jurisdictions.

# Annex A. Data

The analysis combines data from the OECD–Bocconi employer survey with firm-level balance sheet data from Orbis. Summary statistics for the main variables used in the regressions are displayed in Table A.2.

## Non-compete survey data

The OECD and Bocconi University jointly developed two surveys, one for employers and one for employees, to study the prevalence and characteristics of non-compete and related clauses in 15 OECD countries, which represent a variety of labour market and social models. The surveys follow the methodology used in comparable exercises previously undertaken in the United States (Prescott, Bishara and Starr, 2016[45]), Australia (Andrews and Jarvis, 2023[46]; Andrews, Brennan and Buckley, 2024[47]) and Italy (Boeri, Garnero and Luisetto, 2025[48]) and were fielded by Ipsos – see OECD (2026[5]), Chapter 5, Annex 5.A for detailed information of the surveys.

## Employer-level survey

The employer-level survey targeted individuals responsible for employment contracts, retention and hiring practices in for-profit firms with five or more employees in Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, Korea, Mexico, New Zealand, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and Italy. Non-profit organisations, micro-employers (1-4 employees) and selected sectors (public administration and defence, education, health and social work, household services, and extraterritorial organisations) were excluded. The sampling frame was drawn from Dun & Bradstreet, complemented with Orbis, based on NACE Rev. 2 codes and firm size. A stratified random sampling design was applied across 16 cells, defined by four firm-size classes (5-9; 10-49; 50-249; 250+) and four top-level NACE sector groups.

Given that most firms are very small, it is important to take steps to ensure that the survey of firms captures a reasonable slice of the labour force, particularly in larger countries. Accordingly, we used a mixed sampling approach that combines employee-proportional and firm-proportional allocation. Employee-proportional sampling weights firms by their employment size, ensuring larger employers (who collectively account for most employees) are adequately represented. Firm-proportional sampling gives equal weight to all firms regardless of size, preserving representation of small businesses that dominate the firm population. Depending on the size of the available sampling frames (in particular of large firms), a 75% employee-proportional / 25% firm-proportional allocation was used in Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Korea, Mexico, Poland, Spain and the United Kingdom, while a 33% employee-proportional / 67% firm-proportional allocation in Portugal, a 20% employee-proportional / 80% firm-proportional allocation was used in Switzerland, a 18% employee-proportional / 82% firm-proportional allocation was used in New Zealand and a 15% employee-proportional / 85% firm-proportional allocation in Sweden.

A pilot test preceded fieldwork, which ran from the end of June to mid-September 2025. The main fieldwork resulted in 6 112 completed interviews with firms employing over 1.4 million employees in total – country-specific information is available in Table A.1. Final weights were constructed based on the stratification cells (industry and firm size) to reflect the employee distribution in each country. Since the employer survey took place by telephone based on random sampling, common sources of potential selection bias related to online surveys – limited internet access and self-selection into online panels – do not apply. While standard non-response bias remains possible, the neutral framing of the survey invitation and the very low drop-out rate after employers were first presented with questions on non-compete clauses suggests that this risk is limited.

## Employee-level survey

The worker-level survey targeted private-sector employees aged 18-64 in the aforementioned countries, apart from Italy. It combined: (i) a general population sample of 2 000 private-sector employees to measure the overall incidence of non-compete clauses; and (ii) a boost sample to reach 1 000 observations of employees known to have signed a non-compete clause (i.e. targeting only workers with a non-compete clause), enabling detailed analysis of clause content and employee experiences. Sampling used non-probability online access panels, with respondents selected through a mix of targeted and random draws. Panels applied extensive quality-control checks to ensure respondents were real, fresh (i.e. have not answered to other surveys in the previous days) and unique. The sample was stratified by age, sex and region (following official statistics), with additional efforts to balance education level and sector of employment. Post-stratification weights were constructed to align the sample to the socio-demographic profile of the target population in each country. A pilot test preceded fieldwork. Data collection ran from end of July to mid-September 2025, yielding 31 762 completed interviews in total – country-specific information is available in Table A.1.

As with any survey, the data may be subject to sample selection. Three potential sources are relevant: incomplete internet coverage, self-selection into online panels, and survey non-response. In practice, these risks appear limited. In all countries, the share of households with internet access at home is above 90%, reducing concerns about digital exclusion. The only exception is Mexico, where internet access was 72% in 2023. While panel membership may reflect unobservable characteristics, the panel used is broadly representative of the population on observable traits and is primarily designed for general consumer research rather than labour market topics, making systematic bias related to non-compete coverage unlikely. Finally, standard non-response remains a concern, but the neutral framing of the survey invitation and the very low dropout rate once questions on non-compete clauses were introduced (about 1%) suggest that selective attrition is limited.

## Measuring the prevalence of non-compete and related clauses

The employer-level survey on non-compete and related clauses was used to construct an industry-country level measure of non-compete prevalence using the following steps. The firm-level incidence of non-compete clauses is computed as the mid-point of the range of employees reported to be covered by a non-compete clause, for firms that selected “definitely yes” or “probably yes” to using non-compete clauses. The industry-country level estimate is then obtained by aggregating the firm-level incidence measures, weighing by firm size and the appropriate survey weights. A similar approach is followed for computing the incidence of non-disclosure agreements (NDAs). No-poaching agreement awareness, on the other hand, is defined as the proportion of firms aware of no-poaching agreements in their industry.

The non-compete survey covers 16 NACE Rev. 2 1-digit industries: agriculture, forestry & fishing; mining and quarrying; manufacturing; electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply; water supply; construction; wholesale and retail trade; transportation and storage; hospitality; information and communication; financial and insurance activities; real estate activities; professional, scientific and technical activities; administrative and support service activities; arts, entertainment and recreation; and other service activities.

Industry-country cells with less than 10 firms present in the survey are discarded to improve reliability of the estimated non-compete clause incidence at the industry-country-level. Reflecting the population of firms in each country, the distribution of firms across these industries is highly uneven. Hence, most industry-country cells have more than 10 respondent firms, and those that are removed due to a small sample size are mainly concentrated in agriculture, forestry, fishing and mining. The former three are not included in Orbis, and therefore would not have been included in the merged firm-level data regardless of sample size.

## Orbis firm-level data

The Orbis database is a firm-level cross-country longitudinal dataset maintained by Bureau van Dijk. The data is processed using the methodology adopted by Gal (2013[29]) and Kalemlİ-Özcan et al. (2024[30]). The steps include retaining firms with full calendar year records, removing duplicates by excluding consolidated accounts if an unconsolidated account is available for the same firm in a given year, and dropping observations with missing key variables.

The key firm-level variables for the analysis are labour productivity, number of employees and firm age. Labour productivity is measured as the natural logarithm of value added per employee. Value added is constructed by summing Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortisation (EBITDA) and employee costs, instead of using Bureau van Dijk's measure. This approach offers better coverage and avoids issues arising from Bureau van Dijk treating missing components as zero, which can lead to inaccurate or unreasonable results (Gal, 2013[29]; Andrews, Criscuolo and Gal, 2016[31]).

Employment growth is measured as the difference in the logarithm of number of employees between year t-1 and year t. Similarly, labour productivity growth is the difference in the logarithm of labour productivity between year t-1 and year t. Frontier firms are defined as the top 5% of firms in each NACE Rev. 2 2-digit industry. The productivity gap to the frontier is then computed as the difference between the labour productivity of each firm and the average labour productivity of frontier firms in the firm's industry. Frontier growth is then computed as the change in the average log labour productivity of the global productivity frontier (the top 5% of all the firms in the NACE Rev. 2 2-digit industry). All firm-level variables have been trimmed at the 99th and the 1st percentile to reduce the influence of large outliers on the empirical estimates.


## Sample for baseline regression analysis

The industry-country level measure of non-compete incidence is matched with firm-level Orbis data, at the 1-digit NACE Rev. 2 level. Countries included in the merged dataset are Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, Korea, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden and the United Kingdom. Although data for New Zealand, Mexico and Switzerland is available through Orbis, these countries are not included in the productivity dataset due to data limitations. Firm value added is made internationally comparable by using industry-level PPP deflators. These are not readily available for New Zealand and Switzerland. For Mexico, the number of firms included per industry is judged to be too small for conducting robust within-industry estimation.

The baseline regressions are estimated using data from 2022 (reflecting changes between 2021 and 2022) and 2023 (where changes are from 2022 to 2023). The total number of observations in the merged data for these years is 5,136,749, consisting of 3,546,190 unique firms. The sample for estimation is restricted to firms with at least 10 employees for the baseline reallocation regressions, and firms with non-missing data for all required variables. This results in a sample of 1,009,130 observations, consisting of 623,502 firms. These numbers are reduced to 941,158 observations and 586,183 firms for the baseline catch-up growth regressions, as frontier firms are dropped.

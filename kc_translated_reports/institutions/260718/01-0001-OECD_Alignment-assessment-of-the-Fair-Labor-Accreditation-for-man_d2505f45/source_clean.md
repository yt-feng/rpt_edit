# 1 Initiative, assessment scope and methodology

## About the Fair Labor Accreditation for manufacturing

Fair Labor Association's (FLA) Fair Labor Accreditation for manufacturing (hereafter “Standard”) applies to companies in the manufacturing accreditation programme, namely brands and Tier 1 production facilities in the garment and footwear sector. Companies are assessed against the ten Principles of Fair Labor and Responsible Sourcing (FLA, 2022[1]) and Principles of Fair Labor and Responsible Production (FLA, 2022[6]), noting that expectations are adapted for companies that source and those that produce goods. These principles cover key elements of due diligence, with a focus on labour issues, including systems, frameworks, policies and procedures a company must have in place to ensure that workers’ rights are respected and are revised every five years. In addition, companies are required to uphold the Fair Labor Code (FLA, 2021[7]) in their Tier 1 supply chain. The FLA complements its standards with training, tools and guidance to support companies in strengthening their systems and practices. In June 2026, 30 companies, both brands and Tier 1 manufacturers in the garment and footwear sector, were accredited (FLA, 2026[8]), with an additional 15 companies currently pursuing accreditation. The FLA offers a second accreditation programme for companies in the agriculture sector, covering Tier 4. See Table 1 for a summary of FLA’s scope of activities, as taken into account in the assessment, and as described in more detail below:

Accreditation process and performance levels: FLA member companies are required to pursue accreditation. To achieve accreditation, companies must demonstrate the effective implementation of policies, procedures and tools to identify, prevent and address labour risks across their operations and supply chains. Before being accredited, companies follow a structured, multi-year accreditation pathway. The process is designed around a series of five milestones which guide companies in progressively strengthening their management systems. The first milestones focus on establishing and demonstrating effective systems for implementing, monitoring and remediating workplace standards, while the final milestone reflects the ongoing maintenance and continuous improvement of these systems. Once accreditation is granted, companies are assessed annually against a third of the principles to verify that they are maintaining compliance with the Standard. In addition, FLA audits an agreed percentage of a company's Tier 1 manufacturers, which are included in the accreditation report.

## Methodology

The assessment is based on the OECD's alignment assessment methodology (OECD, 2024[3]). For the Fair Labor Accreditation for manufacturing (hereafter “Standard”), the OECD conducted an assessment of written policies and standards (Standards Assessment). This assessment did not include an assessment of the initiative's implementation of its written standard or its internal governance and management systems. $^{1}$

A Standards Assessment evaluates the extent to which the initiative's written policies and standards align with the relevant OECD Due Diligence Guidance, here the OECD Garment Guidance.

Standards Assessments are based on:

\- a desktop review of relevant written policies, standards, tools and methodology documents; and

• scoping interviews with staff, as relevant.

To assess the alignment of initiatives in the garment and footwear sector, the OECD has developed an alignment assessment tool (OECD, n.d.[4]) that translates the OECD Garment Guidance into assessment criteria and guidance. Each due diligence criterion is evaluated and assigned a rating ranging from not aligned, partially aligned, fully aligned or out of scope. There are a small number of criteria which are only scored in an assessment where the initiative is fully aligned, as they cover actions that companies are encouraged rather than expected to take. The governance criteria are separately evaluated and assigned a rating of fully addressed, partially addressed or not addressed. The approach taken to evaluate and rate criteria depends on the initiative's scope and core activities, including whether it sets requirements for and evaluates companies' due diligence performance, or facilitates due diligence through tools, guidance or other activities.

The overall assessment result for the Standards Assessment is based on the formula in Section 2.

## Facilitation versus verification initiatives

For the purpose of OECD alignment assessments, the OECD distinguishes between initiatives that:

(1) facilitate or support the due diligence process of an enterprise, including by carrying out due diligence activities on behalf of companies, but which do not assess or certify business practices, sites or products against requirements ("facilitation initiatives" $^{2}$ ); and.

(2) set requirements for and assess or certify individual business practices, sites or products (“verification initiatives” $^{4}$ ).

Many initiatives carry out a mix of facilitation and verification activities, and in these situations, the assessment can be adapted to cover both aspects.

Verification initiatives also often reward different levels of performance to participating or assessed enterprises (e.g. bronze, silver, gold or levels 1, 2 and 3). The rewarded performance level can be based on fulfilling all of the initiative's mandatory criteria, meeting specific thresholds or scoring points from a mix of criteria. An alignment assessment can focus on one specific performance level if agreed upon in the scoping exercise.

## Box 1. Assessing written standards of verification initiatives

The OECD's Due Diligence Guidance, including the Garment Guidance, sets out recommendations by governments to enterprises. The voluntary nature of the language in the standards reflects this. In contrast, verification initiatives require certain business practices, evaluate performance against them and allow enterprises to make corresponding public claims. Verification initiatives often use language that varies in its degree of obligation. Mandatory criteria can use terms such as “shall” or “must,” while aspirational criteria tend to use terms such as “could,” “may” or “should” to express recommendations or other optional criteria. These differences in language are important because they determine what verification initiatives expect from their participating or member enterprises for a certain performance level, or in order to issue a product or facility certificate and the degree of oversight they have. While the exact wording varies according to each initiative, it is important that any requirements are set out in clear language for stakeholders. Vague, aspirational, or inconsistent language between documents can result in core due diligence elements being entirely outside the scope of what the verification initiative requires and checks. Recommendations are in practice outside the scope of verification activities.

As a result, the alignment assessments do not consider recommendations to be at the same level or have the same weight as requirements as they do not inform whether a company is certified or not, or the claims that a company can make as a result. The assessment otherwise risks allowing for fully aligned results for verification initiatives where the initiative does not, in practice, expect companies to fulfil the criterion.

This focus on requirements does not flow from the international instruments themselves, but from the fact that the objective of verification initiatives is to require certain business practices from companies and to allow companies to make specific public claims in terms of their performance against those requirements, whether through a certification, product label or performance level. If a verification initiative aims to align with OECD Due Diligence Guidance, this involves translating the OECD's voluntary recommendations into mandatory requirements and integrating them into the initiative's systems and operations.

## Scope of the FLA Standards Assessment

This standard-only assessment did not include an assessment of FLA's implementation of its standard or of its internal governance and management systems. Further, alignment assessments do not evaluate or draw conclusions about the adequacy of due diligence conducted by individual enterprises that participate in, are members of or are accredited by the initiative under assessment (OECD, 2024[3]).

For the Fair Labor Accreditation for manufacturing, the OECD conducted an assessment of written policies and standards (Standards Assessment). This section sets out the main assessment activities carried out for the Standards Assessment, as well as key methodological considerations and scoping decisions.

For the Standards Assessment, the OECD has carried out the following activities:

\- a desktop analysis of the written policies and standards listed in Annex B.

## Key methodological considerations:

\- As FLA assesses and accredits company performance, this Standards Assessment focuses on the extent to which the Standard sets clear and consistent requirements for companies, consistent with the content in the alignment assessment criteria (see Box 1 on verification initiatives).

\- While the OECD Garment Guidance expects all companies to conduct due diligence on their own operations and supply chains (OECD, 2018, p. 17[9]), the assessment was tailored to the specific scope of the Standard and therefore focuses on FLA's requirements relating to companies' own operations and Tier 1 manufacturers (see key scoping decisions).

\- The alignment assessment criteria also contain “encouraged criteria” (see grey-shaded criteria in Annex A). Although recommendations generally fall outside the scope of alignment assessments of verification initiatives (see Box 1), the “encouraged criteria” are an exception. Criteria of verification initiatives can be rated as fully aligned with these “encouraged criteria” if their standards incorporate these aspirational elements. Encouraged criteria are only counted when fully aligned.

## Key scoping decisions included:

\- Cut-off date for documents: The OECD evaluated only documents that were adopted before June 2025; documents developed or revised after this date were out of scope (see Annex B). The assessment took into account selected minor changes made during the assessment period, which clarified relevant terms and converted recommendations into requirements.

\- RBC risk issues: FLA was assessed against the expectations in the OECD Garment Guidance as they relate to labour risks.

\- Supply chain segment: The Standard was assessed against a Tier 1 supply chain scope. Criteria related to supply chain due diligence were therefore interpreted and rated per FLA's Tier 1 supply chain scope. Two criteria exclusively relating to the supply chain beyond Tier 1 were out-scoped and marked “out of scope” in Annex A.

\- Own operations: The Standard's due diligence criteria apply to companies' own facilities. The Standard was therefore assessed against all criteria relating to own operations in the OECD Garment Guidance.

\- Due diligence steps: The Standard sets expectations that relate to all six steps of the due diligence framework and was therefore assessed against all steps.

Please refer to the section “Scoping and planning” in the OECD alignment assessment methodology (OECD, 2024[3]) for more information on scoping decisions.

This chapter presents the findings of the assessment. Findings are presented in a bar chart displaying the number of criteria found to be fully aligned, partially aligned or not aligned per step and calculation formulae. A number of strengths and areas for improvement are given in a narrative format to illustrate key findings. As the OECD conducted a standards-only assessment, only the overall rating for the Standards Assessment is given. For a full breakdown of ratings, see Annex A.

## Calculation formulae

The overall conclusion of the Standards Assessment is based on the following formula:

\- Fully aligned: (Overarching criteria $\varnothing = 100\%$ "fully aligned") + (Steps 1-6 + additional criteria $\varnothing = 80\%$ or more criteria "fully aligned") + (no "not aligned" criteria)

\- Partially aligned: All other combinations between “fully aligned” and “not aligned”

\- Not aligned: (Overarching criteria = less than 80% of criteria “partially aligned” or “fully aligned”) and/or (Steps 1-6 + additional criteria ∅ = 25% or more criteria “not aligned”)

## General strengths

\- The Standard includes expectations related to all six steps of the OECD Garment Guidance, with alignment across many important elements of the due diligence process, such as purchasing practices, senior-level accountability, engagement with workers, as well as root cause analysis to inform prevention and mitigation actions.

\- The principles and accreditation matrix are well-structured and provide a consistent degree of obligation. Additional guidance documents provide helpful guidance on the requirements.

\- As the pathway to accreditation is structured into five steps, companies are encouraged to progressively strengthen their systems before being assessed.

\- As manufacturers are eligible to apply for accreditation, the Standard promotes the uptake of due diligence among manufacturers.

## General opportunities for improvement

\- The Standard's focus on requiring companies to conduct due diligence on Tier 1 manufacturers and own facilities risks incentivising companies to focus their due diligence on certain tiers. As a result, companies may not focus on those tiers that are associated with the most significant (i.e. severe and likely) risks and impacts along their supply chain.

\- The documentation of processes that companies are required to conduct (e.g. risk scoping) often remains implicit rather than formally required.

\- While terminology does not need to exactly match the terms used in the OECD Garment Guidance, the Standard uses the term “remediation” to refer to preventative and mitigation actions under Step 3, rather than only for remediation per Step 6. Although a guidance document provides further clarification (FLA, 2026[10]), the interchangeable use of these terms risks misunderstandings among companies familiar with OECD due diligence standards.

\- While the standard and guidance documents are well structured, there are some inconsistencies between them which risk inconsistent interpretation and assessment in some cases.

Given that the majority of criteria were rated partially aligned, observations on strengths and opportunities for improvement are combined for each of the points below.

\- Preventative approach: The Standard emphasises preventive due diligence through addressing companies’ own contribution to adverse impacts, including purchasing practices, product development, corrective action plans and root cause analysis. It also emphasises gathering information on Tier 1 manufacturers’ preventative measures. The Standard places greater emphasis on the implementation of measures than on RBC policy commitments. It highlights verification, monitoring, integration of learnings (Step 4) and grievance mechanisms (Step 6).

\- Integrating RBC into decision making: The Standard emphasises the integration of RBC due diligence considerations into decision making (e.g. senior-level approval and oversight, responsibilities, resource allocation). Some limitations in the risk scoping exercise and the focus on Tier 1 manufacturers more broadly may prevent companies from effectively incorporating RBC due diligence into decision making, due to limited awareness of risks and impacts (see Step 2).

\- Dynamic approach: The Standard emphasises the importance of continuous improvement. It expects companies to seek to understand why certain impacts have not yet been prevented or mitigated and to respond appropriately. However, some elements remain static, with limited requirements to adjust efforts in response to changing contexts, the lack of effectiveness of measures, or predefined prevention and mitigation measures.

\- Prioritisation (risk-based approach): The Standard's approach to due diligence is not fully risk-based (e.g. static labour code instead of a policy commitment to conduct due diligence and prioritise the most significant risks). The Standard requires companies to identify high-risk Tier 1 manufacturers during risk scoping and to reassess them when serious violations occur in order to focus resources on higher-risk facilities. However, it does not consistently require consideration of likelihood and severity in decision-making, including when contexts are likely to have changed, as factors such as production capacity and broader country risks are also allowed to be considered.

\- Meaningful engagement with stakeholders: The Standard includes engagement with workers, unions and civil society, primarily through audits (e.g. worker interviews), training, the implementation of prevention and mitigation plans, grievance mechanisms as well as during disengagement. However, it does not anchor such engagement across other due diligence steps (e.g. policy, risk identification, measuring effectiveness, remedy) and does not further define the nature of engagement (“meaningful engagement”). It also places too great emphasis on companies’ engagement with civil society organisations rather than affected stakeholders, while noting there is a procedure for engaging with unions.

## Key strengths

\- The Standard has comprehensive requirements on companies' due diligence management systems, with clear assignment of responsibilities and internal co-ordination (e.g. between sourcing and compliance teams), senior-level accountability, staff training and communication (FLA, 2022, pp. 1.1.A-C; 3.2.A; 4.1[11]; 2022, pp. 3-4[1]). Senior leadership is required to formally commit to workplace standards and assign responsibility (FLA, 2022, p. 1.1.A.1; 1.1.C.1[11]).

\- The Standard requires companies to allocate resources and integrate due diligence considerations into decision making, including linking sourcing decisions to supplier performance and conducting pre-sourcing assessments (FLA, 2022, p. 1.1.A.3; 1.1.A.5; 4.2.B.1[11])

\- The Standard requires companies to commit to upholding core labour standards in owned facilities and Tier 1 manufacturers (FLA, 2022, p. 2[1]; 2022, pp. 2.1.A.7-2.1.A.8[11]).

## Key opportunities for improvement

\- While the Standard requires companies to commit to the FLA Fair Labor Code, $^{4}$ responsible purchasing practices and some elements on subcontracting, homeworkers and grievance mechanisms, it does not fully set out several RBC due diligence policy commitments (e.g. commitment to meaningful stakeholder engagement; hearing and addressing all complaints; definitions and expectations regarding subcontracting) (FLA, 2022, p. 2.3.B.9; 7.1.A.3; 1.1.B.21[11]).

\- The Standard requires companies to engage with stakeholders in practice (e.g. civil society strategy, procedure for engaging with unions). It neither requires companies to formally commit to engagement nor defines engagement in line with OECD due diligence standards. It also places too great emphasis on engagement with civil society organisations rather than affected stakeholders (FLA, 2022, p. 1.1.B.4[11]).

\- Even though companies are required to uphold the FLA Fair Labor Code, they are not required to develop and regularly update their own risk-based policies (FLA, 2022, p. 7.2.C.1[11]).

\- Documentation of company processes often remains implicit. The Standard does not require due diligence information management systems beyond the comprehensive requirements for supplier data (e.g. list of facilities, compliance and workplace information, including historic audit data, labour disputes, existence of collective bargaining agreements) which may limit the systematic tracking of due diligence over time (FLA, 2022, p. 6[1]).

Given variation in ratings within topics, observations are combined below:

\- The Standard requires companies to identify labour risks in their own operations and Tier 1 supply chain (“risk analysis”) considering selected risk factors (e.g. country risk, labour trends, known violations) (FLA, 2022, p. 5.2.B; 5.2.B.1[11]) and impacts of purchasing practices (FLA, 2022, p. 2.1.B.2[11]). However, as the Standard covers other risk factors only for potential corrective action (e.g. business model, product risks), its approach does not fully correspond with a systematic risk scoping exercise.

\- While companies are expected to prioritise high-risk Tier 1 manufacturers, prioritisation is not based on severity and likelihood and includes non-risk-based factors (e.g. production capacity), which may limit the effectiveness of identifying the most significant risks for in-depth assessments (FLA, 2022, p. 5.2.B.1[11]). For owned facilities, the Standard requires all sites to be assessed.

\- For prioritised suppliers and all owned production sites, the Standard requires companies to conduct assessments such as pre-qualification assessments, regular evaluation of labour impacts and supplier measures (e.g. grievance mechanisms, workers' awareness) (FLA, 2022, pp. 5.1.A; 5.3.A; 5.3.C.4–5.3.C.5[11]) and of policies and procedures, where impacts continued (FLA, 2022, p. 2.1.B.1[11]). It requires companies to adapt the frequency of assessments based on risk profile (FLA, 2022, p. 5.2.B.1[11]) but does not require assessments when contexts have likely changed.

\- Auditing is the primary assessment tool for company's own monitoring (FLA, 2022, p. 5.2.A.5[11]), which does not sufficiently encourage the use of complementary methods to gather information on manufacturing facilities (e.g. surveys, site visits, engagement). The Standard requires engagement with workers during the in-depth assessments (FLA, 2022, p. 5.3.A.1; 5.3.A.3; 6.1.B.3[11]) but not in designing assessment methodologies or adapting methodologies when audit findings do not correspond to expectations.

\- The Audit Field Observation tool specifies that companies have to ensure that the team conducting the supplier assessments have local knowledge and qualifications (FLA, 2026[12]). Companies have to address any shortcomings, for example by providing feedback to the auditor. FLA assesses auditor qualifications and behaviour through selected audit field observations.

\- The Standard requires companies to assess their involvement in identified impacts (i.e. cause, contribute, directly linked) with a focus on purchasing practices. It expects companies to ensure that other areas such as product development do not create impacts in practice but does not require them to assess how product development decisions may give rise to impacts in the first place.

## Key strengths

\- The Standard requires companies to implement a range of internal measures to prevent and mitigate labour risks and impacts with defined timelines (“remediation plans”), including responsible purchasing practices, pricing models that consider wage-related risks, supplier incentives and pre-sourcing assessments (FLA, 2022, pp. 2.1.A.7–2.1.A.8; 2.4.B; 2.2.A.5; 5.1[11]). It also promotes supplier engagement and support, particularly through training and collaboration on corrective actions (FLA, 2022, p. 4.4.A; 8.3.A.5[11]).

\- The Standard places a particularly strong emphasis on addressing companies' own contribution to labour impacts through purchasing practices and product development, requiring companies to align forecasting, costing, sourcing and material decisions with consideration for labour impacts and fair compensation. The inclusion of supplier feedback on purchasing practices strengthens this approach and reflects good practice in addressing buyer-driven risks (FLA, 2022, pp. 2.1.A.7–2.1.A.8; 2.3.C.1[11]).

\- The Standard requires companies to establish and implement their own prevention and mitigation policies and procedures for the Tier 1 supply chain that address how the company will work to prevent and mitigate labour impacts with defined timelines and evidence of implementation (“remediation plans” in the Standard). The nature of a “plan” remains implicit (FLA, 2022, p. 2.1.A.6[11]). A strong emphasis on root cause analysis and continuous improvement supports longer-term prevention and mitigation of risks and impacts (FLA, 2022, p. 8.1.A.2; 8.2.A.2; 8.2.B.1[11]). It also promotes worker involvement in the development and implementation of prevention and mitigation action plans (FLA, 2022, p. 5.3.A; 8.1.B.5[11]).

\- The Standard includes provisions on responsible exit and disengagement (“retrenchment” in the Standard), requiring companies to consider alternatives first (“last resort”). If disengagement is the last resort, companies are required to consider impacts on workers and ensure legally owed benefits are respected (FLA, 2022, pp. 2.1.A.11; 5.4.B.1-9[11]; 2023, p. 14[13]). However, communicating the decision of disengagement to factory management and to union representatives remains a recommendation in the Standard and is set out in the corresponding guidance document. This risks increasing the likelihood of impacts on workers (e.g. payment of wages) due to short-notice changes.

## Key opportunities for improvement

\- The Standard includes some expectations related to taking immediate action to stop existing impacts through the 24-hour alert finding policy, requiring Tier 1 manufacturers to submit remediation plans shortly after identifying critical issues (FLA, 2020[14]). However, it focuses on the submission of plans by manufacturers rather than the actions of companies taken to stop causing or contributing to critical danger.

\- The Standard does not require company measures to prevent and mitigate impacts in their own operations and Tier 1 supply chain to be proportionate to the severity of the impact, nor does it require expert inputs where risks are severe or technically complex.

\- The use of leverage to prevent or mitigate impacts remains implicit, requiring examples of actions the company has taken in the case that the facility has not made progress. The Standard does not require companies to pool leverage with other companies.

\- While terminology does not need to exactly match the terms used in the OECD Garment Guidance, provided underlying concepts and intent are equivalent, the Standard uses the term “remediation plan” for future preventative and mitigation actions per Step 3 and remediation per Step 6. Although a guidance document provides further clarification (FLA, 2026[10]), the interchangeable use of these terms risks misunderstandings among companies.

Given the number of partially aligned criteria, observations on strengths and opportunities for improvement are combined below.

\- The Standard requires companies to verify corrective actions of manufacturers through audits and corrective action follow-up processes, including root cause analysis and review of recurring non-compliances to support continuous improvement (FLA, 2022, p. 5.3.C.5; 8.1.A; 8.2.A; 8.3.B.2[11]). However, this validation is not risk-based as it focusses on measuring the effectiveness of training and purchasing practices rather than the range of due diligence measures that may have been agreed upon under step 3 prevention and mitigation measures.

## Key strengths

\- The Standard requires companies to publicly report on processes that provide access to remediation, including all reported grievances and company-provided responses to grievances (FLA, 2022, pp. 1.1.B.20–1.1.B.22[11]).

\- The Standard requires companies to show how they engage with union and/or worker representative structures in their owned manufacturing facilities.

\- The Standard includes safeguards related to confidentiality and protection from retaliation in specific contexts (e.g. worker interviews and grievance mechanisms) (FLA, 2022, p. 5.3.A.3; 6.1.C.2[11]), while a comprehensive requirement to ensure that all external communications do not pose risks to affected stakeholders remains implicit.

\- The Standard requires companies to publish an annual sustainability report that reports on progress in preventing and mitigating labour impacts; however, some key aspects of what the reporting has to include (e.g. due diligence management systems, engagement with affected stakeholders, most significant risks, prioritisation rationale, effectiveness of measures) are only detailed in a guidance document (FLA, 2026[15]).

## Key opportunities for improvement

\- The Standard does not require companies to ensure that public communication, nor communication with affected stakeholders, is accessible and tailored to impacted stakeholders, nor does it require that communication is sufficient to allow stakeholders to evaluate the adequacy of company responses to impacts.

\- The Standard does not require companies to formally report or communicate to stakeholders on how they have addressed severe human rights risks, except for written responses to labour issues raised by civil society organisations.

## Key strengths

\- The Standard requires companies to provide for or co-operate in an appropriate remedy for impacts it has caused or contributed to, and expects companies to establish a grievance mechanism for their own operations as well as a back-up channel should the operational-level mechanism for Tier 1 manufacturers fail (FLA, 2022, p. 6.2.A.5[11]).

\- For its own operations grievance mechanism, the Standard references all United Nations Guiding Principles (UNGP) effectiveness criteria, but some are not fully operationalised (i.e. accessibility, predictability, equitability) (FLA, 2022, p. 6.1.A.11; 6.2.A2; 5.3.A.1; 6.1.C.2; 6.1.F.2[11]).

\- The Standard requires companies to publish grievances and responses (FLA, 2022, p. 1.1.B.22[11]).

\- FLA's own complaint mechanism includes key features aligned with UNGP criteria (e.g. legitimacy, predictability, transparency, accessibility), including being accessible on the initiative's website in multiple languages.

## Key opportunities for improvement

\- The Standard only specifies details on companies' obligation to provide for or co-operate in the remediation of impacts that they have caused or contributed to only in a guidance document (see also terminology discussion on “remediation” in Step 3).

\- The effectiveness criteria for grievance mechanisms in own operations are not equally applied in the section on the back-up grievance mechanism. The mechanism is not required to cover the criteria on equitability, transparency or being dialogue-based. The other effectiveness criteria are not fully operationalised (i.e. legitimacy, accessibility, predictability).

\- The Standard does not require companies to incorporate lessons learned into policies and due diligence systems, nor to use grievance data as a core input for continuous improvement.

\- FLA's grievance mechanism does not fully operationalise the criteria on equitability and being dialogue-based. For example, if FLA agrees with the company's assessment of a complaint, then the complainant is informed in writing and given only a short period to reply, while two-way dialogue remains optional. FLA publishes complaints received through the own channel.

## Key strengths

\- The Standard requires companies to collaborate with other enterprises, including for shared assessments (FLA, 2022, p. 1.1.B[11]; 2022, p. 9[17]); and scaling up corrective actions.

## Key opportunities for improvement

\- The Standard does not require companies to, where appropriate, collaborate with enterprises, trade unions, civil society or other stakeholders to pool leverage or provide remedy.

\- While the Standard enables some degree of information sharing and transparency (e.g. through published assessments and supplier data), it does not explicitly require collaboration to increase sector-wide transparency (FLA, 2022, p. 8.1.C.1[11]; 2022, p. 9[17]).

\- The Standard does not require companies that participate in collaborative initiatives to understand their role in facilitating due diligence components or to accurately represent the scope and results of these initiatives in their public communications.

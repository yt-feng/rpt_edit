## Abstract

This paper examines the conditions under which renminbi invoicing has expanded despite the continued dominance of the United States (US) dollar in international trade. Using IMF trade invoicing data for 70 economies from 2016 to 2023, as reported by Boz et al. (2025), we show that RMB invoicing is systematically shaped by trade dependence on the People's Republic of China (PRC), exchange rate regimes, capital and foreign exchange controls, settlement infrastructure, and the strength of political and institutional ties with the PRC. While dollar-oriented exchange rate regimes and capital liberalization tend to restrict use of the renminbi, renminbi invoicing is expanding in economies with remaining controls and deeper integration into PRC-led financial, logistical, and digital networks, indicating that RMB internationalization is a gradual, complementary process rather than a substitute for the dollar.

Keywords: Dominant Currency Paradigm, internationalization of the renminbi, invoice currency, foreign exchange controls, logistics

JEL Classification: F23, F31, F33

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Since the late 2010s, the environment surrounding currency choice in international trade and cross-border investment has been undergoing a major transformation. Although there has been no major change in the United States (US) dollar's dominant position in global trade, movements to reevaluate excessive reliance on the US dollar have become apparent in various countries against the backdrop of rising geopolitical and political-economic risks, such as the prolonged conflict between the US and the People's Republic of China (PRC), the normalization of financial sanctions triggered by the Russian invasion of Ukraine, and the recent resurgence of trade friction through tariff policies. Policy initiatives aimed at promoting so-called “local currency transactions”—where trade and investment are conducted either in the domestic currency or in that of the counterparty—are being advanced, primarily in emerging economies and Global South countries.

Rather than being seen as a radical challenge to the existing international monetary system, these moves should be understood through the lens of practical risk management and economic security. While US dollar invoicing still holds an overwhelming advantage in terms of liquidity, inertia, and network effects, it is also susceptible to political influence through sanctions and financial regulations. Particularly for countries that have faced the risk of restricted access to international payment infrastructure and major banking networks, currency diversification is increasingly seen not merely as a cost-cutting measure but as an institutional option for maintaining stable external transactions. From a geopolitical perspective, countries located far from the United States (most of which are emerging markets or developing nations) tend to use the US dollar as their invoicing currency and remain dependent on it, though data indicate that this dependence is decreasing in some major economies. Since 2021, the correlation between the use of invoicing currencies and geopolitical distance from the currency's issuing country has trended downward, suggesting that geopolitical fragmentation is progressing. Since the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the influence of geopolitical distance on the choice of invoicing currency has become pronounced. In countries far from the US and the eurozone, the use of the dollar and the euro has declined, with greater reliance on the renminbi, local currencies, and other third-country currencies. Conversely, in countries that have become more distant from the PRC, there has been a tendency to reduce the use of domestic and third-country currencies in favor of the US dollar. $^{1}$

Previous research, primarily conducted by the IMF, has empirically demonstrated the phenomenon known as the “Dominant Currency Paradigm” (DCP), in which the dominant position of the US dollar exerts a strong influence on corporate pricing and the adjustment of trade volumes. In recent years, however, attention has focused on the fact that, even as the DCP persists, the use of nondollar currencies is expanding in certain regions and sectors. The PRC’s efforts to internationalize the renminbi have steadily expanded its use in trade settlements and certain capital transactions through government-led institutional reforms, such as currency swap agreements, the establishment of clearing banks, and the development of the Cross-border Interbank Payment System (CIPS). Furthermore, in ASEAN countries, alongside the expansion of intraregional trade, the use of regional currencies is progressing through frameworks such as the Local Currency Settlement Framework (LCSF). In specific countries and regions, although their share in actual trade statistics remains small, the presence of currencies other than the US dollar is becoming clear.

Furthermore, as the world's largest energy importer, the PRC has promoted the use of the renminbi in transactions with resource-exporting countries, with an increasing number of renminbi settlements in oil and gas imports from suppliers such as the Russian Federation and Saudi Arabia. Such a shift in currency choice for resource and energy transactions—driven by developments such as the Russian invasion of Ukraine and the expansion of bilateral settlements in non-US dollar currencies—indicates that dedollarization is increasingly supported by transaction-based demand, particularly among emerging economies. The growth of local-currency invoicing, especially for import payments, underscores the importance of exchange rate risk allocation between exporters and importers and highlights the role of domestic factors, including exchange control regimes, financial market development, and external balance sheet structures, in shaping currency choice.

The purpose of this paper is to conduct an empirical analysis of the conditions under which renminbi-invoiced transactions expand, based on factors derived from a detailed examination of published statistics and institutional frameworks across various countries, primarily in Asia, as conducted by Tomizawa and Shimizu (2026). Tomizawa and Shimizu (2026), based on data published by governments and central banks in countries other than the PRC, noted that transactions denominated in local currencies and the renminbi have been increasing in countries such as Thailand and Brazil. They highlighted geopolitical factors that had not previously been identified in existing research as drivers of local currency use, the implementation of policies promoting such use, technological factors such as the movement of people and the spread of digital payments, and the construction of payment infrastructure—all of which may contribute to the expansion of renminbi usage. The contribution of this study lies in the fact that, by cross-referencing the mechanisms promoting local currency transactions and the results of factor analysis revealed through the analysis of local currency-denominated transactions in various countries and regions, we extracted new explanatory variable data from various statistics and publicly available information, constructed a dataset, and identified a new model using various “PRC policy factor” variables—which had not been employed in previous studies—as explanatory variables.

Furthermore, the finding of significance for many of these variables constitutes a key contribution of this study. Specifically, using the share of renminbi-denominated invoices in each country's exports, as published by Boz et al. (2025, IMF) as the dependent variable, and by attempting to quantify not only the factors indicating the internationalization of the renminbi used in previous studies but also new factors, we examine the roles played by factors such as currency and capital controls, financial infrastructure, and the formation of regional economic blocks. Through this, we aim to present a perspective that views the impact of recent changes in the global situation on the international monetary system not as a simple binary opposition of “dedollarization” but as a more multilayered, gradual structural change.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section 2, we summarize the key independent and dependent variables used in empirical analyses of currency choice and invoice currency in major prior studies, as well as the findings obtained. Next, in Section 3, we analyze phenomena that have not previously been identified as factors promoting local currency usage—specifically, those driving the increasing use of local currencies other than the US dollar—and attempt to quantify them as explanatory variables. Then, in Section 4, we conduct an empirical analysis using the share of the renminbi as the invoice currency in each country's exports and imports to the rest of the world as the dependent variable, and the new explanatory variables discussed in Section 2. Finally, in Section 5, we summarize our conclusions and future research directions in discussing the results.

## 2. RECENT RESEARCH ON INVOICE CURRENCY CHOICE

This section summarizes the main findings of recent research on currency choice and invoice currencies, including the Chinese renminbi, as well as the explanatory variables used in these analyses.

Boz et al. (2025, IMF) analyze invoicing–currency patterns in global trade and the impact of geopolitical factors (Table 1). Specifically, they conduct an empirical analysis using the share of invoicing currencies (USD, EUR, RMB) in each country's exports as the dependent variable, and the share of exports to the issuing countries of those currencies (the United States, the eurozone, and the PRC) as independent variables. Their main findings indicate that a limited number of currencies, including the US dollar and the euro, continue to dominate as invoicing currencies in global trade, and their shares remain stable even in the face of geopolitical factors. On the other hand, while the use of the renminbi has been steadily increasing since the early 2010s and expanding beyond Asia, its share remains relatively small.

Furthermore, from a geopolitical perspective, countries located far from the United States (most of which are emerging markets or developing countries) tend to use the US dollar as the invoicing currency and remain dependent on it, while the study indicates that this dependence is decreasing in some major economies. Since 2021, the correlation between the use of invoicing currencies and the geopolitical distance from the currency's issuing country has trended downward, suggesting that geopolitical fragmentation is progressing. In countries geographically distant from the United States and the eurozone, the use of the US dollar and the euro has declined, with a shift toward the Chinese yuan, domestic currencies, and third-country currencies. Conversely, countries that have become more distant from the PRC have tended to reduce their use of domestic and third-country currencies in favor of the US dollar.

In addition, the paper analyzes the relationship between the share of exports of resources and primary commodities. While commodities such as oil are still frequently traded in US dollars, and although transactions in renminbi and other currencies have increased recently, the paper states that no systematic evidence has been found to substantiate the effectiveness of policy initiatives aimed at reducing dependence on the US dollar in oil exports.

The paper provides a dataset covering 132 countries from 1990 to 2023, showing the share of invoicing currencies for exports and imports, specifically the US dollar, the euro, the renminbi, and other currencies (such as domestic currencies not listed above).

Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023, IMF) analyze the use of the renminbi as an international currency, regional patterns, and the role of local-currency swaps and offshore renminbi clearing banks, both of which are implemented by the People's Bank of China. They note that while the global use of the renminbi is still in its early stages, accounting for only 2% of global settlements, there are significant regional differences in usage, with its use expanding particularly in Asia and certain emerging market economies (such as Mongolia, the Lao People's Democratic Republic, Chile, Türkiye, and Argentina). Furthermore, their empirical analysis confirms that local-currency swaps and renminbi clearing banks are effective in promoting the use of the renminbi in international settlements. Furthermore, the study noted that political distance (measured using the correlation of countries' voting patterns at the UN General Assembly to gauge political distance from the PRC) and geographical distance are also key factors influencing the use of the renminbi. In particular, it indicated that the renminbi is spreading across Asia and that its role as a regional currency is likely to expand further.

Georgiadis et al. (2021, ECB) noted that approximately 40% of global trade is denominated in US dollars, far exceeding the US's share of global trade (approximately 10%). They demonstrated that strategic complementarity (the incentive to price in the same currency as competitors) and integration into global value chains (GVCs) reinforce the use of the dollar, concluding that the dollar holds a dominant position as a “reserve currency” even in transactions with third countries.

On the other hand, with regard to the euro, the report noted that while euro-denominated transactions are common within, and in trade with, Europe, the euro plays a weaker role as a “vehicle currency” in transactions with third countries than the dollar, and that policy support from European authorities has been limited. In contrast, while the PRC’s expanding trade has strengthened the US dollar’s dominance and relatively diminished the euro’s status, the authors noted that the People’s Bank of China’s currency swap agreements are promoting renminbi-denominated transactions and that the renminbi’s internationalization relies more on policy than on fundamentals. The report demonstrated that the existence of currency swap agreements makes it easier for overseas financial institutions to raise funds and extend loans in renminbi, and for importers and exporters to choose renminbi for settlement. Furthermore, the agreements mitigate the risk of renminbi liquidity shortages, lower the barrier to companies selecting the renminbi as an invoicing currency, and increase the number of first-time users through network effects. Furthermore, the authors confirmed a trend of increasing renminbi-denominated trade invoices in countries that have signed swap agreements, as they showed that the greater a country’s trade share (dependence) with the PRC, the more renminbi invoices rise, with this trend being particularly strong for imports. They also pointed out that while the increase in renminbi invoices reduces the use of the US dollar and the euro, the impact on the US dollar is particularly significant.

Furthermore, Ito and Chinn (2014), who focus on the rise of the renminbi in the 2010s and summarize its impact on RMB usage, analyzed factors such as the level of financial market development and capital account liberalization (KAOPEN index) that influence the choice of invoicing currency in international trade. In other prior research, Novy (2006) argued that firms' choice of invoicing currency is influenced by their intention to hedge costs, exchange rate volatility, and currency correlations. Furthermore, with respect to trade involving the United States, he explained that the US dollar is overwhelmingly used as the invoicing currency because raw materials such as oil are priced in US dollars, leading firms to tend to choose the US dollar to hedge their costs. Using a dataset spanning 24 countries and ten currencies, the model was empirically tested, confirming that currency correlations play a significant role in the selection of vehicle currencies such as the US dollar and the euro.

Amiti, Itskhoki, and Konings (2022) analyzed the determinants of currency choice in exports and imports, as well as the impact of these choices on exchange rates, prices, and quantities, using data on currency selection by Belgian firms. Currency choice is an active decision-making process at the firm level and exhibits temporal persistence. Small firms with low import dependence tend to set prices in euros (the producer's currency), and exchange rate fluctuations are fully reflected in their prices. Conversely, large firms with high import dependence tend to set export prices in foreign currencies (particularly the US dollar) and are less susceptible to fluctuations in the euro exchange rate. The choice of import currency influences the selection of export currency; specifically, firms that import in US dollars tend to set export prices in US dollars as well. The impact of exchange rates on prices is determined not only by factors that affect flexible pricing—such as a firm's import dependence and market share—but also by price rigidity stemming from currency choice. For firms that set prices in foreign currencies, exchange rate fluctuations are not fully reflected in prices due to price rigidity. The impact of price rigidity persists for over a year, gradually diminishing over time. Furthermore, exchange rate fluctuations affect not only prices but also export volumes. While the elasticity of volume with respect to price changes is small in the short term, it becomes significant after one year, suggesting that volume adjustments take time.

Brüggen, Georgiadis, and Mehl (2025, ECB) note that renminbi-denominated transactions have risen sharply since 2015, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic, escalating geopolitical tensions, and the emergence of US–PRC tensions since 2020. The increase in renminbi-denominated invoices is driven by factors such as the Middle East (energy from Saudi Arabia and the UAE), Africa (resources and investment), and East Asia (regional supply chains). They also estimated how the share of invoice currencies (USD/EUR/RMB) used in imports and exports is influenced by changes in geopolitical distance, trade volume, and bloc formation, showing that as geopolitical distance increases, the use of the US dollar and the euro tends to decrease, while that of the renminbi tends to rise. Furthermore, in terms of trade blocks (trade block share), the study found that as the proportion of trade within a specific block—such as the EU or the PRC—increases, the use of that block's currency (EUR or CNY) tends to rise. On the other hand, the results indicated that invoice currency selection is influenced more by political, institutional, and strategic factors than by price fluctuations, and that the renminbi is expanding primarily in countries geographically close to the PRC within these bloc-based structures.

The following three points are commonly identified in the above prior research as recent characteristics of invoice currency selection. First, in international trade, invoice currencies are highly concentrated in a small number of major currencies, such as the US dollar and the euro, and it has been repeatedly demonstrated that these currencies possess strong dominance and stickiness. Despite the declining shares of the United States and the eurozone in global trade, the shares of the US dollar and the euro as invoice currencies have remained stable or have even increased, indicating strong inertia in invoice currency selection. Firms have an incentive to price in the same currency as their competitors and supply chain practices, and network effects, through global value chains and strategic competitive complementarity, reinforce and entrench currency selection in a self-perpetuating manner. As a result, the US dollar autonomously maintains its status as the primary vehicle currency. Furthermore, dollar-denominated transactions remain central in major commodity trades such as oil; while instances of transactions using the renminbi and other currencies are increasing, they have not yet led to a significant shift in the global share of invoicing currencies.

Second, the expansion of renminbi usage in international transactions since the 2010s has been steady but limited, a trend that is particularly pronounced in Asia and some emerging economies, including in its share of invoicing currency. Nevertheless, the expansion of RMB-invoiced swap agreements and the development of clearing banks have lowered the institutional and transaction cost thresholds for RMB usage through the formation of settlement networks and the provision of liquidity, significantly boosting its use as an invoicing currency. In recent years, amid rising geopolitical tensions and supply chain restructuring, the use of the renminbi for invoicing has expanded as economic ties with the PRC have strengthened in regions such as the Middle East, Africa, and East Asia.

Third, the choice of invoicing currency is determined by both macroeconomic factors and corporate behavior. The choice of currency is an active, sustained decision-making process for firms, and high import dependence and large firm size strengthen the preference for key-currency-invoiced transactions, such as the US dollar. Furthermore, differences in invoicing currencies affect the real economy through exchange rate pass-through and volume adjustments; it has been shown that countries with a high proportion of dollar-denominated transactions exhibit greater sensitivity in import prices and trade volumes to fluctuations in the dollar exchange rate. Recent empirical studies on the Chinese renminbi have found that geopolitical factors, such as geographic and political distance, also influence currency choice. Geographical and political distance from the PRC affects the use of the yuan, while distance from the United States and Europe affects the use of the US dollar and the euro, respectively. It has been confirmed that as the share of intra-block trade increases, the currency representing that block tends to be used more frequently as the invoicing currency.

## 3. EXAMINATION AND ANALYSIS OF EXPLANATORY VARIABLES RELATED TO INVOICE CURRENCY CHOICE

In this section, building on prior research and the factors promoting the use of local currencies identified by Tomizawa and Shimizu (2026), we will digitize and examine the explanatory variables for promoting renminbi-invoiced transactions.

## 3.1 Exchange Rates and Their Institutional Factors

In previous studies, exchange rates have been viewed not as factors that directly influence invoice currency choice but rather as factors that exert an indirect influence through the allocation of foreign exchange risk, price adjustment mechanisms, and the institutional environment. Novy (2006) demonstrates that firms select invoice currencies to hedge against cost fluctuations caused by exchange rate movements, arguing that exchange rate volatility and intercurrency correlations—rather than the level of the exchange rate itself—are key factors driving the preference for the US dollar as a vehicle currency. Furthermore, Boz et al. (2020) demonstrated that the choice of invoicing currency significantly influences the pass-through of exchange rate fluctuations to prices and trade volumes, revealing that countries with a higher proportion of dollar-denominated invoices exhibit greater sensitivity in import prices and trade volumes to dollar exchange rate fluctuations. This highlights the importance of the reverse causal relationship, in which exchange rates affect the real economy through currency choice. Recent research has placed greater emphasis on the impact of exchange rate regimes on invoice currency choice rather than on exchange rate fluctuations themselves. Boz et al. (2025) demonstrated that when a destination country is pegged to a specific currency, firms have a stronger incentive to invoice in that currency, noting that strategic complementarity through the exchange rate regime self-reinforces currency choice. Previous research on the renminbi also suggests that while an exchange rate regime anchored to the US dollar inhibits renminbi-denominated invoicing, the development of institutional and financial infrastructure plays a crucial role in expanding the use of nondollar currencies.

In this paper, we include the exchange rate as one of the explanatory variables, using it as the rate of change of a trading partner's currency against the renminbi. In terms of exchange rates, there are several patterns in prior research, such as using logarithms or volatility, or incorporating bilateral exchange rates between the countries in question or exchange rates against the US dollar. For example, Boz et al. (2025) use the logarithm of the exchange rate against the dollar as an explanatory variable to account for the possibility that the share of invoice currencies is influenced by exchange rate changes. In addition to these accounting issues, previous studies have found that exchange rate volatility and inflation rate differences are important determinants of invoice currency choice. Ito and Chinn (2014) include exchange rate volatility as an explanatory variable, because when currency values fluctuate frequently or significantly, transaction costs rise due to an increase in the exchange rate risk premium; consequently, firms and investors tend to avoid using that currency, preferring more stable “hard currencies,” which leads to a tendency to curb the use of the country’s own currency for trade invoicing and settlement. On the other hand, Shimizu et al. (2024) conducted an empirical analysis using Japanese customs data on the yen-denominated share in bilateral trade between Japan and its trading partners, and their results indicate that a weaker yen reduces the yen-denominated share, suggesting that exchange rates influence the choice of invoice currency through various channels.

Since the aforementioned Chinese data indicate that the renminbi-denominated share expanded as the renminbi appreciated, this paper will use the rate of change in the exchange rate between the trading partner's currency and the renminbi for the renminbi-denominated share under consideration. This approach is aimed at verifying whether market expectations of a stronger renminbi may increase the incentive for firms in other countries to use the renminbi. Another reason for using rates of change rather than logarithmic values is that, since this analysis uses the renminbi-denominated share of 70 countries—including emerging economies—from 2015 to 2023 as a sample, we considered it more appropriate to use rates of change to verify the impact of renminbi appreciation and depreciation on the renminbi-denominated share, thereby eliminating the influence of the absolute magnitude of the exchange rate itself.

With respect to exchange rate regimes, this study includes an anchor currency dummy variable. Ilzetzki, Reinhart, and Rogoff (2019) define an anchor or reference currency as a major currency that countries effectively use as a benchmark for exchange rate stability. In other words, an anchor currency is a foreign currency that a country uses as a benchmark (anchor) to stabilize the value of its own currency, and it is the currency at the center of that country's exchange rate policy. Countries that adopt an anchor currency maintain a policy-driven link to that currency to enhance exchange rate stability and the predictability of trade transactions; consequently, firms can mitigate exchange rate risk by using that currency for their trade transactions. In Boz et al. (2025), the share of exports to countries pegged to a given currency is used as a measure of strategic complementarities. The study shows that when a large proportion of a firm's export destinations are pegged to a specific currency (USD/EUR/RMB), the firm has a stronger incentive to invoice in that currency. In this study, following the approach of Boz et al. (2025), we used the revised dataset (2021) from Ilzetzki, Reinhart, and Rogoff (2019) to create a dummy variable indicating whether a country uses the US dollar, the euro, or a basket as its anchor currency. For example, since Malaysia changed its anchor currency from the US dollar to a basket of the US dollar and the euro in January 2017, the US dollar dummy variable is set to "1" for 2016 and "0" for 2017 and subsequent years. Although this dataset reclassifies the exchange rate regimes and anchor currencies for 194 countries and regions since 1946, since data are only available up to 2019, for the 70 countries included in this empirical analysis, we extended the exchange rate anchor classifications from before 2019 after confirming that there were no changes in the classified anchor currencies in the IMF AREAER (2020–2023 editions) for 2020 and beyond.

According to the classification by Ilzetzki, Reinhart, and Rogoff (2019), as of 2021, 103 countries used the US dollar as their anchor currency, 55 countries employed the euro, and 12 countries utilized a currency basket. No country uses the Chinese renminbi as its anchor currency; even in the case of Mongolia, which has a high degree of dependence on the renminbi, the classification designates the US dollar as the anchor currency rather than a peg to the renminbi. A breakdown of anchor currencies by region is shown in Table 5, with the US dollar being the primary anchor in Asia, North America, and Latin America. As for the euro, it is widely adopted not only in Europe but also in Africa.

## 3.2 Examination of Capital Market Factors

In this paper, we examine several original variables that may influence the choice of the Chinese renminbi as an invoice currency, in addition to macroeconomic variables. The first is capital market factors. We consider using the number of capital controls each country has introduced and maintains as a variable.

Ito and Chinn (2014) analyzed the impact of capital account liberalization (KAOPEN index) on invoicing currency choice and found that countries with open capital accounts tend to invoice in euros or their own currency. The KAOPEN index is a composite index of four dummy variables (0/1) based on the classification in the IMF's Annual Report on Exchange Arrangements and Exchange Restrictions (AREAER), and the index is constructed using principal component analysis (PCA) of these dummies. According to Chinn and Ito (2006), the four dummy variables used are based on the presence or absence of the following AREAER categories: (1) restrictions on capital transactions; (2) restrictions on the current account; (3) the existence of multiple exchange rates for capital transactions; and (4) mandatory remittance of export proceeds. The value of the first principal component is used as the KAOPEN Index, with higher values indicating greater capital account liberalization.

Data for the KAOPEN index, known as the “Chinn–Ito Index,” are publicly available for 181 countries covering the period from 1970 to 2023. For example, a value of 2.28 indicates that restrictions on capital transactions, etc., are loose (or virtually nonexistent) in that country, while a value of -1.94 indicates the strictest level of regulation (Table 6). For example, while countries such as Japan, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Germany show that regulations are virtually nonexistent, many emerging economies such as Brazil and the PRC exhibit negative values, suggesting that significant regulations are in place.

In practice, there are examples where restrictions on investment eligibility for residents and nonresidents in securities investments have been gradually eased, or where regulations have been relaxed in specific areas such as lending and debt guarantees.

The extent to which such regulations exist indicates the degree of freedom with which a country's currency, or the currency of the counterparty, can be used in international transactions. If such transactions are not permitted, it becomes difficult for companies located in that country to conduct trade and capital transactions denominated in renminbi or their own currency. This study counts the five currencies and foreign exchange regulations mentioned above—which are considered most likely to directly influence the choice of invoice currency—and uses them as variables. Specifically, for these five items, we checked the presence or absence of regulations for each country as listed in AREAER, counted them (1 for “regulated,” 0 for “unregulated”), and used this as a variable for currency and foreign exchange regulations (minimum 0 to maximum 5, where a score of 5 indicates the strictest currency and foreign exchange regulations).

Therefore, this study focuses on capturing and counting the overseas expansion and activities of Chinese-owned financial institutions. As an example, as cited in the CEPR (2025) article, in 2023, Argentina faced a severe dollar shortage, and as its foreign exchange reserves were depleted, there was a rapid expansion of RMB-invoiced settlements. It has also been noted that, amid a severe dollar shortage, companies found it difficult to procure dollars, making RMB settlements a practical alternative; furthermore, companies that had been transacting with banks with high ratios of USD-denominated loans were slower to switch to the renminbi. Partly due to strong government support for renminbi settlement, companies that already had business relationships with the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC) $^{5}$ reportedly had a significantly higher proportion of renminbi-denominated transactions than other companies. On the other hand, following the change in government in November 2023, the use of the renminbi plummeted amid improved dollar supply and changes in exchange rate policy, falling to less than 10% as of March 2024. This example demonstrates that if the institutional foundation of Chinese banks' overseas networks is established and bilateral relations progress smoothly, the internationalization of the renminbi can proceed in accordance with surrounding conditions. Behind this development, factors such as deregulation allowing local residents (domestic corporations) and others to use the renminbi as a trading currency, the formation of offshore renminbi and interbank markets centered on clearing banks capable of procuring renminbi locally, and the existence of local currency swap agreements between the renminbi and the partner country's currency—which underpin these markets—are also crucial. $^{6}$

Commercial banks' entry into various countries takes the form of local subsidiaries, branches of their Chinese headquarters, or representative offices. A key characteristic is that local subsidiaries offer the greatest flexibility in branch operations, whereas branches may face restrictions on the scope of business activities permitted by authorities (for example, they may be allowed only to extend foreign-currency loans in the host country, but not local-currency loans or retail deposit services). In the case of Argentina mentioned above, since the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC) entered the market in 2013, the period from 2015 to 2018 was scored as “3”; and since a Bank of China branch was established in 2019, “2” was added, resulting in a total count of “5” for the period from 2019 to 2023.

## 3.4 Other PRC Government-Led Policies: Number of Visits by PRC Government Officials and Their Counterparts, and Other Related Policies

In prior research, Boz et al. (2025, IMF) and Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023, IMF) used voting behavior at the UN General Assembly as an indicator of political proximity to the currency issuer. They demonstrated that a greater political distance (i.e., lower political proximity) is negatively correlated with the use of that country's currency. In this paper, rather than using variables indicating geopolitical distance or relations with the PRC, we used PRC-led policies aimed at strengthening relations with the PRC as explanatory variables to promote the use of the renminbi.

## (1) Number of visits by PRC government officials and their counterparts

In this paper, as candidate variables indicating such geopolitical distance or relationships, we first considered whether a country is a member of Global South groupings such as BRICS and the SCO. In fact, within these groups, measures such as the use of local currencies in transactions among member states and the development of independent settlement infrastructures are being considered to promote transactions denominated in local (domestic) currencies; thus, a certain correlation with the choice of invoicing currency is assumed. However, these groups are still in the developmental stage, have few participating countries, and show limited overlap with the countries in this panel dataset, raising concerns over an insufficient sample size.

Another variable to consider is whether a country participates in the PRC's Belt and Road Initiative. To increase RMB-invoiced transactions, the PRC government facilitates foreign exchange trading between the renminbi and 17 “Belt and Road currencies” on platforms such as the CFETS direct exchange market (Report on the Internationalization of the Renminbi 2024); however, the specific currencies are not identified in the report. The report also notes that, as of the end of 2023, 68 financial institutions from Belt and Road participating countries had entered the PRC’s interbank foreign exchange market.

According to the above report (2023), the value of cross-border renminbi payments between the PRC and countries jointly building the Belt and Road Initiative reached 7.1 trillion yuan (up 4.4% year-on-year), accounting for 16.9% of the total value of cross-border renminbi payments between the PRC and the rest of the world. Of this total, payments related to goods trade amounted to 2.3 trillion yuan (up 75.7% year-on-year), those related to services trade amounted to 230.75 billion yuan (up 35.4% year-on-year), and payments related to direct investment amounted to 780.793 billion yuan (up 19% year-on-year), all showing strong growth. Furthermore, as of September 2023, local currency swap agreements have been concluded with 30 countries jointly building the “Belt and Road” (with agreements in place with 40 countries and regions worldwide at that time), and RMB clearing banks have been established in 17 countries participating in the joint construction of the “Belt and Road” (with 31 banks designated across 29 countries and regions worldwide at that time).

Furthermore, on the “Belt and Road Network” website, $^{10}$ which the PRC government uses to promote Belt and Road construction, the number of countries for which Belt and Road-related activities and investment information are provided stands at 156 $^{11}$ (as of 18 February 2026). However, this figure includes countries that have already announced their intention to withdraw from the Belt and Road Initiative (such as Italy in 2023 and Panama in 2025). According to data provided by the Green Finance & Development Center at Fudan University’s Fanhai International School of Finance (FISF), between 146 and 150 countries have signed MOUs with the PRC regarding Belt and Road cooperation, and a specific list has been published; however, the report also notes that there is uncertainty regarding this list. On the other hand, the China Foreign Investment Statistical Bulletin (2018) compiles figures on foreign direct investment (FDI) into the PRC from 62 “Belt and Road countries” as part of a trend analysis of FDI from these nations. However, since some other information sites suggest that the number of Belt and Road countries is approximately 130, it is difficult to determine the definition of participating countries or the accuracy of the figures.

Taking this situation into account, we use the number of leadership-level exchanges between the PRC and each country as a variable representing political proximity, rather than focusing on membership or participation in multilateral organizations. Specifically, as part of the internationalization of the renminbi, we counted the number of overseas visits made by the President of the PRC or the Premier of the State Council since 2010, when renminbi settlements effectively began (note that for President Xi Jinping, visits made after 2010 while he was Vice President [March 2008 – March 2013] were also included in the counts for visits by the President and the Premier). Conversely, for visits to the PRC by leaders from partner countries, we similarly limited the count to exchanges at the presidential or prime ministerial level (extracting visit data from various website reports as of the end of 2025). $^{12}$ For example, if the president of Country A visited the PRC in 2010, 2015, and 2017, the score would be “2” for 2016 and “3” for 2017 and beyond. In this empirical model, the total number of visits between the PRC and the partner country was used as a variable. Visits made to attend international conferences were also included in the visit count, as bilateral meetings often take place on the sidelines of such events.

In actual diplomacy, foreign ministers generally lay the groundwork prior to heads-of-state visits, followed by summit-level diplomacy. Seizing opportunities for exchanges between heads of state, efforts are made to elevate the level of bilateral partnership and produce outcome documents regarding various forms of cooperation, such as the signing of memorandums of understanding on major infrastructure projects or (in the case of the PRC) the confirmation of cooperation under the Belt and Road Initiative. In the financial sector as well, often a package of measures is advanced in conjunction with, or around the timing of, summit-level exchanges, including the conclusion (new or renewed) of local swap agreements between the renminbi and the partner country's currency, the establishment of renminbi clearing banks, the launch of direct trading on the Shanghai CFETS, and the entry of Chinese commercial banks into the partner country. Such government-driven incentives can be viewed as a “jumpstart,” as discussed in Tomizawa (2023).

For example, around the time of Brazilian President Lula's visit to the PRC in 2023, arrangements were made to enable RMB settlement between the two countries' central banks, and the Brazilian subsidiary of the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC) was designated as an RMB clearing bank. In Japan as well, following the 2011 summit, efforts to promote financial cooperation between Japan and the PRC were advanced under the agreement reached there, leading to the introduction of direct yen–RMB trading in both markets. During the next round of exchanges in 2018, a series of developments followed, including the establishment of a renminbi clearing bank in Tokyo, the conclusion (or resumption) of a yen–renminbi currency swap agreement, the approval of a 200 billion yuan investment quota for qualified institutional investors in renminbi (to promote securities investment), and the issuance of "Panda bonds"—renminbi-denominated bonds for nonresidents—by two Japanese megabanks within the PRC. In this way, high-level exchanges not only yield immediate effects—such as strengthening trade and investment cooperation and advancing financial cooperation mechanisms and infrastructure development in the financial and monetary sectors—but also yield long-term benefits. Therefore, I believe it is more natural to count these exchanges using a method that accounts for the increasing number of variables with each visit.

## (2) China–Europe Railway Express Dummy

Previous research by Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023, IMF) uses the geographical proximity (distance) between a capital city and Beijing as an explanatory variable and finds a significant negative relationship with the use of the renminbi in SWIFT settlements (i.e., the greater the distance, the lower the proportion of renminbi settlements). On the other hand, since geographical distance is thought to reflect various factors, such as ease of trade, logistics, financial networks, and cultural proximity, it can also serve as a proxy for these heterogeneous factors.

Therefore, in this study, instead of using numerical values for the geographical distance between capitals, we considered the existence of the “China Railway Express (CR Express),” a freight rail network connecting the PRC with Central Asia and European countries, as a factor. In the PRC, there had long been plans for a railway connecting Lianyungang in Jiangsu Province with Amsterdam in the Netherlands, but in 2011, a route connecting Chongqing to Duisburg, Germany, via Kazakhstan and the Russian Federation, was launched. This freight route has developed into a transportation corridor linking cities in the central and western PRC with Europe, replacing costly air freight.

When President Xi Jinping announced the “Belt and Road” initiative in 2013, this freight railway became a core project of the national strategy, and efforts to expand its routes have been carried out subsequently. At the 2017 Belt and Road International Cooperation Summit, the railway sectors of seven countries—including the PRC, Germany, the Russian Federation, and Poland—formally signed a cooperation agreement to standardize operating rules. Furthermore, in 2019, the International Freight Consignment Note (a single consignment note) was introduced, eliminating the need to recreate documents in each country and significantly enhancing convenience. Even during the COVID-19 pandemic, while other logistics routes stagnated, rail freight transport proved to be well suited to transporting medical supplies and e-commerce goods, leading to an increase in the number of trains and the volume of cargo transported.

Table 15 shows that the China Railway Express currently operates in 128 cities across the PRC, and the service connects 232 cities across 26 European nations and over 100 cities in 11 Asian countries. To date, a cumulative total of 110,000 railcars have been deployed, transporting a cumulative cargo volume of 11.7 million TEUs (20-foot equivalent units) (China–Europe Railway Express Development Report 2025). While it is difficult to pinpoint exactly when the routes began, the service functions as a major logistics artery connecting European countries, Central Asia, and the PRC, with goods being transported (imported and exported) via sea routes from both ends of the network.

## (3) Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) on E-Commerce

As explained in Section 2 and in Tomizawa (2023), the PRC and Southeast Asia, in particular, are regions where digital adoption is spreading rapidly, and human mobility is more active than in other regions. We have long believed that data on these factors provide key insights into promoting trade transactions denominated in local currencies.

In regard to digital payments, we believe that the increase in overseas travel by Chinese citizens and business travelers may lead to Chinese mobile payment tools such as Alipay and WeChat Pay playing an expanding role in connecting with overseas markets and facilitating renminbi settlements. Meanwhile, in Southeast Asian countries, efforts are underway as part of the ASEAN initiative to expand networks for instant bilateral settlements using cross-border QR code payments. As for Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs), countries such as the PRC, Thailand, and the UAE are continuing to conduct cross-border connectivity experiments. While it is difficult to predict the pace at which these wholesale digital currency payment initiatives will progress, if a certain level of connectivity is achieved, it is expected to have a significant impact on the existing payment network centered on SWIFT.

As indicated by the ADB (2025), one of the defining characteristics of the Asian region is the high volume of human mobility. Consequently, the diverse development and use of mobile payment methods driven by this factor, as well as their optimization, are expected to advance further. Looking at trends in the number of travelers between the PRC and ASEAN, the number of visitors from the PRC had been growing significantly, particularly before the COVID-19 pandemic. While this trend aligns with rising incomes among Chinese citizens and the expansion of Chinese companies into foreign markets, data on the number of outbound travelers from the PRC are not published on a country-by-country basis (except when released by destination countries such as

ASEAN nations). Furthermore, the PRC's National Bureau of Statistics has not published data on inbound travelers to the PRC from major countries since 2018, making it difficult to quantitatively assess the actual volume of travel to and from the PRC.

While the movement of people slowed amid the COVID-19 pandemic, so-called “e-commerce” saw significant growth in both domestic transactions within the PRC and in trade between the PRC and other countries. As online retail (cross-border e-commerce), market sales via platforms (B2C), and parcel and express logistics from the PRC to overseas (EMS, cross-border express) have increased, the total value of cross-border e-commerce imports and exports in 2024 was projected to reach 2.7 trillion yuan, as previously mentioned. This represents a 14% year-on-year increase, surpassing the PRC's overall imports and exports growth rate of 5%. Interindustry collaboration has also led to growth in e-commerce beyond online retail (B2B trade). For example, for the ordering and procurement of agricultural products, 65 production bases across 19 countries have been established as e-commerce supply sources through partnerships with overseas agricultural production bases. Furthermore, the digitization of trade—including logistics and customs clearance—is advancing, creating a framework for the expansion of total transaction volume.

While the full breakdown of the PRC's digital trade and e-commerce volumes by country remains unclear, it is reasonable to assume that the signing of such MOUs, backed by the PRC's production and consumption capacity, is somehow linked to the proportion of RMB-invoiced transactions in trade and investment. For example, under these initiatives, efforts are underway with Thailand to link the online sale of Thai durians and other products to the Chinese market, enabling rapid customs clearance. It is believed that Chinese consumers generally pay in renminbi for purchases made on online platforms, and as the volume of transactions with partner countries increases, this could lead to a trend toward promoting transactions in the local currencies of both countries or direct currency exchange. While previous studies have rarely used digital variables in the choice of local currency for invoicing, this study adopted a dummy variable in which countries that have signed e-commerce MOUs are assigned a value of “1.”

## 4. EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS OF FACTORS PROMOTING RMB-INVOICED TRANSACTIONS

As explained in the literature review, empirical analyses of the determinants of trade invoicing currency selection have been conducted using various datasets. This paper attempts an empirical analysis of the factors that have promoted RMB-invoiced transactions in each country.

## 4.1 Examination of Dependent Variables

In each country, the share of trade with the PRC (exports and imports) in total global trade is undeniably on an overall upward trend. This can be attributed to the PRC's growing trade volume, particularly the increasing role of the PRC as a final consumption destination driven by rising incomes and purchasing power among Chinese citizens (individuals), as well as improvements in the manufacturing capabilities of Chinese enterprises. On the other hand, as pointed out in some of the prior studies cited in the previous section, it is natural to assume that the PRC's external trade relations are not solely characterized by an increase in market share but also include factors (headwinds) that reduce the PRC's share, against the backdrop of geopolitical trends—including changes in US–PRC relations—a shift in supply chains, and the overseas expansion of Chinese enterprises.

When viewed from the perspective of invoicing currency, the share of renminbi-invoiced transactions in each country's total exports and imports generally falls within the range of each country's share of bilateral trade with the PRC, as confirmed by the country-specific data in Tomizawa and Shimizu (2026). Thus, for all countries, the use of the renminbi appears to be limited, for the time being, primarily to bilateral trade with the PRC.


On the other hand, as regards the use of the renminbi in import transactions in Brazil, it is a fact that the renminbi is beginning to be used not only in bilateral trade between the PRC and Brazil but also in transactions between Brazilian companies and firms in economies such as Japan; Taipei, China; Southeast Asia; and the Russian Federation. However, given that the use of the renminbi in global transactions remains limited at this point, it is currently more natural to assume that, in most cases where the renminbi is used as an invoice currency, it is selected and utilized within the context of that economy's (or company's) bilateral trade with the PRC.

As part of the empirical analysis in this paper, we first considered using the share of invoice currencies (including those denominated in the renminbi) for each economy, based on publicly available data. As mentioned above, in Japan (although the data are semi-annual), the share of RMB-invoiced transactions in trade with Asia (both exports and imports) has been published since the second half of 2011, and the share in global trade has been published since the second half of 2013. $^{15}$ Furthermore, since the first half of 2023, the share of renminbi-invoiced transactions in trade with the PRC and Hong Kong, China has also been published. In the Republic of Korea, the Bank of Korea publishes currency settlement shares for trade with the rest of the world and for bilateral trade with the PRC. In 2023, the share of renminbi settlements in the Republic of Korea's total imports from around the world was $2.4\%$ , and it was $10.4\%$ in imports from the PRC; similarly, the share of renminbi settlements in exports was $1.7\%$ of total exports and $8.0\%$ of exports to the PRC. However, as shown in Tomizawa and Shimizu (2026), only a limited number of economies publish data on the share of invoice and settlement currencies, including the renminbi. Furthermore, even when such data are published, they often appear on a quarterly or semi-annual basis, and there are significant limitations on the period covered—for example, in Europe, only data from the 2020s onwards are publicly available.

In this analysis, the dataset by Boz et al. (2025, IMF), published in September 2025, added the renminbi share to the invoice currency shares data (broken down by US dollar, euro, and home currency) previously reported by the same authors in their 2020 paper. Among widely available data on invoice currency shares, this dataset has the broadest coverage, spanning 132 countries and regions and extending up to 2023. For example, the 2023 data show that the share of renminbi-invoiced transactions in the Russian Federation and Mongolia has grown to around 20%–30%.

In Tomizawa and Shimizu (2026), the authors analyzed the shares and trends of the renminbi as an invoice currency in Asian countries and major Western nations based on data published by central banks and ministries of finance. However, for countries such as the Russian Federation and Mongolia, no data had been released by their respective ministries of finance or central banks as of July 2025, when the paper was written. Table 19 compares the renminbi-denominated share figures disclosed by public institutions in each country with the shares reported by Boz et al. For example, in Malaysia, the data published by the central bank are ad hoc and limited in scope (only the renminbi share of total trade in 2023), whereas Boz et al.'s dataset provides continuous coverage from 2013 onward. No discrepancies were found with data from national central banks, ministries of finance, and other sources.

## 4.2 Empirical Analysis Model

In this study, we conduct an empirical analysis of country-level panel data covering the period 2016 to 2023 to examine the factors driving the growth of the renminbi-invoiced share of trade, while previous studies, such as Boz et al. (2025), utilized data on the currency shares of trade settlements in global exports and imports, and Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023) calculated the renminbi share using SWIFT transaction data. However, as explained in the previous section, this paper uses the renminbi-denominated share data published by Boz et al. (2025) as the dependent variable and constructs a dataset that delves deeper into the explanatory variables related to the renminbi internationalization policies (currency swap lines and offshore renminbi settlement banks) implemented by the People's Bank of China (PBC) used in the analysis by Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023).

In this paper, following prior studies such as Novy (2006), Goldberg and Tille (2008), and Ito and Chinn (2014), which analyzed the roles of fundamentals and policy in determining the choice of trade settlement currency, we employ the following regression model.

$$
R M B _ {i, t} ^ {\mathrm{k}} = \alpha_ {0} ^ {k} + \alpha_ {1} ^ {k} \cdot R M B _ {i, t - 1} ^ {k} + \alpha_ {2} ^ {k} \cdot M F _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {3} ^ {k} \cdot E R _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {4} ^ {k} \cdot C h i n a P o l i c y _ {i, t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} ^ {k}\tag{1}
$$

The dependent variable, $RMB_{i,t}^{k}$ , uses the renminbi-denominated share of country i's exports and imports (k denotes exports and imports) at time t (data published by Boz et al. 2025). $^{18}$ Since the share data are available only through 2023, the analysis period is set from 2015 to 2023, when various renminbi internationalization policies began to progress in earnest. The sample consists of 70 countries, as in Boz et al. (2025). However, since missing values occur when data for certain explanatory variables are unavailable, this constitutes an unbalanced panel analysis. Boz et al. (2025) used a time-only fixed-effects model, Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023) employed fixed-effects and random-effects models (including pooled OLS), and Ito and Chinn (2014) used a random-effects model; therefore, this paper tests all of these models and summarizes the results. $^{19, 20}$

As an explanatory variable, we first use the renminbi-denominated share from the previous period. This is because inertia plays a role in the choice of invoice currency, as confirmed by Boz et al. (2025) and Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023), and we adopted a model consistent with previous research. $^{21}$ Next, the vector $MF_{i,t}$ contains data representing country i's macro fundamentals, including country i's GDP (log), the value of exports and imports with the PRC (log), and the trade share with the PRC (the ratio of the value of exports and imports with the PRC to country i's total exports and imports). $ER_{i,t}$ contains data on foreign exchange and capital markets, including the rate of change in country i's exchange rate against the renminbi; a foreign exchange anchor dummy variable that takes a value of 1 if country i's exchange rate regime uses the US dollar as an anchor and 0 otherwise; an indicator of the degree of regulation in country i's foreign exchange and capital markets; and finally, China Policy $_{i,t}$ , which comprises various explanatory variables, including the

PRC's policy measures regarding the internationalization of the renminbi and the current status of such policies.

As regards the selection of explanatory variables, as detailed in the previous section, the basic model uses the KAOPEN index (2.28 to -1.94; a value closer to 2.28 indicates looser restrictions on capital transactions in that country) as an indicator of the degree of regulation in country i's foreign exchange and capital markets. In the extended model, instead of the KAOPEN index, we counted five items related to regulations on local and foreign currencies (which are considered to have a more direct impact depending on whether the country's own currency or the counterparty's currency is selected as the invoice currency) and foreign exchange. We assigned a value of 1 if regulations were in place and 0 if they were not, creating a variable with a range from 0 to 5. A score of 5 indicates the strongest currency and foreign exchange regulations.

Next, regarding the state of financial infrastructure, in the basic model, a clearing dummy was set to 1 if a renminbi clearing bank was established in country i and to 0 otherwise. Furthermore, in the extended model (as an alternative to quantifying currency swap agreements), we quantified the number of Chinese banks operating locally. We scored the business scale of local subsidiaries of the PRC's five major commercial banks entering each country as 3, branches of the Chinese headquarters as 2, and representative offices as 1.

Furthermore, as a policy variable in the extended model and an indicator of political proximity, we added a variable calculated by summing the number of visits by PRC leaders (President, Premier) to partner countries and the number of visits by partner country leaders (President, Prime Minister) to the PRC since 2010, when the internationalization of the renminbi effectively began.

In terms of geographical proximity, we designated the 36 countries connected to the China–Europe Railway Express—a key transport infrastructure of the Belt and Road Initiative that links the PRC and Europe via the Silk Road—as countries eligible for the China–Europe Railway Express dummy variable, assigning a value of 1 to these countries and 0 to those not connected. Finally, as a digital-related variable, we assigned a value of 1 to the DX-related dummy variable for countries that have signed an e-commerce (EC) MOU with the PRC to promote EC trade, and 0 to countries that have not signed such an agreement.

## 4.5 Discussion

Based on the above empirical results, it has become clear that the share of renminbi-invoiced trade exhibits strong persistence and is systematically influenced by trade dependence on the PRC, exchange rate regimes, capital controls, settlement infrastructure, and strengthened political, institutional, and logistical ties. In particular, it was consistently confirmed for both imports and exports that while a US dollar-oriented exchange rate regime and the liberalization of capital movements tend to suppress the use of renminbi-invoiced transactions, renminbi-invoiced trade is promoted in emerging and developing economies where exchange controls remain in place, where the local currency tends to depreciate against the renminbi, where the PRC has established a railway infrastructure network, and where political ties with the PRC are strong. The empirical results of this study indicate that the expansion of RMB-invoiced trade does not proceed automatically through an increase in trade scale alone but is determined by the persistence of practices, the institutional environment, the exchange rate regime, and qualitative aspects of relations with the PRC. While this finding is generally consistent with existing theories explaining the use of international currencies, it carries several important implications.

## (1) Currency Internationalization and the Role of “Inertia”

The fact that the one-period lag term of the renminbi-invoiced share was strongly and significantly positive for both imports and exports is consistent with discussions of \*\*network externalities and inertia\*\* regarding the use of international currencies. Existing research has pointed out that once the use of an international currency becomes established, it persists in a self-reinforcing manner through reduced transaction costs, learning effects, and the solidification of contractual practices. The results of this study suggest that, even though the renminbi remains a limited international currency compared to the dollar, a similar inertia can be observed even at the stage of partial internationalization. This can be interpreted as supporting the view that RMB internationalization is not a “phenomenon that progresses all at once” but rather a process that gradually takes root in specific countries and trade relationships.

## (2) Trade Dependency and Choice of Transaction Currency

The finding that a country's share of trade with the PRC consistently promotes RMB-invoiced transactions is consistent with theories regarding dominant trading partners. International trade theory posits that a trading partner's market size and bargaining power influence the choice of contract currency, and the results of this study indicate that RMB-invoiced settlements are more readily accepted when the PRC is a major trading partner for a specific country.

On the other hand, the finding that the volume (scale) of trade itself does not necessarily have a positive effect is significant. This implies that renminbi-invoiced transactions do not expand naturally due to “large-scale trade” but are instead selectively employed within specific institutional and policy frameworks, which suggests that the use of international currencies depends heavily not only on market size but also on institutional design and policy guidance.

## (3) Exchange Rate Regimes and Currency Competition

The finding that the exchange rate peg to the US dollar suppresses RMB-invoiced transactions in both imports and exports can be understood from the perspective of \*\*currency competition\*\*. In countries where the exchange rate system is designed around the dollar, foreign exchange risk management, financial products, and settlement infrastructure are established on the premise of dollar denomination, making the switching costs to renminbi-invoiced transactions high.

This outcome indicates that the internationalization of the renminbi is not proceeding as a comprehensive replacement for the US dollar but is constrained by differences in exchange rate regimes and financial structures. In other words, the renminbi is not a “complete substitute” for the dollar; rather, its use is likely expanding as a complementary currency within specific institutional environments.

## (4) Capital Controls and Institutional Trust

The fact that the capital account openness (KAOPEN) was negative and statistically significant appears, at first glance, to contradict currency internationalization theory. Conventional wisdom holds that the liberalization of capital movements promotes the international use of the currency; however, the results of this study suggest that capital controls may be supporting renminbi-invoiced trade.

This is consistent with the PRC's policy strategy of “advancing renminbi internationalization in stages, starting with trade settlements, prior to capital liberalization.” It can be interpreted that countries with freer capital mobility tend to prefer existing international currencies such as the dollar or the euro, thereby reducing the relative appeal of the renminbi, whereas within a certain regulatory environment, the renminbi is more easily integrated into institutional frameworks.

## (5) The Role of Political, Institutional, and Logistical Factors

The consistently and significantly positive effects of variables such as visits by PRC government officials, the China–Europe Railway Express, and DX-related MOUs indicate that renminbi-invoiced trade is supported not only by market mechanisms but also by the deepening of political and institutional relationships.

This implies a shift from the conventional framework, which views the use of international currencies as a “neutral outcome of spontaneous market choice,” toward one that emphasizes state-led and relationship-dependent aspects. In the case of the renminbi, it is following an internationalization path distinct from that of typical reserve currencies, in that infrastructure development, diplomatic relations, and policy coordination drive currency usage.

## (6) Asymmetry Between Imports and Exports

Finally, the fact that the significance of certain factors differs between imports and exports suggests that RMB internationalization depends primarily on the PRC's policy intentions and trade structures. In particular, the stronger influence of settlement infrastructure and institutional factors on imports indicates that the renminbi may have first gained traction as a “currency for payments from the PRC.”

In summary, the findings reveal that while RMB internationalization is still in its early stages, it is a gradual process with strong inertia. It depends heavily not only on the scale of trade with the PRC but also on institutional and relational factors and is expanding in a complementary manner within the dollar-centered international monetary system. This presents a more limited and institution-dependent picture of RMB internationalization, in contrast to the view that characterizes it as the “rise of a new reserve currency to replace the dollar.”

## 5. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE RESEARCH

In this paper, we organize the variables used in prior research on the choice of invoice currency in trade. We then cross-reference these with the results of analyses of the mechanisms that promote local currency transactions and the factors that may facilitate them. We meticulously extracted new candidate variable data from various statistics and publicly available information and conducted an empirical analysis. In this paper, we used data on renminbi-invoiced shares published by Boz et al. (2025), and we constructed and analyzed a dataset that delves deeper into the explanatory variables related to the renminbi internationalization policies implemented by the People's Bank of China (PBC)—such as currency swap lines and dummy variables for offshore renminbi settlement banks—which were used in the analysis by Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023), by framing them as the activities of Chinese banks operating abroad.

In addition to the macroeconomic factors used in previous studies, the authors focused on capital control variables related to currencies where regulations are considered to have a stronger association with local currency transactions, and constructed variables based on these. Furthermore, to refine the geopolitical distance indicators (such as the distance between capitals and UN voting behavior dummies) presented in previous studies, the analysis counted and analyzed connections via freight railways—a critical infrastructure—and high-level exchanges among leaders. Additionally, with regard to digital factors (not used in previous studies), the study organized and used the existence and duration of bilateral e-commerce agreements as variables.

The results reveal that although the internationalization of the renminbi is still in its early stages, it is a gradual process with strong inertia; it depends heavily on institutional and relational factors rather than on trade scale and is expanding in a complementary manner within the dollar-centered international monetary system. In other words, based on the experience of RMB internationalization to date, we have revealed that a combination of factors—particularly strong government initiatives, geopolitical elements, and digital factors—has led to the use of the renminbi in trade and investment. Furthermore, the finding that the renminbi is being chosen for transactions between countries with strict foreign exchange controls suggests that it is possible to increase the use of local currencies even in closed environments where currency and capital controls remain in place, which this paper considers a contribution.

On the other hand, it is important to note that the PRC's experience may not apply to all developing countries. To avoid falling into the so-called “middle-income trap,” developing countries must strive for stable macroeconomic management. At the same time, they should implement orderly currency and capital controls in step with the country’s economic growth rate when it is experiencing momentum. In the medium term, this will lead to exchange rate stability, enhanced capital-raising capacity, and corporate growth, thereby strengthening confidence in the domestic currency. Even in developed countries like Japan, where there is limited room for deregulation, steps could be taken to deepen domestic financial markets and enhance their appeal. By seizing “opportunities” such as the digitization of currency and the movement of people, and by strengthening cooperation and collaboration with emerging economies, it may be possible to increase yen-denominated transactions within the Asian region and enhance the yen’s presence as a regional currency.

One of the remaining issues identified through this study is that, while previous research found that a dummy variable for currency swaps with the PRC had a significant and positive effect on renminbi-invoiced transactions, using the actual swap amount rather than a dummy variable did not yield a significant result. Although we attempted to address this by comparing the swap amount to the country's import value, the result remained insignificant. Since it is believed that swap agreements with the PRC contribute to the expansion of RMB usage in practice, further refinement of the explanatory variables will likely be necessary. Second, there is the relationship between capital transactions—such as outward and inward direct investment—and the choice of currency for trade settlement. We had expected to find that in countries engaging in outward and inward direct investment with the PRC, the occurrence of RMB payments and receipts would lead to increased use of the renminbi for trade settlement, but this was not the case. We analyzed and identified the mechanisms that promote local currency—renminbi transactions in the PRC; however, due to capital (investment) flows with regions unique to the PRC, such as Hong Kong, China and Macau, China, we were unable to clarify the relationship between local currency-denominated transactions and capital transactions. The relationship between the establishment of capital-circulation routes to overseas markets through the relaxation of capital controls and the use of the local currency remains a topic for further research. Furthermore, Africa—which could not be examined in this study due to data limitations—is a region with which the PRC is building relationships and is a potential candidate for inclusion in the renminbi zone.

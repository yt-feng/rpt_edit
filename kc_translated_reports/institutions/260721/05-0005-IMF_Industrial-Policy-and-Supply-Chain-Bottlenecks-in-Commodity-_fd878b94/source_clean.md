## 1 Introduction

Interdependencies created by decades of globalization and the explosive growth of cross-border supply chains have become increasingly weaponized. Over the last decade, countries have progressively resorted to tariffs, sanctions, export controls, and other economic tools to pursue geopolitical objectives, most recently in the form of Western restrictions on semiconductors and Chinese restrictions on critical minerals (Dürr and Heilmann, 2025). As a result, global supply chains — long shaped exclusively by cost optimization — now face potential disruptions and geoeconomic fragmentation. For policymakers in import-dependent countries, this raises questions about how to de-risk critical supply chains and which industrial policies are best suited to do so.

These questions are important in global markets for rare earth elements (REEs), which saw the introduction of export licensing requirements and industrial policy announcements in 2025. A chokepoint is more likely to emerge when an input is difficult to substitute and three further conditions align: (i) extreme geographic concentration in a single country, (ii) a potential for disruption and, (iii) barriers to rapid diversification. Rare earths satisfy all three. On April 4, 2025, shortly after the U.S. imposed higher import tariffs on most of its trading partners, China — the world’s top producer of rare earth elements across all segments of the value chain including mining and refining — introduced export licensing requirements for seven REEs and permanent magnets, citing national security concerns and international non-proliferation obligations (Ministry of Commerce of the People’s Republic of China, 2025). These export licensing requirements caused dramatic slowdowns in magnet exports to the rest of the world between April and June, but proved short-lived, with monthly Chinese export volumes quickly returning to their positive trend and displaying double-digit year-on-year growth rates. $^{1}$ In response, several countries have launched industrial policies aimed at de-risking REE production.

These supply chain disruptions and new industrial policies to build alternative supply chains all highlight the urgent need to better understand the economics of rare earths. To this end, this paper asks: how achievable are meaningful levels of REE supply chain de-risking under alternative industrial policy scenarios over the next decade, and at what fiscal cost? We find that while substantial relative to the size of the market, the fiscal costs associated with the required industrial policies would likely be manageable, particularly under simultaneous actions among various importing countries. $^{2}$

To analyze alternative industrial policies, we develop a calibrated, dynamic trade model of the global rare earths market featuring two stages of production: mining (upstream) and refining (downstream). In the first stage, miners extract raw REE compounds and sell them to refiners on a global market for unprocessed REEs. Refiners then separate these compounds into REE oxides, which are sold on a global market to manufacturers such as permanent magnet producers. In each country/block, a representative miner and a representative refiner invest in productive capacity to maximize profits, taking prices as given.

The model is calibrated to the global market for rare earths by partitioning the world into six countries/regions: the US, China and Myanmar (which, following industry convention, is referred to as China+), Europe, Japan, other Anglophone countries (Canada, Australia), and rest-of-world (RoW). This partitioning reflects each country or region's role in the REE supply chain — as a major producer (China+), a significant consumer (Japan, Europe), a source of untapped mining potential (Anglophone countries), or an economy actively pursuing supply chain de-risking through industrial policy (the US) — with some countries spanning multiple roles.

The model calibration draws on detailed market, industry, and geological data, with particular attention to mining and refining costs across countries. We follow a two-step procedure. First, baseline cost parameters are derived from a breakeven analysis of rare earth projects worldwide. For producers outside China, we evaluate 21 feasibility studies, computing rare earth breakeven prices (in \$/kg) after harmonizing key parameters such as discount rates. For China, breakeven costs are inferred from the 2006–2010 period, when China held a near-monopoly in REE production and the domestic industry was highly competitive, making observed prices a reasonable proxy for marginal costs. $^{3}$ Because cost data by production stage in China are not directly observed, the mining–refining decomposition is implemented transparently as a calibration assumption disciplined by project-level evidence from other producing countries and by the model's price and production targets. Second, taking the baseline cost parameters as given, slope parameters of the cost functions are calibrated to match each country's observed mining and refining production shares. Together, these two steps anchor the cost calibration in both forward-looking project-level estimates and historically revealed cost advantages.

Next, we ask the model to quantify the impact of a major supply disruption, which we proxy by simulating a hypothetical fragmentation of global REE markets into two blocks. We use the model to assess the effects of two industrial policies designed to mitigate the potential macroeconomic effect of the supply disruption: investment subsidies and stylized downstream revenue-support policies (henceforth also referred to as price floors) applied to refining/oxide separation (the most crucial processing stage). We study two implementation scenarios: (1) unilateral action by the United States, in which the policy instruments apply only to U.S.-based producers, and (2) simultaneously among all various net importing countries (U.S., EU, Japan, Australia, and other economies), in which instruments are applied uniformly across participating countries.

To illustrate the trade offs involved with each policy, instruments are calibrated to increase the share of domestic rare earth consumption that is not dependent on the largest import source by 15 percentage points in the U.S., from 10 to 25 percent. This measure of diversification can be interpreted as a metric of exposure to the dominant supplier. This is 15 percentage points higher than our baseline at unchanged policies and in line with IEA projections that imply—under announced projects outside China and stated government policies—that diversification levels outside of China would increase by 16 percentage points, from 8 to 24 percent, over the next decade (International Energy Agency, 2025).


Second, investment subsidies are typically more fiscally efficient than the price floors when evaluated in present-value terms. This reflects the fact that investment subsidies are targeted at new capacity, whereas the revenue-support instrument also generates windfall gains for incumbent producers by supporting existing production. As a result, for a given de-risking target, the net present value of subsidy payments is lower than that of revenue support through price floors. To achieve the 25 percent target under the unilateral (simultaneous) scenario, U.S. fiscal costs associated with the investment subsidy over the first decade amount to 134 (90) percent of the U.S. REE market size—equivalent to about \$1.13 billion (\$0.76 billion). $^{4}$ At the same time, investment subsidies are more costly in the short term than price floors as they front-load fiscal outlays, with costs declining in the long term once investment is largely limited to replacing depreciated capital.

Third, simultaneous action among various importers reduces the fiscal cost of achieving a given de-risking target. For example, when the participating net-REE importing economies simultaneously incentivize investment in refining, individual countries' de-risking is achieved through a less concentrated buildup of capacity, as part of the fiscal cost is outsourced to participating countries. Under simultaneous action, those economies can also leverage the U.S. relatively higher efficiency in REEs processing and see substantial gains in de-risking, at comparatively lower fiscal costs.

Fourth, fiscal costs increase disproportionately with the degree of industrial policy intervention. For example, with higher CAPEX subsidies the U.S. can attain higher levels of de-risking levels in the downstream market, but the fiscal cost increases faster than the attained de-risking rate. This result suggests that very high levels of de-risking can be extremely costly, whereas moderate levels are quickly achievable and at moderate costs.

Contribution to the literature. Our paper contributes to the literature on geoeconomics, which studies how countries leverage economic interdependencies to pursue geopolitical objectives (Clayton et al., 2026). Within this literature, Clayton et al. (2024) develop a theory of economic coercion in which large powers exploit trade and financial dependencies, highlighting the risks of highly concentrated supply chains. Kooi (2025) complements this perspective by formalizing the national security externality — the idea that markets may undervalue resilience in strategic sectors — and quantifies the value of reshoring semiconductor production capacity to the US. On the policy side, Baquie et al. (2025) argue that the effectiveness of industrial policies depends critically on careful design, with poorly targeted interventions yielding limited gains. While these contributions provide useful conceptual frameworks, the literature rarely quantifies the costs and effectiveness of specific policy instruments within particular supply chains, with Kooi (2025) being a notable exception for semiconductors.

We contribute by developing a calibrated dynamic model of the global rare earth supply chain that incorporates investment decisions in both mining and refining capacity and allows the evaluation of alternative policy instruments, including investment subsidies and price floors. The model captures the mining-to-refining bottleneck that characterizes rare earth supply chains, distinguishing between upstream extraction and the technologically more complex refining stage where global supply is most concentrated. Our results provide a cost-based rationale for the thesis in Clayton et al. (2024) that for chokepoint inputs, modest diversification can substantially improve resilience: we find that fiscal costs rise exponentially as de-risking targets increase, so that a small amount of supply chain diversification delivers the largest gains per dollar spent.

A second strand of the literature examines how trade restrictions reshape critical mineral supply chains. Chen (2025) examines mineral export restrictions as a form of industrial policy and finds that such policies reduce upstream exports while sometimes encouraging downstream processing, although empirical evidence of broader industrial upgrading remains limited. Kee and Xie (2025) study Indonesia's nickel export ban and show that while the policy succeeded in attracting foreign investment and raising the domestic value added ratio of exports, it also led to the entry of less efficient firms, highlighting the unintended allocative costs of such interventions. Turning to rare earths specifically, Alfaro et al. (2025) study how the 2010 REE supply shortage induced directed technological change in downstream industries abroad, increasing efficiency in the use of REEs and reshaping trade patterns in REE-intensive sectors. Shifting to the importer perspective, Shao et al. (2026) shows that a substantial share of global REE consumption occurs indirectly through manufactured goods exported by China, implying that dependence on Chinese REEs is significantly larger than raw mineral trade statistics suggest. Our paper contributes to this literature by developing a quantitative framework that jointly models upstream extraction and downstream processing across countries, allowing us to evaluate how specific industrial policy interventions such as subsidies and price floors reshape global REE production and trade patterns.

A third strand of the literature assesses the criticality of REEs and the macroeconomic consequences of supply disruptions. Nassar et al. (2015, 2020) develop frameworks for assessing mineral supply risk, identifying REEs as among the most strategically exposed inputs due to their concentrated production and importance for high-technology industries. $^{5}$ Relatedly, Bogmans et al. (2026) quantify how a REE supply shock propagates through input–output networks, amplifying GDP losses across advanced economies. Our paper builds on these findings by developing a dynamic model of the global REE supply chain that can assess how investment and production decisions respond to policy interventions over time. Rather than tracing the macroeconomic effects through input–output networks as in Bogmans et al. (2026), we study the impact of a market bifurcation on the REE supply chain itself, disentangling the effects on upstream and downstream markets.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 documents key features of REE markets and supply chains, including the greater geographical concentration in refining than in mining, that motivate the structure of the quantitative model. Section 3 presents a dynamic trade model of global markets for raw and refined REEs, and Section 4 describes the calibration strategy. Section 5 reports the quantitative results, including the effects of different industrial policies in various net-REE importing countries, and the trade-off between fiscal costs and de-risking targets. Section 6 concludes.

## 2 Descriptive Statistics

This section highlights three key empirical features of REE markets that motivate the structure of the model. First, a small number of elements capture most of the market value. Second, geographical concentration is greater in processing than in mining. Third, this concentration has proved remarkably persistent across several price-spike episodes, particularly in processing.

## 2.1 The Concentrated Economics of Rare Earths

REEs are a group of 17 chemically similar metals, typically grouped into light (LREEs) and heavy (HREEs) based on atomic weight. HREEs are significantly less abundant in the earth's crust than LREEs and typically occur in lower concentrations, making them more difficult to extract at scale. Rare earths are employed in small quantities in various manufacturing sectors including automotive production, wind energy, military applications, semiconductors, and consumer electronics. They provide unique properties that are difficult to replicate cheaply, notably in applications requiring strong magnetic performance, thermal stability, or high-power density.

The economics of rare earths are highly concentrated. Four elements—neodymium (Nd) and praseodymium (Pr), both LREEs, and dysprosium (Dy) and terbium (Tb), both HREEs account for 96 percent of total rare earth oxide (REO) market value despite representing only 23 percent of production by weight (MS, 2025). Two forces drive this imbalance. On the demand side, permanent magnets—the fastest-growing application of rare earths—account for roughly 75–85 percent of total REE demand by value (International Energy Agency, 2025). $^{6}$ On the supply side, the natural composition of deposits skews production toward high-abundance, low-value elements such as lanthanum and cerium, while the "magnet-4" are produced in much smaller quantities. The economic weight of the magnet-4 has risen markedly since 2015, as investments in wind power and electric vehicles following the Paris Agreement have lifted demand for neodymium magnets. $^{7}$

## 2.2 Global Supply Chain Structure and Concentration by Stage

The rare earth supply chain consists of four sequential stages. Ore is first extracted and transported to nearby benefaction facilities, where mechanical and chemical processes concentrate it into a mixed rare earth compound. This intermediate product is then subjected to solvent extraction, a technically demanding process involving hundreds of sequential separation steps, to isolate individual rare earth elements into oxides or other compounds. In the final stage, these materials are reduced to their metallic form and combined with other inputs to produce alloys, such as NdFeB permanent magnets (U.S. Department of Energy, 2022).

During the 2000s China grew to become the primary source of global rare earth supply, reflecting technical expertise acquired over decades, a large domestic market, established infrastructure, extensive and farsighted policy support, and relatively flexible regulations. Today, China's primary position varies significantly by rare earth type and supply chain stage. Diversification has been most pronounced for the mining of LREEs: China's share of global LREE mining peaked at roughly 97 percent in 2010 and has since declined to around 58 percent in 2024 (Figure 1) (Nassar et al., 2023; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026). New production from Australia, the United States, and Myanmar has contributed to this shift. $^{8}$

Despite a trend of diversification in the mining of LREEs, China remains the leader over downstream processing stages, accounting for approximately 85–90 percent of global oxide separation capacity and 90–95 percent of metal refining (Figure 2) (Bedford, 2025). Separation is the central technical bottleneck in the supply chain. It is capital- and energy-intensive, environmentally damaging, and requires highly specialized expertise and intellectual property developed over decades. As a result, most rare earth concentrates, regardless of where they are mined, are shipped to China for processing. This limits the extent to which upstream diversification translates into overall supply chain diversification.

Furthermore, for HREEs, concentration is even more extreme and operates at every stage. As shown in Figure 2, which groups China (including Myanmar) accounts for 98 percent of mining, 96.5 percent of oxide separation, 95 percent of metal refining, and 89.5 percent of permanent magnet manufacturing. $^{9}$ Unlike LREEs, HREEs face a dual structural bottleneck: mining has seen virtually no geographic diversification, compounding the processing constraints documented above. $^{10}$ In short, for LREEs, the critical supply chain vulnerability lies not in mining—which has diversified — but in processing, where concentration remains entrenched. For HREEs, the challenge is more fundamental as both mining and processing currently exhibit a high degree of geographic concentration.

## 2.3 Persistence of Concentration: Technology, Costs, and Lead Times

Despite rising demand, some temporary supply shortages, and a wave of industrial policy interventions since 2025, diversification in REE supply chains has remained slow. This persistence reflects the interaction of technological complexity, high capital intensity, and unfavorable project economics outside established production hubs. The concentration in separation and refining in China reflects decades of accumulated technical knowledge and infrastructure that are not easily replicated, while the regulatory burden and specialized expertise required for processing make entry difficult even under favorable market conditions.

New REE projects outside China also face long lead times, typically requiring 10–15 years or more to move from discovery to commercial production, owing to permitting, environmental regulation, financing, and process development (International Energy Agency, 2025). New entrants must replicate not only physical infrastructure but also the tacit knowledge embedded in production processes, further raising effective entry costs.

Project-level economics reinforce these constraints. A bottom-up assessment of 21 REE projects outside China reveals substantial dispersion in capital and operating costs, with many projects situated on the upper end of the global cost curve (Figure 13). Only a limited number of projects are profitable at current price levels, while many require substantially higher prices to become viable. Historical price dynamics have compounded this problem: following the 2010–2011 price spike, REE prices declined sharply and remained subdued for an extended period, and broader price volatility has since made it difficult to justify the large and irreversible investments required to develop new capacity outside China. $^{11}$

## 2.4 Industrial Policies

China's introduction of REE export licensing requirements in April 2025 triggered an immediate market slowdown: permanent magnet exports fell by roughly 70 percent year-on-year at the trough, while Dy and Tb prices in European markets nearly tripled by mid-2025. Although monthly Chinese export volumes quickly returned to trend growth—displaying double-digit year-on-year growth rates—the episode was followed by accelerated industrial policy efforts across major economies aimed at de-risking supply chains. It remains to be seen whether these efforts will materially alter existing dependencies.

These efforts have focused on three approaches. First, price floors and offtake agreements have been concluded to provide investment certainty in volatile markets, as many REE projects are not commercially viable at current neodymium prices (about \$55/kg). A July 2025 agreement between the U.S. government and MP Materials introduced a price-protection mechanism for key REE products, and price floors more broadly featured prominently in high-level G7 critical minerals discussions. In the quantitative analysis below, we capture this policy family with a stylized revenue-support instrument for refiners; this should be interpreted as a tractable proxy for downside protection rather than as a literal state-contingent contract. Second, governments have provided direct financial support—through equity stakes, loans, and grants—to supply capital and signal long-term commitment. Third, international agreements signed in October 2025 between the United States and Japan, Malaysia, Thailand, and Australia, alongside the G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan, promote joint financing and coordinated procurement, mobilizing an estimated \$6.4 billion in public and private funding to de-risk REE supply chains.

## 3 Model

The market of rare earth elements (REEs) is divided in two stages of production, i.e., the upstream and the downstream. In the first stage of production, raw REEs are extracted by miners and sold to refiners in a global market for raw REEs at the global price $p_{t}^{x}$ , i.e., the upstream price. Subsequently, refiners transform raw REEs into a refined product that is sold in the global market for refined REEs at the global price $p_{t}^{y}$ , i.e., the downstream price. There are J countries/blocks in the REE market with a representative miner and a representative refiner in each country $j \in \{0, \ldots, J\}$ . In what follows, we refer to miners (refiners) as upstream (downstream) producers and use these terms interchangeably throughout.

## 3.1 Miners

Each representative REE miner $j \in \{0, \ldots, J\}$ operates competitively (i.e., taking prices as given), by extracting a non-exhaustible endowment of the natural resource $x_{jt}$ . Miners across countries are characterized by differences in extraction costs and investment costs. In the spirit of Anderson et al. (2018) and Kellogg (2024), REE extraction follows an exponential decline with rate $\eta_{j} > 0$ . Letting $i_{jt}^{x}$ denote the rate of investment in new extraction capacity, the rate of extraction $x_{jt}$ evolves according to the law of motion:

$$
x _ {j t} = (1 - \eta_ {j}) x _ {j t - 1} + i _ {j t} ^ {x}\tag{1}
$$

Potentially, each country j can introduce subsidies to extraction capacity and investment in new extraction capacity. $^{12}$ The sequences of mining subsidies $\{s_{jt}^{x}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ and extraction investment subsidies $\{s_{jt}^{ix}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ are taken as given by miners. In addition, miners take as given the sequence of upstream REE prices $\{p_{t}^{x}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ . We assume that miners incur two sources of costs: investment in new capacity costs (CAPEX), denoted by $c_{j}^{ix}(i_{jt}^{x})$ , and extraction costs (OPEX), denoted by $c_{j}^{x}(x_{jt})$ . We further assume that investment increases extraction capacity immediately, i.e., there is no time-to-build. $^{13}$ Letting $\beta_{j} \in (0,1)$ denote the discount factor, REE extraction in country j is given by the solution to the following problem:

$$
\max _ {x _ {j t}, i _ {j t} ^ {x}} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {\infty} \beta_ {j} ^ {t} \left(\underbrace {p _ {t} ^ {x} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {x}) x _ {j t}} _ {\text { Net revenues }} - \underbrace {(1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i x}) c _ {j} ^ {i x} (i _ {j t} ^ {x})} _ {\text { Net CAPEX }} - \underbrace {c _ {j} ^ {x} (x _ {j t})} _ {\text { OPEX }}\right)\tag{2}
$$

subject to:

$$
x _ {j t} = (1 - \eta_ {j}) x _ {j t - 1} + i _ {j t} ^ {x}
$$

$$
x _ {j t} \geq 0
$$

Denoting with $\lambda_{jt}^{x}$ the Lagrange multiplier associated with the law of motion of extraction capacity, the optimality conditions of the miners' problem are given by: $^{14}$

$$
\lambda_ {j t} ^ {x} = (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i x}) \frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {i x} (i _ {j t} ^ {x})}{\partial i _ {j t} ^ {x}}
$$

$$
\lambda_ {j t} ^ {x} = \beta_ {j} (1 - \eta_ {j}) \lambda_ {j t + 1} ^ {x} + p _ {t} ^ {x} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {x}) - \frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {x} (x _ {j t})}{\partial x _ {j t}}
$$

plus (1). The combination of the two first order conditions leads to the simple expression:

$$
\underbrace {(1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i x}) \frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {x} (i _ {j t} ^ {x})}{\partial i _ {j t} ^ {x}}} _ {\text { net   current   marginal   CAPEX }} = \underbrace {\beta_ {j} (1 - \eta_ {j}) (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i x}) \frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {x} (i _ {j t + 1} ^ {x})}{\partial i _ {j t + 1} ^ {x}}} _ {\text { net   future   discounted   marginal   CAPEX }} + \underbrace {p _ {t} ^ {x} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {x}) - \frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {x} (x _ {j t})}{\partial x _ {j t}}} _ {\text { net   extraction   margin }}\tag{3}
$$

where the left-hand side of the (3) represents the (net) marginal cost of one additional unit of CAPEX, and the right-hand side represents its marginal benefit, given by the next-period extraction margin, i.e., the difference between net marginal revenues and marginal OPEX, as well as the discounted savings induced by frontloading CAPEX, i.e., reducing future CAPEX.

For each country j the rate of extraction $x_{jt}$ is determined by the system of equations given by (1) and (3). We define $R_{t}^{s}$ as the aggregate quantity of REEs mined in the global market at time t and supplied to refiners, which is defined as:

$$
R _ {t} ^ {s} \equiv \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} x _ {j t}
$$

Notice that $x_{jt}$ is a function of the contemporaneous intermediate price $p_t^x$ .

## 3.2 Refiners

Similarly to miners, each representative REE refiner $j \in \{0, \ldots, J\}$ operates in a competitive fashion (i.e., taking prices as given). Refiners purchase raw REEs $z_{jt}$ from the global market at the given price $p_{t}^{x}$ . Refining raw REEs requires investment in refining capacity $y_{jt}$ , which follows an exponential decline at rate $\delta_{j} > 0$ . Letting $i_{jt}^{y}$ denote the rate of investment in new refining capacity, the level of refining capacity $y_{jt}$ evolves according to the law of motion:

$$
y _ {j t} = (1 - \delta_ {j}) y _ {j t - 1} + i _ {j t} ^ {y}\tag{4}
$$

Equivalently to miners, refiners' output $y_{jt}$ is determined by their refining capacity. We also assume that the refining technology is linear in its input and satisfies the equation:

$$
y _ {j t} = z _ {j t}\tag{5}
$$

Each country j can introduce subsidies to refining capacity and investment in refining. $^{15}$ The sequences of refining subsidies $\{s_{jt}^{y}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ and refining investment subsidies $\{s_{jt}^{iy}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ are taken as given by refiners. In addition, refiners take as given the sequences of raw REE prices $\{p_{t}^{x}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ and refined REE prices $\{p_{t}^{y}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ . We assume that refiners incur two sources of costs: capacity investment costs (CAPEX), denoted by $c_{j}^{iy}(i_{jt}^{y})$ , and refining costs (OPEX), denoted by $c_{j}^{y}(y_{jt})$ . Letting $\beta_{j} \in (0,1)$ denote the discount factor, REE refining in country j is given by the solution to the problem:

$$
\max _ {z _ {j t}, y _ {j t}, i _ {j t} ^ {y}} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {\infty} \beta_ {j} ^ {t} \left(p _ {t} ^ {y} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {y}) y _ {j t} - p _ {t} ^ {x} z _ {j t} - (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i y}) c _ {j} ^ {i y} (i _ {j t} ^ {y}) - c _ {j} ^ {y} (y _ {j t})\right)\tag{6}
$$

subject to:

$$
y _ {j t} = z _ {j t}
$$

$$
y _ {j t} = (1 - \delta_ {j}) y _ {j t - 1} + i _ {j t} ^ {y}
$$

By combining the first three constraints and replacing them in the profit function, the problem can be rewritten as:

$$
\max _ {y _ {j t}} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {\infty} \beta_ {j} ^ {t} \left(\left(p _ {t} ^ {y} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {y}) - p _ {t} ^ {x}\right) y _ {j t} - (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i y}) c _ {j} ^ {i y} (y _ {j t} - (1 - \delta_ {j}) y _ {j t - 1}) - c _ {j} ^ {y} (y _ {j t})\right)
$$

Denoting with $\mu_{jt}$ the Lagrangian multipliers associated with the capacity utilization constraint, the first order necessary conditions are given by:

$$
\underbrace {(1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i y}) \frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {i y}}{\partial i _ {j t} ^ {y}}} _ {\text { net   current   marginal   CAPEX }} = \overbrace {\underbrace {p _ {t} ^ {y} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {y}) - p _ {t} ^ {x}} _ {\text { gross   refining   margin }} - \underbrace {\frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {y}}{\partial y _ {j t}}} _ {\text { net   current   marginal   OPEX }}} ^ {\text { net   refining   margin }} + \underbrace {\beta_ {j} (1 - \delta_ {j}) (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i y}) \frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {i y}}{\partial i _ {j t + 1} ^ {y}}} _ {\text { net   future   discounted   marginal   CAPEX }}\tag{7}
$$

where the left-hand side of (7) represents the (net) marginal cost of one additional unit of CAPEX, and the right-hand side represents its marginal benefit, given by the next-period refining margin, i.e., the difference between the gross refining margin and the marginal OPEX, as well as the discounted savings induced by frontloading CAPEX, i.e., reducing future CAPEX.

We define $R_{t}^{d}$ as the aggregate quantity of raw REE demanded in the global market, which is defined by:

$$
R _ {t} ^ {d} \equiv \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} z _ {j t} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} y _ {j t}
$$

Notice that $R_{t}^{d}$ is a function of the contemporaneous intermediate price $p_{t}^{x}$ .

## 3.3 Final Good Demands

We assume that each country j demands the processed/refined REE material according to the function:

$$
q _ {j t} = f (a _ {j t}, p _ {t} ^ {y})\tag{8}
$$

where $a_{jt} \in R^{+}$ is a country-specific and time-specific demand shifter. In the quantitative analysis of the model we will construct a set of demand functions that grow over time, which reflects the anticipated demand growth for refined REEs.

## 3.4 Equilibrium

Let J denote the set of all countries in the global REE market. We can then define the competitive equilibrium for the global market as follows:

Definition 1. Given the initial conditions $(x_{j0}, y_{j0})$ , final demand shifters $\{a_{jt}\}$ , and subsidy rate sequences $\{s_{jt}^{x}, s_{jt}^{y}, s_{jt}^{ix}, s_{jt}^{iy}\}$ for each country $j \in J$ , a competitive equilibrium for the global REE market is given by allocations $\{x_{jt}, y_{jt}, q_{jt}\}_{j \in J, t > 0}$ and prices $\{p_{t}^{x}, p_{t}^{y}\}_{t > 0}$ such that:

(i) for each $j \in J$ , the allocations solve the miners' problem (2), the refiners' problem (6), and are consistent with (8);

(ii) the market for raw REEs clears: $\sum_{j\in \mathcal{J}}x_{jt} = \sum_{j\in \mathcal{J}}y_{jt}$ ;

(iii) the market for refined REEs clears: $\sum_{j\in \mathcal{J}}y_{jt} = \sum_{j\in \mathcal{J}}q_{jt}$ .

## 3.5 Functional Forms

Following the approach of Kellogg (2024), in the remainder of our analysis we assume that the marginal cost of investment in extraction capacity (miners' marginal CAPEX) and the marginal operating cost of extraction (miners' marginal OPEX) are, respectively, given by:

$$
\frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {i x}}{\partial i _ {j t} ^ {x}} = \alpha_ {j} ^ {i x} + \gamma_ {j} ^ {i x} (x _ {j t} - (1 - \eta_ {j}) x _ {j t - 1})
$$

$$
\frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {x}}{\partial x _ {j t}} = \alpha_ {j} ^ {x}
$$

where $\alpha_{j}^{ix}$ , $\gamma_{j}^{ix}$ and $\alpha_{j}^{x}$ are country-specific coefficients. Then, country j's supply of raw REEs at time t solves the following second-order difference equation:

$$
\begin{array}{c} x _ {j t} = \Lambda_ {j t} ^ {- 1} \left[ \gamma_ {j} ^ {i x} (1 - \eta_ {j}) (\beta_ {j} (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i x}) x _ {j t + 1} + (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i x}) x _ {j t - 1}) - (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i x} - \beta_ {j} (1 - \eta_ {j}) (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i x})) \alpha_ {j} ^ {i x} + \right. \\ \left. + p _ {t} ^ {x} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {x}) - \alpha_ {j} ^ {x} \right] \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

$$
\Lambda_ {j t} \equiv \gamma_ {j} ^ {i x} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i x} + \beta_ {j} (1 - \eta_ {j}) ^ {2} (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i x}))
$$

In a similar fashion, the marginal cost of investment in refining capacity (refiners' marginal CAPEX) and the marginal operating cost of refining (refiners' marginal OPEX) are, respectively, given by:

$$
\frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {i y}}{\partial i _ {j t} ^ {y}} = \alpha_ {j} ^ {i y} + \gamma_ {j} ^ {i y} (y _ {j t} - (1 - \delta_ {j}) y _ {j t - 1})
$$

$$
\frac {\partial c _ {j} ^ {y}}{\partial y _ {j t}} = \alpha_ {j} ^ {y}
$$

where $\alpha_{j}^{iy}$ , $\gamma_{j}^{iy}$ and $\alpha_{j}^{y}$ are country-specific coefficients.

Then, country $j$ 's demand of raw REEs is given by:

$$
\begin{array}{c} y _ {j t} = \zeta_ {j t} ^ {- 1} \left[ \gamma_ {j} ^ {i y} (1 - \delta_ {j}) (\beta_ {j} (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i y}) y _ {j t + 1} + (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i y}) y _ {j t - 1}) - (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i y} - \\ - \beta_ {j} (1 - \delta_ {j}) (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i y})) \alpha_ {j} ^ {i y} + p _ {t} ^ {y} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {y}) - p _ {t} ^ {x} - \alpha_ {j} ^ {y} \right] \\ \zeta_ {j t} \equiv \gamma_ {j} ^ {i y} (1 + s _ {j t} ^ {i y} + \beta_ {j} (1 - \delta_ {j}) ^ {2} (1 + s _ {j t + 1} ^ {i y})) \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

We assume that the individual country $j$ 's demand for the refined REE product is given by the isoelastic static demand function:

$$
q _ {j t} = a _ {j t} \left(p _ {t} ^ {y}\right) ^ {- \epsilon}
$$

where, as already defined, $a_{jt}$ is the demand shifter and $\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}^{+}$ is the elasticity of substitution of the demand with respect to its price, which is assumed to be common across all countries. Then, the world's aggregate demand for refined REEs is given by:

$$
Q _ {t} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} q _ {j t} = A _ {t} \left(p _ {t} ^ {y}\right) ^ {- \epsilon}
$$

where $A_{t} \equiv \sum_{j=1}^{J} a_{jt}$ is the aggregate demand shifter for refined REEs.

## 3.6 Steady State Equilibrium

We construct the steady state of the model in which no industrial policies are implemented, i.e., $s_{j}^{x}, s_{j}^{ix}, s_{j}^{y}, s_{j}^{iy} = 0$ for all $j \in J$ . Furthermore, we assume that the discount factor and the depreciation rates for miners and refiners are common across countries. Let $\beta \equiv \frac{1}{1+r}$ , where r is the discount rate. Then, the steady state of the model is characterized by the following equations. On the supply side, mining output by producer j is given by:

$$
x _ {j} = \frac {p ^ {x} - \alpha_ {j} ^ {x} - \beta^ {x} \alpha_ {j} ^ {i x}}{B _ {j} ^ {x}}\tag{11}
$$

where $\beta^{x} \equiv 1 - \beta(1 - \eta) = \frac{r + \eta}{1 + r}$ and $B_{j}^{x} \equiv \gamma_{j}^{ix}\eta \beta^{x}$ . Processing output is:

$$
y _ {j} = \frac {p ^ {y} - p ^ {x} - \alpha_ {j} ^ {y} - \beta^ {y} \alpha_ {j} ^ {i y}}{B _ {j} ^ {y}}\tag{12}
$$

where $\beta^{y} \equiv 1 - \beta(1 - \delta) = \frac{r + \delta}{1 + r}$ and $B_{j}^{y} \equiv \gamma_{j}^{iy} \delta \beta^{y}$ . On the demand side, aggregate demand is:

$$
Q = A (p ^ {y}) ^ {\epsilon}\tag{13}
$$

where $A \equiv \sum_{j} a_{j}$ . Market clearing requires $Q = Y$ and material balance requires $Y = X$ , where $Y \equiv \sum_{j} y_{j}$ and $X \equiv \sum_{j} x_{j}$ .

The equilibrium prices satisfy two conditions. First, the mining price as a function of the processed material price is:

$$
p ^ {x} = \frac {p ^ {y} \Gamma^ {y} - \Psi^ {y} + \Theta^ {x}}{\Gamma^ {y} + \Gamma^ {x}}\tag{14}
$$

Second, the processed material price satisfies the nonlinear equilibrium condition:

$$
A (p ^ {y}) ^ {\epsilon} (\Gamma^ {y} + \Gamma^ {x}) + \Theta^ {x} \Gamma^ {y} + \Psi^ {y} \Gamma^ {x} = p ^ {y} \Gamma^ {y} \Gamma^ {x}\tag{15}
$$

or, stated otherwise:

$$
A (p ^ {y}) ^ {\epsilon} = \frac {(p ^ {y} \Gamma^ {y} - \Psi^ {y}) \Gamma^ {x} - \Theta^ {x} \Gamma^ {y}}{\Gamma^ {y} + \Gamma^ {x}}\tag{16}
$$

The parameter bundles are defined as follows:

\- $\Gamma^y \equiv \sum_j \frac{1}{B_j^y} = \sum_j \frac{1}{\gamma_j^{iy} \delta \beta^y}$ (aggregate processing supply responsiveness)

\- $\Psi^y \equiv \sum_j \frac{\alpha_j^y + \beta^y \alpha_j^{iy}}{B_j^y}$ (weighted processing costs)

\- $\Gamma^x \equiv \sum_j \frac{1}{B_j^x} = \sum_j \frac{1}{\gamma_j^{ix} \eta \beta^x}$ (aggregate mining supply responsiveness)

\- $\Theta^x \equiv \sum_j \frac{\alpha_j^x + \beta_j^x \alpha_j^{ix}}{B_j^x}$ (weighted mining costs)

## 4 Calibration

In this section we describe the calibration strategy used to derive the quantitative implications of the global REE market model.

## 4.1 Calibration overview

We calibrate the model to a 2024 base year using data from publicly available feasibility studies, industry reports, and the academic literature. The calibration proceeds in four steps. First, we fix the demand elasticity and country-level demand shifters using published elasticity estimates and observed consumption shares. Second, we set the cost intercept parameters—operating costs $(\alpha_{j}^{x}, \alpha_{j}^{y})$ and investment cost intercepts $(\alpha_{j}^{ix}, \alpha_{j}^{iy})$ —using a breakeven analysis of rare earth projects. $^{16}$ Where separate cost evidence for mining and refining is unavailable—as is the case for several major producers, including China—the decomposition should be read as an explicit calibration assumption rather than as a directly observed accounting split. Third, we decompose the investment cost slope parameters in two-parts: a common element $\kappa^{is}$ and a country-specific element $\bar{\gamma}_{j}^{is}$ , such that $\gamma_{j}^{is} = \kappa^{is} \bar{\gamma}_{j}^{is}$ for $s = \{x, y\}$ . We then calibrate the investment cost slope parameters $(\bar{\gamma}_{j}^{ix}, \bar{\gamma}_{j}^{iy})$ to match observed mining and refining production shares across regions. Fourth, we pin down two remaining scale parameters $(\kappa^{ix}, \kappa^{iy})$ , which govern the absolute level of equilibrium prices. Table 1 summarizes all parameter values.

## 4.2 Demand Parameters

The short-run price elasticity of demand for refined REEs is set to $\varepsilon = -0.15$ , based on the unweighted average of elasticity estimates for the four magnet REEs (Nd, Pr, Dy, Tb) from Shojaeddini et al. (2025). This low elasticity reflects the essential role of these elements in permanent magnets and the limited substitutability of REEs in downstream applications over short horizons. $^{17}$

Country-level demand shares are constructed as follows. China accounts for 50 percent of global refined REE consumption in the base year, following Shao et al. (2026). The remaining 50 percent is allocated across other regions in proportion to their shares of Chinese REE exports in 2019 (also from Shao et al. (2026)). Given these shares and the isoelastic demand specification $q_{j} = a_{j}(p^{y})^{\varepsilon}$ , we back out the demand shifters $a_{j}$ that rationalize observed 2024 consumption at the equilibrium refined price. We then solve the model using the standardized demand shifters given by $\bar{a}_{j} \equiv \frac{a_{j}}{\sum_{j} a_{j}}$ , which allow us to express each country's demand as a share of total world demand when the price of the refined good equals 1.

Demand growth rates are taken from the IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook (International Energy Agency, 2025), which projects annual growth of 4.7 percent during 2025–2029, 1.47 percent during 2030–2034, and zero thereafter. In the model, these enter as exogenous shifts in the demand parameters $a_{j}$ over time, preserving relative country shares.

## 4.3 Cost Intercept Parameters

The model features four cost intercept parameters per region: operating costs for mining $(\alpha_{j}^{x})$ and refining $(\alpha_{j}^{y})$ , and investment cost intercepts for mining $(\alpha_{j}^{ix})$ and refining $(\alpha_{j}^{iy})$ . We calibrate these using a two-step procedure that first recovers integrated breakeven prices and then decomposes them across the two supply chain stages.

Breakeven methodology. For producers outside of China, we evaluate 21 rare earth projects with published feasibility studies. For each project, we compute the NdPr-equivalent breakeven price—the constant real price at which net present value equals zero under an 8 percent real discount rate—taking into account operating expenditures (OPEX), upfront capital expenditures (CAPEX), mine life, annual production volumes, and project-specific payability assumptions. $^{18}$ The representative breakeven price outside of China, expressed in 2024 USD per kilogram of NdPr, is \$77/kg, of which \$43/kg reflects operating costs and \$34/kg reflects the annualized

capital charge.

We map the capital component into the model's investment cost intercept using the user-cost identity:

$$
\text { CAPEX   annuity } = \frac {r + \eta}{1 + r} \alpha_ {j} ^ {i},\tag{17}
$$

where r = 0.08 is the discount rate and $\eta = 0.035$ is the annual depreciation rate of productive capacity. $^{19}$ This expression reflects the annual cost of maintaining one unit of productive capacity: each year, a fraction $\eta$ of capacity depreciates and must be replaced, and the capital tied up in the investment carries an opportunity cost of r. Inverting equation (17) yields $\alpha_{j}^{i} = CAPEX\ annuity / \left(\frac{r + \eta}{1 + r}\right).^{20}$ Applied to the integrated capital charge of 34/kg, this gives a total investment cost intercept for producers outside of China of $\alpha^{i} = 34/0.1065 \approx 318 USD/kg$ of annual NdPr capacity.

Mining-refining decomposition. Because the model distinguishes between upstream mining and midstream oxide separation, we decompose the integrated cost estimates into stage-level parameters. This requires two assumptions, both guided by project-level data and industry evidence. $^{21}$

First, we allocate 70 percent of operating costs to mining and 30 percent to refining, reflecting the relatively higher labor, energy, and material costs associated with extraction and beneficiation. Since the operating cost intercepts in the model correspond directly to per-kilogram operating costs, this yields $\alpha^{x} \approx 30$ USD/kg and $\alpha^{y} \approx 13$ USD/kg. $^{22}$ Second, we allocate 40 percent of annualized capital costs to mining and 60 percent to refining. The heavier capital weight on refining reflects the high capital intensity of chemical separation plants relative to extraction. Applying equation (17) to each stage separately yields the investment cost intercepts $\alpha^{ix} \approx 127$

USD/kg and $\alpha^{iy} \approx 191$ USD/kg. $^{23}$ Under this decomposition, mining accounts for approximately 56 percent of the integrated breakeven cost outside of China, and refining for 44 percent. $^{24}$ The resulting parameter values are $\alpha^{x} = 30$ , $\alpha^{ix} = 127$ , $\alpha^{y} = 13$ , and $\alpha^{iy} = 191$ USD/kg for all regions outside of China.

We then normalize all cost intercepts by $\alpha^{iy}$ , the marginal investment cost of refiners outside of China, and denote the transformed variables with a "bar." $^{25}$ Hence, for all economies outside of China, the parameter values are $\bar{\alpha}^{x}=30/191$ , $\bar{\alpha}^{ix}=127/191$ , $\bar{\alpha}^{y}=13/191$ , and $\bar{\alpha}^{iy}=191/191$ .

Chinese cost parameters. For China, we anchor the calibration to the historical median NdPr price of \$42.5/kg (in 2024 dollars), inferred from the 2006–2010 period during which China held a near-monopoly in REE production and the industry had not yet undergone state-led consolidation. Under these conditions, competition among firms drove prices toward marginal cost, making observed prices a reasonable proxy for breakeven costs.

Decomposing this single breakeven into stage-level cost parameters requires three assumptions. First, we assume that 75 percent of this breakeven reflects operating costs and 25 percent capital costs, consistent with China's established infrastructure, scale efficiencies, and accumulated process know-how. This implies total operating costs of approximately \$31.9/kg and a total annualized capital charge of approximately \$10.6/kg. $^{26}$ Applying equation (17) to the total capital charge gives a total investment cost intercept of $\alpha^{i} \approx 99$ USD/kg. $^{27}$

Second, to decompose across supply chain stages, we allocate roughly 69 percent of China's total breakeven cost to mining and 31 percent to refining, yielding stage-level total costs of approximately \$29.3/kg for mining and \$13.2/kg for refining. $^{28}$ This allocation is a calibration assumption reflecting China's disproportionately strong cost advantage in refining, so that refining accounts for only 31 percent of total costs in China, compared to 44 percent in projects outside of China.

Third, guided by the higher capital intensity of oxide separation relative to extraction, we allocate approximately 30 percent of the annualized capital charge to mining and 70 percent to refining. This is a calibration assumption motivated by the fact that even in China, oxide separation remains more capital-intensive than extraction, requiring specialized solvent extraction circuits and environmental containment infrastructure. Applying equation (17) to each stage separately yields $\alpha^{ix} \approx 30$ USD/kg and $\alpha^{iy} \approx 69$ USD/kg. $^{29}$ The operating cost intercepts follow residually: subtracting each stage's annualized capital charge from its total cost gives $\alpha^{x} \approx 26$ USD/kg and $\alpha^{y} \approx 6$ USD/kg. $^{30}$

A notable feature of this calibration is that the majority of the total cost gap between Chinese producers and those outside China—approximately 58 percent—originates in the refining stage rather than mining. $^{31}$ This pattern is consistent with the well-documented concentration of China's technological advantage in oxide separation, the most technically demanding and capital-intensive segment of the rare earth supply chain (Bedford, 2025). We then normalize the cost intercepts of Chinese miners and refiners. This yields $\bar{\alpha}^{x} = 26/191$ , $\bar{\alpha}^{ix} = 30/191$ , $\bar{\alpha}^{y} = 6/191$ , and $\bar{\alpha}^{iy} = 69/191$ .

Cost uniformity outside of China. We assume identical cost parameters across all regions outside China. Our evaluation of 21 projects reveals that, while these projects are on average substantially less competitive than Chinese production, there is little evidence of a systematic cost ranking across non-Chinese producers. This assumption simplifies the calibration and focuses cross-country variation on differences in production scale, as captured by the investment slope

parameters.

## 4.4 Investment Slope Parameters

The investment cost slope parameters $\gamma_{j}^{ix}$ and $\gamma_{j}^{iy}$ govern the convexity of investment costs and thereby influence the equilibrium scale of production in each region. First, we define $\gamma_{j}^{is} \equiv \kappa^{s} \bar{\gamma}_{j}^{is}$ for each sector $s = \{x, y\}$ , where $\kappa^{s} > 0$ is common across all countries and $\bar{\gamma}_{j}^{is} > 0$ is country-specific. We calibrate these parameters to match observed 2024 mining and refining production shares, with the U.S. normalized to $\bar{\gamma}_{US}^{ix} = \bar{\gamma}_{US}^{iy} = 1$ .

Identification. The key identification argument is that, in steady state, relative production levels across regions are determined by the ratios of $\gamma$ parameters. To see this, consider two regions outside of China, j and m, with identical cost intercepts. From the steady-state mining expression, $x_{j} = (p^{x} - \alpha^{x} - \beta^{x}\alpha^{ix}) / (\gamma_{j}^{ix}\eta\beta^{x})$ , it follows that

$$
\frac {x _ {j}}{x _ {m}} = \frac {\gamma_ {m} ^ {i x}}{\gamma_ {j} ^ {i x}}.\tag{18}
$$

Because cost parameters are identical across all regions outside China, gross profit margins cancel, and relative output depends only on the inverse ratio of slope parameters. An analogous relationship holds for refining. Normalizing $\bar{\gamma}_{US}^{ix}=1$ , the slope parameter for any region outside of China, j, is therefore

$$
\bar {\gamma} _ {j} ^ {i x} = \frac {x _ {\mathrm{US}}}{x _ {j}},\tag{19}
$$

where $x_{j}$ denotes the 2024 mining production share of region j. A similar argument holds for refining.

China. For China, the same logic applies but the cost intercepts differ from those outside China, so gross margins no longer cancel. Taking ratios of the steady-state expressions for China and the U.S. yields

$$
\bar {\gamma} _ {\mathrm{CHN}} ^ {i x} = \frac {x _ {\mathrm{US}}}{x _ {\mathrm{CHN}}} \cdot \frac {p ^ {x} - \alpha_ {\mathrm{CHN}} ^ {x} - \beta^ {x} \alpha_ {\mathrm{CHN}} ^ {i x}}{p ^ {x} - \alpha_ {\mathrm{US}} ^ {x} - \beta^ {x} \alpha_ {\mathrm{US}} ^ {i x}},\tag{20}
$$

and analogously for refining. Evaluating this expression requires the equilibrium mining price $p^{x}$ , which depends on all model parameters through the steady-state price conditions. In practice, we compute $\gamma_{CHN}^{ix}$ jointly with the price-level calibration described in the next subsection.

Zero-production regions. Several regions exhibit zero production in one segment of the supply chain in the base year: the EU and Japan have no mining, while the Anglophone region has no refining. In these cases, the $\gamma$ parameter is not identified by equation (18). We therefore assign large values to these parameters, reflecting prohibitively high investment adjustment costs consistent with the absence of current production. Operationally, these values are chosen so that zero-production regions remain inactive at the initial price vector; the global material balance condition X = Y is then satisfied by market clearing rather than by these parameters per se.

## 4.5 Price-Level Calibration

The procedure in Section 4.4 pins down the relative $\gamma$ parameters across regions but leaves their absolute scale—and hence the level of equilibrium prices—undetermined. We close the calibration by introducing two scaling parameters, $\kappa^{ix}$ and $\kappa^{iy}$ , which multiply all mining and refining slope parameters, respectively. Formally, the effective investment cost slope for region j in mining is $\kappa^{ix}\bar{\gamma}_{j}^{ix}$ , and analogously $\kappa^{iy}\bar{\gamma}_{j}^{iy}$ for refining.

These parameters are identified by using two equilibrium price targets. First, we numerically match the refined price to $p^{y} = 1$ in the initial steady state, which serves as a normalization of the unit of account. $^{32}$ Second, we target a mining-to-refining price ratio of $p^{x}/p^{y} = 0.55$ , so that the mining stage receives 55 percent of integrated revenues in steady state. This ratio is consistent with our breakeven decomposition, which attributes approximately 55 percent of total costs to the mining segment, for both Chinese producers and those outside of China.

Given all other parameters, $\kappa^{ix}$ and $\kappa^{iy}$ are jointly determined by the steady-state equilibrium price conditions. The calibrated values are $\kappa^{ix} = 600$ and $\kappa^{iy} = 5{,}000$ .

## 4.6 Numerical Algorithm

Given the model parameters, we first compute the deterministic steady state of the economy in the absence of industrial policy. We begin with an initial guess for the final good price and solve for the optimal steady-state levels of mining and refining capacity for each economy over a grid of intermediate good prices $p^{x}$ . For each point on the grid, we construct aggregate demand and supply and compute the equilibrium price such that excess supply of raw REEs is zero. We then obtain the final good price, update our initial guess, and iterate until convergence. Next we solve for the model dynamics in a similar fashion, using an open-loop equilibrium approach, i.e., one in which the paths of all policies are determined at time t = 0. We start from the initial conditions $\{x_{j,-1}, y_{j,-1}\}_{j=1}^{J}$ and construct initial guesses for the time path of the final good price, $\{p_{t}^{0,y}\}_{t=0}^{T}$ , as well as for the state variables, $\{x_{j,t}^{0}\}_{j,t}$ and $\{y_{j,t}^{0}\}_{j,t}$ . Given these guesses, we solve the system of difference equations forward for each point on the intermediate good price grid and compute the equilibrium price at each date t. We then use the equilibrium prices and quantities obtained from the model to update the guessed price path and state variables, and iterate until convergence.

## 4.7 Summary

Table 1 reports the complete set of calibrated parameters. Several features of the calibration merit emphasis. First, the cost intercepts embed a large competitive gap between producers in China and those in other regions, with roughly half of this gap concentrated in the refining stage. Second, China's low $\gamma$ values reflect its dominant production shares—the investment cost function is relatively flat, allowing China to scale capacity at low marginal adjustment cost. Third, the high $\gamma$ values for regions with zero production imply that entry would require substantial policy intervention to overcome steep investment costs, a feature that is central to the policy experiments analyzed in the following sections. Fourth, the model is solved using standardized parameters only, i.e., parameters denoted with a bar.

We also report the results of the matching moments exercise used to obtain the baseline calibration in Table 2. As noted above, $\bar{\gamma}_{\mathrm{CHINA}}^{ix}$ is chosen to match China's steady-state in mining, which is approximately 0.8. Given the construction of the remaining marginal mining CAPEX parameters, this procedure also allows us to recover the exact mining shares of all the other countries/blocks. Similarly, we choose $\bar{\gamma}_{\mathrm{CHINA}}^{iy}$ to match China's steady-state share in refining, which is approximately 0.91. Finally, we set $\kappa^{ix}$ and $\kappa^{iy}$ to normalize the steady-state price of the refined product $p_{SS}^{y}$ to 1, and obtain a relative price of the mined product $p_{SS}^{x} / p_{SS}^{y}$ around 0.55.

## 5 Quantitative Results

This section analyzes the quantitative implications of the global REE model described in this paper. First, we begin by presenting the results arising from simulating the model under current policies. We further expand the discussion of the structure of the global REE market by examining the supply and demand elasticities. Second, we describe the consequences of a potential global REE supply shock. Third, we discuss the effects of introducing different industrial policies in net importing economies, focusing our attention on the special case of the US. $^{33}$ Fourth, we show that the fiscal cost of industrial policies is convex in the degree of attained de-risking. Throughout this section we refer to "importing countries" and "economies outside China" interchangeably as in the special case of REEs, and given the current market structure, the two groups coincide.

## 5.1 Baseline Results

Using the calibration strategy outlined above, we simulate the model for a period of T = 150 years starting in 2025. $^{34}$ In the model we use as initial conditions the observed levels of mining and refining quantities in 2024, outlined in Table 1. We feed the model with the anticipated demand growth rates described in Table 1 and construct the paths of mining and refining output, final good demand, and price levels. We report the results of the baseline in Figure 4.

The projected increase in demand raises mining and refining production across all the economies, with only modest changes in their distributions across countries. In the case of the U.S., the increase in refined REE production leads to only a marginal improvement in de-risking relative to China. The price of the refined REE good increases on impact to accommodate the initial excess demand from consumers, and rises further during the period of stronger demand between 2030 and 2034. As demand growth stabilizes and investment in mining and refining increases, prices begin to decline and converge to a new steady state. In the new steady state, equilibrium prices remain higher, due to the quadratic functional forms of OPEX and CAPEX.

We investigate the degree of rigidity in both upstream and downstream segments of the market by analyzing demand and supply elasticities for both miners and refiners, in both the short-run and the long-run. In order to compute the short-run supply and demand elasticities we use (9) and (10), which yield the following expressions for elasticities:

On impact, the supply disruption causes a sharp increase in prices in both markets, i.e., the market for mined and refined products. In the first year, the price of refined REEs spikes to almost 80 times the baseline price, while mined REE products experience a smaller increase, around 7.4 times the baseline price. The structure of the downstream market plays a key role. Since refineries outside China are less productive than Chinese ones, the shock has a much larger impact on refining than on mining. Additionally, the price of mined products is partially capped by the positive net supply shock in the upstream market, as China is a net importer of raw materials.

The increase in prices plays two roles. First, it reduces demand for refined REE products, which falls by around 48% on impact. Second, it stimulates investment in refining capacity, leading to a more than threefold increase in the supply of refined products. The expansion in refining, in turn, raises demand for mined inputs, which increases to about 1.3 times its baseline level. As a result, the equilibrium price of mined products also rises on impact, although less than that of refined products. Despite the initial positive net supply shock for miners outside of China, the disruption in the downstream market is large enough to drive up both prices and increase upstream production in order to restore equilibrium in the downstream market. The strong response of prices, combined with the limited adjustment of quantities, highlights the low supply and demand elasticities of the REE market in the short run.

In the long run, CAPEX investment in both upstream and downstream markets gradually expands capacity. As a result, mining (refining) output rises to about 2.1 (4.5) times its pre-shock level, and prices begin to decline relatively quickly. In the upstream market, the equilibrium price falls from 7.4 to 1.7 times the baseline price, remaining about 70% above the baseline. Similarly, in the downstream market, the equilibrium price of refined products declines from 80 to 2.5 times the baseline, i.e., about 150% above baseline. The combination of higher production and lower prices allows final consumers to recover part of the lost demand relatively quickly, which converges to around 89% of its baseline level, implying a long run loss of about 11% of the baseline demand. Contrary to the short run, quantities adjust more flexibly in the long run, highlighting our finding that supply and demand elasticities of the REE market are higher in the long run.

## 5.3 Industrial Policies

In response to rising vulnerabilities in REE supply, countries and firms are adopting a range of adaptation strategies. Stockpiling provides a short-term buffer against disruptions, but does not address underlying structural dependence, while recycling offers longer-term potential, although it cannot yet serve as a primary supply source. Reshoring and import diversification have emerged as the main medium-term responses, despite uncertain viability given long development timelines, coordination challenges, and potential shortages of skilled labor.

Because reshoring is costly, policymakers must balance efficiency losses in normal times against expected disruption losses in crisis periods. Optimal de-risking is best understood as an insurance policy: the premium paid in normal times (efficiency losses) should be commensurate with the expected damage in crisis times (disruption losses). Moreover, security benefits are likely to exhibit diminishing returns, with the first 10-20 percent increase in de-risking delivering the largest reduction in vulnerability. This means that even modest increases in de-risking can substantially reduce vulnerability to supply disruptions at relatively low efficiency costs (Clayton et al., 2024).

To improve the degree of de-risking, which we define as the total consumption of refined REEs that is either produced at home or from other countries other than the market leader, we ask the model to assess the effects of two industrial policies (investment subsidies and stylized revenue-support policies) applied to refiners, under two implementation scenarios: (1) unilateral action by the United States, in which the policy instruments apply only to U.S.-based producers, and (2) simultaneous action among all importing countries (U.S., EU, Japan, Australia, and other economies), in which instruments are applied uniformly across participating countries. Because trade in the model is frictionless and homogeneous, the resulting country-level de-risking statistics should be interpreted as reduced-form concentration measures rather than literal bilateral sourcing predictions. We focus on refiners only because, as discussed above, a global supply shock primarily affects the downstream segment, while the impact on miners is more limited. $^{35}$

We start by selecting a constant CAPEX subsidy rate (or revenue-support rate) that would allow the U.S., in this example, to reach a 25% level of de-risking by 2035. This is 15 percentage points higher than our baseline and in line with IEA projections which show that, under currently announced projects outside China and stated government policies, de-risking levels outside of China would rise from about 8 percent to roughly 24 percent over the next decade. We then compare the fiscal cost of achieving the same benchmark increase in de-risking for the U.S. by allowing all importer countries to simultaneously implement similar policies. The same kind of exercise with very similar results could have been run focusing on either one of the importing countries of our model. We then repeat the same exercise for a more ambitious target of 50%.

In the model, the revenue-support instrument is implemented as a proportional sales subsidy to refiners rather than as a literal state-contingent floor that binds only when market prices fall below a trigger. Accordingly, the revenue-support rate for refiners is defined as $s_{jt}^{y} \equiv f_{j}^{y} - 1$ , where $f_{j}^{y} \geq 1$ denotes the policy wedge set by the policymaker in country j. This formulation captures the broad idea of downside revenue support for refiners, but it should not be interpreted as a full contractual representation of an offtake agreement or a state-contingent contract. Conversely, the CAPEX subsidy satisfies $s_{jt}^{iy} \in [-1, 0]$ .

We next describe how the degree of de-risking is computed. For each country $j \in \{1, \ldots, J\}$ and each period $t \in \{0, \ldots, T\}$ , we construct the matrix of net import flows $M_{t}$ , which collects the net flow of imports of refined REEs from country j to country i. We begin by computing each country's share of global exports as:

$$
x s _ {j, t} = \frac {\max \left(y _ {j , t} - q _ {j , t} , 0\right)}{\sum_ {i} \max \left(y _ {i , t} - q _ {i , t} , 0\right)}
$$

Given that goods are homogeneous across countries, we then allocate each country's net imports in proportion to each net exporter's share of the world's exports as follows:

$$
m _ {i j, t} = x s _ {j, t} \cdot \max \left(q _ {i, t} - y _ {i, t}, 0\right)
$$

where $m_{ij,t}$ is the generic element of the matrix of net import flows $M_{t}$ at time t that indicates imports from country j to country i. For each country i, we define the degree of de-risking as:

$$
\sigma_ {i, t} = 1 - \frac {\max _ {j \in \mathcal {J}} m _ {i j , t}}{q _ {i , t}}
$$

where $\max_{j\in J} m_{ij,t}$ denotes country i's imports from its largest supplier. $^{36}$ According to our measure, a self-sufficient country is a country that does not import excessive amount of consumption from any single country. Values of $\sigma_{i,t}$ close to 1 indicate high levels of de-risking, whereas values close to 0 indicate strong dependence on exports from any single country.

However, simultaneous action among various importers does not necessarily imply lower subsidy rates or lower revenue-support wedges. Intuitively, because the U.S. targets refined products produced outside of China, the industry's largest exporting country, it can rely on production from all other participating countries. Since the U.S. is relatively more efficient than other importers, inducing additional production in less efficient countries may require stronger policy support, as is the case for CAPEX subsidies under the $25\%$ de-risking target.

While the degree of de-risking is independent from the policy instrument, the fiscal cost of these industrial policies differs substantially. Figure 7 reports the fiscal cost across all policy experiments. Fiscal costs $\Phi_{jt}$ are expressed relative to baseline consumption as follows:

$$

(i) For a given policy instrument, simultaneous action reduces the fiscal cost to achieve a given level of de-risking fo the U.S. and all participating countries. As all net REEs importers increase their output, the U.S. can attain higher de-risking levels with a smaller expansion in domestic production and relying more on imports from other participating countries, thereby limiting fiscal costs even when policy rates are comparable (or slightly higher). In a similar fashion, as simultaneous action increases global REE output more than unilateral action, part of the increment is absorbed by consumption in all other countries, who successfully manage to partially de-risk their supply chain.

(ii) The fiscal cost of CAPEX subsidies is initially higher than that of the revenue-support policy, but substantially lower in the long run. On the one hand, investment precedes production during growth phases. Therefore, CAPEX subsidies frontload fiscal costs in the early years, with lower costs later on as investment needs decline. On the other hand, the price floor backloads fiscal costs: as production and prices rise over time, fiscal costs increase and remain elevated as the economy converges towards a new steady state.

(iii) The fiscal cost increases faster than the increase in achieved de-risking levels. Taking unilateral revenue support as a reference, the cost of achieving 25% de-risking levels by 2035 is about 13.4% of baseline consumption in 2025, whereas the cost exceeds 44.5% if the U.S. was targeting 50% by 2035. In the long-run, the fiscal cost of the unilateral revenue-support policy stabilizes around 41.6% of baseline consumption for a 25% target, but rises to over 269% for the 50% target. Hence, doubling the de-risking target more than doubles the fiscal cost. Similar results are observed also for the other policy instruments.

Overall, for a 25% de-risking target, the total fiscal cost of industrial policies over the period 2025–2035 is large in terms of market size. CAPEX subsidies amount to 134% of the annual U.S. REE market under unilateral action and 90% under simultaneous action among various importers. The stylized revenue-support policies are significantly more expensive, reaching 248% under unilateral policies and 172% under simultaneous policies. Fiscal costs are even higher when targeting 50% de-risking benchmarks.

## 6 Conclusion

This paper shows that disruptions to REE supplies could substantially affect supply chains. Avoiding trade tensions and restrictions remains the first-best outcome to promote steady REE supply. Model-based analysis suggests that de-risking supply chains through targeted industrial policies is fiscally costly. However, costs are lower if de-risking is pursued by various importers simultaneously and if policy instruments directly target the expansion of new production capacity. Focusing on CAPEX subsidies and price floor mechanisms, we found that simultaneous action allows participating countries to achieve higher levels of de-risking at relatively moderate fiscal costs, particularly when CAPEX subsidies are implemented. Finally, we showed that more ambitious de-risking targets become increasingly costly, with fiscal costs rising exponentially as

de-risking targets increase.

Future research could take a more granular view of the rare earth element complex, which, after all, consists of 17 distinct yet chemically related elements, with the aim of developing a framework that distinguishes between heavy (HREE) and light (LREE) elements and embeds these distinctions in the modeling of supply constraints. Additional policies – like stockpiling or re-shoring – could also be analyzed and the group of producer countries could be enlarged beyond China.

## APPENDIX

## A Model calibration and incentive price methodology

This section documents the data construction and project-level methodology used to calibrate the cost intercept parameters described in Section 4.3. The analysis is based on a bottom-up reconstruction of production, cost, and revenue characteristics for individual rare earth mining (plus processing) projects outside China. These project-level estimates are used to derive the incentive prices employed in Section 4.3 and to construct the economic supply curve shown in Figure 13.

Project scope and coverage. The dataset is constructed from a mine-by-mine assessment of rare earth projects located in Australia, Sub-Saharan Africa, Brazil, Canada, Greenland, Sweden, and the United States. Projects are included only where sufficient public documentation is available, such as technical reports, economic assessments, and prefeasibility or feasibility studies. These documents were identified primarily through the S&P Capital IQ Pro database and supporting project disclosures, occasionally supplemented with more recent publicly available information. The resulting dataset covers 21 non-Chinese projects with sufficient cost, production, and technical information to reconstruct project-level economic metrics.

Manual reconstruction of production profiles. For each project, technical documentation was manually reviewed to extract total rare earth oxide (TREO) production volumes, reported oxide distributions, and annual production quantities for individual rare earth oxides. These data were then used to identify the elements and corresponding annual production volumes that are economically relevant for the incentive price calculations.

Treatment of non-REE revenues. Where applicable, project documentation reports additional by-product revenues from non-rare earth elements. When detailed production data were available, annual volumes of these non-REE minerals were multiplied by corresponding commodity prices to estimate associated revenue streams. In some cases, projects report only nominal annual revenue figures for non-REE minerals, which were incorporated directly into the revenue calculations. However, the incentive price used to construct the supply curve in Figure 13 excludes non-REE revenues so that the break-even condition is determined solely by rare earth project economics.

Capital expenditure. Initial capital expenditures (CAPEX) were taken directly from project documentation. Where reported, sustaining capital expenditures were also included.

Operating expenditure. Operating costs (OPEX) were reconstructed from project documentation using one of three approaches depending on data availability. First, where total life-of-mine operating costs were reported, average annual OPEX was calculated as total life-of-mine operating costs divided by the expected mine lifespan. Second, where studies directly reported an average annual operating expense, this value was used as stated. Third, where operating costs were reported as a unit cost (US\$ per kilogram of TREO), annual OPEX was calculated by multiplying the reported unit cost by average annual TREO production.

Inflation adjustment and discounting. All monetary values were converted to a common 2024–25 cost basis using a constant annual inflation rate of 2%. In addition, a uniform real discount rate of 8% was applied across all projects. These adjustments were applied consistently to CAPEX, OPEX, and revenue components in order to ensure comparability across projects with feasibility studies produced in different years.

Project classification and realized price factors. Projects were grouped into two categories: those producing mixed rare earth chemical products and those producing separated rare earth oxides. Projects producing mixed rare earth chemical products were assigned a realized price factor of 62.5%, while projects producing separated rare earth oxides were assigned a factor of 90%. These factors capture the difference in realized value between partially processed intermediate products and more refined separated oxides, and ensure that differences in processing depth are reflected consistently in project revenues.

Incentive price definition and break-even condition. For each project, we compute an NdPr-equivalent production metric by converting reported rare earth element revenues into an equivalent quantity expressed in NdPr terms. Specifically, we use all revenue information available in project economic documents (e.g., feasibility studies, economic analyses). For some projects, this consists only of revenues from neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium, while for others it covers a broader set of rare earth elements (e.g., Lanthanum, Cerium, Gadolinium, Yttrium, Samarium, Europium). We aggregate these revenues and divide by the 2024 average NdPr oxide price, yielding an implied NdPr-equivalent production capacity:

$$
q ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}} \equiv \frac {\sum_ {i} p _ {o , i} q _ {o , i}}{p _ {o} ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}}}\tag{21}
$$

This normalization converts a project's multi-element revenue stream into a single NdPr-equivalent production metric, effectively capturing the contribution of all reported rare earth elements into a common NdPr-based quantity. $^{38}$ The net present value of a representative project can be written as:

$$
\mathrm{NPV} (p) = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left(p q ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}} + \mathrm{Rev} ^ {\mathrm{nREE}} - \mathrm{OPEX}\right) \frac {1}{(1 + r) ^ {t}} - \mathrm{CAPEX}\tag{22}
$$

where OPEX denotes total cash operating costs, CAPEX measures initial capital expenditures, $Rev^{nREE}$ represents annual revenues from non-REE minerals (e.g., gallium), r is the discount rate, and T is the project life. Prices and quantities are assumed constant over time. Setting NPV = 0 and solving for p yields the incentive price:

$$
p ^ {*} = \frac {\mathrm{CAPEX} + \Omega \cdot (\mathrm{OPEX} - \mathrm{Rev} ^ {\mathrm{nREE}})}{\Omega \cdot q ^ {\mathrm{NdPr}}}\tag{23}
$$

where $\Omega\equiv\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{(1+r)^{t}}$ is the annuity factor. The incentive price $p^{*}$ thus defines the minimum NdPr oxide price at which projected discounted cash flows recover the initial capital outlay, making the project economically viable.

Construction of the economic supply curve. Using the project-level incentive prices defined in equation (23), projects are ranked in ascending order of break-even cost and aggregated cumulatively according to their production capacity. The horizontal axis of Figure 13 reports cumulative NdPr-equivalent production capacity in tons per annum, constructed by converting total project revenues into an equivalent quantity expressed in NdPr terms, while the vertical axis reports the corresponding incentive price in US\$/kg of NdPr oxide. Both quantities and prices are therefore expressed in NdPr terms. $^{39}$ This procedure produces a stepwise economic supply curve that preserves project-level resolution while illustrating the cost distribution of potential non-Chinese rare earth supply. Project labels identify regions, including AFR (Africa), LA (Latin America), NA (North America), OC (Oceania), and RoW (rest of the world).

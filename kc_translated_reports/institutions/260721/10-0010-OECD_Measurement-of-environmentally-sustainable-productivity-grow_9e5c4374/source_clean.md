This report reviews six indices of environmentally sustainable productivity growth (SPG) that integrate agricultural total factor productivity (TFP) growth and trends in four agri-environmental externalities at the national level.

The findings reveal only minor differences across indices when environmental dimensions are assigned low weights, but these differences widen as weights increase. This variation reflects index-specific conditions under which SPG exceeds TFP. Some indices require externalities to grow more slowly than outputs or input (relative decoupling) while others require externalities to decrease (absolute decoupling).

Three case studies (Mexico, The Netherlands and New Zealand), illustrate potential drivers of SPG. In Mexico, improved market access resulting from trade agreements, alongside shifts in agricultural support appeared to be key factors. In the Netherlands, regulatory measures targeting ammonia, fertilisers and manure management contribute to positive SPG outcomes. In New Zealand, performance is associated with export-driven output growth, herd dynamics, and the adoption of new technologies.

## Key Messages

\- This report compares six indices $^{1}$ of environmentally sustainable productivity growth (SPG $^{2}$ ) that integrate agricultural total factor productivity (TFP) growth and trends in four agri-environmental externalities (emissions of greenhouse gases (CH $_{4}$ , N $_{2}$ O, CO $_{2}$ ), ammonia (NH $_{3}$ ) emissions and nitrogen and phosphorus run-off and leaching) at the national level.

• The results show that:

In the case of three of the reviewed indices (EATFPO, EATFPI, ESPII), SPG exceed traditional TFP growth when the selected environmental externalities grow more slowly than agricultural outputs or inputs (relative decoupling).

For the other three indices (ESPIO, WEPI, WEPO), SPG exceed TFP growth only if environmental externalities decline faster than increases in outputs (ESPIO) or in TFP (WEPI/WEPO) (absolute decoupling).

All SPG metrics provide useful information regarding the relative growth rates of and outputs and inputs and environmental externalities. However, positive SPG does not necessarily imply good environmental performance or declining environmental externalities.

\- Across all metrics, differences between SPG and TFP growth increase with the number of environmental dimensions and with the magnitude of their unit prices, reflecting the reduced relative weight of traditional TFP.

\- Case studies for Mexico, the Netherlands and New Zealand illustrate how SPG, which incorporates environmental externalities, compares to traditional TFP growth estimates.

○ Estimated SPG generally exceeds TFP growth for three indices (EATFPO, EATFPI or ESPII) in the three countries, as they experienced relative decoupling of environmental impacts. However, for the other three indices (ESPIO, WEPI, WEPO), SPG is lower than TFP growth for Mexico and New Zealand. This can be explained by the simultaneous increases in environmental externalities and in agricultural outputs and inputs (i.e. no absolute decoupling).

\- Factors explaining trends in agri-environmental performance are country-specific:

In Mexico, major shifts in agricultural support policies during the 1980s/90s, coupled with trade liberalisation and technology adoption in selected value chains, were key drivers of output growth. While externalities increased with this expansion, they did so at a slower pace.


In New Zealand, SPG dynamics reflect structural factors, shocks and technology adoption. In the 1990s, the rapid expansion in herd sizes was followed by a rapid increase in ammonia and greenhouse gases ( $CH_{4}$ , $N_{2}O$ and $CO_{2}$ ). From the mid-2010s, as herd sizes stabilised and with the adoption of improved technologies and practices, the economic value of environmental externalities per unit of input and output began to decline.

# Executive Summary

Achieving sustained output growth in an environmentally sustainable way is key to ensuring food security, while simultaneously providing livelihoods for farmers and improving the environmental sustainability of the sector. This requires producing more with less; achieving sustainable productivity growth is therefore essential for increasing output while reducing environmental pressures. Traditional productivity metrics, however, typically focus only on conventional outputs and inputs, leaving aside environmental externalities.

Several indices have been proposed in the literature to measure increases in agricultural productivity in ways that account for environmental dimensions. This report reviews alternative measures using six indices of environmentally sustainable productivity growth (SPG), by comparing their sensitivity to methodological choices. The six indices considered are: environmentally adjusted TFP indicators with input (EATFP-I) and output (EATFP-O) adjustments, the environmentally sustainable productivity index with input (ESPII) and output (ESPIO) adjustments, and the weak environment-productivity growth indicator where the weights of the externality are given by their importance relative to the value of inputs (WEPI) and outputs (WEPO).

Calculating an environmentally sustainable productivity index relies on several assumptions and choices, including the intended use of the index, the environmental dimensions included, and the perspective of the environmental valuation (producer vs. societal valuation). Each of these choices involves trade-offs between simplicity of estimation for improved interpretation versus more realistic representation with complex estimations. While many choices are technical, the perspective used for the valuation also depends on the intended use of the index and whether the index intends to capture sector-specific or broader social welfare impacts.

The analysis shows that SPG metrics differ in important ways. Some treat the environment as an output, others as an input, and they aggregate traditional and environmental outputs and inputs in different ways. As a result, growth rates derived from different indices vary, as do the conditions under which SPG rates exceed those of TFP.

\- Most indices rely on the concept of an environmentally adjusted output (or traditional output) growing faster than traditional input (or environmentally adjusted input).

\- Some indices (EATFPI, EATFPO, ESPII) require only relative decoupling between the output or inputs and externalities for their growth rates to exceed those of traditional TFP.

\- Others (WEPI, WEPO, ESPIO) require absolute decoupling (i.e. a decrease in the overall level of externalities at a certain speed).

These differences stem from the way in which different methods aggregate environmental aspects and outputs or inputs.

Positive SPG rates do not necessarily imply a good environmental performance of the agricultural sector or that the production methods can be maintained over the long-term without degrading natural ecosystems. Instead, SPG metrics provide a valuable measure of economic performance that integrates the environment. While this report does not assess the environmental performance of agricultural production systems, it highlights the need for complementary analyses with indicators that also track the levels of environmental externalities.

As an illustration, SPG metrics and TFP growth rates were estimated under several different methodological assumptions for Mexico, the Netherlands and New Zealand. These estimations were conducted from 1990 onwards, using agri-environmental indicators representing four environmental externalities (direct emissions of greenhouse gases $CH_{4}$ , $N_{2}O$ and $CO_{2}$ , ammonia emissions, and estimates of nitrogen leaching and phosphorus run-off). Two insights emerge:

\- First, when weights assigned to the environment are low relative to those assigned to traditional outputs/inputs, differences across the indices are small. This is intuitive as traditional TFP drives these results in these cases.

\- Second, as the weight assigned to the environmental externalities increases, differences between indices become more visible. In countries where relative (but not absolute) decoupling of externalities from output/input has occurred (e.g. Mexico and New Zealand), SPG rates are generally higher than TFP growth for 3 out of 6 indices. For the Netherlands, where the reduction of externalities has been faster than output and input growth since the 1990s, SPG rates are consistently higher than traditional TFP.

Further country-level insights can be drawn by disaggregating trends in SPG metrics.

\- Components explaining SPG differ across countries: whereas environmental performance (and ammonia emissions in particular) contributes the most towards SPG in the Netherlands, the growth in traditional outputs and inputs remain the key components explaining SPG in the cases of Mexico and New Zealand, with adjustments being relatively small.

\- Drivers of these components are country-specific: in Mexico, improved market access as a result of trade agreements, alongside shifts in agricultural support and output expansion are considered key factors. In the Netherlands, regulatory factors (especially targeting ammonia, fertilisers and manure) are considered key drivers of positive adjustments to SPG. In New Zealand, export-driven output expansion, herd dynamics, and technology or practice adoption are seen as important explanations for the observed performance over time.

## 1 Introduction

Environmentally sustainable productivity growth – ensuring that output increases are achieved in environmentally sustainable way – is a key element in addressing the triple challenge of food systems (OECD, 2021[1]). Globally, total factor productivity (TFP) growth remains the main driver of output growth since the 1990s (Fuglie, Morgan and Jelliffe, 2024[2]). At the same time, agriculture continues to have a substantial environmental footprint, accounting for approximately 70% of global freshwater demand (Gruère, Shigemitsu and Crawford, 2020[3]), occupying nearly 40% of terrestrial land (OECD, 2019[4]) and accounting for approximately 14% of emissions of greenhouse gases (GHG), $^{3}$ consisting of CO $_{2}$ , CH $_{4}$ , N $_{2}$ O (Tubiello et al., 2021[5]; FAO, 2024[6]).


Monitoring improvements across both dimensions underscores the need to use metrics capable of capturing both production and environmental dimensions. By combining information on agricultural production and agri-environmental performance, measures of environmentally adjusted productivity growth offer one potential way forward (OECD, 2024[8]). Integrating environmental dimensions into productivity metrics, however, remains complex.

The challenge of constructing environmentally sustainable productivity measurements is not confined to the agricultural sector. Beyond the agricultural sector, economy-wide metrics, such as the Environmentally adjusted multifactor productivity (EAMFP), already exist (Cárdenas Rodríguez et al., 2023[9]; Cárdenas Rodríguez, Haščič and Souchier, 2018[10]). In the agricultural sector, multiple approaches have been proposed to measure environmentally sustainable productivity growth (SPG), as summarised in recent publications (OECD, 2022[11]; Bureau and Antón, 2022[12]). Differences in the metric used, the environmental dimensions considered, and the valuation of environmental externalities make direct comparison across different SPG metrics challenging.

The aim of this paper is three-fold. First, it aims to highlight several methodological choices when developing an SPG metric applied to the agricultural sector. This includes the choice of the index, the choice of the environmental dimensions to be included and the value (weight) assigned to the environmental externalities. Second, using three OECD countries as case studies (Mexico, the Netherlands, and New Zealand $^{4}$ ), the sensitivity of SPG rates to these different choices (choice of index, weights, and aggregation method) is tested. Finally, the paper then interprets the country-specific SPG rates considering the country-specific performance across various dimensions of agri-environmental performance and proposes different alternatives to visualise the results.

# 2 Overview of several existing environmentally adjusted productivity metrics

## Developing a SPG metric requires several important methodological choices

Developing an environmentally adjusted productivity metric requires several key methodological choices. $^{5}$ This paper discusses three particularly important ones: a) the choice of index, b) the selection of environmental dimensions to include; and c) the approach used to value environmental externalities in constructive SPG estimates.

The first choice relates to the index which is used to coherently aggregate the traditional productivity components (outputs and inputs) and the environmental dimensions. Several ways have been proposed to measure SPG. This paper focuses on Environmentally adjusted TFP indices using a growth accounting framework (EATFP), the Environmentally Sustainable Productivity Index (ESPI), as well as the Weak Environmental Performance (WEP) metric. All indicators are explained in the next sub-section $^{6}$ .

The second key methodological choice concerns the environmental dimensions included in the SPG metric. Previous research has incorporated environmental dimensions into productivity measures in different ways. Some studies have adjusted productivity metrics for weather shocks, whereas others have focused on accounting for single or multiple environmental externalities (Baldoni et al., 2025[13]; Ang, Dakpo and Pieralli, 2022[14]; Gu, Hussain and Willox, 2019[15]; Chancellor et al., 2021[16]; Nanere et al., 2007[17]; Fuglie et al., 2020[18]; Färe et al., 1993[19]; OECD, 2022[11]). Importantly, although environmentally adjusted metrics can, in principle, incorporate both positive and negative externalities, most existing research concentrates on negative externalities.

The third choice relates to the valuation of the environmental variables and the selection of weights associated with the environmental dimension. Constructing an SPG metric requires determining the relative weight of the environmental dimension(s) vis-à-vis production. Importantly, while this is, to a large extent, a methodological choice, this choice has profound effects on the impact of the included environmental dimensions on the growth rates. Beyond this, it can also lead to a different interpretation of the metrics. Producer-centric valuations of the environment, while arguably more methodologically consistent, $^{7}$ disregard off-farm impacts of the externalities (e.g. on health, biodiversity). Valuations that go beyond farm abatement costs capture broader costs associated with the externality. However, it is often argued that they do so at the expense of consistency with standard production theory. From a technical perspective, while producer-centric valuations remain more common in the literature, different authors have opted for different methodological choices.

Existing evidence suggests that methodological choices may have a sizeable effect on the estimated SPG rates. A recent study focusing on the agricultural sector in EU Member states tested the sensitivity of SPG metrics to alternative methodological choices, with a focus on index selection and weights (Baldoni et al.,

2025 $^{[13]}$ ). The authors identify several factors to be important in determining how different SPG rates will be from traditional TFP growth rates, including the choice of index, the unit value assigned to the environmental externality, and whether externalities are included by adjusting the input or output.

Methodological choices also have two additional implications. First, the perspective used to value environmental externalities—whether based on producer or societal valuations—changes what is being measured, ranging from a proxy of producer profitability growth to an indicator that captures the value and costs of production from a societal perspective, which is referred to by some authors as the profitability of production from society's perspective (Henningsen, 2025[20]). Aligning the valuation perspective with the intended use of the index is therefore essential. Second, methodological choices related to environmental valuation may constrain index selection. When environmental externalities are large relative to output values, output-adjusted SPG measures, such as those used by Baldoni et al. (2025[13]) can theoretically result in negative adjusted outputs. In such cases, particularly under high valuations of externalities, input-adjusted indices have desirable properties as they avoid specifying negative adjusted outputs.

## A summary of several indices proposed in the literature

## Total Factor Productivity TFP

Total Factor Productivity (TFP) is widely used as an aggregate measure of economic performance in the agricultural sector (OECD, 2022[11]) and has been the main driver of output growth since the 1990s (Fuglie, Morgan and Jelliffe, 2024[2]). Improvements in agricultural TFP are an important component of sustainable growth in the agricultural sector as they reflect the capacity to produce greater quantities of outputs with fewer inputs, which is important to preserve natural resources.

Mathematically, TFP is defined by a ratio of aggregate output to aggregate inputs:

$$
T F P _ {t} = \frac {Y I _ {t}}{X I _ {t}} (1)
$$

Since agriculture is a multi-output multi-input activity and $YI_{t}$ and $XI_{t}$ are often expressed as quantity indices where the relative importance of the different outputs and inputs are given by revenue and cost shares. In the case of the FAO output index used by USDA, it is given by $YI_{t} = \frac{\sum_{i} p_{y,i}^{0} q_{i}^{t}}{\sum_{i} p_{y,i}^{0} q_{i}^{0}}$ , where the output index is given by the aggregate value of production (sum of the production of each commodity i at time t $(q_{i}^{t})$ , valued at a time-invariant base year price for a given commodity $(p_{y,i}^{0})$ ) divided by the aggregate value of production in the base year (defined as the sum of the production of each commodity i at the base period $(q_{i}^{0})$ valued at a time-invariant base year price $(p_{y,i}^{0})$ ).

While TFP growth implies that aggregate output is growing faster than aggregate input, it does not provide any information on potential environmental consequences of output growth (Bureau and Antón, 2022 $^{[12]}$ ). This has motivated the adjustment of TFP metrics to include environmental externalities and several indices have been proposed. This paper focuses on five indices $^{8}$ of environmentally adjusted productivity. For the conceptual exposition below, time-invariant prices are assumed. $^{9}$

## Environmentally Adjusted Total Factor Productivity (Output) [EATFPO]

The first metric considered is the Environmentally adjusted TFP with output adjustment (EATFPO). This metric requires constructing an environmentally adjusted output (by aggregating together traditional agricultural outputs and externalities). It measures the ratio of the environmentally adjusted output to traditional input. Mathematically, this metric generally takes the following form:

$$
E A T F P O _ {t} = \frac {E A O I _ {t}}{X I _ {t}}\tag{2}
$$

Where the numerator $(EAOI_{t})$ is an environmentally adjusted output index over aggregate input index $(XI_{t})$ . A common way of adjusting the output is by assigning a monetary value to the externalities and subtracting the estimated monetary value of negative externalities $^{10}$ ( $p_{e} * E_{t}$ in the case of one externality) from the total monetary value of traditional output ( $Y_{t} = \sum_{i} py_{i}^{0} q_{i}^{t}$ ) (Bureau and Antón, 2022 $^{[12]}$ ). If this approach is used and if time-invariant prices are assumed, $EATFPO^{11}$ in time t relative to a base year can be expressed as:

$$
E A T F P O = \frac {\frac {E A O _ {t}}{E A O _ {0}}}{\frac {\sum_ {j} p x _ {j} ^ {0} X _ {j} ^ {t}}{\sum_ {j} p x _ {j} ^ {0} X _ {j} ^ {0}}} = \frac {\frac {\sum_ {i} p _ {y , i} ^ {0} q _ {i} ^ {t} - p _ {e} E ^ {t}}{\sum_ {i} p _ {y , i} ^ {0} q _ {i} ^ {0} - p _ {e} ^ {0} E ^ {0}}}{\frac {\sum_ {j} p _ {x , j} ^ {0} X _ {j} ^ {t}}{\sum_ {j} p _ {x , j} ^ {0} X _ {j} ^ {0}}}\tag{3}
$$

Conceptually, treating externalities as bad outputs is more intuitive since externalities (at least the ones considered in this paper) are generally by-products of externality-causing intended outputs (Murty, Robert Russell and Levkoff, 2012 $^{[21]}$ ). However, mathematically and from a practical perspective, treating externalities as bad outputs also has disadvantages. Specifically, conceptually, it can result in low or negative adjusted outputs (when costs of externalities exceed value of production). In such situations, this can limit the range of plausible shadow values $^{12}$ (to avoid negative net outputs) $^{13}$ and it can also potentially lead to more volatile growth rates.

## Environmentally Adjusted Total Factor Productivity (Input) [EATFPI]

The second index considered is the Environmentally-adjusted TFP with an input adjustment [EATFPI] – In this measure, the adjustment is done by constructing an environmentally-adjusted input measure. This is done by augmenting the input (the denominator) in such a way that it includes both traditional inputs (typically land, labour, capital, feed and fertiliser in the case of the USDA ERS International Agricultural Productivity database) and environmental externalities. In this case, conceptually, externalities can be seen as proxies for consumption of natural resources which are treated as an additional input. This metric therefore takes the ratio of traditional output and environmentally-adjusted input index $(EAII_{t})$ and can be expressed as:

$$
E A T F P I _ {t} = \frac {Y I _ {t}}{E A I I _ {t}} (4)
$$

If prices for both inputs and externalities can be obtained equation (4) can be expressed as:

$$
E A T F P I _ {t} = \frac {\frac {\sum_ {i} p _ {y , i} ^ {0} q _ {i} ^ {t}}{\sum_ {i} p _ {y , i} ^ {0} q _ {i} ^ {0}}}{\frac {E A I _ {t}}{E A I _ {0}}} = \frac {\frac {\sum_ {i} p _ {y , i} ^ {0} q _ {i} ^ {t}}{\sum_ {i} p _ {y , i} ^ {0} q _ {i} ^ {0}}}{\frac {\sum_ {j} p _ {x , j} ^ {0} X _ {j} ^ {t} + p _ {e} E ^ {t}}{\sum_ {j} p _ {x , j} ^ {0} X _ {j} ^ {0} + p _ {e} E ^ {0}}} (5)
$$

Adjusting traditional TFP by extending the inputs is conceptually less straightforward as it treats environmental externalities as consumption of the environment. However, mathematically, it has convenient properties, including the fact that the denominator is never negative, does not impose a limit on the shadow prices, and, in cases where the values of externalities are large relative to output and inputs and change quickly, growth rates are likely to be less sensitive.

## Environmentally Sustainable Productivity Index [ESPI-O]

The third index considered in this paper is the Environmentally Sustainable Productivity Index [ESPIO] – The ESPI is an index-value based approach which is closely related to work on sustainable productivity indices (O'Donnell, 2018[22]; O'Donnell, 2022[23]; Njuki and O'Donnell, 2025[24]). The environmentally sustainable productivity index is defined as the ratio of an environmentally adjusted output index over an input index (Cobourn et al., 2024[25]). Mathematically, it is expressed as:

$$
E S P I O _ {t} = \frac {E A O I _ {t}}{X I _ {t}} = \frac {(Y I _ {t}) ^ {1 - \alpha} (E I _ {t}) ^ {- \alpha}}{X I _ {t}} (6)
$$

Importantly, however, while the ESPI also relies on the concept of an environmentally adjusted output, there are important differences with the EATFPO metric. First, all quantity indices $(YI_{t}, EI_{t}, XI_{t})$ are expressed against a common base-year value $^{14}$ for all countries which enables cross-country comparisons of the levels of ESPI (Cobourn et al., 2024 $^{[25]}$ ). Second, there is an important difference in terms of how the Environmentally adjusted output is constructed. Whereas EATFPO metrics usually rely on prices to aggregate market and non-market outputs, the ESPI uses a parameter $\alpha$ which captures the relative importance of traditional output and bad outputs (i.e. externalities). As a result, while the relative importance of the bad outputs in the EATFP metric depends on their calculated monetary value (which implies that the weight of the value of the environmental externality in the environmentally adjusted output will be country-specific), the ESPI assumes this is defined by a alpha, which is constant across all countries in the original paper (Cobourn et al., 2024 $^{[25]}$ ).

## Environmentally Sustainable Productivity Index with input adjustment [ESPII]

The fourth index considered in this paper is a modified version of the Environmentally Sustainable Productivity Index [ESPII] where the adjustment is done via the inputs rather than outputs. While this index does not feature in the contribution that proposes ESPI (Cobourn et al., 2024[25]), it is calculated in this paper to facilitate the comparison with SPG metrics that adjust inputs rather than outputs (i.e. EATFPI). Mathematically, adjusting ESPI through the denominator can mathematically be expressed as

$$
E S P I I _ {t} = \frac {Y I _ {t}}{E A I I _ {t}} = \frac {Y I _ {t}}{(X I _ {t}) ^ {1 - \alpha} (E I _ {t}) ^ {\alpha}} (7)
$$

Where the parameter $\alpha$ , bounded between 0 and 1 $^{15}$ , now expresses the relative importance of the externalities vis-à-vis traditional inputs, rather than outputs (as in ESPIO). There are two reasons for introducing this index. The first is that it is more comparable to the EATFPI since both treat externalities in a similar fashion. The second reason is that it has a slightly different logic to ESPIO which results in different conditions (more in-line with EATFPI) as to when ESPII exceeds TFP. Specifically, while the evolution in the nominator of the ESPIO essentially looks at whether improvements in environmental performance keep up with improvements in production (i.e. for a positive growth in output it requires a reduction in the externalities at a specific pace for EAOI to exceed YI), ESPII asks whether a country is able to keep the growth in EI lower than that of XI (i.e. consumption of the environment below the increase in use of inputs). As will be seen in subsequent sections, this has implications in terms of how the metrics behave relative to traditional TFP.

## Weak Environment-Productivity Growth [WEP]

The final metric considered is the weak environment-productivity (WEP) growth. The underlying idea of the WEP is to allow full substitution between an environmental outcome and TFP growth (Henderson and Lankoski, 2023[26]). The way the WEP is constructed is by adjusting TFP growth rates by calculating a weighted average of traditional TFP growth and growth of environmental metric(s) $^{16}$ (Henderson and Lankoski, 2023[26]). Mathematically, it is expressed as:

$$
\boldsymbol {g} (\boldsymbol {W E P}) = (1 - \alpha) * g (T F P) - \alpha * g (E)\tag{8}
$$

Where the parameter $\alpha$ denotes the relative weight given to the growth of environmental externalities (emission intensity of GHG emissions in the original paper) and TFP. One important difference between WEP and the four other metrics outlined above is that, while all other metrics adjust either the output or input, the WEP uses a weighted average of unadjusted TFP growth and growth of environmental externalities. In the case of WEP, two versions of the WEP will be used, namely WEPI and WEPO. The only difference between these two is the value of alpha. In short, two values of alpha are considered, one that is based on the relative importance of externalities vis-à-vis inputs (WEPI) and another vis-à-vis outputs (WEPO). Values used are provided in Table A A.1 in the appendix. While these are, strictly speaking, not different indices (the only difference is the weights assigned to the growth of the externalities), these will be referred to separately in the results section.

## Interpretation: How do different SPG indices compare?

By taking a ratio of aggregate output to aggregate inputs, TFP can be intuitively interpreted as the capacity to produce “more with less” (i.e. more traditional output with less traditional inputs). TFP growth rate also has an intuitive interpretation in that it is the difference between the growth rate of aggregate output and aggregate input (i.e. the growth in output that cannot be attributed to the growth in inputs). A positive growth rate over time therefore implies that aggregate output is increasing faster than aggregate input.

The interpretation of environmentally adjusted productivity metrics is more complex and index-specific. However, for indices that adjust output and inputs, growth rates are positive when environmentally-adjusted output growth exceeds input growth (in the case of output adjustment) or when output growth exceeds environmentally-adjusted input growth (in the case of input adjustment). As such, environmentally adjusted productivity metrics still capture the spirit of “producing more with less”. However, as will be shown later, the interpretation can differ and the conditions under which the growth rate of SPG metrics exceeds those of traditional TFP differ depending on the metric.

The different indices described in the previous section all share several important features.

## All reviewed SPG indices use “weights” to aggregate output and externalities and converge towards TFP when weights are 0

An important common feature across all reviewed metrics is the reliance on explicit or implicit valuation of the environment. All metrics rely on some form of aggregation between environmental and non-environmental outputs/inputs that relies on weights. Such weights can be based on externality prices, explicit parameters (e.g. alpha coefficient in ESPI). If time-invariant prices are used and denoting the importance of the environment as gamma $^{17}$ , this implies that metrics in equations (3), (5) and (6)-(8) can be represented in index form as a function of a parameter $\gamma$ (Table 1 and derivations are provided in Annex A).

This has two implications. First, all SPG metrics described tend to traditional TFP as the weight assigned to the environmental variables approaches 0 (i.e. as $p_{e}$ tends to 0 in equations (3) and (5) or as $\alpha$ tends to 0 in equations (6)-(8)). This implies that differences in growth rates across the different indices will increase as the weight of the environment increases since the relative weight assigned to factors captured in traditional TFP will be lower. Second, it is possible to calculate gamma in a way that is conceptually similar across indices (i.e. set gamma to reflect the gamma observed in EATFPI or EATFPO) such that gamma has the same interpretation across all metrics. $^{18}$

## Despite similarities, the nature and interpretation of the weight differs across indices

One important difference relates to how the importance of the environment ( $\gamma$ ) is determined across indices (Table 1). In EATFP metrics, $\gamma$ has an intuitive economic interpretation but requires the user to know/estimate the unit price of the externalities (the ratio of the value of the externalities relative to the total joint economic value of outputs/inputs and externalities, which is likely to be country-specific). ESPI and WEP, on the other hand, have imposed the same weight across all countries (though this need not be the case), but provide greater flexibility in the way that $\gamma$ is set by allowing it to vary between 0-1. When $\gamma$ is set arbitrarily the more intuitive economic interpretation is lost, but the added flexibility also means that $\gamma$ can be set in a way that preserves an intuitive interpretation. It represents the sensitivity of the policy maker or the society towards environmental issues and can be interpreted as the “weight” given to environmental externalities compared to economic outcomes that can be measured at market prices. If the user so decides, it can be set in an analogous way to gamma in EATFP metrics. This is the approach followed in the paper, to ensure a greater degree of comparability and to preserve the intuitive interpretation of gamma from EATFP metrics.

## SPG is larger than TFP growth when output/input growth is sufficiently decoupled from environmental externalities growth

The conditions for an environmentally adjusted productivity growth (EAPG) to be positive are quite similar across metrics (except for WEP). SPG rates will be positive whenever the growth of environmentally adjusted output exceeds that of traditional input (in the case of EATFPO and ESPIO) or when output growth exceeds that of environmentally adjusted input growth (in the case of EATFPI and ESPII) (Column (2) of Table 2). In the case of WEP, the interpretation is slightly different as it requires that the weighted growth rate of TFP compensates the weighted growth rate of the externality. $^{19}$

Another important commonality across the different metrics is that for SPG rates to exceed TFP growth rates always requires a sufficient degree of decoupling between outputs/inputs and externalities. The definition of “sufficient” level of decoupling, however, differs across indices (Column (3) of Table 2). In this sense, indices can broadly be classified in two types, depending on the degree of decoupling required. Specifically:

\- Some indices require relative decoupling between the externality and output/input (EATFPO, EATFPI, ESPII) – For these indices, for the SPG rate to exceed the growth rate of TFP, the growth rate of the externalities needs to be slower than that of output (EATFPO) or input (ESPII, EATFPI). These metrics therefore require relative decoupling between the considered environmental externalities and output or input for the growth rates of these metrics to exceed those of TFP.

\- Other SPG metrics require absolute decoupling between output/TFP growth and externality growth (ESPIO, WEP) - In the cases of WEP and ESPIO, for the growth rate of these metrics to exceed TFP growth, they require a reduction in environmental externalities at a pace faster than that of output growth. In other words, assuming that output increases, these indices require not only absolute decoupling (negative growth of environmental externalities) but that the externalities decrease at least as fast as output (ESPIO) or TFP (WEP) increases. Only when output growth is negative do these indices allow for growth in environmental externalities to be compatible with higher levels of SPG than TFP growth. This therefore implies that, for growing levels of output/TFP (which is the general case), these two metrics require stronger environmental performance for their growth rates to exceed that of TFP when compared to the other three.

## Choice of dimensions: What is included and how has practical consequences

When choosing the environmental dimensions, a decision needs to be made regarding which externalities to consider and in what form to consider them. Conceptually, both positive and negative externalities can be included in an environmentally adjusted metric. Ideally, both types of externalities would be included as agriculture delivers both agri-environmental services (e.g. habitat provision, visual amenities, care farming) and is also associated with negative environmental externalities (Pretty et al., 2000[27]). There are several examples of attempts to value both positive and negative externalities in agriculture with examples including Canada and the Netherlands (Jongeneel, Polman and Cornelis Van Kooten, 2016[28]; Skolrud et al., 2020[29]) which have all found the economic value of positive externalities to be substantially lower than those of negative externalities. However, when calculating SPG metrics for multiple countries, a focus on negative externalities is more common, not least because of a lack of comparable data sources on quantities and values of positive externalities across countries. This makes the inclusion of positive externalities more challenging and most attempts to propose EAP metrics at the national level have focused on the inclusion of negative externalities.

Even when considering only negative externalities, the choice of the externalities to include is likely to differ based on the purpose of the exercise. There are examples in the literature that focus on the inclusion of different types of externalities, including GHG emissions (Ang, Dakpo and Pieralli, 2022[14]; Henderson and Lankoski, 2023[26]), water (Vrachioli and Tzouvelekas, 2022[30]; Fuglie et al., 2020[31]), soil erosion (Nanere et al., 2007[32]), pesticides (Ball et al., 2001[33]; Wang, Hussain and Yan, 2024[34]) as well as several attempts to include several dimensions simultaneously (Nin-Pratt, 2025[35]; Ball et al., 2001[33]; Cobourn et al., 2024[25]). Below, four important aspects related to the inclusion of externalities are highlighted.

One fundamental constraining factor influencing the choice of externalities is data availability. For analyses based on the OECD agri-environmental indicator database, for instance, data gaps vary by indicator. There are fewer data gaps for indicators related to GHG emissions, nutrient balances, and, to some extent, ammonia emissions. On the other hand, data gaps are much larger for indicators related to domains such as biodiversity, water and erosion (Cobourn et al., 2024[25]; Henderson and Lankoski, 2023[26]). Another potential aspect that can inform the choice of dimensions beyond data availability relates to the relative importance of the dimensions being considered in monetary terms (i.e. the overall economic importance of the externality). Across valuation studies carried out in the Netherlands, Canada and the UK, GHG emissions, ammonia emissions and metrics related to nutrients consistently ranked as important in terms of their overall economic value (Pretty et al., 2000[27]; Skolrud et al., 2020[29]; Jongeneel, Polman and Cornelis Van Kooten, 2016[28]). $^{20}$

Another second important aspect relates to the definition used for specific externalities. In the case of GHG emissions at the national level, for instance, the definition used (e.g. including/excluding Land use, land use change and forestry [LULUCF]) as well as the methodology used to estimate the level of emissions (e.g. Tier 1 vs. Tier 2 data) can have different implications in terms of the results. Different authors have chosen different definitions of GHG emissions when adjusting these metrics. Some authors have focused on narrower definitions of GHG emissions from agriculture (Cobourn et al., 2024[25]), whereas other authors have adopted broader definitions that include additional components, such as net forest conversion (Ang, Dakpo and Pieralli, 2022[14]). While the choice is very clear for farm-level analysis, arguments can be made for both choices when considering national-level analyses. This matters for two reasons. First, the two different definitions (including or excluding LULUCF) may imply different total quantities (and hence different economic value of the externalities, especially if using external shadow prices). Second, in some cases (e.g. Australia), trends in total quantities between the two definitions may be very different.

A third aspect that is important is to avoid overlap between dimensions. For some environmental externalities there may be an overlap. For instance, since part of the total nutrient inputs contributes to GHG emissions, it may be problematic to include both without adjusting nutrient indicators. It is therefore important to avoid double-counting of certain externalities.

Finally, not all externalities may be relevant for every country or sector within a country. It is thus important to have clarity on the purpose of the index. In instances where indices are expected to be used solely for temporal comparisons within a country/sector, this could justify the inclusion of a set of externalities most relevant to the country. In other contexts, when the possibility of cross-country comparison is an intended objective, it may be important to consider sets of externalities relevant to the group of countries as a whole, even if some externalities may not be relevant for one individual country/sector.

This analysis focuses on four negative externalities: agricultural GHG emissions (excluding LULUCF), ammonia emissions and estimated leaching and run-off calculated as a share of the total nutrient balance. An in-depth explanation of the definitions and approach used is provided in Annex C.

## Choices related to environmental valuation: Different approaches to the valuation of the environment will impact results and change the interpretation of the SPG metrics

Environmental externalities can be valued in different ways, and this can introduce substantial uncertainty around the values attributed to the environment as well as create challenges in terms of methodological consistency. Different authors have approached the question of environmental valuation differently (Bureau and Antón, 2022[12]). Most studies rely on shadow prices, either estimated endogenous prices (i.e. prices estimated using statistical techniques directly from the data) or use external prices $^{21}$ (e.g. abatement costs, estimated damages from the externality) from the literature. $^{22}$ One important aspect of using shadow prices is that they enable the combination of environmental and productivity metrics, which means that they at least capture environmental considerations to some extent. Different shadow prices have different strengths and weaknesses value improvements in environmental performance differently.

## Estimated shadow prices are more methodologically consistent, but are sensitive to methodological choices and can preclude the inclusion of important externalities

Estimated endogenous prices (i.e. shadow prices) are more theoretically consistent assuming that opportunity costs of abatement activities can be modelled as foregone production of output (Bureau and Antón, 2022[12]). Relative prices between environmental externalities stemming from such an approach are more comparable than if these are sourced from difference sources. Popular approaches to estimate shadow prices in this way include regression analysis, stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) and data envelopment analysis (DEA).

However, the econometric estimation of shadow prices faces several challenges. First, estimates are generally sensitive to the choice of estimation approach and the sample used (Luu and de Menna, 2025[36]; Bureau and Antón, 2022[12]). Second, estimated shadow prices are also likely to be sensitive to the definition of the externality (e.g. on-farm vs. AFOLU GHG emissions $^{23}$ ) and there are also challenges related to econometric estimation of shadow prices for externalities whose values are only observed at irregular intervals (e.g. erosion). Third, econometric estimation of shadow prices can condition the dimensions that can be included in an environmentally adjusted productivity metric. In cases where estimated shadow prices are negative or statistically insignificant, a given dimension may not be included, even when it is of policy relevance. For instance, in the case of the estimation of environmentally adjusted MFP for OECD countries, authors do not account for five pollutants as coefficients were statistically insignificant (Cárdenas Rodríguez, Haščič and Souchier, 2018[10]). This can occur for a variety of reasons, including estimation issues, lack of internalisation of the externality, or there simply being no trade-off between the externality and output. While this paper focuses only on environmental sustainability and does not consider social dimensions of sustainability, the issue of the lack of a statistically significant relationship between output and the externality is likely to also be important when considering the social dimension of sustainability where trade-offs may be harder to detect econometrically.

Beyond estimation challenges, endogenous shadow prices face two important limitations. First, they focus on marginal changes, with their magnitude reflecting the cost to a farmer of reducing a given externality — typically in terms of foregone revenue or profit. The focus on marginal changes implies that they often do not capture features such as tipping points and do not provide information on whether the level of externalities is sustainable or not. Second, when producer abatement costs and societal damages are misaligned—for example, with low abatement cost and high damage—endogenous shadow prices may not adequately reflect the harm they cause. In the absence of an incentive framework that conveys these signals to farmers, the weights assigned to the externalities may understate their societal impact.

## External shadow prices provide more flexibility, allow the inclusion of off-farm damages of externalities, but generally imply a lower degree of methodological consistency

An alternative to using estimated prices is to use external shadow prices from the literature (termed external in this report) (Bureau and Antón, 2022[12]). This can be done, for instance using market approximations of the abatement costs (e.g. prices in emission trading schemes). Such an approach values the externality from the perspective of a producer. However, they do not capture the damage to society as a whole (Bureau and Antón, 2022[12]), which would imply using valuations of the environment that are based on factors that extend beyond the farm (i.e. in this paper, this is referred to as from the perspective of society). If societal damages exceed by a large amount of private abatement costs, then using estimated shadow prices (or external shadow prices using a producer perspective) underestimate the value of the externality to society (Henningsen, 2025[20]). Using external shadow prices provides added flexibility for policymakers to capture societal damages (if this is the intended purpose) and can help ensure that every dimension has positive prices $^{24}$ and can be included. External shadow prices may also be more intuitive to understand for policymakers.

However, shadow prices from external sources are often sourced from different sources that use different methodologies and are often associated with substantial levels of uncertainty, which can create methodological inconsistencies for two reasons. First, it may distort the relative prices of the different environmental externalities. $^{25}$ Second, when considering a societal perspective, external shadow prices can also create an inconsistency with the inputs and outputs (which are valued using the perspective of the producer). The degree of the inconsistency depends on the degree to which input costs and output at market prices approximate their social value.

## The choice of environmental valuation can affect the interpretation of the metric

The chosen environmental valuation can also have implications on the interpretation of SPG. While many aspects of the valuation of externalities are technical questions (e.g. the choice of estimator), the perspective used to value the environment also depends on what is intended to be captured by the environmentally adjusted metric. Different approaches have been used in the literature. Valuation from a producer perspective is more common and more methodologically consistent since outputs and inputs are also valued from the same perspective. $^{26}$ However, whenever societal damages of the externalities far exceed abatement costs, weights assigned to the externalities are too low from a society's perspective $^{27}$ (Henningsen, 2025 $[20]$ ) and that using producer-centric valuations would lead to a lower weight assigned to the environment. In terms of interpretation, shifting from a producer-centric valuation to a society-centric valuation means that the focus shifts from the evolution of private profitability (i.e. the trends in changes of output and inputs valued from the perspective of the producer) to profitability of production from a societal perspective (i.e. societal valuation of outputs and costs of production).

## Main results of the case study analysis

This section presents the average growth rates obtained when calculating the growth rates using the different metrics, environmental dimensions, and values for the environmental externalities. An overview of the limitations of the analysis is provided in Section 3.3.

With regards to the data used, value of output data is sourced from FAOSTAT and includes crop and livestock output but excludes aquaculture. The input index is sourced directly from the USDA International Agricultural Productivity data product (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024[37]). With regards to the choices of externalities, the focus is on ammonia emissions, GHG emissions from agriculture (excluding LULUCF), and estimated run-off and leaching for nitrogen and phosphorus. For ammonia and GHG emissions, data are sourced directly from the Agri-environmental database of the OECD (OECD, 2025[38]). For nitrogen and phosphorus, an estimate of run-off and leaching is computed by combining nitrogen and phosphorus inputs from the OECD Agri-environmental database with estimates of the fraction of run-off and leaching. Estimates of run-off and leaching come from national inventory reports and national sources (in the case of nitrogen), or global modelling assumptions and/or national sources (in the case of phosphorous). A list of the variables used, their sources and assumptions used for the estimates are available in Annex C (Table A C.1, Table A C.2).

To aggregate the environmental variables, outputs and inputs, valuations of the different environmental externalities are used. For producer-centric valuations, most external shadow prices are sourced from either country-specific or global studies that analyse abatement costs/alternatives. For societal valuations, the paper relies on already-existing databases that provide ranges of damages of the included externalities (De Vries et al., 2024[39]; Lord, 2023[40]). However, in some cases (e.g. phosphorous in New Zealand), national sources that provide a proxy of replacement costs are used. The list of prices used is summarised in Table A C.3. In cases where indices depend on an exogenous parameter (e.g. ESPI), these have been calculated in a way that replicates the logic followed EATFPO (for ESPIO) and EATFPI (for ESPII) to ensure a greater degree of comparability. When doing this, alpha is calculated as the share of the value of the externalities as a share of the joint economic value of externalities and outputs/inputs. ## Comparing the growth rates of different SPG metrics: Differences become starker when the environment becomes more important

This section focuses on a comparison of the results obtained for each of the three case study countries using six different indices (EATFPI, EATFPO, ESPIO, ESPII, and two versions of WEP (WEPI and WEPO) $^{28}$ ). For each index, adjustments are done for six different combinations of environmental indicators, namely adjusting for 1) on-farm GHG emissions only; 2) ammonia emissions from agriculture only; 3) estimated nitrogen run-off and leaching; 4) estimated phosphorus run-off and leaching; 5) a combination of GHG emissions, nitrogen and phosphorus (i.e. exclude ammonia); and 6) a combination of all externalities together. For each specification, two different sets of prices are used. One based on costs from a producer perspective (usually abatement costs derived from the literature) and a proxy of societal costs that goes beyond the costs incurred by producers.

With regards to the construction of the indices, for EATFP metrics shadow prices are used directly to adjust the output/input. $^{29}$ For all metrics where the weight is set using an exogenous parameter (ESPII, ESPIO, WEPI, WEPO), $\gamma$ is set as the ratio of the value of the externality(ies) over the joint monetary value of the externalities plus the value of inputs or outputs (i.e. $\gamma = \frac{p_{e}E_{0}}{p_{x}X_{0} + p_{e}E_{0}}$ for ESPII and WEPI and $\gamma = \frac{peE_{0}}{pyY_{0} + peE_{0}}$ in the case of ESPIO and WEPO). These values of $\gamma$ are chosen to make these metrics more comparable to the EATFP metrics (Table 1.).

The comparison of growth rates across the different specifications highlights several main aspects across the three case study countries (Table 3-Table 5). $^{30}$

First, with the exception of New Zealand for the specification focused on GHG emissions when ESPI-O is used, differences between traditional TFP and SPG metrics are small when adjusting for only one environmental dimension individually valued from a producer perspective (columns (1)-(4) of Table 3-Table 5). $^{31}$ This result is intuitive since in these cases the weight of the environmental externality remains relatively small. Therefore, in these cases the majority of the growth in environmentally adjusted productivity metrics is driven by traditional TFP (i.e. changes in output and inputs). This is also consistent with economy-wide estimates of environmentally adjusted TFP, which find that differences with respect to traditional TFP are small when the prices of the externalities imply a small weight of the environment relative to TFP (De Ridder and Lukasz, 2025 $^{[41]}$ ).

Second, differences in growth rates across different metrics become larger as more dimensions are included (columns (5)-(6) and (11)-(12)) and as higher environmental valuations are used (i.e. from a societal perspective, columns (7)-(12)). The main reason for this is that higher valuations of individual externalities and additional environmental dimensions all lead to higher weights assigned to the environment. As a result, differences in growth rates between outputs, inputs, and externalities, as well as the properties of indices more apparent.

A third aspect that is common across the different countries is that, generally, growth rates obtained when computing EATFPO exceed all others. EATFPI and ESPII typically provide the second highest values (usually above traditional TFP values). ESPIO, WEPI and WEPO exhibit the lowest growth rates across all countries, and this becomes especially clear when multiple externalities are included.

EATFPO, EATFPI and ESPII require relative decoupling between the externalities and output (EATFPO) or input (EATFPI and ESPII) for their growth rates to exceed that of traditional TFP (the value of TFP for each country is provided in the footnote of the respective Table). Given that in all analysed countries most externalities have grown slower than output and inputs (this is analysed in the next section), the growth rates of these indices exceed that of traditional TFP. The growth rates of EATFPO are higher because in all case study countries output has grown faster than inputs and therefore the differential with respect to the growth rates in externalities is higher.

On the other hand, ESPIO, WEPI and WEPO require absolute decoupling at a specific minimum pace (i.e. that the decrease in the growth rate of externalities is at least as high as the increase in output). Of the three case studies, this has only happened in the Netherlands. This is because output growth was slower (which makes it easier to achieve the condition), there were very large decreases (from a high initial level) in several externalities, and that these were concentrated on externalities with very high societal valuations in the EU (typical ranges for such valuations of ammonia emissions in the EU go from EUR 17 000–EUR 39 500 per tonne (De Vries et al., 2024[39])). In the cases of Mexico and New Zealand, these rates are lower than traditional TFP because output growth rates were very fast. Despite sometimes-large decreases in the externality intensity metrics in the agricultural sector, the overall level of externalities increased. This leads to a large decrease in the average growth rate when compared with average TFP for these two countries, especially when accounting for multiple externalities and using valuations from a societal perspective.

## Mexico case study

## Narrative of trends in output and agri-environmental indicators (AEIs)

Between 1990 and 2022, the gross value of Mexican agricultural output more than doubled. Increases in the use of inputs were necessary to allow for this expansion, but overall outputs grew at a much faster rate than inputs. This resulted in Mexico exhibiting an average TFP growth rate of 1.75%, substantially above of the OECD average of approximately 1%. Doubling production, however, led to an increase in negative environmental externalities, though these grew at a much slower rate than output increases. However, despite the fast increase in TFP during the observed period, recent analyses find substantial spatial heterogeneity in TFP growth and that low technical efficiency has constrained further productivity growth (Ortega Diaz and Guerrero, 2025 $^{[42]}$ ).

Reforms starting in the 1980s and continuing through the 1990s led to profound changes in the Mexican agricultural sector. Prior to the reforms that started in the 1980s, Mexican agriculture was heavily protected, characterised by a policy mix that consisted of Agricultural tariffs and quotas, minimum prices for key staple commodities, input subsidies and a more extensive role of state-owned enterprises (OECD, 2024[43]). Liberalisation of the sector started in the 1980s which led to large decreases in price support (ibid). The signature of the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) in 1994 led to a phasing out of state intervention in two of the main staples (maize and beans) (Anderson and Valdés, 2007[44]; OECD, 2024[43]) as well as a major shift in the producer support system, away from price support and towards direct payments (e.g. Programa de Apoyos Directos al Campo - PROCAMPO). The very deep trade reforms pursued by Mexico during this time created an incentive for a higher degree of export orientation in Mexican agriculture and the reduction in input subsidies increased incentives to use inputs more efficiently. Over time this was a key driver of output growth.

In 1995, however, Mexico experienced its worst financial crisis since the 1930s and a substantial devaluation of its national currency, causing economic hardship to the overall population, but also contributing to an improvement of its sectoral trade balance (Ros, 2001[45]; Mckenzie World Bank, 2006[46]; Puyana, 2012[47]). In addition to these factors, technology (e.g. improved varieties) also played an important role for specific commodities (e.g. wheat) (Ortiz et al., 2008[48]) and a structural shift towards higher value-added commodities (e.g. fruits, vegetables, dairy, poultry $^{32}$ ) also contributed to the large increases in output.

The performance evolution of agri-environmental indicators in the 1990s was mixed. Shifts away from input subsidies, combined with the devaluation of the national currency following the 1995 crisis initially put a downward pressure on input use (especially fertiliser use), $^{33}$ which led to lower levels of nutrient surplus for most of the 1990s. However, these started to rebound towards the end of the 1990s, with foreign demand and the recovery from the crisis playing a role. GHG emissions also remained relatively stable between 1990 and 2000, mostly as a result of decreases in input use and relatively stable intensity of livestock, with livestock units per hectare remaining constant during the 1990s (FAO, 2025 $^{[49]}$ ). Nevertheless, while overall livestock units remained constant over the period, there were large shifts in composition, with dairy herd sizes increasing by 24% and poultry units increasing by almost 60%, while non-dairy cattle herds decreased by 11%, caprine herds decreased by 16.3%, and horses decreased by 33% (Gobierno de México, 2025 $^{[50]}$ ).

## The Netherlands case study

Narrative of trends in output and AEIs


During the 1990s, output growth was positive but volatile, with most of traditional TFP growth explained by the decreases in input use (Figure 4, panel a; Figure 5 panel a). During the same period, a steady decrease in the GHG emissions and nutrient inputs was observed, alongside a rapid decrease in agricultural ammonia emissions which approximately halved during the decade (Figure 4 panels b and c) and Figure 5 panel b).

Several factors explain these trends. At the EU level, the 1990s marked the beginning of the “MacSharry” CAP reform which reduced the levels of price support and led to a shift in income support (OECD, 2024[43]). It also marked the introduction of the Nitrates Directive, with a renewed focus on some agri-environmental externalities (OECD, 2015[57]). At the same time, technological improvements (e.g. improved crop genetics) contributed to yield gains (Rijk, van Ittersum and Withagen, 2013[58]) alongside structural changes under way in the Dutch agrifood sector, such as a shift towards export-oriented agriculture, horticulture, alongside farm consolidation (with a $20\%$ reduction in the number of holdings during the 1990s; (FAO, 2025[59])) enabled output gains alongside reductions in inputs.

On the environmental side, several factors contributed to the reductions in the externalities. Structural shifts in the livestock sector contributed to reducing environmental pressures, with large decreases witnessed in herd sizes of cattle (17%) and increases in poultry (12%) between 1990 and 2000 (van der Net et al., 2024[60]). EU level policies, such as the 1992 “MacSharry” CAP reform and the Nitrates Directive, led to reductions in price support and to a shift towards direct payments (still tied to land and animals), but also increased incentives for greater efficiency in the use of resources and inputs. National policies also played an instrumental role, especially for ammonia and nutrient balances. In 1993, legislation was introduced to enable a system of environmental permits. The 1990s also saw a substantial tightening of manure policy. The soil protection Act of 1986 authorised restrictions on the quantity of applied manure (Backus, 2017[61]). In addition, more stringent regulations related to application methods, timing and storage of manure and mineral fertilisers were put in place between 1993 and 1999 (Fraters et al., 2004[62]). This included a requirement to ensure that manure storage infrastructure was covered (a subsidy was introduced to support this), and a requirement to inject manure directly (Fraters et al., 2004[62]; Backus, 2017[61]). In 1998, the Mineral Accounting system (MINAS) was introduced and it included a penalty for farmers exceeding a certain threshold of nutrient surplus (Backus, 2017[61]). These factors contributed to incentivise the adoption of improved manure storage and increased adoption of specialised fertiliser spreading equipment. Combined with favourable structural trends, these contributed to a large reduction in ammonia emissions, as well as phosphorus and nitrogen surpluses.


Agri-environmental indicators continued to improve substantially (compared to 1990 levels) during the 2000s. At the structural level, livestock numbers continued to decrease, especially for pigs (van der Net et al., 2024[60]). With regards to policies, the 2003 CAP reform decoupled most support from production and made cross-compliance mandatory (OECD, 2024[43]). Domestically, manure and ammonia regulations continued to evolve and were key drivers behind improved nutrient balances and further ammonia reductions. The MINAS system continued until 2005, after which it was abandoned following an European Court of Justice (ECJ) ruling in 2003, which led the Netherlands to switch to a regulation based on application rates, where the ceilings were gradually reduced over time (Backus, 2017[61]). This was complemented with a system of production rights for pigs and poultry introduced in 2006 (RIVM, 2024[64]) aimed at capping growth in livestock and fostering efficiency gains. In addition to this, the fertiliser price hike of 2008/2009 also put a downward pressure on nutrient surpluses.


Progress on agri-environmental indicators has slowed down since 2010 and improvements have been less consistent. Previous efficiency means that further reductions are more costly and approaching technical limits. However, different factors have also led to both positive and negative incentives to improve agri-environmental performance. At the EU level, the CAP greening was introduced in the 2013 CAP reform which tied a share of direct payments to greening measures and introduced additional elements, such as eco-schemes. Beyond changes in the CAP, the increased adoption precision agriculture (sensors, variable rate application), transition towards improved housing in the poultry sector, and the introduction of the manure removal system in 2014 requiring farmers to process part of their manure and the increase in manure exports $^{35}$ also put a downward pressure on ammonia emissions and nutrient inputs (van der Net et al., 2024 $^{[60]}$ ; Backus, 2017 $^{[61]}$ ; Kempenaar, 2020 $^{[65]}$ ; RIVM, 2024 $^{[64]}$ ). Nevertheless, the abolition of the EU milk quotas in 2015, led to an increase in the number of dairy cattle reversing the decreasing trend that had been observed in the previous decades (RIVM, 2024 $^{[64]}$ ; Berkhout, Meulen and Ramaekers, 2023 $^{[67]}$ ). In order to counter this trend, following a compulsory reduction in livestock in 2017, the phosphate production rights has been applied to the dairy sector since 2018 (RIVM, 2024 $^{[64]}$ ). In 2015, the Government also introduced the Programmatic approach to Nitrogen (PAS) which allowed permits based on anticipated future reductions. In 2019, a court ruling found it to be invalid casting doubt on the whole system. Finally, in 2022, a decrease in the level of phosphorus inputs is observed, probably driven by the increases in international prices observed following the beginning of Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine. $^{36}$

## New Zealand

## Narrative of trends in output and AEIs

There was a substantial expansion of output in New Zealand between 1990 and 2022, with value of output (measured in constant international USD) growing at an average annual growth rate of 1.6% (Figure 7). The expansion of output in New Zealand was driven primarily by growth in the dairy and horticultural sectors $^{37}$ which supplied international markets, with the proportion of milk produced being exported exceeding 80%. $^{38}$ Such an expansion in output largely fueled by international demand for dairy products led to fast rate of input growth (at 1.4% a year) and was accompanied by an increase in all the externalities included in the analysis, namely agricultural GHG emissions and ammonia emissions, as well as estimates of nitrogen and phosphorus run-off and leaching. However, the trends in these indicators were very uneven across indicators during the analysed period.

A wave of profound economic reforms started in 1984, and these led to profound changes in New Zealand's economic and agricultural policy. These reforms dismantled a system of agricultural support that was based on widespread use of subsidies, which heavily benefitted sheep and wool, input subsidies and import controls (Hugonnet, 2025[68]; Ge, 2025[69]). The reforms in the agricultural sector were accompanied by economy-wide reforms that resulted in a devaluation of the exchange rate, a liberalisation of the financial sector, and a reduction in restrictions affecting capital flows (Hugonnet, 2025[68]).

These reforms had two effects that explain observed trends. First, they fully exposed New Zealand's agricultural sector to market forces and set the basis for an export-based agricultural sector. Second, it fundamentally changed the incentive structure of the agricultural sector. This particularly affected sheep producers, who were the most reliant on Government support. The changes in trade policy and increased exposure to international markets also created incentives for the intensification of agriculture.


These structural shifts put additional pressure on the environment. The shift from sheep to dairy and a more intensive model of agriculture led to an increase in ammonia emissions and GHG emissions, as well as higher nitrogen and phosphorus inputs. GHG emissions, in particular, were closely aligned with herd sizes. In an attempt to mitigate the environmental consequences of economic activity, the Government introduced the Resources Management Act in 1991 which replaced and strengthened a number of existing statutes related to environmental management and planning. The Government also operated two novel soil quality monitoring programmes starting in the mid-1990s which tested different indicators and identified soil and land use management combinations of risk (Sparling et al., 2004[75]; Sparling and Schipper, 2004[76]).

In the 2000s, rates of overall TFP growth in New Zealand decreased substantially, becoming negative. While output growth increased (to $2.2\%$ p.a.), so did input growth ( $2.4\%$ p.a.). The overall trend of decreasing sheep populations and increasing dairy populations continued in the 2000s, a further decline of approximately $30\%$ in sheep population and a $36\%$ increase in dairy cattle herds. The overall trend of intensification of agriculture also continued. In addition to the structural trends, the 2000s also saw the creation of Fonterra, the country's largest cooperative), as well as the signature of two important Free Trade Agreements, with China (2008) and ASEAN (2010). These changes opened new markets, contributed to a better positioning of New Zealand in global value chains (especially dairy) and accelerated export growth, positively contributing to output trends (Verevis and Üngör, 2021[77]). With regards to negative pressures, the financial crisis in 2008/09 financial crisis put a downward pressure on output and also led to reductions in inputs, partially as a result of higher fertiliser prices.


Since 2010, overall TFP growth has been negative with output continuing to grow at a high pace (1.1% p.a.), but not faster than input growth (1.4% p.a.). Since 2010, sheep population continued to decline and dairy herd sizes increased until they peaked circa 2014, before starting to gradually drop. The trend of input intensification continued, but gains to pasture productivity also started to markedly slow since 2011 (Chapman et al., 2025[72]). The COVID pandemic in 2020 also put a downward pressure on output growth towards the end of the analysed period.

## SPG trends over time and decomposition

In the New Zealand case, adjusting TFP metrics to account for environmental externalities leads to a slight increase in productivity growth from 0.21% p.a. to 0.31% p.a. (Figure 9). The main reason why the two metrics (adjusted and unadjusted) have similar growth rates is that the economic value of the externalities increased at a similar pace as input use.

However, the small differences in the overall trend mask bigger differences across the different subperiods. During the 1990s, the large changes in composition of the livestock sector led to a situation where the growth rate of the environmental externalities outpaced that of input use, which led to a lower EATFPI value (1.0%) than traditional TFP (1.3%). This coincided with a period of sustained increase in the dairy herd sizes. However, for the next two decades, while traditional TFP metrics suggest a negative TFP growth rate, EAP metrics suggest a slightly positive one. This is attributable to the slowdown in the growth rate of the different externalities since 2000, as a result of efficiency gains, widespread adoption of management practices, as well as a stabilisation of herd sizes.

The contribution of externality growth was limited compared to that of input growth for all decades except the 1990s when the growth in externality values was higher than that of input growth (Figure 9, panel b). Within externalities, ammonia and GHG emissions had the highest contributions to the adjustment of the productivity metric (Figure 9, panel c). In the case of ammonia, it is an externality with a high social price that increased the most during the period.

## Limitations of the analysis

It is important to acknowledge limitations of the analysis, some of which are common to SPG metrics in general, and others specific to the country case study analysis.

One important aspect is that discussion in this paper focuses exclusively on growth rates, rather than levels. This is a limitation, as levels are highly relevant from a policy perspective. Cross-country comparison of TFP levels, even in the absence of environmental externalities, is also challenging. The database used in this paper (ERS IAP database) is non-axiomatic, which means that, while it allows for comparisons of growth rates, it does not allow for comparison of productivity levels. However, carrying out such analysis in contexts where relevant data allows for comparison of levels (e.g. analyses at sub-sectoral levels), while outside the scope of this paper, could be important from a policy perspective.

The focus on growth rates, rather than levels is a limitation that extends to environmentally adjusted productivity metrics, as these do not measure sustainability in an absolute sense. Growth rates of SPG metrics mostly measure the difference in growth rates between an environmentally adjusted output/input and a traditional input/output. This has four implications. First, estimated SPG does not necessarily imply that production is sustainable. Second, depending on the metric, it may not even imply that the absolute value of the externalities is decreasing. Third, even when metrics are improving, they may not be improving everywhere and there may be places where agriculture is becoming more unsustainable. $^{40}$ Lastly, because SPG is measured in growth terms, estimates can be sensitive to initial conditions. Traditional TFP growth tends to slow at higher output levels, while substantial relative reductions in environmental externalities are more likely to occur where initial levels are high. This highlights the need to interpret SPG estimates within their specific context in order to accurately capture these dynamics.

Therefore, while growth of environmentally SPG is a necessary condition for sustainability, it does not imply that production is sustainable. There are alternative frameworks that try to measure sustainability in a more absolute sense, such as the Sustainable Agriculture Matrix (SAM) approach (Zhang et al., 2021[85]). The focus of these measures is not to measure integrated economic performance, but focus instead on comparing raw values of economic, environmental and social variables against pre-defined sustainability thresholds, often based on expert opinion. The two approaches (SPG and focusing on absolute levels of externalities) are therefore complementary, but looking in-depth at sustainable production metrics is beyond the scope of this paper.

In this paper, the focus is on comparing how different methodological assumptions impact the estimated SPG for the case study countries. The aim is therefore to compare trends of SPG and how these compare to TFP growth rates within a country. This differs from what is generally done in many TFP analysis where the focus is cross-country comparisons (Salazar, Tadeo and Alvarez, 2024[86]; Agnew and Nakelse, 2024[87]; Fuglie, Morgan and Jelliffe, 2024[2]). As a result, in order to reflect country-specificities the approach in this paper uses different prices to value environmental externalities across countries (to reflect different realities and abatement costs in different countries), which does not allow for cross-country comparisons of growth rates in this paper. $^{41}$ Given the focus of the paper of temporal comparisons within a country, it is justified to use country-specific values. This also serves to highlight the differences in valuations of the different externalities obtained from the literature, as well as challenges to obtain comparable values across different countries (see Annex C, Table A C.3. for the assumptions used). However, importantly, having an indicator more fit-for-purpose for cross-country comparisons would require further harmonisation of methodologies and valuations of the environmental externalities. $^{42}$

Another aspect relates to important sources of uncertainty around valuation of environmental externalities. Econometric estimation of shadow prices can differ substantially depending on data, specification and method chosen (Luu and de Menna, 2025[36]) and the same applies for external shadow prices, where typical ranges of environmental valuation are large. In the present analysis, given the number of specifications tested and the number of externalities included, values, rather than ranges were used. The main reason for this is that using ranges for each individual externality would create a very large number of potential combinations of values which could hamper interpretability of the results. The assumptions used in this paper are described in Annex C, but it is important to note the substantial uncertainty around shadow prices, especially when using valuations from a societal perspective. For GHG emissions, estimates of social cost of carbon have a large range, with estimates ranging from USD 40-525 per tonne of $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ equivalent (Tol, 2023[88]) and some recent empirical analyses even use values above this range, with ranges from USD 252 to USD 928 per tonne of $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ equivalent used in a recent study (De Ridder and Lukasz, 2025[41]). Similarly, in the EU context, for instance, estimates of damages related to ammonia emissions are accompanied by substantial uncertainty, with plausible ranges in the EUR 18 200 - EUR 39 500 per tonne range with similar uncertainty emerging for nitrogen and phosphorus (De Vries et al., 2024[39]).

Another limitation relates to the choice of externalities included. In this paper, the approach was to include the same set of externalities across the studied countries and also to use, to the extent possible, context-relevant shadow prices for these externalities. However, it is important to highlight that not all externalities matter equally for all countries and sectors and having country- or sector-specific choices of externalities can make analysis more tailored to national contexts. In addition to this, the availability of regular and consistent data at national level constrains the choices of the dimensions used to adjust environmentally adjusted productivity measurements. In this analysis, for example, positive externalities are not included, nor are some important environmental dimensions (e.g. water in the case of Mexico). The reasons are twofold. First, for several externalities there are data gaps that preclude their inclusion for a yearly time series either because they may be calculated at irregular intervals (e.g. soil erosion) or because of the lack of datasets with comparable data sources across OECD countries (e.g. most positive externalities $^{43}$ ).

Finally, there are also uncertainties related to the estimate of run-off and leaching used. As explained in Annex C, the current approach relies on a constant fraction of leaching and run-off as a proportion of total inputs (often from National Inventory Reports in the case of Nitrogen and using a global average in the case of phosphorus). The fact that this fraction is assumed to be time-invariant means that it will not fully capture improvements in countries where this fraction has changed over time. $^{44}$

## 4 Conclusion

Several approaches exist to measure SPG. This analysis tested the importance of three methodological choices (weights, dimensions and indices) in the context of SPG metrics using three country case studies.

A key finding is that the weights assigned to the environment are central in explaining differences between TFP and SPG rates. When environmental weights are low (i.e. as a result of few dimensions or because unit values of externalities are lower), the SPG results closely track traditional TFP. This is intuitive as, in this case, growth is mostly driven by trends in outputs and inputs, rather than those of agri-environmental performance. Differences across indices become more pronounced as environmental weights increase, reflecting that some rely on partial decoupling between the externalities and output/input and others rely on absolute decoupling and require externalities to decrease at a certain pace.

Three case studies results illustrate this pattern. Across all countries, differences between SPG and TFP were generally small when valuing single externalities at producer prices. However, they became larger as weights increased. For all countries, SPG rates tended to exceed TFP growth rates when all externalities were accounted using indices that rely on partial decoupling. However, in the cases of Mexico and New Zealand, this was not the case when using indices that rely of absolute decoupling. This should not be interpreted as poor performance, as achieving absolute decoupling alongside cumulative output expansions exceeding 50% over three decades would be extremely challenging.

While the methodology of the paper does not allow for a formal causal attribution of SPG rates to specific policies and technologies, the decomposition of SPG also showed that potential drivers of SPG are country-specific. In the cases of Mexico and New Zealand, trade policies, high SPG rates coincide with periods of profound reforms of agricultural support systems, as well as the adoption of technologies and practices. In the case of the Netherlands, high SPG rates coincide with regulations (especially ammonia, manure and fertiliser) which were identified as potential key precursors of large reductions in several of the externalities.

Finally, the paper serves to illustrate that there are different ways to compute SPG metrics that can help interpret SPG performance at country level. Under certain conditions, the choice of index will have a limited practical impact on the estimated growth rates. The way in which externalities are valued and the dimensions included, on the other hand, have a consistently high impact on the SPG rates. It is therefore important to carry out sensitivity checks of these methodological choices. The most appropriate methodology is thus likely to depend on the intended use of the SPG measure, its intended interpretation, and the desired perspective of the valuation and dimensions to be included.

# Annex A. Derivations to express all indicators as a function of an environmental weight ( $\gamma$ )

An important aspect to bear in mind is that, while the valuation of the environment does not have an explicit parameter across all different metrics used to measure SPG, all metrics assign a weight to the environment. In a sense, especially in the case of constant prices all metrics can be expressed as a function of such a parameter, which will be called gamma in this paper.

Starting with traditional EATFP metrics, these metrics rely on the concepts of environmentally adjusted output or inputs to adjust traditional TFP metrics. Typically, they rely on augmenting the inputs (in the case of an input adjustment) or deducting the externalities from the value of output (in the case of an output adjustment).

## EATFP – Output adjustment

EATFP metrics adjusted via the output, SPG (or EATFP) is given by the difference in the growth rates of environmentally adjusted output and traditional inputs. Importantly, while there is typically no explicit value for $\gamma$ in the numerator, in such frameworks the relative importance of the environmental dimensions is determined by the relative magnitudes of externalities and traditional outputs.

For the discussion that follows, one output, one externality and one input are assumed, and prices are kept constant over time for simplicity. Defining:

$Y_{t}$ - Quantity of agricultural output (excluding aquaculture) at time t

$E_{t}$ - Quantity of a given environmental externality at time t

$YI_{t}$ - Index value of agricultural output (excluding aquaculture) at time t

$EI_{t}$ - Index value of environmental externality at time t

$p_{e}$ - Time-invariant unit price of the environmental externality E

$p_{y}$ - Time-invariant unit price of output Y

Environmentally adjusted output can be defined as:

$$
E A O _ {t} = p y Y _ {t} - p e E _ {t}\tag{1}
$$

A normalised version of Equation (1) can be re-written as:

$$
E A O _ {t} = \left(p _ {y} + p _ {e}\right) * \left(\left(\frac {p _ {y}}{p _ {y} + p _ {e}}\right) * Y _ {t} - \left(\frac {p _ {e}}{p _ {y} + p _ {e}}\right) * E _ {t}\right)\tag{2}
$$

Where the relative importance of the traditional output and the environmental externality are given by the terms $\left(\frac{p_{y}}{p_{y}+p_{e}}\right)$ and $\left(\frac{p_{e}}{p_{y}+p_{e}}\right)$ . If $\gamma=\frac{p_{e}}{p_{y}+p_{e}}$ and $(1-\gamma)=\frac{p_{y}}{p_{y}+p_{e}}$ , equation (2) can be re-written as:

$$
E A O _ {t} = (p _ {y} + p _ {e}) * ((1 - \gamma) * Y _ {t} - \gamma * (E _ {t}))\tag{3}
$$

Where the term $\left((1-\gamma)*Y_{t}-\gamma*(E_{t})\right)$ captures the relative importance of the traditional and environmental externalities. Equation (3) allows us to recover the value of the environmentally adjusted output at time t. This can be slightly modified to recover a value of environmentally adjusted output indexed at time t (for simplicity let us assume time 0).

Defining:

$$
Y I _ {t} = \frac {Y _ {t}}{Y _ {0}} \text { and } E I _ {t} = \frac {E _ {t}}{E _ {0}}
$$

Then an EAO at time t can be rewritten as:

$$
E A O _ {t} = p _ {y} * (Y _ {0} * Y I _ {t}) - p _ {e} * (E _ {0} * E I _ {t})\tag{4}
$$

The EAO can be divided by the value of the environmentally adjusted output at time 0 to obtain an EAO index.

$$
E A O I _ {t} = \frac {1}{p _ {y} Y _ {0} - p _ {e} E _ {0}} \Big (p _ {y} * (Y _ {0} * Y I _ {t}) - p _ {e} * (E _ {0} * E I _ {t}) \Big)\tag{5}
$$

Equation (5) can be rewritten as:

$$
E A O I _ {t} = \frac {p _ {y} Y _ {0} + p e E _ {0}}{p _ {y} Y _ {0} - p _ {e} E _ {0}} \left(\frac {p _ {y} Y _ {0}}{p _ {y} Y _ {0} + p e E _ {0}} (Y I _ {t}) - \frac {p _ {e} E _ {0}}{p _ {y} Y _ {0} + p e E _ {0}} (E I _ {t})\right)\tag{6}
$$

lf:

$$
W = \frac {p _ {y} Y _ {0} + p e E _ {0}}{p _ {y} Y _ {0} - p _ {e} E _ {0}}; \gamma = \frac {p e E _ {0}}{p y Y _ {0} + p e E _ {0}} \mathrm{and} 1 - \gamma = \frac {p y Y _ {0}}{p y Y _ {0} + p e E _ {0}}
$$

Equation (6) can be rewritten as:

$$
E A O I _ {t} = W * \left((1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t} - \gamma * E I _ {t}\right)\tag{7}
$$

Where W is a scaling factor, and the relative weights given to the traditional outputs and environmental externalities are provided by their relative weight in the total absolute combined economic value of output and externalities. In EATFP metrics with an output adjustment, the EATFP index can therefore be expressed as:

$$
E A T F P O _ {t} = \frac {E A O I _ {t}}{X I _ {t}} = \frac {W * ((1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t} - \gamma * E I _ {t})}{X I _ {t}}\tag{8}
$$

This is important to highlight that, while value of $\gamma$ is not explicit in EATFPO metrics as it is in ESPI (where $\gamma = \alpha$ ), the metric can nevertheless be expressed in a similar way.

## EATFP – Input adjustment

In the case of an input adjustment, a similar case can be made. Specifically, in the case of one input and constant prices:

$X_{t}$ - Quantity of input X

$XI_{t}$ - Input index

$p_{x}$ - Time-invariant unit price of input X

In the case of input-adjusted EATFP metrics, the adjusted denominator is typically given by an expression of the form:

$$
E A I _ {t} = p _ {x} X _ {t} + p _ {e} E _ {t}\tag{9}
$$

This expression can be rewritten as:

$$
E A I _ {t} = (p _ {x} + p _ {e}) \left(\frac {p _ {x}}{p _ {x} + p _ {e}} X _ {t} + \frac {p _ {e}}{p _ {x} + p _ {e}} E _ {t}\right)\tag{10}
$$

If:

$$
X I _ {t} = \frac {X _ {t}}{\mathrm{X} _ {0}} \text {and} E I _ {t} = \frac {E _ {t}}{E _ {0}}
$$

The environmentally-adjusted input can be re-written as:

$$
E A I _ {t} = p _ {x} * (X _ {0} * X I _ {t}) + p _ {e} * (E _ {0} * E I _ {t})\tag{11}
$$

This value can be indexed by dividing the expression in equation (11) by $EAI_{0}$ . This gives us:

$$
E A I I _ {t} = \frac {1}{p _ {x} X _ {0} + p _ {e} E _ {0}} * \left(p _ {x} * (X _ {0} * X I _ {t}) + p _ {e} * (E _ {0} * E I _ {t})\right)\tag{12}
$$

Where equation (12) can be re-written as :

$$
E A I I _ {t} = \left(\frac {p _ {x} X _ {0}}{p _ {x} X _ {0} + p _ {e} E _ {0}} * X I _ {t} + \frac {p _ {e} E _ {0}}{p _ {x} X _ {0} + p _ {e} E _ {0}} * E I _ {t}\right)\tag{13}
$$

If $(1 - \gamma) = \frac{p_xX_0}{p_xX_0 + p_eE_0}$ and $\gamma = \frac{p_eE_0}{p_xX_0 + p_eE_0}$ , then equation (13) can be re-written as:

$$
E A I I _ {t} = (1 - \gamma) X I _ {t} + \gamma * E I _ {t}\tag{14}
$$

Which means an EATFP index adjusted through the input side can be expressed as:

$$
E A T F P I _ {t} = \frac {Y I _ {t}}{E A I I _ {t}} = \frac {Y _ {t}}{(1 - \gamma) X I _ {t} + \gamma * E I _ {t}}\tag{15}
$$

Where gamma can be interpreted as the weight of the externality in the total value of inputs used.

## WEP indicator

In the case of WEP, the weight given to the indicator is simply the weight given to the environmental dimensions and it is chosen by the user. Using the notation $g(.)$ to denote a growth rate, WEP is simply:

$$
g (W E P) = (1 - \alpha) * g (T F P) - \alpha * g (E)\tag{16}
$$

In other words, in the case of WEP, $\gamma = \alpha$ . As such:

$$
g (W E P) = (1 - \gamma) * g (T F P) - \gamma * g (E)\tag{16}
$$

One important point, however, is that in WEP the interpretation of gamma differs somewhat. Whereas in EATFP measures gamma is used to adjust the output or input in levels, in the case of WEP the adjustment is done directly through the growth rates.

# Annex B. Derivations of the necessary conditions for SPG to exceed TFP growth

## When is SPG positive and how does it relate to TFP?

Using the previously defined nomenclature:

$YI_{t}$ - Index of agricultural output (excluding aquaculture) at time t

$XI_{t}$ - Input index at time t

$EI_{t}$ - Index of the environmental externalities (for the time being, let's assume only one for ease of exposition)

The TFP index at time t is given by:

$$
T F P _ {t} = \frac {Y I _ {t}}{X I _ {t}}\tag{18}
$$

It therefore follows that the growth rate of TFP is given by:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {T F P _ {t}}{T F P _ {t - 1}}\right) = \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right) - \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{19}
$$

In other words, TFP growth will be positive when growth rate of outputs exceeds the growth rate of inputs. In other words, TFP growth is positive when there is at least relative decoupling between output and inputs (i.e. when more is produced with less).

## EATFP – Output adjustment

As will be seen below, similar expressions can be derived for environmentally adjusted metrics. Taking logs this can be expressed as:

$$
\ln (\mathrm{EATFPO} _ {\mathrm{t}}) = \ln (E A O I _ {t}) - \ln (X I _ {t})\tag{20}
$$

And the growth rate can be given by:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {E A T F P O _ {t}}{E A T F P O _ {t - 1}}\right) = \ln \left(\frac {E A O I _ {t}}{E A O I _ {t - 1}}\right) - \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{21}
$$

What this expression means is that SPG will be positive whenever the rate of growth of the environmentally adjusted output exceeds that of the traditional inputs. By combining equation (19) with equation (21), (21) is larger than (19) when:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {E A O I _ {t}}{E A O I _ {t - 1}}\right) > \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{22}
$$

Since $EAOI_{t} = W * \left((1 - \gamma) * YI_{t} - \gamma * EI_{t}\right)$ , it follows that for condition (22) to hold growth rate of the environmentally adjusted output needs to exceed that of traditional output. This occurs when:

$$
\frac {W * \left((1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t} - \gamma * E I _ {t}\right) - W * \left((1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t - 1} - \gamma * E I _ {t - 1}\right)}{W * \left((1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t - 1} - \gamma * E I _ {t - 1}\right)} > \frac {Y I _ {t} - Y I _ {t - 1}}{Y _ {t - 1}} (2 3)
$$

Rearranging (23):

$$
\frac {\left((1 - \gamma) * (Y I _ {t} - Y I _ {t - 1}) - \gamma * (E I _ {t} - E I _ {t - 1}\right)}{\left((1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t - 1} - \gamma * E I _ {t - 1}\right)} > \frac {Y I _ {t} - Y I _ {t - 1}}{Y _ {t - 1}} (2 4)
$$

Defining $YI_{t}-YI_{t-1}$ as $\Delta YI$ and $EI_{t}-EI_{t-1}$ as $\Delta EI$ , the inequality can be expressed as:

$$
\left((1 - \gamma) \Delta Y I - \gamma \Delta \mathrm{E} I\right) * Y _ {t - 1} > \Delta Y I * \left((1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t - 1} - \gamma * E I _ {t - 1}\right)\tag{25}
$$

Expanding both sides:

$$
(1 - \gamma) \Delta Y I * Y I _ {t - 1} - \gamma \Delta E I * Y _ {t - 1} > (1 - \gamma) * Y I _ {t - 1} * \Delta Y I - \gamma * E I _ {t - 1} * \Delta Y I \tag {26}
$$

Subtracting both sides implies that:

$$
- \gamma * \Delta E I * Y _ {t - 1} > - \gamma * E I _ {t - 1} * \Delta Y I (2 7)
$$

Which can be rearranged as:

$$
\frac {\Delta Y I}{Y _ {t - 1}} > \frac {\Delta E I}{E I _ {t - 1}} (2 8)
$$

What this implies is that condition (22) holds (i.e. the growth rate of SPG exceeds TFP) when the growth rate of traditional output exceeds that of the externality.

## EATFP – Input adjustment

For EATFP with an input adjustment, taking logs implies that:

$$
\ln (\mathrm{EATFPI} _ {t}) = \ln (Y I _ {t}) - \ln (E A I I _ {t})\tag{29}
$$

And the growth rate can be given by:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {\mathrm{EATFPI} _ {t}}{\mathrm{EATFPI} _ {t - 1}}\right) = \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right) - \ln \left(\frac {E A I I _ {t}}{E A I I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{30}
$$

What this expression means is that SPG will be positive whenever the rate of growth of outputs exceeds that of the environmentally-adjusted inputs. By combining equation (19) with equation (30), (30) is larger than (19) when:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {E A I I _ {t}}{E A I I _ {t - 1}}\right) <   \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{31}
$$

Since $EAII_{t} = (1 - \gamma)XI_{t} + \gamma * EI_{t}$ , it follows that the following inequality needs to hold:

$$
\frac {(1 - \gamma) X I _ {t} + \gamma * E I _ {t} - ((1 - \gamma) X I _ {t - 1} + \gamma * E I _ {t - 1})}{(1 - \gamma) X I _ {t - 1} + \gamma * E I _ {t - 1}} <   \frac {X I _ {t} - X I _ {t - 1}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\tag{32}
$$

Rearranging (32):

$$
\frac {(1 - \gamma) * (X I _ {t} - X I _ {t - 1}) + \gamma (E I _ {t} - E I _ {t - 1})}{(1 - \gamma) X I _ {t - 1} + \gamma * E I _ {t - 1}} <   \frac {X I _ {t} - X I _ {t - 1}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\tag{33}
$$

Defining $XI_{t}-XI_{t-1}$ as $\Delta XI$ and $EI_{t}-EI_{t-1}$ as $\Delta EI=$ :

$$
\frac {(1 - \gamma) * (\Delta X I) + \gamma (\Delta E I)}{(1 - \gamma) X I _ {t - 1} + \gamma * E I _ {t - 1}} <   \frac {\Delta X I}{X I _ {t - 1}}\tag{34}
$$

Expanding both sides implies that:

$$
(1 - \gamma) \Delta X I * X I _ {t - 1} + \gamma \Delta E I * X I _ {t - 1} <   (1 - \gamma) * X I _ {t - 1} * \Delta X I + \gamma * E I _ {t - 1} * \Delta X I\tag{35}
$$

Which simplifies to:

$$
\gamma \Delta E I * X I _ {t - 1} <   \gamma * E I _ {t - 1} * \Delta X I\tag{36}
$$

Which simplifies further to:

$$
\frac {\Delta E I}{E I _ {t - 1}} <   \frac {\Delta X I}{X I _ {t - 1}}\tag{37}
$$

Which essentially means that the growth rate of SPG exceeds the growth rate of TFP when the growth rate of traditional output exceeds that of the externalities.

WEP

$$
(1 - \gamma) * \frac {(T F P _ {t} - T F P _ {t - 1})}{T F P _ {t - 1}} - \gamma * \frac {E I _ {t} - E I _ {t - 1}}{E I _ {t - 1}} > \frac {(T F P _ {t} - T F P _ {t - 1})}{T F P _ {t - 1}}\tag{38}
$$

This can be re-arranged as:

$$
\gamma \left(\frac {\Delta E I}{E I _ {t - 1}} + \frac {\Delta T F P}{T F P _ {t - 1}}\right) <   0\tag{39}
$$

For $\gamma > 0$ , this that the term inside the brackets needs to be negative, which simplifies to:

$$
\frac {\Delta E I}{E I _ {t - 1}} <   - \frac {\Delta T F P}{T F P _ {t - 1}}\tag{40}
$$

In other words, this implies that in the case of WEP, for growth rates of WEP to be higher than those of TFP, the rate of abatement of externalities needs to exceed the growth rate of TFP.

## ESPI – Input adjustment

For ESPI with an input adjustment, taking logs leads to the following equation:

$$
\ln \left(\mathrm{ESPII} _ {t}\right) = \ln \left(Y I _ {t}\right) - (1 - \gamma) * \ln \left(X I _ {t}\right) - \gamma * \ln \left(E I _ {t}\right)\tag{41}
$$

And the growth rate can be given by:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {\mathrm{ESPII} _ {t}}{\mathrm{ESPII} _ {t - 1}}\right) = \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right) - (1 - \gamma) * \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right) - \gamma * \ln \left(\frac {E I _ {t}}{E I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{42}
$$

What this expression means is that SPG will be positive whenever the rate of growth of outputs exceeds that of the environmentally adjusted inputs. By combining equation (19) with equation (42), (42) is larger than (19) when:

$$
(1 - \gamma) * \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right) + \gamma * \ln \left(\frac {E I _ {t}}{E I _ {t - 1}}\right) <   \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{43}
$$

Which simplifies to:

$$
\gamma * \ln \left(\frac {E I _ {t}}{E I _ {t - 1}}\right) <   \gamma * \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{44}
$$

Which is equivalent to:

$$
\frac {\Delta \mathrm{E} I}{E I _ {t - 1}} <   \frac {\Delta X I}{X I _ {t - 1}}\tag{45}
$$

Which essentially means that the growth rate of SPG exceeds the growth rate of TFP when the growth rate of traditional output exceeds that of the externalities.

## ESPI – Output adjustment

For ESPIO with an input adjustment, taking logs implies that:

$$
\ln \left(\mathrm{ESPIO} _ {t}\right) = (1 - \alpha) \ln \left(Y I _ {t}\right) - \ln \left(X I _ {t}\right) - \alpha \ln \left(E I _ {t}\right)\tag{46}
$$

And the growth rate can be given by:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {\mathrm{ESPIO} _ {t}}{\mathrm{ESPIO} _ {t - 1}}\right) = (1 - \gamma) * \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right) - \ln \left(\frac {X I _ {t}}{X I _ {t - 1}}\right) - \gamma * \ln \left(\frac {E I _ {t}}{E I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{47}
$$

Combining equation (47) with equation (19) implies that ESPIO growth rates are higher than TFP growth rates when:

$$
(1 - \gamma) * \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right) - \gamma * \ln \left(\frac {E I _ {t}}{E I _ {t - 1}}\right) > \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{48}
$$

Which further simplifies to:

$$
\gamma * (\ln \left(\frac {E I _ {t}}{E I _ {t - 1}}\right) + \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right)) <   0\tag{49}
$$

For a positive value of $\gamma$ this requires:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {E I _ {t}}{E I _ {t - 1}}\right) <   - \ln \left(\frac {Y I _ {t}}{Y I _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{50}
$$

In other words, a similar condition to that required by WEP applies. The growth rate of the environmentally adjusted metric will exceed that of traditional TFP when the speed of abatement of the externality exceeds the speed of output growth.

In the case of nutrient balances, since the shadow values used in the analysis are generally based on kg of nutrient removed from water, there is an attempt to adjust the overall nutrient balances to capture the average proportion of the nutrient surplus that reaches water bodies (e.g. leaching and run-off). ## Decomposing the results

Using a log decomposition of EATFPI can be decomposed as:

$$
\ln \left(\frac {E A T F P I _ {t}}{E A T F P I _ {t - 1}}\right) = \ln (\frac {Y _ {t}}{Y _ {t - 1}}) - \ln \left(\frac {E A I _ {t}}{E A I _ {t - 1}}\right)
$$

Where EAI is the value of environmentally adjusted input (not the index). The contribution of output is given simply by the (log) growth rate of output. For the environmentally adjusted input, it needs to be separated further into the contribution of inputs and externalities. The contribution of the externalities can be calculated in the following way:

$$
C o n t E x t e r n a l i t i e s _ {t, t - 1} = \left(\frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {p _ {e} E _ {t}}{p _ {x} X _ {t} + p _ {e} E _ {t}}\right) + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}{p _ {x} X _ {t - 1} + p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}\right)\right) * \ln \left(\frac {p _ {e} E _ {t}}{p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}\right)
$$

Which is essentially the average weight of the externalities as a share of the total value of inputs plus externalities times the growth rate in the value of externalities. Note that for the contribution of a single externality, the calculation is essentially the same. The only difference is that the growth rate of that externality is multiplied by the ratio of the monetary value of that externality as a share of the value of Inputs plus externalities.

Similarly, the contribution of traditional inputs between periods t-1 and t is given by:

$$
C o n t i n p u t s _ {t, t - 1} = \left(\frac {1}{2} \left(1 - \frac {p _ {e} E _ {t}}{p _ {x} X _ {t} + p _ {e} E _ {t}}\right) + \frac {1}{2} \left(1 - \frac {p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}{p _ {x} X _ {t - 1} + p _ {e} E _ {t - 1}}\right)\right) * \ln \left(\frac {p _ {x} X _ {t}}{p _ {x} X _ {t - 1}}\right)
$$

To produce the decomposition figures the average contributions of the different components is calculated.

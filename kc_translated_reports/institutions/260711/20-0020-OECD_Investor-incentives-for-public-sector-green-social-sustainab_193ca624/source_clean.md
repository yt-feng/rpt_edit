## Abstract

This report aims to contribute to an improved understanding of how investors view public sector green, social, sustainability and sustainability-linked (GSSS) bonds, and to strengthen development actors' approach to supporting issuances in developing countries. It draws from the insights shared by respondents to the 'OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds' and subsequent consultations with investors and other market players. It explores the factors influencing investor interest and risk appetite for GSSS bonds, and expectations on future market developments. Considering these findings, it provides policy considerations and concrete action plans to help donors, issuing governments in developing countries and other market players increase the attractiveness of public sector GSSS bond issuances and investments in developing countries for investors. Building on those insights, the paper points to several options for future research.

# Executive summary

The global bond market will be critical to meeting unprecedented investment needs. Globally, the fixed income market reached USD 145.1 trillion in 2024 compared to the USD 126.7 trillion equity market, and borrowing from sovereigns and corporates continues to rise (SIFMA Research, 2025 $^{[1]}$ ; OECD, 2025 $^{[2]}$ ). As part of this asset class, green, social, sustainability and sustainability-linked (GSSS) bonds are powerful instruments to drive financing – especially from the private sector – towards sustainable development, by linking scale, sustainable impact and long-term duration. GSSS bonds can therefore be a particularly important part of the solution to the alarming annual USD 4 trillion Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) financing gap (OECD, 2025 $^{[4]}$ ). For developing countries, GSSS bonds also hold advantages – including diversifying the investor base, signalling commitments to sustainability, and building a track record for further issuances.

Yet GSSS bond issuances in developing countries remain rare. They are a small share of total bond issuances: in 2024, GSSS bonds accounted for only 5% of annual emerging market issuance (IFC-Amundi, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ). In 2025, only 17% of the overall green, social and sustainability bond market was issued in developing countries (dropping to 5% when not including the People's Republic of China). This trend is also true in the sustainability-linked bond market: in 2025, 24% of the total issued amount came from issuers in ODA-eligible countries, dropping to 17% when excluding the People's Republic of China). While recognising that debt instruments are not appropriate for all types of entities, these figures indicate that there is room for GSSS bond issuances to grow significantly across developing countries.

Effectively scaling GSSS bond issuances in developing countries therefore requires more issuances coming from developing countries. Previous OECD reports in the GSSS bond series have focused on how donors of the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) can enable GSSS bond issuances in developing countries (Dembele, Schwarz and Horrocks, 2021 $^{[7]}$ ; OECD, 2022 $^{[8]}$ ; OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). However, effectively scaling issuances also rests on greater investor demand towards these regions and instruments. This paper therefore aims to cast light on how investors view public sector GSSS bonds, especially from developing countries. It draws from the insights shared as part of the ‘OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds’ as well as from follow-up consultations with investors and other market players. The focus is on factors influencing investor interest and risk appetite for GSSS bonds, and expectations on future market developments. While recognising that the uncertain – and changing – geopolitical and macroeconomic environment influences investors’ perception of risk and of GSSS bonds, their insights remain crucially important, including for ensuring that actions by providers of development finance concretely respond to their needs and focus on practical solutions.

Based on the above, the paper proposes the following policy considerations on how to strengthen donors' approaches to GSSS bonds in developing countries:

\- Support the development of harmonised and interoperable locally-adapted standards, principles and taxonomies.

\- Provide technical assistance (TA) for the implementation of budgetary reporting standards to identify eligible expenditures.

\- Prioritise developing sustainability-focused bond mandates for domestic funds.

\- Deliver technical assistance for post-issuance disclosure and impact measurement.

\- Consider the potentially unexpected implications of blended finance approaches on GSSS bond issuances.

Recognising the importance of other stakeholders in creating a strong GSSS bond market, the paper also presents the following considerations:

\- Public sector issuers in developing countries need to prioritise liquidity buffers and flexibility in the issuance strategy.

\- Other market players need to increase the inclusion of developing country GSSS bond issuances in benchmark indices.

## The OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds: aims and scopes

The annual financing gap for achieving the 2030 sustainable development goals (SDGs) now stands at an alarming USD 4 trillion (OECD, 2025 $^{[4]}$ ). The scale and urgency of the challenge is clear – and will require an increase in all sources of finance. The global bond market is a powerful way to mobilise capital, including from the private sector, towards sustainable development and resilience, due to its size (in 2024, the outstanding global fixed income market stood at USD 145.1 trillion compared to the USD 126.7 trillion global equity market capitalisation) (SIFMA Research, 2025 $^{[1]}$ ) and long-term focus of its investors. At the same time, despite the complex macroeconomic and geopolitical context, borrowing levels continue to reach historic highs: in 2026, governments and corporations are expected to borrow USD 29 trillion from markets. This represents a 17% increase compared to 2024 (OECD, 2026 $^{[3]}$ ). This all increases the relevance of the bond market – for issuers, investors and policymakers alike. Within this, green, social, sustainability and sustainability-linked (GSSS) bonds are particularly relevant: they link scale with impact, and have received strong interest from issuers and investors alike (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). In recent years, the market has shown resilience and robustness (Luxembourg Green Exchange, 2025 $^{[11]}$ ; BNPP Asset Management, 2026 $^{[12]}$ ). This paper builds on the insights shared as part of the 'OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds' (hereafter "OECD-LuxSE survey") to contribute to an improved understanding of how investors view public sector GSSS bonds, especially from developing countries.

The GSSS bond group can be further divided into two sub-categories – green, social and sustainability (GSS) bonds and sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs) – which differ based on their underlying mechanisms. $^{1}$ Both instruments hold significant advantages for developing country issuers – including diversifying the investor base (also internationally), signalling commitments to sustainability, and building a market and track record for further issuances from the private sector (World Bank Treasury, 2022 $^{[13]}$ ). GSS bonds also concretely drive financing towards sustainable projects, while providing transparency around this. SLBs, on the other hand, incentivise behaviour towards sustainability targets, while increasing accountability towards these. As such, GSS bonds and SLBs are complementary instruments, and they can be used depending on issuers' ultimate financing needs (OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). In a context of increased pressure and urgency to progress towards climate action and the SDGs, these factors make them particularly important for public sector issuers. $^{2}$

Yet the reality is that issuances are not occurring in the countries or regions that have the greatest financing needs, and which could therefore benefit the most from these issuances (see (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ), (OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ) and Box 1.1). Here, it is important to note that this doesn't necessarily reflect a lack of interest in GSSS bonds from issuers. GSSS bonds are debt instruments, and this means that they are not appropriate for all entities. Developing country issuers may face additional constraints when entering this space (see Box 1.2). Factors relating to the issuer's macroeconomic environment or institutional setting may also inhibit capital market transactions – ranging from prevailing debt distress, low capital market development, missing regulation or lacking pipelines of sustainable assets (OECD, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ). Still, there is room for developing countries to increase their issuance of GSSS bonds, which represent only 5% of annual emerging market bond issuance (IFC-Amundi, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ). The OECD series on GSSS bonds outlines concrete recommendations for members of the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) (referred to hereafter as donors) to effectively overcome challenges in issuing GSSS bonds in developing countries (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). A crucial starting point of these recommendations is that their use must be carefully assessed and evaluated.

## Box 1.1 Data trends in the GSSS bond market

The GSSS bond market has been in constant evolution since its birth in 2007 (EIB, 2022 $^{[14]}$ ). It faced very significant annual growth until 2022, where challenging market conditions resulted in a decrease in issuances (although this market downsize was less pronounced than in the overall bond market). Since then, the market picked back up again – reflecting its resilience and robustness (BNPP Asset Management, 2026 $^{[12]}$ ). As of 2025, it continues to be mostly composed of use-of-proceeds instruments, and green bonds remained the dominant instrument, accounting for 61% of the market in 2025. In the same year, social bond issuances made up 12%, while sustainability bond issuances had another strong year (increasing to 23% from 22% in 2024). In contrast, SLBs continued to struggle: in 2025 they made up 3% of the market (down from 4% in 2024).

Despite the potential that GSSS bonds hold for financing sustainable development, issuances from ODA-eligible countries, which have the greatest financing needs, account for a small share of the total market. In 2025, only 17% of the overall green, social and sustainability (GSS) bond market was issued in developing countries (dropping to 5% when not including the People's Republic of China). This trend is also true in the SLB market: in 2025, only 24% of the total issued amount came from issuers in ODA-eligible countries, dropping to 17% when excluding the People's Republic of China. As of 2025, Europe continues to dominate the SLB market. At the same time, despite the first sovereigns entering the SLB market as issuers in 2022, public sector players have so far remained reluctant to enter this space. As of Q1 2026, four sovereigns have issued SLBs: Chile, Uruguay, Thailand and Slovenia.

Yet the challenge is multi-faceted, as investors themselves currently have very limited allocation towards developing countries. Shifting only 1.1% of these assets under management towards sustainable activities in developing countries would close the SDG-financing gap (OECD, 2020 $^{[15]}$ ). This highlights the powerful opportunity of increasing alignment with the SDGs (OECD, 2020 $^{[15]}$ ). The challenge is not a lack of resources per se, but ensuring that these flow towards developing countries effectively.

## The OECD-LuxSE survey on investor incentives for public sector GSSS bonds: aims and scopes

The OECD-LuxSE survey therefore focused specifically on investor views on GSSS bonds. It aims to complement previous reports in the OECD GSSS bonds series and present quantitative and qualitative insights into both the incentives and disincentives for investors with regard to GSSS bonds issued by public sector actors. To ensure that GSSS bonds live up to their potential for financing sustainable development, the views of investors are important to ensure that actions by issuing governments and donors are concretely responding to the needs of investors, and focused on practical solutions. At the same time, and especially given the volatile and uncertain geopolitical and macroeconomic environment between the time the survey was conducted (April – September 2024) and the publication of this report, it remains important to contextualise the views of investors and recognise the changing nature of these as well. It is also necessary to situate the findings of this survey within recent developments in global debt markets – which proved resilient in 2025, while borrowing levels reached historic highs (OECD, 2026 $^{[3]}$ ). Developing countries have faced additional dynamics – for example, in 2024, the average interest rate for these countries reached the highest level since before the 2009/2009 financial crisis (World Bank Group, 2025 $^{[16]}$ ).

The survey focused specifically on public sector issuances. This is due to the advantages and potential that GSSS bonds hold for these actors, who have a clear responsibility to drive progress towards the SDGs and climate goals. Local public sector entities are typically the main counterparts to donors' development co-operation programmes in developing countries: this therefore makes them particularly relevant when looking to strengthen donor interventions. At the same time, the private sector continues to be the main player in the GSSS bond market (Luxembourg Green Exchange, 2025 $^{[1]}$ ), pointing to the relevance of seeking to understand how to effectively scale issuances from the public sector.

## Survey methodology

This paper takes the perspectives and insights shared as part of the OECD-LuxSE survey and follow-up consultations as a starting point for an improved understanding of how investors view public sector GSSS bonds and for strengthening development actors' approach to supporting GSSS bonds in developing countries. The aim of this paper, therefore, is not to present a comprehensive overview of the global investment landscape of GSSS bonds – which has been the focus of other complementary studies (see Annex A).

Instead, the OECD-LuxSE survey was designed to provide insights into investors' incentives and disincentives regarding public sector GSSS bonds, via the use of mixed methods. It included a quantitative component, through the analysis of the survey's responses, and a qualitative angle, through ample space for investors to elaborate on their replies, also via follow-up consultations. The survey's structure aims to outline the present and future trends in the public sector GSS bond and SLB markets respectively, hence targeting investors who hold, have held or intend to hold these instruments. The diversity of responses – from both global and domestic investors across advanced and emerging markets – provide a glimpse into investors' perspectives on GSSS bonds. It also reflects the small presence of investors in developing countries and in thematic bonds.

The findings of the survey provide insights into investors' views on public sector GSSS bonds. Since most GSSS bond activities, especially from the public sector, take place in advanced markets, the survey focuses on the public sector GSSS bond universe more broadly. At the same time, the questions implicitly look at factors that are likely to be more pronounced in developing country contexts (eg. risk factors, changing demand and supply dynamics). Follow-up consultations also had a particular focus on the differences between developing and advanced markets.

As such, the survey points to areas and considerations that donors, including the DAC, and development actors more broadly, can prioritise to support the public sector GSSS bond market in developing countries and to make these instruments more attractive to investors. At the same time, and as mentioned, not all survey questions focused exclusively on developing country issuances. This means that policy considerations coming out of survey responses are highly relevant to donors, but also to policymakers in both developed and developing countries. Indeed, certain actions – for example the harmonisation of local standards and principles, or efforts relating to benchmarks and indices – are important in advanced markets as well as developing countries.

The survey was divided in eight sections. The first two sections aimed to map the investor type and thematic strategy for GSSS bonds, while the rest touched upon incentives and demand for these instruments, as well as trade-offs and risk factors affecting investment. Another part of the study also focused on investors' outlook and expectations regarding the market. This adds a forward-looking component to the study. A comparison between the short- and long-term is also included in the survey, by investigating the micro and macro risk factors, as well as trade-offs within the broader GSSS bonds category and compared to conventional vanilla bonds. The final part of the study looked at concrete policy considerations for the donor community, developing country issuing entities and the market more broadly to support public sector GSSS bonds in developing countries and make these more attractive to investors.

## Overview of participating investors

A total of 51 investors were invited to take part in the survey, and 11 responses were received between $24^{\text{th}}$ April and $25^{\text{th}}$ September 2024. Despite the relatively small sample size, surveyed investors collectively manage more than USD 5.7 trillion in assets under management. $^{3}$ Most of the survey respondents (10 out of 11 respondents) classified as asset managers, followed by commercial banks. The survey focused on investors who hold, have held or intend to hold GSSS bonds; within this broader GSSS category, a minority of surveyed investors also indicated to invest in Sukuk bonds with a GSSS classification. In a similar vein, the findings show that an important share of surveyed investors also hold non-GSSS bonds issued by public sector actors (82% hold inflation-linked bonds and longer-dated securities, while 64% hold variable rate notes). From a geographical point of view, the public sector GSSS bonds which surveyed investors hold or intend to hold (in the coming 12 months) are issued in the Middle East and North Africa, followed by East Asia & Pacific, Latin America & Caribbean and Europe.

As part of the survey, investors were also asked to specify the themes aligned with either the use of proceeds (in GSS bonds) or the key performance indicators (KPIs) (for SLBs) of bonds issued by public sector actors that they currently hold or intend to hold. Here, a distinction was made between (I) thematic bonds, in line with existing standards and the SDGs, and (II) more cross-cutting thematic bonds, relating for example to themes like gender inclusion or human rights. Most investors pointed to thematic bonds within their investment strategy and approach, with climate mitigation, adaptation, and protection and restoration of biodiversity and ecosystems as the primary themes aligned with use of proceeds and KPIs. Indeed, ambitious KPIs on SDGs and GHG emission targets represent the main factors for investors to invest in labelled bonds (World Bank, 2024 $^{[17]}$ ). By contrast, cross-cutting thematic bonds are a secondary focus for surveyed investors. This may be due to the fact that it can be more difficult to establish an effective use of proceeds and key performance indicators, as in the case of health and social sectors.

This OECD-LuxSE survey has several limitations, which concern its methodology and implications. In the first place, the limited sample size represents a key constraint in the analysis. In a similar note, the geographical focus within this research is not comprehensive of the heterogeneity of issuances across the countries within the same region. For instance, in the case of the Latin America and Caribbean region, the level of development of the GSSS bonds market is very diverse, with some countries more at the forefront of innovative financial instruments use compared to others (OECD, 2024 $^{[18]}$ ). Additionally, some types of GSSS bonds, including Sukuk bonds with a GSSS bond classification, tend to reflect a niche corner of capital markets specific to certain regional investors and are hence not generalisable. In this regard, it is worth highlighting that the surveyed investors are not a representative sample of the broader investment landscape, as they are already investing in GSSS bonds and, hence, sensitive to sustainable development themes. To this end, the findings of the OECD-LuxSE survey analyse the views of a specific subset of investors on GSSS bonds and the role of donor support in this area. Ultimately, scaling capital flows towards developing country GSSS bonds will also rest on these issuances meeting the needs of investors: the quantitative and qualitative insights of those investors already active in this space are therefore highly valuable. As mentioned, the changing geopolitical and macroeconomic environment – particularly pronounced between the time the survey was conducted, and this report published – may also have changed investors' perception of GSSS bonds and aversion to risk.

## Box 1.2

## The limitations of GSSS bonds for developing country issuers

GSSS bonds offer important opportunities to align capital markets with countries' climate and development objectives. Yet this does not make them a one-size-fits all or one-shot solution for developing countries.

First, GSSS bonds are debt instruments, meaning that their use should presuppose an issuer's ability to take on more debt. This can make them unsuitable for countries already facing fiscal pressures, which, paradoxically, includes many of those most affected by climate change. This therefore means that their use needs to be carefully considered and managed. This is especially important in light of the growing global concern over debt sustainability – amid rising interest rates, high refinancing needs, and increasing debt service levels (OECD, 2025 $^{[4]}$ ).

Further, issuing bonds requires significant technical and financial capacity, which some entities in developing countries may lack. This is especially true for first time issuances. Since developing countries already struggle to access international bond markets at competitive rates, the additional transaction costs associated with GSSS issuances – such as external verification, impact reporting, and monitoring sustainability goals – can further discourage participation in the GSSS bond market (OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ).

In the case of use-of-proceeds instruments, issuers face additional challenges. One point relates to the lack of pipelines of bankable assets. Relatedly, even when fiscal space allows for issuance and associated spending on sustainability, the pace of implementation of sustainability projects often lags behind issuance progress. High-capacity countries, such as Chile, exemplify this challenge: while Chile has demonstrated strong issuance capabilities by leveraging its established green bond framework and capital market development (OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ), a broader challenge remains in aligning bond issuances with timely implementation of sustainability projects and subsequent impact realisation. This can be seen from Chile's allocation reports, where most green bonds from 2019, 2020 and 2021 remain yet to be allocated (Ministerio de Hacienda, Gobierno de Chile, 2023 $^{[19]}$ ). Such discrepancies can lead to underutilisation of funds and potential investor scepticism, without the corresponding social or environmental impact expected from these financial instruments. As such, instruments like SLBs can be powerful alternatives as they are not linked to underlying assets but rather the achievement of sustainability targets.

## Complementarity with other financial instruments

GSSS bonds must therefore be seen as one of many sustainable finance instruments available to entities in developing countries, based on their financing needs as well as the macroeconomic and institutional setting. This includes a range of alternatives – including concessional loans, grants, and private placements. Furthermore, integrating sustainable debt instruments with debt-for-nature or climate-related debt clauses could provide a means to ensure that sustainability commitments do not exacerbate fiscal pressure, offering a more robust framework for financing long-term environmental and social objectives (OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ).

This chapter presents findings from the section of the OECD-LuxSE survey focusing on incentives and demand drivers influencing investment decisions for GSSS bonds. Given the specificities of GSS bonds and SLBs, these questions looked at the two sub-groups separately – and compared them either to conventional bonds, or to each other. The findings build on a qualitative analysis of responses to different survey questions as well as qualitative insights from investors, including quotes. As such, the findings provide insights and perspectives into how and why investors choose to invest in GSSS bonds.

According to surveyed investors, the decision to invest in public sector GSS bonds over conventional bonds with a similar risk profile is driven primarily by two factors: (I) alignment with institutional investment strategy and sustainability goals, and (II) the potential for long-term return due to mitigation of potential risks factors (Figure 2.1). Transparency and disclosure of the issuer's environmental and social impact represent another key factor influencing investments in GSS bond types. Consultations with investors revealed that while the different factors outlined in Figure 2.1 can all play a role in the choice between a public sector GSS bond or conventional bond, this will ultimately be influenced by the particular investment strategy applicable to each portfolio or fund. At the same time, when asked to choose between a conventional and GSS bond offering from the same hypothetical sovereign government – with the same maturity, coupon rate, and price – all survey respondents chose the GSS bond. This was the case even without specific blended finance or other incentives to render the GSS bond more attractive. Anecdotal evidence from sovereign issuers reflects this as well: during roadshows, investors overwhelmingly support the issuance of GSSS bonds, and specifically green bonds.

"We may invest in public sector GSS bonds over conventional bonds where suitable for the investment strategy of a particular portfolio."

Respondents also indicated that a bond contract which clearly defines criteria on the issuer's obligations on the allocation, reporting and disclosure on the use of proceeds, represents the driving characteristic reinforcing the investment case for GSS bonds (compared to conventional bonds issued by the same public actors with a comparable coupon rate). Interestingly, while investors do favour GSS bonds which align with a domestic or regional taxonomy and/or domestic or international standard, only 45% of investors see this as the most important characteristic driving the investment choice (over a conventional bond).

## FINDING 2:

While a majority of investors view GSS bonds as comparable in risk to conventional bonds and expect similar returns, financial returns continue to be important factors driving investment decisions.

"Some of [the assessment of the risk-return profile of public sector GSS bonds] depends on the type of mandate/how client objectives define our fiduciary duty. For mandates in which sustainability is an investment objective, we have to weigh the trade-off between returns and impact. In most other settings, we have to consider risk-reward specific to each issuer/instrument, where sustainability factors & market technicals are more of an input to, rather than an outright driver of, the investment decision."

When assessing the risk-return profile of GSS bonds compared to conventional bonds, the majority of respondents reveal that they perceive GSS bonds as comparable in risk to conventional bonds – and expect similar returns. One investor explained this by describing GSS and conventional bonds as 'pari passu': the financial metrics are the same, meaning that the probability of the public sector issuer defaulting on the two bonds is exactly the same. Conversely, the same investor added that the fact that an issuer has a sustainable issuance programme is a positive sign, but this should, in theory, reflect well on all its issuances, not just GSS bonds. As illustrated in Figure 2.2, an important share of investors go as far as considering GSS bonds as less risky, due to their alignment with long-term environmental and social trends. They are therefore willing to accept potentially lower returns for this lower risk. This is known as the 'greenium', whereby issuers of GSS bonds have, at times, achieved a pricing benefit compared to conventional bonds. This finding is especially interesting given that the evidence of the greenium in practice is limited and contradictory, especially across different issuer types and market maturities (Bolton et al., 2022 $^{[20]}$ ; Chouard and Jourde, 2024 $^{[21]}$ ; Duguid, 2022 $^{[22]}$ ).

In looking at how investors view SLBs, particularly those issued by public sector issuers, it is important to recognise the state and size of the market (see Box 1.1). The SLB market is relatively novel (with the first issuance in 2019) and has seen a decline since 2022 (Luxembourg Green Exchange, 2025 $^{[1]}$ ). Public sector SLB issuances therefore represent a very small fraction of overall public sector issuances. In addition, as the findings demonstrate, the fact that the coupon adjustment serves as an enforcement mechanism for issuers' sustainability targets is an additional determinant in the decision to invest in SLBs compared to conventional bonds. Changes in the structural and/or financial characteristics of the SLB in this way therefore contribute to enhancing credibility and accountability of the issuer. At the same time, however, several respondents pointed to the fact that – from the investor perspective – the coupon step-ups and downs are so small that they are essentially financially immaterial. This is in line with findings from other studies: indeed, most traditional bonds have a penalty of 125-basis points in the case of a drop in the issuer's credit rating – which is significantly larger than the 25-basis point step-up penalty of most SLBs (IFC-Amundi, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ).

Nonetheless, the issuers' perspective differs from the investors', considering the mandate of public debt managers to achieve the lowest possible cost. Still, setting appropriate targets and coupon adjustments is critical to the credibility of the issuer. Research has shown that, on average, the so-called SLB premium (which is the difference between the yield of an SLB compared to that of a conventional bond with a similar structure) is higher than the step-up penalty. This means that "issuers are able to lower their cost of debt by issuing SLBs even if sustainability targets are not met" (IFC-Amundi, 2024[5]). At the same time, and in the longer term, should an issuer's debt stock be made up of a larger share of SLBs, then even small financial adjustments would become increasingly more material to both issuers and investors.

Most investors reported that they do not face limitations – stemming from regulation (e.g. the EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR)) or internal restrictions – that constrain their ability to invest in SLBs. This is an interesting finding, particularly given that the limited SLB market growth is often attributed to uncertainty over whether SLBs qualify as 'sustainable investments' and can therefore be included within Article 8 and Article 9 funds (Graves, CFA, 2024 $^{[23]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). This finding may also be a reflection of the targeted pool of investors consulted. However, there is undeniably some lack of clarity regarding SLBs, and investors are responding to this in different ways. For example, one respondent noted that: "sovereign SLBs are currently not eligible for classification as 'Sustainable Investments' per our internal counsel/external advice, which limits their appeal in Article 8/Article 9 funds." Other investors have specific methodologies to evaluate high-quality SLBs: to be eligible for investment in Article 8 and Article 9 funds, SLBs must meet these criteria.

FINDING 4:

Investors typically do not have an ex-ante preference for GSS bond or SLB formats

"We have no ex-ante preference for GSS or SLB formats. Both can be useful. The key for us is that best practice is followed in each case."

"We prefer GSS issuances [compared to conventional bonds], all else equal, but the challenging situations are when all else isn't exactly equal."

When asked whether their investment strategy favours public sector GSS bonds or SLBs, investors generally tend to regard both sub-groups with similar preference and interest. The survey's participants indicated the main advantages of public sector GSS bonds and SLBs, respectively, although there is no explicit orientation a priori towards one over the other.

According to the findings, the most cited factor making GSS more attractive than SLBs is the transparency and tangible nature of the projects financed. This is in line with other investor surveys, which found a preference for knowing which projects will be funded using GSS bonds (World Bank, 2024 $^{[17]}$ ). Since GSS bonds fund specific projects, issuers face impact and allocation reporting requirements on the use of proceeds and expected impact on a yearly basis (ICMA, 2022 $^{[24]}$ ). For investors, these features can therefore help make GSS bonds more suitable for governance control reasons and impact reporting, in contrast to SLBs. This can also perhaps contribute to making GSS bonds more clearly align with overall investment strategies. As pointed out in Findings 1 and 3, alignment with institutional investment strategies is the driving factor determining investment decisions.

In comparing between public sector GSS bonds and SLBs, some investors pointed to a preference for simplicity. Highly complex or tailored instruments, which can at times include SLBs, mean that investors need to spend more time and resources to evaluate and compare different issuances (see Annex B). In consultations, some investors therefore pointed to the effectiveness of green bonds in this regard – which replicate the successful conventional bond coupon and yield structure which investors are already familiar with. This preference for simplicity – or rather the dislike for complexity – is also true for bond issuers. Previous OECD work has already pointed to the added complexity of issuing GSSS bonds compared to conventional bonds – and this is especially pronounced for developing countries (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). However, even in light of limited evidence of the ‘greenium’, debt management offices continue to see significant advantages for issuing GSS bonds – with the most important being the diversification of the investor base (World Bank Treasury, 2022 $^{[13]}$ ).

FINDING 5:

Investors' ability to invest in public sector GSS bonds or SLBs does not depend on superseding criteria relating to the overall credit rating of the issuer

"We make an issuer-level determination on all investments [...] When we've decided

to allocate to an issuer, we will turn to the instrument-level decision to determine

the best security type/characteristic to execute that decision. This is the stage

where we will consider GSS/SLBs. Technical issues are a big consideration

in this decision. But the overall issuer analysis is still the critical step in the process.

Credit ratings, specifically, are a more minor input in the decision."

For the majority of survey respondents, the ability to invest in public sector GSS bonds or SLBs does not depend on superseding criteria with respect to the overall credit rating of the sovereign or entity. This does not mean that credit rating is not an important factor in the investment decision. Yet many investors explained that they can – at least in theory – invest across the credit rating spectrum, and that investments are evaluated and determined first and foremost on an issuer-by-issuer basis. Here, the decision will be determined by a wide number of factors – including financial data, credit rating, and a broader evaluation of the issuer (including sustainability criteria). Therefore, the credit rating is a relevant factor – but it will mostly influence the decision to invest (or not) in an issuer, rather than the decision to invest (or not) in a GSSS bond over a conventional bond. Indeed, once the choice to allocate to an issuer has been made, the decision will turn to the instrument type – which therefore includes conventional bonds, GSS bonds and SLBs. Here, a range of technical factors relating to the bond itself will therefore be considered, rather than more overarching factors relating to the credit rating.

One stakeholder also stressed the fact that when comparing investment options, they will indeed assess the issuer and instrument – but that ultimately the comparison of attractiveness is made versus a similar investable universe (e.g. issuers with a similar credit rating). Another stakeholder agreed that ratings typically do not impact the decision between GSSS and conventional bonds, with the exception of lower rating contexts. Here, in the case of GSSS bonds, issuers may be less credible when it comes to delivering on their use-of-proceeds and therefore be at a greater risk of greenwashing.

This chapter presents findings from the section of the OECD-LuxSE survey focusing on the risk factors – both short-term macroeconomic and institutional – that influence investor appetite to hold public sector GSS bonds and SLBs. It also presents the findings from another section of the survey, which aimed to determine how investors see the outlook and expectations of the GSSS bond market – therefore focusing on supply and demand dynamics. The findings therefore provide insights into how investors perceive the GSSS' bond market evolution. The findings are presented quantitatively, through survey responses, and qualitatively with insights and direct quotes drawn from follow-up consultations with investors.

Here, it is important to acknowledge the highly volatile and uncertain geopolitical and macro-economic context – especially between the time the survey was conducted (between April and September 2024) and the publication of this report. These dynamics are likely to have further impacted investors' perceptions of GSSS bonds, and could have impacted their appetite as well. It is therefore necessary to situate these findings within a broader understanding of developments in the global bond market – including increasing borrowing levels and costs, and the growing role of more price sensitive investors (OECD, 2026 $^{[3]}$ ).

## FINDING 6:

Investors' appetite for GSSS bonds is influenced by macroeconomic and institutional risk factors, but these are typically consistent across conventional bonds as well

"The perceived economic risk for a country, is the same across conventional, GSS and SLB bonds. Ultimately, they are all pari-passu bonds."

"Any country is exposed to such risks!"

Investors were asked to indicate the major material risk factors that could affect their appetite to hold GSSS bonds in the next 12 months. Here, material risk factors were defined as those that can lead to defaults, restructurings, hyperinflation or other financial repression that would impact bond repayment in the next 12 months. Such material risks were further subdivided into two categories. The first relates to major short-term global, regional, and supranational risk factors - which are mostly of macroeconomic nature. The second relates to institutional factors of the issuer itself. These two risk categories are interdependent, with the institutional component affecting the economic structure of a country or region, and vice-versa.

From a macroeconomic perspective, the findings report that the physical risks and exposure to natural disasters, affecting a country's ability to repay, along with creditworthiness perceptions, associated to the country or region of interest, represent the main concerns for investors to hold GSSS bonds (see Figure 3.1). The fact that creditworthiness is identified as a high risk is noteworthy, especially given the fact that investors also noted that the ability to invest in public sector GSSS bonds does not depend on superseding criteria with respect to the overall credit rating (see Finding 5). While this may therefore seem like a contradictory finding, it in fact supports the explanation from Finding 5, whereby investors consider the credit rating as a relevant factor influencing the decision to invest in an issuer more broadly, rather than as a factor influencing the choice between a conventional or GSSS bond. On a different note, economic growth rates and transition risks, associated with policy and regulatory changes, do not classify as determinant risk factors affecting investors' sentiment towards holding GSSS bonds.

Relating to institutional risk, political instability/uncertainty and weak governance/institutions were identified as the major risk factors for investors (see Figure 3.1). By contrast, the revision of financial regulations and operational risks – related to IT risk and human capital – within the country do not classify as material risks to the survey's participants.

In assessing both the macroeconomic and institutional risks for a country, investors pointed to the fact that these are ultimately the same across all public sector bond types (conventional, GSS and SLB). Again, this reinforces the fact that investment decisions are made first and foremost at an issuer-level (see Finding 5), where macroeconomic and institutional risk factors will be evaluated. One investor also pointed to the fact that the ratio of local to external debt is also an important factor to consider – but that this is again relevant for all bond types. When asked about the countries or regions most exposed to these risks, investors had diverging views. Some responded that they consider countries with weak institutions, precarious public finances, high debt-to-GDP ratios, and limited financing options as more at-risk. Others, instead, responded that ultimately any country is exposed to such risks.

## FINDING 7:

Looking ahead, investors expect the dynamics in both the GSS bond and SLB market to improve

Respondents were asked to forecast the outlook and expectations in the GSS bond and SLB markets, in the coming 12 months. Within the GSS bond market, the findings suggest an expected increase in investors' demand for public sector GSS bonds, as well as in governments' commitment to these instruments. Investors pointed to an expectation for more investment towards climate mitigation and adaptation. This can lead to a greater number and size of projects backed by GSS bonds – which in turn can also mean that investor demand, eligible expenditures, and range and maturities of issuances also increase. In parallel, the supply of guidelines, taxonomies, and regulations supporting the use of GSS bonds is expected to improve. This is also true for the supply of externally reviewed GSS bonds. These can be interpreted as a direct consequence to the increased interest from investors and issuers alike, as these actors expect greater clarity on the market.

Looking at the SLB market, investors expect an improvement in both investors' demand for and governments' commitment to SLBs. In a similar vein, the supply of externally reviewed public sector SLBs and supply of taxonomies and regulations are foreseen to rise. As mentioned in Finding 4, a number of investors see significant potential for public sector SLBs – therefore pointing to an expected market growth in this area. On this topic, some investors also anticipate municipal SLBs to increase specifically.

Investors' expectations for the GSSS bond market are in line with the broader market trend, which saw the GSSS bond issuances pick back up and have the second-best year ever in 2024 (Luxembourg Green Exchange, 2025 $^{[11]}$ ). This is despite increasingly polarised and fragmented views on environment, social and governance (ESG) factors, especially in the US (Clifford Chance, 2025 $^{[25]}$ ). SLBs issuances have seen a steady decline since 2022, but the fact that investors are hopeful about the future of these instruments is a promising development, and one which development actors must capitalise on by supporting the market.

Interestingly, the level of regulatory fragmentation and revision is predicted to remain relatively stable in the case of both GSS bonds and SLBs. On the one hand, the aforementioned increase in the supply of taxonomies and standards may be associated with increased harmonisation across different jurisdictions, for GSS bonds and SLBs. On the other hand, a number of investors pointed to the challenges that fragmentation poses. For example, the politicisation of the sustainable finance market can result in conflicting regulations between regions. This can lead to confusion – thus decreasing confidence from investors and issuers alike. At the same time, international investors in particular recognised the need for local adaptations of global GSSS bond standards – while noting that for them these can truly add an additional layer of complexity. Harmonisation, already called for in previous OECD reports, is therefore extremely important (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). In addition to this, investors also reported that they hope to see these (harmonised) regulations and taxonomies designed in ways that truly incentivise GSSS bonds over conventional bonds.

The final section of the survey aimed to identify specific instruments and policies that can be used to make GSSS bonds more attractive to investors, by overcoming the risks previously identified. The focus here was explicitly on developing country issuances of public sector GSS bonds and SLBs, with the aim of identifying concrete ways to scale these. Questions therefore focused both on the actions that issuing governments themselves can take, and on the priorities for donors to support issuances and mitigate risks. As previously mentioned, donors are the main target audience of the policy considerations. ## Public sector issuers in developing countries

POLICY CONSIDERATION 1:

Prioritise liquidity buffers and flexibility in the issuance strategy

"[It is] hard to boil this down to a specific set of actions, independent of starting conditions."

Investors were asked to indicate the mitigation tool(s) used by issuing developing country governments that would ease their concerns on macroeconomic and institutional risk factors (as outlined in Finding 6). As the findings suggest, liquidity buffers $^{5}$ represent the primary mitigation tool that can ease investors' macroeconomic and institutional concerns. Here, it is important to acknowledge that such liquidity buffers will be the result of specific policy choices on the ground – relating for example to monetary and fiscal policies, and long-term political consensus around these. At the same time, a strong understanding of the factors determining liquidity buffers (e.g. the stock of external liquidity relative to near-term external liabilities) is also needed. In this view, the donor community can provide technical support in a range of areas – including on sound debt management practices, encouraging broad economic dialogue across government and political lines in favour of financial stability, and supporting revenue collection – in order to strengthen emerging markets and developing economies' (EMDEs) adaptive capacity to fiscal shocks. In fact, when assessing a country's creditworthiness, investors consider various risk factors, including liquidity risk. Having robust liquidity buffers reassures investors that the government can manage short-term financial challenges, reducing concerns about default risk (World Bank, 2024 $^{[30]}$ ). Investors pointed to liquidity buffers being particularly important for small island developing states (SIDS) – which can be particularly vulnerable to climate change.

Following liquidity buffers, flexibility in the issuance strategy was rated as the second most important instrument for issuing governments. This flexibility allows them to adapt to market conditions, investor demand, and economic needs, hence addressing investor apprehensions related to certain risk factors, including liquidity risk and market volatility.

While a strong communications strategy on behalf of the issuer was not selected as particularly important in the survey questionnaire, it was brought up in a number of follow-up consultations. For example, in the context of newer SLBs, one investor pointed to the importance of explaining the materiality and rationale behind chosen KPIs and SPTs – to ultimately justify their ambitiousness. Similarly, linking these to the issuer's overall long-term strategy and funding – and communicating about this – was deemed important. In this view, emerging tools, such as the Assessing Sovereign Climate-related Opportunities and Risks (ASCOR), are key to inform and facilitate the dialogue between investors and issuers (Transition Pathways Initiative, 2024 $^{[31]}$ ).

Investors also value greater co-ordination between different government agencies, to a similar extent. This is positive: a World Bank survey found that all sovereign issuers of GSS bonds consulted have established inter-ministerial committees, to co-ordinate on bond reporting and proceeds allocation (2024 $^{[13]}$ ).

Commenting more broadly, investors also pointed to the fact that mitigation tools from issuing governments are useful and important in supporting all types of issuances – not just GSSS bonds. This once again stresses the fact that investment decisions are driven at the issuer-level, rather than the instrument level. At the same time, one investor also commented that while the mitigation tools outlined can all be important, it is difficult to prioritise them without considering the starting conditions of the issuer and issuance.

Donors and policymakers

POLICY CONSIDERATION 2:

Support the development of harmonised and interoperable locally-adapted standards, principles and taxonomies

Investors were asked to indicate the key priorities for donors to further support the quality and supply of GSS bonds and SLBs issued by public sector actors in developing countries. The first questions focused in particular on standardisation and risk reduction. As the results demonstrate, investors see a strong need for donors to support the development of harmonised and interoperable locally-adapted standards, principles and taxonomies. By definition, taxonomies and standards provide a classification system that helps identify sustainable activities, reduce risks of greenwashing, address market fragmentation, and shift investments towards key sustainability goals (OECD, 2023[9]). Here, it is necessary to recognise the importance of the local context: taxonomies and standards must therefore reflect the realities and expectations of different countries to create country ownership (OECD, 2024[10]). Within this field, several donors have been active in supporting the development of green taxonomies and standards. For instance, the World Bank's Sustainable Finance Advisory Program has supported many of the first GSS bonds issued by emerging markets in most continents, and it also supports countries in developing green/sustainable finance taxonomies (World Bank Group, n.d. [32]).

This gives greater importance to the donor recommendations made in previous OECD reports, under the Five Is framework – the OECD framework which highlights the major policy areas where donors can support the growth of the GSSS bond market. Under the "impact" policy area in particular, OECD work has called for donor support in the development of SLB and GSS bond standards, principles and frameworks that are adapted to local realities, while also reflecting market best practice (OECD, 2023[9]; OECD, 2024[10]). Indeed, in consultations, investors explained that they hope to see more technical assistance (TA) to establish sustainability frameworks for different contexts and issuers. Not only does this give issuers greater clarity, but it also makes issuances more credible in the eyes of investors. Here, robust investor relations and DMO platforms can be particularly important, such as the Institute for International Finance (IIF) Standards on Investor Relations, which help define communications channels between sovereign borrowers and market participants (Institute of International Finance, 2022[33]).

Investors also pointed to a need to continue promoting the ICMA Principles as the global best practice, and ensuring that local issuances are adapted to this. Increasing standardisation across taxonomies is particularly important in the context of GSS bonds: it can help reduce the risk of greenwashing and enhance market integrity and transparency by ensuring that these instruments genuinely contribute to sustainability goals. While such standardisation and harmonisation are crucial in developing countries, it should also be a priority in the development of GSSS bond standards, principles and frameworks in advanced markets, to ensure that the market globally is aligned. This therefore makes it highly relevant also for policymakers more broadly. In this context, it remains important to support sustainable finance frameworks with binding standards, at the national level, through an effective coordination among different financial stakeholders, as well as solid monitoring and evaluation mechanisms (OECD, 2024 $^{[18]}$ ).

POLICY CONSIDERATION 3:

Provide technical assistance for the implementation of budgetary reporting standards for the identification of eligible expenditures

In parallel, another donor priority, according to investors, should be the implementation of budgetary reporting standards for the identification of eligible expenditures. This is in line with findings from other surveys as well, where investors expressed strong preference for clarity over the projects which will be funded using GSS bonds (World Bank, 2024 $^{[17]}$ ). In fact, expenditure transparency is directly linked to a country's ability to attract capital flows, and it enables clear communication on impact and mitigates greenwashing accusations. Budgetary reporting standards ensure transparency on how bond proceeds are allocated to specific projects or activities and how targets are achieved in line with sustainability objectives. This can therefore enhance investors' confidence in an issuance by reducing the risk of misuse or misallocation of funds. Clear reporting standards can also facilitate the comparability and evaluation of the performance, progress, and effectiveness of GSSS bonds.

Donors and other development actors can therefore provide technical assistance to support issuers to develop and implement budgetary reporting standards. One way of doing this could be making their support programmes conditional to the use of budgetary reporting standards for the identification of eligible expenditures. As with the previous point, this point is also highly relevant in advanced markets. Especially given that the majority of GSSS bond issuances are currently in advanced markets, ensuring that strong reporting practices are in place here helps set strong examples for other issuers to follow.

Donors can also support countries more broadly to develop strong green budget tagging $^{6}$ practices, also for traditional bond issuances which have a sustainability component. More transparency regarding a country's expenditures and revenues with respect to climate and the environment would support opening a country's entire debt stock to investment from sustainability-oriented funds. This could address some constraints of increasing GSSS bond issuances in developing countries, such as limited fiscal space. Donors can therefore support countries in developing green budget tagging approaches, for example in line with the OECD Green Budget Tagging Introductory Guidance & Principles (OECD, 2021 $^{[34]}$ ). The Impact Disclosure Guidance is another useful tool for entities, especially in EMDEs, to produce impact measurement and reporting frameworks for their strategies and development plans (Impact Disclosure Taskforce, 2024 $^{[35]}$ ).

POLICY CONSIDERATION 4:

Prioritise developing sustainability-focused bond mandates for domestic funds

Investors were also asked to highlight the key priority areas for donors, in terms of capacity building and market development. The first high-priority area identified relates to supporting the development of sustainability bond mandates for domestic funds, including sovereign wealth funds and development banks. Green bond mandates for domestic institutions have the potential to signal commitment to sustainability objectives (International Finance Corporation, 2020 $^{[36]}$ ). At the same time, these investor mandates can signal confidence in GSSS bond instruments themselves – thus bringing in more local and international investors alike. In the long term, these spillover effects will ultimately lead to enhancing overall market confidence and, in turn, contribute to increased local capital market development. In that respect, donors and development actors can provide capacity building to domestic funds – to increase their awareness and understanding of GSSS bonds, and thus facilitate investment. Naturally, this also rests on a willingness and leadership – including politically – to invest in these instruments. Similarly, developing green bond mandates for local development banks – as issuers – has the potential to build market trends, therefore contributing to local market development and setting examples for other potential issuers to follow. Here too, donors can provide capacity building relating to the issuer perspective as well.

Here, one investor also pointed to how donor investment mandates can help overcome some of the credibility challenges of SLBs. As mentioned, there continues to be a lack of clarity on SLBs within SFDR. Increased donor investment in these instruments could therefore ensure credibility across multiple levels (KPIs and SPTs, issuers... etc.) and therefore help mainstream them among investors.

POLICY CONSIDERATION 5:

Deliver technical assistance targeted towards post-issuance disclosure and measurement of impact

Investors also see donor support in post-issuance disclosure and measurement of impact as a priority. In the context of use-of-proceeds bonds, post-issuance disclosure ensures transparency and accountability on how the proceeds are allocated (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2019 $^{[37]}$ ). In the context of performance-based SLBs, post-issuance reporting typically relates to disclosure "relevant for assessing [sustainability performance target] performance leading to a potential adjustment of [the...] financial and/or structural characteristic(s) of the bond" (ICMA, 2023 $^{[38]}$ ). Therefore, disclosing and measuring impact can help increase investors' confidence and trust in the issuer and local market, jointly with informing future investment decisions by establishing a track record. Crucially, they can also help build a strong reputation for GSSS bonds themselves, thus countering potential greenwashing accusations and increasing investor demand more broadly. These reputational costs can have broader implication for the issuer, for example making it harder to raise capital through future bond issuances. In this view, donors are increasingly supporting the case for increased transparency and accountability of the use of proceeds, against eventual greenwashing accusations. For example, the World Bank Sustainable Finance Program provides capacity building support to develop post-issuance allocation and impact reports (World Bank Group, n.d. $^{[32]}$ ).

Donors can therefore provide TA to issuers, to support them in producing post-issuance reports that are rooted in strong data. Here, however, it is important to note that reporting must also be adapted to the context of developing countries – which often lack the human and financial resources to measure and report on impact (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ). In the provision of their TA, donors are therefore well-placed to bridge the gap between the realities of developing country issuers, and the expectations of international investors. Data also underpins credible and ambitious GSS bonds, and donors can therefore provide funding and capacity building to strengthen data capacities in developing countries. The Five Is framework explores the 'Impact' dimension of GSSS bonds in depth, providing concrete recommendations in this regard (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ).

Donors can also facilitate the accessibility of reports for investors. At the same time, investors face difficulties in locating and accessing impact and allocation reports, and these have varying structures and levels of granularity (World Bank, 2024 $^{[17]}$ ). Assessing these therefore requires significant resources, which investors often lack. Investors therefore called for a greater harmonisation in reporting practices. Here, donors can support with the creation of a platform or database to centralise reports. Initiatives like the Inter-American Development Bank's Green Bond Transparency Platform – which provides benchmarked data on green bond issuances in the Latin America and Caribbean region – have been recognised for promoting harmonisation and standardisation in reporting practices (IADB, 2022 $^{[39]}$ ). Donors could therefore explore the replication and expansion of this to other regions and GSSS bonds more broadly. As a starting point, donors should also support public sector issuers in creating a dedicated webpage (e.g. on the Ministry of Finance website) hosting information on all issuances, to facilitate access to information (World Bank, 2024 $^{[17]}$ ).

POLICY CONSIDERATION 6:

Consider the potentially unexpected implications of blended finance approaches on GSSS bond issuances

"While mitigation tools are interesting – it is also interesting to keep

GSSS issuance as plain vanilla, and as similar to conventional as possible

(size, liquidity, benchmark eligibility etc), to make sure they are scalable

(every issuer can come to market) and broadly attractive (as opposed

Investors unanimously support and appreciate the ways in which donor support can mitigate risks related to GSSS bonds and support market development more broadly. Still, some investors pointed to the need to better explore and understand the unexpected effects of such blended finance approaches.

First, and as mentioned in Finding 4, a number of investors stressed a preference for simplicity over highly complex or tailored transactions. In light of this, they noted that some types of donor support – ranging from guarantees to the creation of local standards – can potentially mean that investors need to take additional risks compared to a conventional issuance. This can also impact the liquidity of the bond – as other investors may also be making the same calculations regarding risk. Without downplaying the importance of different types of donor support, this underscores the difficulty of coupling investor needs and preferences with those of issuers.

A second point raised by investors relates to how the use of guarantees can at times lead to issuances being excluded from benchmarks. OECD work has established the critical role of guarantees in supporting GSSS bond issuances (it is explored as part of the 'insurance' policy area of the Five Is framework) and in mobilising private finance (OECD, 2023[9]; OECD, 2024[10]; Garbacz, Vilalta and Moller, 2021[40]). It is worth noting that JPM revised its position, whereby the bond was eventually included in the index. While investors still received the bond very well, they did express concern about the exclusion – and especially the long-term effects that this can have on investor interest.

## Box 4.1

## Understanding the effects of partial guarantees on bond issuances: the case of the Ghana 2030 bond

In October 2016, Ghana issued a USD 1 billion international bond ("Ghana 2030 bond"), which received a USD 400 million partial guarantee by the International Development Association (IDA) arm of the World Bank. This was the first time that the World Bank reapplied partial guarantees to public bonds, and the issuance was very successful: it was 100% oversubscribed, and 15% of investors were new, thereby expanding Ghana's investor base.

## The positive effects

According to investors, the partial guarantee was crucial in helping make them comfortable with the bond offering. The World Bank's support more broadly – including a macroeconomic reform programme, and the fact that the World Bank team accompanied the government of Ghana on the bond roadshow – also contributed to investor confidence.

Due to the partial guarantee, both Fitch and Moody's also provided two notch uplifts to the issuance (giving it a rating of B1 / BB-, compared to the standalone sovereign rating of Ghana of B3 / B). Here, it is worth noting that Standard & Poor's does not give a rating uplift to partially-guaranteed sovereign transactions.

## The adverse effects

The Ghana 2030 bond's partial guarantee resulted in it being automatically excluded from the JPM Emerging Market Bond Index – one of the two main indexes in the emerging market asset class at the time. This is because the Index eligibility rules explicitly prohibited partially-guaranteed transactions. The exclusion from the Index therefore limited investor interest, bond liquidity and trading on the secondary market.

Despite this, as mentioned above, the bond was still very well received by investors. Nevertheless, investors did express concern about the index exclusion in the first stance, and JPM eventually changed its index methodology, with the bond being included in the Index.

POLICY CONSIDERATION 7:

Increase the inclusion of developing country GSSS bond issuances in benchmark indices

Investors pointed to the central role of benchmark indices in driving capital towards developing countries (Box 4.2 provides an overview of benchmarks, and their effects on developing countries). On this topic, it is important to acknowledge the diversity of investors. For some, investing in bonds that aren't included in benchmark indices means taking an off-benchmark risk – which requires greater scrutiny and assessment. Others are only able to invest in bonds included in specific indices – and therefore explained that the inclusion of GSSS bonds in these indices is a truly concrete way to maximise investor interest. There is also a realm of investors that can take much smaller size deals, while the loan market is also an option.

The discussion around benchmarks is particularly important and timely given the increase in passive investment strategies. Not only are these on the rise, but they risk locking developing countries out of international investors' portfolios: while passive holdings accounted for 1% of foreign money in public funds in developing countries in 2000, this rose to 37% as of January 2021 (MOBILIST, 2022 $^{[43]}$ ). Benchmark indices are therefore crucial determinants of capital flows, as repeatedly pointed to by investors consulted for this report. A 2016 World Bank study concluded that benchmarks have "statistically and economically significant effects on the allocations and capital flows of mutual funds across countries", while MOBILIST also concluded that the construction of indices is "one of the most important influences of flows to emerging [and frontier] markets" (Schmukler, Raddatz and Williams, 2016 $^{[44]}$ ; MOBILIST, 2022 $^{[43]}$ ). The inclusion of bond issuances from developing countries in benchmark indexes is therefore a fundamental enabler to increase investments in those regions, especially for investors with passive investment strategies.

Yet several challenges exist to ensuring that developing country issuances – including GSSS bonds – are included in benchmark indices. First, size is often an issue. Investors pointed to the fact that the smaller issuance size of developing countries or SIDS means that they are often automatically excluded from bond indices – and therefore from investors' radar. In these contexts, the size of the bond is driven by funding needs, the (limited) size and quantity of green and social assets, and the ability to take on more debt. Reflecting on the actions by donors and policymakers that can most support investor confidence in GSSS bonds, a number of investors in fact explained that at times this support is on issuances that are too small to be included in benchmarks – and therefore ultimately do not impact investors' decision to invest or not.

While benchmarks for emerging markets do exist, they have been criticised for concentrating on a small number of larger emerging markets; and for frequently changing the assets and markets that are included and excluded (MOBILIST, 2022 $^{[43]}$ ). For example, China, Taiwan and South Korea make up over 50% of the MSCI Emerging Markets Index (Theobald, 2022 $^{[45]}$ ). This therefore means that the Index is driven by these countries – and investors therefore inaccurately expect all emerging markets to perform in the same way (Theobald, 2022 $^{[45]}$ ). Among other factors, liquidity issues, market volatility, as well as credit and currency risks can further complicate the index performance measurement. Finally, a World Bank study also explores how the re-classification of countries (for example by upgrading a country from frontier to emerging market) can lead to significant re-allocations of capital from institutional investors – leading to at-times unexpected effects in international capital flows (Schmukler, Raddatz and Williams, 2016 $^{[44]}$ ). This underscores the need to further explore the effects of benchmark indices on capital flows, especially towards developing countries.

## Box 4.2

## Understanding benchmarks and their construction

A benchmark represents a market index, or aggregation of indices, that guides investors to construct and manage a portfolio, from a risk and return perspective, to ultimately gauge their performance against a standard measure. As a market index, a benchmark, by definition, tracks the performance of a broad asset class, including fixed-income investments, thus serving as a key determinant in investment decisions. Nonetheless, benchmarks are an important tool also for managing risks and structuring investment strategies. Through benchmark comparison, investors can measure the different levels of risk associated with fixed-income instruments, as well as help create diversified portfolios aligned with specific investment goals.

In the case of bonds, benchmarks include bond issuances according to methodologies and predetermined criteria, such as size, type of bond and credit rating. There are several well-known indexes, among which Bloomberg Global Aggregate Bond Index, which tracks the largest bond issuers globally, both sovereign and corporate. JPM EMBI Global Diversified Index represents another well-known index, which intends to track liquid, US Dollar emerging market fixed and floating-rate debt instruments issued by sovereign and quasi-sovereign entities. However, despite indexes typically being publicly available on the index provider's website, fixed income securities do not only trade on open exchanges, and bond prices are therefore less transparent.

The standard index methodology tends to reflect a four-step approach when establishing a benchmark. The first step relates to construction, where the index producers tend to follow a specific set of rules according to the type of securities to include. These criteria depend on the target audience for the index and which market segments it refers to. The second step relates to weighting, whereby the index producers choose a specific weighting methodology, which may refer to the variation of market capitalisation, or price weighting, among other methods. The third step is about calculation, and it is foundational to the process: once the weighting method is established, the index value is calculated based on the different components. The review process is the final step: after the index is established and in use, there are regular reviews to ensure the consistency and validity of the index, and, if required, the methodology behind its construction can be updated.

Green, social, sustainability and sustainability-linked (GSSS) bonds can contribute to closing the SDG and climate financing gap by linking scale, long-term duration and impact. Yet issuances from developing countries, which could reap the most significant benefits from these instruments, are very low and account for a small share of these countries' annual bond issuances (OECD, 2023 $^{[9]}$ ; IFC-Amundi, 2024 $^{[5]}$ ; OECD, 2024 $^{[10]}$ ). There is therefore a clear opportunity to scale GSSS bonds in developing countries – which will require more issuances coming from developing countries, and also greater investor demand towards these regions and instruments.

Complementing previous OECD work on GSSS bonds – which focused on how donors can effectively support GSSS bond issuances in developing countries – this report aimed to contribute to an improved understanding of how investors view public sector GSSS bonds, especially from developing countries. It builds on the insights shared as part of the OECD-LuxSE survey and in follow-up consultations – with a focus on the incentives and disincentives for investors regarding GSSS bonds issued by public sector actors, with a focus on developing countries. The survey findings help provide an understanding of the underlining components guiding investment decisions in GSS bonds and SLBs respectively, including how investors view the two instruments, risk factors, and broader market supply and demand dynamics. Crucially, the study found that surveyed investors tend to see both instruments as having advantages, and alignment with the broader institutional investment strategy is a key determinant of investment decisions in GSSS bonds. Investors typically reported having no superseding constraints regarding the overall credit rating of the issuing entity, but rather make decisions on an issuer-by-issuer basis. Relatedly, while macroeconomic and institutional risks are important factors influencing investment decisions, these are not constrained to GSSS bonds but typically consistent across conventional bonds as well.

These findings, while not claiming to comprehensively capture the investor universe, are a starting point for an improved understanding of how investors view public sector GSSS bonds. They are in turn valuable in guiding how to strengthen GSSS bonds in developing countries. The value added of the report consists of identifying policy considerations and action areas that public sector issuers in developing countries, donors and other market players can undertake to make GSSS bonds more attractive to investors. # Annex A – Review of existing surveys

The focus on both quantitative and qualitative insights in the OECD-LuxSE survey make it highly complementary to other existing investor surveys, providing explanations for trends and offering context on investor sentiment. Indeed, the global fixed income market, including GSSS bonds, has been the subject of numerous studies and analyses – including investor surveys.

Several surveys have already been conducted on GSSS bonds. An overview of these, up to 2024, can be found below, with a particular focus on those targeting investors investing in one or more of the following areas: (1) GSSS bonds, (2) public sector bond issuances, and/or (3) developing country bond issuances.

\- Sustainability-linked Sovereign Debt Hub – Investor Questionnaire Results Report (September 2023): The survey – targeting a dozen major institutional sovereign debt asset managers in Europe and the US investing in both emerging and developed markets – aims to better understand what is needed to scale the sovereign sustainability-linked debt market. The survey compares SLBs to GSS bonds, and also delves into specific characteristics of the former (Sustainability-linked Sovereign Debt Hub, 2023 $^{[50]}$ ).

\- RBC 2024 Global ESG Fixed Income Investor Survey (February 2024): Surveying over 110 institutional investors representing more than USD 26.3 trillion assets under management globally, the survey aims to highlight the most salient sustainable finance themes. It explores how investors are integrating ESG considerations into their mandates, and looks specifically at GSSS bond characteristics (RBC, 2024 $^{[51]}$ ).

\- PwC ESG Transformation of the Fixed Income Market (2022): Combines perspectives from both issuers and investors of GSS bonds. It includes 100 answers from actual and potential issuers of GSS bonds, as well as 100 answers from international investors coming predominately from Europe and North America. The survey findings are used as a starting point to provide an overview of the current and future state of the GSS bond market (PwC, 2022 $^{[52]}$ ).

\- Official Monetary and Financial Institutions Forum – Global Public Investors 2024 survey: Surveying 73 central banks globally, with USD 5.4 trillion in international reserve assets, the survey aims to track and understand the asset allocation plans of reserve managers. The 2024 results found central banks to be diversifying their asset allocation within their fixed-income holdings. Some central banks are also adopting more active investment strategies, while also taking on more risk (Official Monetary and Financial Institutions Forum, 2024 $^{[53]}$ ).

\- World Bank Treasury – Sovereign green, social and sustainability bonds surveys (2022): Covering 32 debt management offices (DMOs) in emerging markets and 12 international investors and intermediary banks with USD 20 trillion in assets under management, these two surveys intend to understand the barriers and challenges to sovereign thematic bond issuance in developing economies. The results of both surveys reveal that most sovereigns are interested in the traditional use-of-proceeds bonds rather than SLBs, and are open to issuing in hard and local currencies, aligning with investor interests (World Bank Treasury, 2022 $^{[13]}$ ). Nonetheless, it is important to point out that the sovereign SLB market is still at an embryonic stage, which may affect sovereigns' decision to issue SLBs over GSS bonds.

## Notes

1 GSS bonds are use-of-proceeds instruments, meaning issuers commit to using the proceeds to (re-)finance specific projects considered to have a positive environmental and/or social impact. SLBs are a forward-looking performance-based instrument in that issuers explicitly commit to future improvements in sustainability outcomes with a predefined timeline. For more information, see OECD "Green, social and sustainability bonds in developing countries: The case for increased donor co-ordination" (available here: https://doi.org/10.1787/lcce4551-en) and OECD "Sustainability-linked bonds: How to make them work in developing countries, and how donors can help" (available here: https://doi.org/10.1787/7ca58c00-en).

2 Public sector bonds cover all sub-categories in the classifications as set by the Luxembourg Stock Exchange, and thus include sovereign (e.g. central government), quasi-sovereign (e.g. regional and metropolitan entities), and government agencies (e.g. government-owned pension funds and entities).

3 BPR Bank Rwanda PLC and BK Capital were not included in the final calculation of the total Assets under Management of the participants, due to unavailable data.

4 Compared to advanced markets, developing countries face disproportionately high costs of capital, as a reflection of higher risks – both real and perceived – for investors. Indeed, Spiegel and Schwank find that the average interest cost of outstanding government debt is three times higher in developing countries compared to in developed countries. This has severe consequences, as it means that countries' fiscal space is significantly limited – with implications for spending in key areas like social services. As of 2021, for example, LDCs spent 14% of their domestic revenue (on average) to cover interest payments. More information is available here: https://www.brookings.edu/articles/bridging-the-great-finance-divide-in-developing-countries/.

5 According to the Bank of England Prudential Regulation Authority, liquidity buffers are defined as the "stock of liquid assets, such as central bank reserves or high-quality government debt, that can be easily used to repay obligations as they fall due" – and are important in meeting unexpected changes in cash flows. More information can be found here: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/publication/2020/qanda-on-the-use-of-liquidity-and-capital-buffers.pdf.

6 According to the OECD, green budgeting tagging involves assessing each individual budget measure and giving it a "tag" according to whether it is helpful or harmful to green objectives. More information is available here: https://doi.org/10.1787/fe7bfcc4-en.

7 Fewer survey participants provided an answer to action i. "The sovereign is rated BBB+ with a stable outlook, and is on the Human Rights Watch global list for human rights abuses", compared to the other actions listed in the table.

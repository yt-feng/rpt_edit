## Abstract

This working paper updates the existing OECD methodology for estimating Air Emission Accounts (AEAs) to enhance their comprehensiveness and international comparability. The new methodology draws primarily on data from national greenhouse gas emission inventories, a range of auxiliary data sources including Physical Energy Flow Accounts, as well as OECD experimental estimates of emissions from air and maritime transport.

It improves the quality and granularity of estimates by economic activity (A\*64 industry breakdown), expands the geographical coverage to all OECD countries, and extends the temporal scope of the estimates (1990 to year t-2). It also widens the emission coverage to include international air and maritime transport in line with emission boundaries of the System of Environmental Economic Accounting (SEEA), and incorporates the territory–residence adjustments to comply with the SEEA Central Framework (SEEA-CF).

These improvements complement ongoing international methodological efforts to refine AEA estimates, such as those developed by the IMF and Eurostat, by drawing more extensively on country-specific data and achieving closer alignment with the SEEA-CF.

## 1 Introduction

1. Building on the 2018 OECD methodology (Flachenecker, Guidetti and Pionnier, 2018[1]), this working paper presents an updated approach for estimating Air Emission Accounts (AEAs) in line with the System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA). The updated methodology improves upon the existing 2018 approach by: (1) enhancing quality and granularity of estimates by economic activity (industry) incorporating additional data sources, (2) extending geographical and temporal coverage; (3) including previously excluded international transport emissions and territory-residence adjustments for mobile sources of emissions to comply with the SEEA Central Framework (SEEA-CF). Together, these improvements strengthen conceptual consistency with SEEA principles, international comparability, and the policy relevance of the resulting AEA estimates. At present, the gas coverage is limited to carbon dioxide $(\mathrm{CO}_{2})$ , and no estimates of total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are included. However, the methodology is designed to allow future extensions to other GHGs.

2. This work contributes to international efforts to expand and harmonise the availability of AEA estimates by improving the existing methodology to estimate the AEAs for countries that do not regularly compile official AEAs. Relevant initiatives to date include: (i) the OECD methodology for estimating AEAs (Flachenecker, Guidetti and Pionnier, 2018[1]), which this paper builds upon and updates; (ii) production-based emissions estimates by economic activity developed by the OECD Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation (STI) in the framework of carbon-footprint calculations (Yamano, Lioussis and Cimper, 2024[2]); (iii) Eurostat's AEA estimates for non-European countries used as inputs to their footprint analysis (Eurostat, 2025[3]); and (iv) the AEA estimation tool developed by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) within the context of the G20 Data Gaps Initiative (DGI) (IMF, 2025[4]).

3. While the 2018 OECD methodology represented a milestone in filling gaps for countries not compiling AEAs, it exhibited several limitations:

\- its geographical coverage was restricted to United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) Annex I countries $^{1}$ ,

• GHG coverage excluded fluorinated greenhouse gases,

\- time coverage and industry disaggregation was constrained by the availability of official inventory and output data,

• estimates were not fully aligned with SEEA boundaries, notably omitting international air and maritime transport,

\- it included no adjustments from territory- to residence-principles (see Box 1),

• output data were the sole auxiliary information used to allocate inventories to industries, and

\- road-transport allocations relied on allocation ratios derived from a narrow sample of European countries. $^{2}$

4. Similarly, the IMF and Eurostat estimation approaches do not implement territory–residence adjustments and rely on unvarying parameters across countries and/or years. The present methodology update addresses these limitations.

5. Despite the ongoing international efforts to expand the AEA coverage, including the launch of a global data collection by the OECD and the UN Statistics Division in 2023, major gaps in the availability of official AEAs persist, although it is mandatory to compile AEAs in the European Union (European Union, 2011[5]). According to the 2025 Global Assessment conducted by the United Nations, only 53 countries have compiled AEAs over the past five years, including non-disseminated pilot compilations (UNCEEA, 2026[6]). Beyond the limited geographical coverage, important differences in gas scope, time-series length, and level of detail by industry further constrain the comparability of the AEAs and hence their analytical and policy use. The updated methodology helps broaden the availability of information by providing granular estimates with the A\*64 industry breakdown, a standard aggregation of economic activities into 64 industries (European Union, 2010[7]), and extending the temporal scope to the period from 1990 to year t-2. It also improves international comparability by aligning with the emissions coverage and principles of the SEEA. It is in principle suitable for all OECD countries, owing to the availability of the required input data. In this research, however, it has been applied to non-EU OECD countries that do not currently report official AEA estimates, and to European countries to support plausibility checks.

6. The resulting AEA estimates can support a wide range of analytical and policy applications. They enable emissions to be analysed within the framework and perimeter of national accounts, i.e. alongside macroeconomic variables such as gross domestic product (GDP), value added, and output. This facilitates assessments of emissions intensity and thus analysis of decoupling between environmental pressures and economic growth. When combined with macroeconomic projections, AEAs can also support estimates of future emissions and monitoring of progress towards emission-reduction targets. Finally, AEAs provide essential inputs for carbon-footprint calculations.

7. This paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes the updated methodology for estimating AEAs. Section 3 outlines the data sources used and explains how time series gaps are addressed. Section 4 presents preliminary results, and Section 5 assesses their plausibility across different country groups. Section 6 discusses a possible dissemination strategy. Section 7 concludes with next steps. The Annexes provide additional technical detail, including the correspondence table used to allocate emissions from inventories to economic activities (industries) and households, and detailed plausibility checks.

# 2 Updated methodology

## General approach

8. The proposed methodology update maintains the widely applied practice of allocating national inventory emissions to economic activities (classified by the International Standard Industrial Classification of All Economic Activities (ISIC) or its regional equivalents) and households, the so-called “inventory-first” approach (Eurostat, 2015[8]) (see Box 1 for key differences between emission inventories and accounts). This approach helps preserve consistency between emission inventories and accounts and is consistent with the 2018 OECD methodology, as well as with methods used by the IMF, Eurostat, and many national statistical offices. It also offers practical advantages for extending the geographical coverage of AEA estimates compared with the alternative “energy-first” approach, given the limited availability of Physical Energy Flow Accounts (PEFA) data (see Section 3).

9. Under this approach, national inventory data $^{3}$ serve as the starting point, but several adjustments are made. First, system boundaries are adjusted to go from the territory to the residence principle by deducting emissions generated by non-residents on the territory (e.g. domestic aviation by non-resident airlines bunkering at airports of the reporting country) and adding emissions from international transport by residents as well as domestic transport by residents fuelling abroad. Hereto, OECD experimental estimates of $CO_{2}$ emissions from air and maritime transport are applied. Second, GHG inventory emission source categories are mapped to ISIC economic activities and households using a correspondence table. Where an emission source corresponds to a single ISIC economic activity or households in the correspondence table, emissions are allocated directly to that category (“1-to-1” mapping). Where an emission source corresponds to multiple ISIC economic activities or households (“1-to-many” mapping), emissions from the inventory category are distributed across the relevant ISIC activities and households. To do so, allocation coefficients (weights) across multiple ISIC economic activities or households are derived based on various auxiliary data sources following a preference order, as discussed later.

10. When inventory emissions data are available for both parent and child industry categories, emissions reported in the more detailed child categories are used for mapping, unless this results in implausible or incomplete industry allocation $^{4}$ , or the sum of the child categories does not match the parent category. In the absence of child-category data, emissions are mapped from the parent category.

11. The allocation can be expressed as a matrix multiplication, where emissions by ISIC economic activities and households are obtained by multiplying a matrix of allocation coefficients by a vector of emissions reported in inventory categories:

$$
a = W c,
$$

where a is the resulting vector of emissions by ISIC economic activity and households, W is the allocation (weight) matrix, and c is the vector of emissions by inventory category $^{5}$ . The entries in each column of the allocation matrix W represent how emissions by inventory categories are distributed among mutually exclusive ISIC economic activities and households. If inventory categories are in a one-to-one correspondence with an ISIC or households category, then the corresponding columns of the matrix W would consist of a 1 in the i-th position identifying the ISIC category and zeros elsewhere (i.e. a unit vector).

Otherwise, each column comprises multiple positive entries, each representing the proportion of emissions attributable to the respective ISIC categories. In either case, the sum of the entries in each column of W equals 1.

12. As a result, each entry given by the product $w_{j,k} \cdot c_{k}$ represents the quantity of emissions from inventory category k allocated to ISIC activity or households category j. Therefore, the proposed approach not only enables bridging GHG emission inventories based on the IPCC Guidelines to SEEA AEAs but also expands the information set by providing a more comprehensive and granular data set.

13. The main challenges lie in the length and the level of granularity of the IPCC inventory series, as well as the auxiliary information required to estimate the proportions by which inventory categories are allocated to ISIC economic activities and households (see Section 3 for data availability of inventory data and extension of time series).

14. This section examines the following aspects of the methodology in greater detail: (i) the use of inventory data (i.e. the vector c in the equation above) as the starting point for emissions allocation, (ii) the allocation of inventory emissions to economic activities and households (i.e. multiplication by the matrix W), and (iii) the treatment of air and maritime transport emissions (including fishing), which are handled separately from the allocation of inventory emissions.

## Emissions source classification systems

National GHG emissions inventories are reported by countries to the UNFCCC. The presented methodology uses national inventory data reported through the Common Reporting Table (CRT) as the primary source (see Section 3). Emissions inventories are classified by source categories and subcategories in accordance with the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Guidelines. IPCC source categories refer to emissions generated by specific physical sources and processes. In contrast, ISIC classifies productive activities based on the inputs of goods, services, and factors of production; the process and technology of production; the characteristics of outputs; and the use to which the outputs are put (United Nations, 2008[10]). For instance, IPCC emissions category 3 “Agriculture” includes subcategory 3.A “Enteric fermentation” which describes a biological process. By contrast, ISIC Section A “Agriculture, forestry and fishing” is disaggregated into Division 01 “Crop and animal production, hunting and related service activities”, and further into Group 014 “Animal production” (Sakata, Aklilu and Pizarro, 2024[11]).

Importantly, ISIC classification is based on the principal activity of the firm. For example, emissions from fuel use in agricultural transport are classified under CRT category 1 “Energy” instead of CRT category 3 "Agriculture". In AEAs, however, these emissions are allocated to ISIC Section A “Agriculture, forestry and fishing” if the principal activity is agricultural production. Emissions are allocated to ISIC Section H “Transportation and storage” only when the principal activity of the firm is the provision of transport services (Flachenecker, Guidetti and Pionnier, 2018[1]; Sakata, Aklilu and Pizarro, 2024[11]).

## Territory and residence principles

GHG emission inventories and AEAs also differ in their underlying accounting principles. Emission inventories follow the territory principle, whereby emissions are attributed to the geographical area in which they occur, regardless of the residency of the emitting entity. This means that all emissions generated within a country's borders are included, including those from non-resident units operating on the territory.

In contrast, AEAs are compiled according to the residence principle, consistent with the System of National Accounts (SNA). Under this approach, emissions are attributed to the resident units of an economy, irrespective of where the emissions physically take place. As a result, emissions generated abroad by resident units (e.g. international transport operated by resident companies) are included, while emissions generated domestically by non-residents are excluded. These differences are particularly relevant for activities such as international transport and tourism, where the attribution of emissions can differ significantly between the two accounting frameworks (Sakata, Aklilu and Pizarro, 2024[11]).

## Scope difference of national totals

In national GHG inventories, emissions from international aviation and marine bunkers, as well as $CO_{2}$ emissions from biomass combustion, are reported under memo items (information items) and are excluded from national emissions totals (IPCC, 2006 $^{[12]}$ ). In the case of $CO_{2}$ emissions from biomass combustion, this is to avoid double counting, since these emissions are reported in the LULUCF sector. In contrast, emissions from international transport by resident units are included in national totals in AEAs, and it is recommended that $CO_{2}$ emissions from biomass combustion be recorded separately from emissions arising from the combustion of fossil fuels (United Nations, 2014 $^{[13]}$ ).

In this paper, emissions from international transport by resident units are incorporated directly from the OECD experimental estimates, as described further below. Stationary emissions are allocated from the national inventory categories, excluding $CO_{2}$ emissions from biomass combustion.

## Allocation

## Core allocation strategy

15. The correspondence from CRT categories to ISIC economic activities and households is presented in the OECD reference correspondence table in Annex A. This table excludes the LULUCF category to align with the AEA emission scope under the current SEEA-CF. It supports the A\*64 industry breakdown, ensuring comparability between the resulting estimated AEAs and official AEAs, as the AEA questionnaires of Eurostat and the OECD/UNSD follow this level of detail. Annex A also summarises its development and reviews the main correspondence tables examined.

16. For 1-to-many mappings, inventory emissions are distributed across industries and households using allocation coefficients (weights). When a country reports all detailed emission source categories for $CO_{2}$ emissions, 122 categories are allocated through 1-to-1 mappings and 25 categories through 1-to-many mappings. If detailed categories supporting 1-to-1 mapping are not reported, allocations may rely more on 1-to-many mappings at more aggregated (parent) levels.

17. Given the broad country coverage and long time series considered, country- and year-specific allocation coefficients are essential. While the 2018 OECD methodology derived these coefficients according to the output share of each industry involved in the split (Flachenecker, Guidetti and Pionnier, 2018[1]), the proposed methodology extends this approach by considering additional variables from a wider range of auxiliary datasets to improve the plausibility of industry allocations. The specific selection differs across the various emission categories.

18. Allocation coefficients are derived using a hierarchical set of auxiliary data sources, applied consistently across countries. For each CRT category, the preferred auxiliary data source available is used across the full time series in order to avoid breakpoints in the resulting AEA estimates. Where the preferred auxiliary data source is only available for a limited number of years, it is extended to missing years using the approaches described in Section 3 (e.g. extrapolation or backcasting of PEFA using Energy Balances). Alternative auxiliary data sources from lower levels of the hierarchy are only used where the preferred source is entirely unavailable for a country. The hierarchy of auxiliary data sources applied to different CRT categories is described in the subsections below.

19. It is important to note that, based on the national inventory data for 2021, 29.4% of total CO $_{2}$ emissions excluding LULUCF in Annex I countries combined $^{6}$ fall into categories with “1-to-1” mapping using this methodology’s correspondence table. In addition, inventory category 1.A.1.a “Public electricity and heat production” - a special case of “1-to-many” mapping that is in most cases allocated largely or entirely to ISIC D “Electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply” - accounts for 30.8% of total CO $_{2}$ emissions. Inventory category 1.A.3.b “Road transportation”, which is distributed across all ISIC economic activities and households, accounts for a further 23.3%. Together, these three groups represent 83.4% of total CO $_{2}$ emissions. Consequently, ensuring accurate 1-to-1 mappings in the correspondence table and robust allocation of these key 1-to-many categories is crucial for the overall accuracy of the resulting AEA estimates. $^{7}$

## Hierarchical application of auxiliary data

20. As described in Section 3, the availability of auxiliary data varies across countries, and the preferred data source may be unavailable for some or all years. To ensure in cases of 1-to-many allocations that emissions are allocated using the best available information for each country, a hierarchy of auxiliary data sources is defined for different emission categories. The sub-sections below describe these hierarchies for all emission categories; categories not identified as special cases fall under either fuel combustion (general) or other emission categories (general). The hierarchy is based on conceptual considerations and empirical testing of the performance of the various auxiliary datasets in approaching official AEA estimates as further discussed in Section 5.

## CRT 1.A Fuel combustion (general)

21. For CRT categories under 1.A Fuel combustion, except special cases described below such as road transport, allocation coefficients are derived using the following hierarchical set of auxiliary data sources:

1. PEFA use table: emission-relevant use

2. PEFA use table: total use (i.e. sum of transformation use and end use)

3. IEA Energy Balances

a. Industry breakdown complemented by output

b. Industry breakdown complemented by intermediate consumption

c. Industry breakdown complemented by value added

22. The first auxiliary dataset of choice is PEFA, as it records the use of different energy products by economic activities and households. It is therefore the preferred auxiliary data source when a sufficiently detailed ISIC breakdown is available. Among the various PEFA tables, the preferred choice is the emission-relevant use table, but when this is not available, the total use table is used. Total energy use comprises transformation use (e.g. burning coal to generate electricity) and end use (e.g. using gasoline to power a car). Emission-relevant use is a subset of total energy use and refers to the use of energy products that result in GHG and air pollutant emissions through combustion (Eurostat, 2014[14]). It excludes non-energy use of fuels (e.g. oil used as feedstock for plastics), where carbon is not immediately released to the atmosphere, and is therefore prioritised over the total use table. PEFA data are compiled and collected annually.

23. When PEFA data are used, only fossil-fuel energy products are considered such as “hard coal” and “natural gas (without bio)”, excluding “motor spirit” and “transport diesel”, because they are considered most relevant to road transport emissions which are treated separately. Energy use in terajoules is multiplied by the corresponding emission factors from the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (IPCC, 2006 $^{[12]}$ ) $^{8}$ to derive implied emissions, which form the basis for calculating allocation coefficients.


## Other emissions (CRT categories beyond 1.A) (General)

25. For CRT categories beyond 1.A Fuel combustion (for which energy data are not relevant), except special cases described below, allocation coefficients are derived using the following hierarchy:

1. Output: sourced from the OECD STAN Database for Structural Analysis

2. Intermediate consumption: sourced from the OECD STAN Database for Structural Analysis

3. Value added: sourced from the OECD STAN Database for Structural Analysis

26. Emissions within AEA scope other than those from CRT category 1.A Fuel combustion arise mainly from CRT categories 1.B "Fugitive Emissions from Fuels", 2 "Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU)", 3 "Agriculture" and 5 "Waste". Among these, IPPU accounted for around $80\%$ of such $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ emissions in Annex I countries combined in 2021. CRT categories 3 "Agriculture" and 5 "Waste" are treated as 1-to-1 mappings in the reference correspondence table for $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ (see Annex A) and therefore do not require auxiliary data for allocation. CRT category 4 LULUCF is excluded from the scope of AEAs under the current SEEA-CF.

27. Output and intermediate consumption are considered as more relevant proxies than value added, particularly for IPPU. Output serves as an indicator of the scale of production, and is more directly observed than intermediate consumption. Intermediate consumption reflects production inputs such as raw materials, which are linked to process emissions, although it also includes other components less directly related to emissions (such as electricity and various services). By contrast, value added is conceptually less directly related to emissions, as it includes components such as wages and operating surplus which do not reflect physical production processes, while output also incorporates these components. Therefore, all three economic variables remain imperfect proxies for emissions-related production activities. As discussed in Section 5, empirical results do not provide a strong basis for establishing a definitive hierarchical preference among these economic variables.

Special cases

## CRT 1.A.1.a Public electricity and heat production

28. Inventory category 1.A.1.a “Public electricity and heat production” is one of the largest CO₂ emission sources in many industrialised economies. This category is primarily associated with ISIC D “Electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply”. Autoproducers, such as manufacturing industries that generate electricity/heat for their own use (whether through dedicated generation or combined heat and power processes), are excluded from 1.A.1.a and included in the sectors where they are generated under the IPCC definitions (IPCC, 2006[16]; IPCC, 2019[17]).

29. However, where waste management activities generate electricity or heat as a secondary activity (e.g. waste incineration), the Eurostat AEA Manual recommends allocating emissions to ISIC E37–E39 "Sewerage, waste management and remediation activities", based on supply-table information (Eurostat, 2015[8]). Evidence from the Netherlands' detailed data confirms that such emissions may be non-negligible in specific countries (Netherlands Emissieregistratie, 2025[18]).

30. Allocation follows this hierarchy $^{9}$ :

1. PEFA supply table: Supply of electrical energy (P26) and heat (P27) $^{10}$

2. National Accounts: Supply and Use Table (SUT), Supply of electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning

If neither source is available, emissions are allocated entirely to ISIC D.

31. In the PEFA supply table, supply of electrical energy (P26) and heat (P27) by ISIC D “Electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply” and E37–E39 “Sewerage, waste management and remediation activities” is used to derive allocation coefficients.

32. Where PEFA data are unavailable, the supply table in national accounts is consulted to assess the supply of product “Electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning” by ISIC D and E37–E39. The supply table in national accounts describes the supply of goods and services by product and by type of supply (domestic production or imports) in monetary terms.

## CRT 1.A.3.b Road transportation and related categories

33. Road transport emissions are relevant to virtually all economic activities and households, making their allocation particularly complex.

34. In this paper, it is tentatively assumed that emissions by non-residents on the territory and emissions by residents abroad broadly offset one another – an assumption that warrants further investigation and refinement. The magnitude of territory–residence adjustments is highly country-specific and depends on factors such as geographical characteristics (e.g. country size and land borders), degree of economic integration with neighbouring countries, and relative fuel prices, making residence-based estimation particularly challenging. Where available, Tourism Satellite Account data could help assess the magnitude of differences between emissions from non-residents within the territory and those from residents abroad.

35. Emissions reported under 1.A.3.b.iv "Motorcycles" (where separately identified) are allocated to households (1-to-1 mapping). $^{11}$ Emissions under 1.A.3.b.iii "Heavy-duty trucks and buses" are allocated across industries only. Remaining road transport emissions (e.g. Cars, Light-Duty Trucks) are allocated across both industries and households.

36. CRT 1.A.3.b “Road transportation” and its subcategories are first divided into diesel oil and gasoline using the fuel shares reported in the CRT. $^{12}$ Where the selected auxiliary dataset allows separate treatment of these fuels, fuel-specific allocation coefficients are applied to distribute emissions across industries and households.

37. Allocation coefficients are derived using the following hierarchical set of auxiliary data sources:

1. PEFA use table: Emission-relevant use of motor spirit (P14) and transport diesel (without bio) (P17) $^{13}$

2. PEFA use table: Total use of P14 and P17

3. Official SUTs of sufficient granularity: Use of gasoline and diesel

4. Average allocation coefficients derived from European countries' road transport emissions

38. Energy Balances are not suitable for allocating road transport emissions across industries, as they aggregate all road transport fuel use under the single flow “Road”, without distinguishing industries and households. Therefore, PEFA is the auxiliary dataset of choice if it has a sufficiently detailed ISIC breakdown. Where PEFA is unavailable, official SUTs are prioritised, provided that the product and industry breakdowns are sufficiently detailed to support allocation (e.g. gasoline and diesel, rather than aggregated petroleum products). $^{14}$

39. In the absence of detailed official data on diesel and gasoline use, average allocation coefficients derived from European countries' road transport emissions are used as a fourth-priority option.

40. The resulting industry/households emissions distribution of 1.A.3.b Road transportation is then applied to 2.D Non-energy products from fuels and solvent use, given its link to transport-related products (e.g. transport lubricant). This assumption is unlikely to materially affect overall estimation results, as category 2.D accounts for less than 1% of total $CO_{2}$ emissions based on Annex I countries' national inventories data. Emissions from 1.A.2.g.vii Off-road vehicles and other machinery are allocated using a common coefficient derived from detailed UK data, reflecting the lack of off-road specific data for most other countries; further refinements may be explored. $^{15}$

## Air and maritime transport emissions

41. The proposed methodology does not allocate air and maritime transport emissions (including fishing) from national inventory categories to AEAs. Instead, it directly uses OECD experimental estimates of $CO_{2}$ emissions from air and maritime transport. Residence-based emissions available in this dataset capture transport activities operated by resident companies, irrespective of where fuel is purchased, including emissions associated with bunkering abroad.

42. At present, the OECD experimental estimates of $CO_{2}$ emissions from air and maritime transport do not include an ISIC breakdown. Consequently, emissions from air, maritime transport (all vessels except fishing) and fishing vessels are allocated to ISIC Rev.4 H51 (Air transport), H50 (Water transport) and A03 (Fishing and aquaculture), respectively, in the proposed methodology. $^{16}$

43. The OECD experimental estimates of $CO_{2}$ emissions from air and maritime transport do not cover military aircraft or vessels. In national GHG inventories, emissions from military mobile sources are reported under category 1.A.5.b “Mobile” within non-specific fuel combustion activities. As a result, military transport emissions are included in the AEA estimates only to the extent that they are reported by countries to the UNFCCC.

# 3 Data sources

44. Under the “inventory-first” approach, the geographical and temporal coverage of AEAs is largely determined by the availability and structure of emissions inventory data, as well as by the auxiliary data required to derive the annual allocation matrices. This section summarises the data sources used in the updated methodology and explains how data limitations, such as gaps in time series of data sources, are addressed. The focus is on OECD countries that do not currently report official total-economy AEA estimates.

## Inventories

45. The quality of inventory data inputs has a direct influence on the resulting AEA estimates under the inventory-first approach. Reliance on unofficial inventory estimates may lead to substantial over- or under-estimation of total and sectoral emissions as they can differ considerably from the emission levels reported in official inventories (Cárdenas Rodríguez et al., 2024[19]). In contrast to some other international initiatives for estimating AEAs, the proposed methodology prioritises alignment and consistency with official inventory data as the primary emission data source.

46. Unofficial inventory datasets are used only where necessary to complement sectoral breakdowns and extend time series as described below.

## Official inventories

47. The current methodology uses the UNFCCC Common Reporting Tables (CRT) as the primary source of GHG emissions inventory data. Under the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) of the Paris Agreement, all Parties to the Paris Agreement (i.e. countries and the European Union) are required to report national GHG inventories using the CRT in accordance with the modalities, procedures and guidelines adopted by the Conference of the Parties in 2018 (UNFCCC, 2018[20]). GHG inventory data are broken down by emission source categories in line with the IPCC Guidelines as explained in Section 2. The CRT is the standard reporting format for all Parties since 2024 with no distinction between Annex I and non-Annex I countries. All OECD countries have submitted CRTs to the UNFCCC by 1 October 2025. The primary use of CRT therefore plays a crucial role in prioritising official emissions data and expanding the geographical coverage of AEA estimates.

48. Prior to the introduction of the CRT, the Common Reporting Format (CRF) was the standard reporting format, which was used only by the UNFCCC Annex I Parties. $^{17}$ The CRT and CRF use very similar structures and emission source categories. $^{18}$ Where relevant, the weights across subcategories available from CRF are used to restore the breakdown of CRT categories for the specific country-year combinations.

## Unofficial inventories

49. Since 2024, the International Energy Agency (IEA) has disseminated the dataset “Greenhouse gas emissions estimates matching the energy module of 2006 IPCC Guidelines” (IEA, 2025[21]). This dataset provides emissions estimates for carbon dioxide $\left(\mathrm{CO}_{2}\right)$ , methane $\left(\mathrm{CH}_{4}\right)$ , and nitrous oxide $\left(\mathrm{N}_{2}\mathrm{O}\right)$ . Although limited to IPCC Category 1 “Energy”, it offers extensive geographical coverage and a long time series, and its classification follows the IPCC Guidelines. In limited cases, the IEA dataset provides a more detailed breakdown of emission source categories than official CRT submissions. In such cases, weights across subcategories derived from the IEA estimates are used to complement CRT breakdowns while preserving the level of the parent category.

50. Another unofficial inventory dataset consulted is the HISTCR scenario, which prioritises country-reported data, from PRIMAP-hist. It combines several published sources to create a comprehensive dataset of GHG emissions for all Kyoto gases, covering years from 1750 to 2024 and all UNFCCC member states as well as most non-UNFCCC territories (Gütschow, Busch and Pflüger, 2025[22]). Although its sectoral detailing is limited, PRIMAP-hist is regarded as the unofficial source most closely aligned with national inventory data (Cárdenas Rodríguez et al., 2024[19]). It is therefore used in this paper as an interim source for extending official inventory time series until more complete inventory emissions estimates become available from the OECD Environment Directorate, as described below.

# 5 Discussion and plausibility checks

## Key improvements

77. Applying the methodology and data sources described in Sections 2 and 3 addresses the limitations of the previous methodology, particularly regarding industry detail, geographical and temporal coverage, and alignment with SEEA emission boundaries and residence principle.

78. First, the proposed methodology delivers AEA estimates at the A\*64 industry level plus households, consistent with the detailed templates of the global AEA questionnaire and the Eurostat AEA questionnaire, thereby enhancing international comparability and policy relevance. The quality of these estimates is assessed in the next subsection.

79. Second, by using the CRT as the primary inventory emission data source and a broader range of auxiliary data, the presented methodology extends geographical coverage and can now be applied to all OECD member countries, including those that did not submit total-economy GHG AEAs for dissemination purposes in 2025, i.e. Australia, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Israel, Korea, Mexico, and the United States. Moreover, the use of the CRT and a wide range of auxiliary data, combined with time-series extension techniques, expands temporal coverage. The methodology enables the estimation of AEAs from 1990 (a typical starting year for national inventory compilation) up to year t-2 (2023 as of the pilot estimation).

80. Finally, the proposed methodology aligns closely with the emission boundaries of the SEEA Central Framework (SEEA-CF) by including international transport, while excluding Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) and $CO_{2}$ emissions from biomass. The exclusion of LULUCF is in line with the AEA boundary in the SEEA-CF 2012, (Eurostat, 2015 $^{[8]}$ ; United Nations, 2014 $^{[13]}$ ). ${}^{24}CO_{2}$ emissions from biomass are distinguished from fossil-fuel emissions, in line with SEEA-CF recommendations, and are excluded from the estimates presented in this paper. International transport is included using OECD experimental estimates of $CO_{2}$ emissions from air and maritime transport, allowing the derivation of residence-based emissions for both domestic and international transport.

## Plausibility checks: Approach

## Country groups

81. The plausibility of the AEA estimates produced by this methodology is assessed by comparing the estimates with existing official AEAs. The benchmarking strategy varies by country group, depending on the availability and scope of official AEAs and auxiliary data. Three country groups are considered.

82. The first group comprises countries that compile official AEAs for the total economy, as well as AEA bridging items, AEAs for road transport emissions, and PEFA. Excluding countries with confidential or a shorter temporal coverage of PEFA/AEA data, or that report bridging items in a different structure, this group comprises 18 European countries: Austria, Czechia, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia, and Spain.

83. For these countries, AEAs excluding emissions from air transport, maritime transport and fishing, and road transport can be constructed using bridging items. AEA estimates derived under the proposed methodology—excluding CRT 1.A.3.b “Road transportation” and OECD experimental air and maritime transport estimates—are benchmarked against official AEAs within this restricted scope. In addition, emissions allocated from CRT 1.A.3.b are compared directly with official AEAs for road transport. This detailed benchmarking allows discrepancies to be analysed by emission source. The availability of auxiliary data, including PEFA, also enables sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of using PEFA, Energy Balances, or monetary variables as allocation bases. In line with the mandatory compilation of PEFA in the EU, analysis for this group focuses on the period from 2014 onwards.

84. The second group includes OECD countries with partial or historical $CO_{2}$ AEAs, such as Korea and the United States (historical compilations), and Colombia and Costa Rica (fuel combustion only). For these countries, the temporal coverage of the analysis is aligned with the years for which official AEAs are available (see Table 6). Where necessary, benchmarking is restricted to fuel combustion emissions to ensure comparability in scope.

85. The third group consists of OECD countries that have not yet officially disseminated AEAs for $CO_{2}$ . For Australia, which has historical AEAs for total GHG emissions, estimated $CO_{2}$ AEAs are compared with official GHG totals, where meaningful. For the remaining countries in this group (i.e. Chile, Israel, and Mexico), no AEAs for GHGs have been published. Therefore, the possibilities for quantitative comparisons of estimated emissions by industry are limited.

86. The remaining OECD countries are not considered in the plausibility checks. These are mainly countries that regularly compile total-economy AEAs but do not compile road-transport AEAs or PEFA. Therefore, a separate analysis of total emissions excluding transport is not possible, and the scope for sensitivity analysis using different auxiliary data sources, such as PEFA, is limited. Hence, these countries do not provide additional analytical value compared to Country Group 1. Moreover, these countries are not the primary target group for the application of the proposed estimation methodology, as they already compile official AEAs.

87. Comparison with historical AEAs should be interpreted with care, because the underlying inventory emissions data or other data sources may have been revised in the meantime, or the historical AEAs may have been developed as pilot accounts. AEAs from Australia and Korea were compiled in 2019 and 2018, respectively, while AEAs from United States were reported in 2024.

## Metrics

88. The plausibility of the estimated AEAs is assessed using a set of complementary metrics that jointly evaluate magnitude plausibility, structural misallocation, and growth dynamics. Together, these indicators capture distinct but complementary dimensions of plausibility, enabling a comprehensive assessment of total emission levels, their allocation across economic activities and households, as well as their temporal evolution. These metrics can be computed only when official $CO_{2}$ AEAs are available (i.e. not applicable to Country Group 3).

89. Magnitude plausibility at the national level is assessed using the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of total emissions. MAPE measures the average relative deviation between estimated and official total emissions over time and is defined, for each country c, as:

$$
M A P E _ {c} = \frac {1}{N _ {c}} \sum_ {t} \left| \frac {\hat {\mathrm{E}} _ {c , t} ^ {t o t} - E _ {c , t} ^ {t o t}}{E _ {c , t} ^ {t o t}} \right|
$$

where $\hat{E}_{c,t}^{tot}$ and $E_{c,t}^{tot}$ denote estimated and official total emissions for country c in year t, and $N_{c}$ is the number of years available for comparison.

90. MAPE provides a scale-independent measure of total-level plausibility that is comparable across countries of different size. MAPE focuses exclusively on the accuracy of national totals and does not capture potential misallocation across industries and households.

91. To quantify discrepancies in the industry and household allocation of emissions, the analysis computes the share of misallocated emissions, which measures the minimum proportion of total emissions that would need to be reallocated across industries and households to reproduce the official AEA distribution. For each country-year, total misallocation is calculated as:

$$
M i s a l l o c a t i o n s h a r e _ {c, t} = \frac {1}{2} \frac {\sum_ {i} \left| \hat {E} _ {c , t , i} - E _ {c , t , i} \right|}{E _ {c , t} ^ {t o t}}
$$

where $E_{c,t,i}$ and $\hat{E}_{c,t,i}$ denote official and estimated emissions in industry (or household) i for country c and year t, and $E_{c,t}^{tot}$ denotes total official emissions. The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ avoids double-counting of over- and under-allocations across industries and households.

92. The misallocation share is expressed as a proportion of total emissions, making it scale-invariant with respect to total emission levels. However, its value depends on the level of industry disaggregation: as the industry classification becomes more detailed, measured misallocation generally increases because over- and under-allocations that offset each other at aggregated levels become visible.

93. This document assesses misallocation at three levels of industry detail in Table 7. The medium level corresponds to the breakdown used in the misallocation analysis in the 2018 OECD methodology (Flachenecker, Guidetti and Pionnier, 2018[1])

94. In addition to total misallocation, the analysis examines the industry distribution of misallocated emissions, indicating over- or under-allocation in specific industries and households, expressed as a share of total official emissions. This helps identify which industries and households contribute most to overall misallocation and supports diagnostic assessment of the allocation methodology.

95. Finally, the ability of the AEA estimates to capture temporal dynamics is assessed by comparing growth rates of total emissions. Correlation coefficients between the growth rates of estimated and official AEAs are used as the primary metric to assess whether estimated AEAs replicate observed growth patterns. The mean absolute error of growth rates and directional accuracy (i.e. whether the signs of growth rates match) are used as complementary metrics.

## Road transport emissions

105. Figure 7 presents the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) for road transport emissions. The proposed methodology assumes that emissions by non-residents on the territory are offset by emissions from residents fuelling abroad.

106. MAPE values exhibit considerable variability, suggesting that the simplifying assumption of offsetting non-resident emissions with emissions from residents abroad does not consistently hold across countries. Luxembourg is a clear outlier as a substantial share of road transport emissions on its territory is emitted by non-residents. This reflects the fact that Luxembourg is a transit country and that non-residents from neighbouring countries frequently purchase road transport fuels in Luxembourg because of its significantly lower fuel prices, a phenomenon referred to as “fuelling tourism” (Eurostat, 2015[8]). Under the residence principle, emissions associated with fuels purchased by non-residents on the territory are excluded from official AEAs. As a result, the simplifying assumption can lead to particularly high MAPE values for Luxembourg.

107. Countries that are relatively small and/or have extensive land borders—such as Austria, Hungary, Poland, and Slovenia—display larger discrepancies (above 15%), reflecting the sensitivity of road transport emissions to residence–territory adjustments. In contrast, larger countries with more limited land borders and substantial maritime borders—such as Finland, Italy, Portugal, and Spain—show smaller discrepancies (below 3%).

108. These results indicate that the plausibility of road transport emission estimates depends strongly on country-specific circumstances. Among countries in Groups 2 and 3, the simplifying assumption is likely to be more realistic for geographically isolated countries such as Australia and Korea, who do not have freely accessible land borders.

128. The proposed methodology generates total-economy $CO_{2}$ AEAs with A\*64 industry breakdown for all OECD countries from 1990 to year t-2. $^{26}$ For countries that already disseminate official $CO_{2}$ AEAs, this may result in two parallel sets of estimates for the same country–year–industry/household combinations. While this overlap is useful for calibrating and validating the OECD methodology by comparing the two datasets (as discussed in Section 5), dissemination on the OECD Data Explorer should aim to provide users with a single, clear and coherent dataset.

129. The OECD therefore considers dissemination strategies tailored to five country situations:

i. countries with official AEAs covering a full time series from 1990 to year t-2 with A\*64 industry breakdown;

ii. countries with official AEAs covering a limited time series;

iii. countries with historical AEAs that have been discontinued;

iv. countries compiling official AEAs for fuel combustion only; and

v. countries without official AEAs.

In all cases, official AEA data remain unchanged; OECD estimates do not replace official AEAs for the same country-year-industry/household observations. OECD estimates are used only to (a) disaggregate official totals where additional detail is missing, (b) extend time series beyond the period covered by official data, or (c) fill gaps where official data are obsolete or completely missing. All such data points will be clearly flagged, and the data sources used for individual countries will be documented in the metadata.

130. First, for countries for which official $CO_{2}$ AEAs with a full time series and A\*64 industry breakdown are available and regularly maintained (i.e. excluding historical AEAs), the OECD will disseminate only the official AEAs (currently only applicable to the United Kingdom).

131. Second, for countries with official AEAs covering a limited time series (e.g. mandatory EU reporting starting from reference year 2008), official values should be prioritised for overlapping country-year-industry/household observations, and OECD estimates will not be disseminated where official data are available. Where OECD estimates provide a more detailed industry breakdown, the OECD will use sectoral shares derived from its estimates to disaggregate official totals while preserving the parent-category levels.

132. For backcasting or extrapolating beyond the official reporting period, the OECD will use its estimates to extend the official time series, as they closely replicate the growth patterns of official emissions, particularly for total emissions and emission-intensive industries (see Section 5). The potential use of contribution-based methods to extend official emissions will be explored, following the approach applied to extend the CRT time series described in Section 3.

133. Third, for countries with historical AEAs that have been discontinued several years ago, OECD estimates will be used as the default series for dissemination, reflecting potential revisions to underlying data sources such as national inventories. However, where countries prefer, or where underlying data sources have not undergone substantial revisions, the extension approach described above for regularly compiling countries may instead be applied to preserve historical official values.

134. Fourth, for countries compiling AEAs only for fuel combustion, official AEAs may be backcast or extrapolated using OECD estimates adjusted to the same fuel-combustion scope. OECD estimates for emission sources not covered by the official AEAs (i.e. CRT source categories beyond 1.A “Fuel combustion”) may then be added to the extended fuel-combustion AEAs to derive total AEA estimates.

135. Finally, for countries without official AEAs for $CO_{2}$ , OECD estimates will be disseminated on the OECD Data Explorer.

136. This working paper presents an updated methodology for estimating Air Emission Accounts (AEAs) that advances ongoing international efforts to produce robust, internationally comparable, and policy-relevant emissions statistics, which will be an important foundation for applications such as carbon-footprint calculations (Yamano, Lioussis and Cimper, 2024[2]). Building on the OECD's 2018 methodology, this updated methodology expands the geographical and temporal coverage of AEAs data, aligns more closely with SEEA emission boundaries, applies the residence principle for air and maritime transport, and improves industry granularity through the systematic and hierarchical use of auxiliary datasets. The methodology retains an “inventory-first” foundation while addressing key limitations of earlier approaches, particularly with respect to international transport and industry allocation.

137. The plausibility checks demonstrated that the methodology can reproduce national $CO_{2}$ emission totals, industry structures, and temporal evolution with a high degree of plausibility where input data are consistent and sufficiently detailed, except road transport emissions where significant territory/residence adjustments are necessary. Results also highlight the critical role of data availability in shaping high-quality AEA estimates. To ensure the use of official data sources as much as possible and enhance the applicability of the methodology without relying too much on back-recalculation/extrapolation, comprehensive inventory data are important. PEFA emerges as a key auxiliary dataset for energy-related emission sources to support plausible allocation, especially for obtaining a detailed industry breakdown. Conversely, data gaps—most notably in PEFA coverage—remain a source of uncertainty.

138. Overall, the proposed methodology provides a transparent and replicable framework for generating estimated AEAs that could complement official statistics. It offers a practical basis for improving emissions accounting in countries that do not yet compile full AEAs, and for extending the temporal coverage and improving industry granularity of existing series where official AEA data are available but incomplete. The approach was applied to OECD countries that do not currently report AEAs but can be readily applied to all countries that have the relevant underlying datasets available.

139. Several avenues for the further refinement and extension of this methodology have been identified. These include in order of priority i) the extension of coverage to non- $CO_{2}$ GHGs and $CO_{2}$ from biomass, ii) territory-residence adjustments for road transport, iii) more granular ISIC breakdown to strengthen the relevance for carbon footprint estimation, iv) improving the allocation of emissions from electricity and heat generation based on the energy source mix, v) disaggregating air and maritime transport emissions beyond transport (ISIC H), and vi) improving the country-specific allocation for off-road vehicles. Moreover, plausibility checks could be further refined to assess more closely the industry structure of emissions.

140. In addition, improvements in inventory inputs are expected in both official and unofficial estimates. Under the Paris Agreement's ETF, Parties are expected to continuously improve the transparency, accuracy, completeness, consistency, and comparability of their GHG inventory reporting over time, while taking into account national capacities and circumstances. Where inventory data gaps persist, the OECD Environment Directorate is developing gap-filling methods to produce more detailed and harmonised emission inventory estimates. Should AEAs be extended to include emissions and removals from Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) in the ongoing update of the SEEA-CF, future methodological work would need to explore how to integrate them into AEA estimates.

141. Furthermore, emissions cross-classified by CRT categories and ISIC/household activities generated in the calculation step for AEA estimates could feed into the development of a single emissions database at the OECD, improving the consistency, granularity, transparency, and accessibility of GHG emissions data.

142. To conclude, the updated methodology presented in this paper improves the availability and usability of AEA estimates by expanding the geographical and temporal coverage, enhancing industry granularity, and strengthening alignment with the SEEA-CF. These advances are achieved using inventory data reported by a wider range of countries under the ETF using the new CRT template, the integration of multiple auxiliary data sources, and the incorporation of experimental estimates for transport emissions. The resulting more complete AEAs are expected to enhance their relevance for analytical applications, including carbon footprint calculations, while improving consistency between GHG emission inventories and AEAs. As data availability continues to evolve (e.g. more complete inventory and PEFA data), the approach set out in this note provides a flexible foundation for further improving the coverage, consistency, and policy relevance of AEAs.

# Annex A. Correspondence table

1. Existing correspondence tables mapping UNFCCC CRF/CRT emission categories to ISIC Rev.4 economic activities and households are often limited in scope or consistency. In particular, residual “Other” inventory sub-categories, flexible reporting practices, and the frequent use of “IE (included elsewhere)” notations can blur distinctions between inventory categories and complicate systematic allocation. In addition, some available mappings rely on oversimplified assumptions – for example, assigning all road transport emissions exclusively to land transport activities and households – which can result in implausible industry and household distributions in key emission categories.

2. To address these limitations and enable a more consistent allocation of inventory emissions across countries, the proposed methodology develops a comprehensive reference correspondence table by synthesising information from multiple international and national sources. These include:

\- the 2006 IPCC Guidelines for National GHG Inventories, which provide mapping to ISIC Revision 3 economic activities for several fuel combustion subcategories (IPCC, 2006[16]). $^{27}$

• Eurostat correspondence tables (Eurostat, 2015[8])

\- the default correspondence table embedded in the IMF's AEA estimation tool, and

\- correspondence tables derived from countries' detailed underlying data and methodological documentation

# Annex B. Distribution of misallocated emissions excluding air transport, maritime/fishing, and road transport

1. Figure 14 presents the industry and household distribution of misallocated emissions. Large-emitting industries, such as ISIC C19 “Manufacture of coke and refined petroleum products”, ISIC D “Electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply”, and ISIC H “Transportation and storage”, exhibit higher misallocation shares. This partly reflects the design of the metric, as misallocation is expressed as a share of total emissions; potential under-estimation in smaller industries is therefore limited in absolute terms and contributes less to the overall misallocation share.

2. In constructing the comparison scope, official AEAs are adjusted by subtracting road transport emissions from each industry and the household sector, subtracting international and domestic air/maritime transport by residents from ISIC H using relevant bridging items. In some countries, however, other industries (e.g. ISIC B “Mining and quarrying”) may account for non-negligible transport-related emissions, such as vessels operated for offshore oil extraction. $^{28}$ Adjusting transport emissions exclusively within ISIC H may therefore distort industry comparisons and contribute to misallocation.

3. Among large-emitting categories, only a small share of misallocation is attributed to households with very little variation. This suggests that the allocation of CRT 1.A.4.b “Residential” to households broadly reflects official AEA compilation.

4. Smaller $CO_{2}$ -emitting industries generally exhibit correspondingly smaller misallocation shares. However, some industries, such as ISIC B, E and F, display relatively large variation in misallocation compared to the size of industry emissions.

5. Potential misallocation in ISIC B may partly reflect the adjustment of maritime transport emissions exclusively within ISIC H, as discussed above.

6. Underestimation of $CO_{2}$ emissions in ISIC C19 may partly result from the simplified allocation of emissions from sub-categories under CRT 1.B Fugitive emissions from fuels. While the 1-to-1 correspondence between CRT 1.A.1.b Petroleum Refining and ISIC C19 is relatively straightforward, the allocation of emissions from sub-categories under CRT 1.B categories is more complex and may warrant further refinement. This is particularly relevant when the methodology is extended to cover methane emissions, for which fugitive emissions are a key source.

7. Underestimation of $CO_{2}$ emissions in ISIC E may be linked to allocation from CRT 1.A.1.a “Public electricity and heat production”. The methodology allocates emissions between ISIC D and E based on physical supply of electricity and heat (in terajoules). In many cases, emissions are allocated entirely to ISIC D. Where a split is applied, however, differences in emission intensity of electricity generation between ISIC D and E—particularly when ISIC D relies more heavily on nuclear or renewable energy—may generate industry discrepancies, which warrant further investigation and methodological refinement.

8. ISIC F is closely linked to emissions from CRT 1.A.2.g.v “Construction” and 1.A.2.g.vii “Off-road vehicles and other machinery”. These five-digit categories under 1.A.2 “Manufacturing industries and construction" are not always separately reported in official inventories and are supplemented, where necessary, using corresponding categories from the IEA dataset "Greenhouse gas emissions estimates matching the energy module of 2006 IPCC Guidelines". In addition, allocation of 1.A.2.g.vii currently relies on a coefficient derived from detailed data for a single country due to the lack of internationally comparable off-road energy-use data. These assumptions and data limitations may explain the relatively large misallocations observed for construction in Figure 14 compared with its emissions share in Figure 4.

7. Figure 17 presents the industry and household distribution of misallocated road transport emissions. Misallocation may arise from two main sources: (i) the simplifying assumption regarding territory–residence adjustments, and (ii) the allocation coefficients used to distribute emissions across economic activities and households.

8. ISIC H and households are most directly affected by territory–residence adjustments. For example, road transport emissions from non-resident transport companies fuelling on the territory are included in the OECD estimates, whereas they are deducted in official AEAs. This may contribute to overestimation of emissions in the transport industry. Conversely, road transport emissions from resident tourists fuelling abroad are excluded from OECD estimates, potentially leading to underestimation of household road transport emissions.

9. Misallocation may also arise from the allocation coefficients derived from PEFA energy products P14 "Motor gasoline (without bio)" and P17 "Transport diesel (without bio)". Diesel fuel is used not only for on-road transport but also for off-road purposes, such as agricultural tractors, forklifts in manufacturing facilities or wholesale and retail warehouses, and construction machinery (e.g. bulldozers and cranes).

10. Fuels under PEFA energy products P17 “Transport diesel (without bio)” and P18 “Heating and other gasoil (without bio)” may be chemically identical and therefore not always clearly separable in practice. If P17 includes diesel used for off-road vehicles, this may lead to an overallocation of road transport emissions to industries such as ISIC A “Agriculture, forestry and fishing”, ISIC C “Manufacturing”, ISIC F “Construction”, and ISIC G “Wholesale and retail trade”. As a result, when these CRT off-road categories are reported separately, vehicle-related emissions may be counted both through allocation from off-road CRT categories and through road transport emissions, potentially leading to an overestimation of emissions in these industries. This issue may warrant further methodological refinement.

11. Similarly, PEFA energy product P14 “Motor gasoline (without bio)” is primarily associated with road vehicles but may also be used for other purposes such as gasoline-powered generators, potentially contributing to overallocation of emissions to the corresponding industries.

12. Given the large total-level discrepancies in road transport emissions driven by territory–residence adjustments, the misallocation share—expressed as a proportion of total emissions—tends to be elevated in several countries. Further analysis of industry emission shares in official and estimated AEAs would help disentangle total-level effects from structural allocation differences. In addition, incorporating country-specific data on cross-border road traffic—ideally distinguishing between industries and households—would improve the implementation of territory–residence adjustments for road transport emissions.

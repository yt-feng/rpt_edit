# Executive summary

1. This report presents the Global Forum's analysis of Namibia's compliance with the standard on transparency and exchange of information on request (the standard) and concludes that Namibia is rated as overall Largely Compliant with the standard. This conclusion is based on an assessment of Namibia's legal and regulatory framework in force as of 6 May 2026 and its practical implementation, including in respect of exchange of information (EOI) requests sent and received during the review period from 1 April 2022 to 31 March 2025. Namibia joined the Global Forum in 2019. Hence, the current report is the first assessment of the legal and regulatory framework for transparency and exchange of information on request in Namibia and its implementation in practice.

## Determinations and ratings


Note: The three-scale determinations for the legal and regulatory framework are In place, In place but needs improvement, Not in place. The four-scale ratings for the legal and regulatory framework and its implementation in practice are Compliant, Largely Compliant, Partially Compliant and Non-Compliant.

## Transparency framework

2. Namibia has a comprehensive legal and regulatory framework in place to ensure the availability of ownership, accounting and banking information, although some deficiencies have been identified.

3. Namibian commercial and tax legislation establish broad obligations that ensure the availability of information on the identity and legal ownership of domestic legal entities and arrangements. These entities are required to submit ownership and identity information to the relevant registrars and/or to the Namibian Revenue Agency as part of their tax-compliance obligations, while also maintaining information on their legal ownership. In addition, commercial and tax laws mandate entities to maintain comprehensive accounting records. The legal and regulatory framework further provides mechanisms to ensure the availability of legal ownership, identity and accounting information for relevant foreign legal entities and arrangements.

4. Regarding the availability of beneficial ownership information, since August 2023, companies, close corporations and trusts are required to submit beneficial ownership information to their respective registrars at the time of incorporation, whenever there is a change and annually. Consequently, the reporting of beneficial ownership information is ongoing, and the authorities have already started implementing various oversight activities to ensure that the reported information is accurate, adequate and up to date. These obligations complement the already existing anti-money laundering laws to require the availability of beneficial ownership information for all relevant entities and arrangements. In terms of banking information, banking and anti-money laundering laws contain obligations for banks to keep transactional as well as identity and beneficial ownership information of their clients.

## Key recommendations

5. The key recommendations in this report relate to recent reforms undertaken by Namibia in putting in place a comprehensive system for the availability of beneficial ownership information. The reforms being recent, have not yet been fully implemented and their supervision is nascent. In addition, areas for improvement have been identified in the supervision and enforcement of the obligations that require the availability of accounting information. Additional recommendations have been made regarding deficiencies in the legal framework regarding the need to identify the owners of companies when nominee arrangements are used and the retention of records when banks cease to exist or otherwise cease to operate in Namibia.

6. In 2023, Namibia introduced obligations requiring all companies, close corporations and trusts to identify their beneficial owners, maintain beneficial ownership registers, and submit this information to the Registrar at the Business and Intellectual Property Authority or, in the case of trusts, to the Master of the High Court (Element A.1). Both the registrar and the master have undertaken awareness-raising, sensitisation and enforcement activities to support compliance with these requirements. As a result, 54% of companies, 44% of close corporations and 63% of trusts have submitted their beneficial ownership information, while the remaining entities or trusts that are yet to submit their beneficial ownership information are currently classified as inactive. In addition, although the authorities already conduct quality and completeness checks on the submitted data, they plan to introduce further verification measures and additional sanctions to strengthen compliance. Namibia is recommended to strengthen its supervision and enforcement.

7. In addition, the 2015 binding guideline on the identification and verification of beneficial ownership information was replaced by a more comprehensive version in 2025. The changes explain several aspects relating to the newly introduced obligation for legal entities and legal arrangements to identify their beneficial owners and further enhance the guidance on conducting customer due diligence, which is especially helpful for non-financial sector AML-obliged persons. Particularly, it aligns the ownership-interest threshold with that set out in the Financial Intelligence Act (25%), clarifies on the method to identify beneficial ownership and offers more detailed explanations regarding customer due diligence obligations under the AML/CFT framework (Element A.1). Since these changes are recent, Namibia is recommended to monitor their implementation.

8. Nominee arrangements may be used to hold ownership in Namibian companies. The Companies Act does not require nominee shareholders to disclose their nominee status or the identity of the persons on whose behalf they act to either the company or the authorities. This gap is partially mitigated by the obligation for companies to identify their beneficial owners. However, the existing obligations to identify beneficial ownership do not extend to situations where an individual holds less than 25% of the ownership interest (Element A.1).

9. Entities are required to submit accounting information through periodic tax returns. The Namibia Revenue Agency has undertaken a range of monitoring, supervisory and enforcement activities to promote compliance with these obligations. Nonetheless, some entities, particularly small and medium taxpayers, remain non-compliant with their tax filing requirements (Element A.2). Namibia should strengthen its supervision.

10. Regarding availability of banking information, there is no obligation to ensure that the records of a bank that ceases to exist or otherwise ceases its operations in Namibia, will be maintained for at least five years. Namibia is recommended to address this gap.

## Exchange of information in practice

11. Namibia has a wide network of EOI relationships covering 157 jurisdictions, and the underlying EOI agreements enable the exchange of information in line with the standard. Namibia's competent authority has adequate powers, supported by sanctions to enforce compliance, that allow it to access information for the purposes of exchange of information.

12. During the review period, Namibia received 22 requests and ultimately provided full responses to most of them, except for 2 bulk requests that have only been partially answered so far.

## Key recommendations

13. While Namibia's legal and regulatory framework broadly ensures the confidentiality of exchanged information, there are situations where information may be disclosed to persons who are not envisaged under the standard. Namibia is recommended to address this gap.

14. At the beginning of the review period, the competent authority had a limited understanding of the procedures required to efficiently collect requested information and respond to its peers. This significantly impeded Namibia's ability to provide timely responses. Namibia has strengthened the operations of the competent authority office by providing staff training and formalising robust operational processes. These improvements became evident in the final quarter of the period, during which substantial progress in the handling of EOI requests was observed. Namibia should continue to monitor its activities to ensure that requested information is provided in a timely manner.

## Overall rating

15. Namibia is rated Compliant on Elements B.1, B.2, C.1, C.2 and C.4, Largely Compliant on Elements A.1, A.2, A.3, C.3 and C.5. Namibia is therefore rated overall Largely Compliant with the EOIR standard on a global consideration of its compliance with the individual Elements.

16. This report was approved at the Peer Review and Monitoring Group of the Global Forum on 22 June 2026 and was adopted by the Global Forum on 23 July 2026. A self-assessment on the steps undertaken by Namibia to address the recommendations made in this report should be provided to the Peer Review and Monitoring Group in accordance with the methodology for enhanced monitoring.

# Summary of determinations, ratings and recommendations


# Overview of Namibia

17. This overview provides some basic information about Namibia that serves as context for understanding the analysis in the main body of the report.

18. Namibia was established as a republic after gaining independence from South Africa in March 1990. Namibia is located in Southern Africa and borders the Atlantic Ocean to the west, Angola and Zambia to the North, Botswana to the East and South Africa to the south. Its capital is Windhoek. Namibia has a population of approximately 3.4 million people.

19. Namibia's GDP in 2024 was estimated to be United States Dollars 13.37 billion, translating into a per capita GDP of about United States Dollars 4 413. $^{1}$ The main sectors driving Namibia's economy are mining, agriculture, fisheries and tourism. Namibia's currency is the Namibian Dollar (NAD), and its exchange rate is pegged on a ratio of one to one to the South African Rand. $^{2}$

## Legal system

20. Namibia has a hybrid legal system that combines common and civil law traditions. The sources of Namibian law in their hierarchical order are the Constitution, statutes enacted by parliament, $^{3}$ international law, regulations, common law, customary law, and the doctrine of precedent. EOI instruments that have been ratified and domesticated form part of the domestic legal framework and are categorised under international law. Therefore, the Constitution and statutes prevail in the event of a conflict with an international agreement such as an EOI instrument.

21. Namibia is a unitary state with a centralised system of government where all legislative, executive and judicial authority is vested in the relevant three arms of government, i.e. the executive, legislature and judiciary. Executive authority lies with the president and the cabinet. To ease administration, decentralised administrative structures have been created, and they divide Namibia into 14 regions, each led by a governor appointed by the president and elected councillors. Regional and local governments do not have constitutional autonomy and operate only through powers delegated by the central government. All laws and policies are made by the National Assembly (the legislature) and implemented uniformly across the country.

22. Judicial authority is exercised through independent courts. The structure of the court system starts with lower or Community Courts that primarily operate under customary laws, and next in line are Specialised Courts specific to areas such as tax or labour. Magistrates' Courts handle most civil and criminal cases at the regional and district levels. The High Court acts as an appellate court for decisions from lower courts and presides over serious civil and criminal matters. The highest court in Namibia, serving as both the court of final appeal and the Constitutional Court, is the Supreme Court.

23. Regarding tax disputes, a person dissatisfied with a tax decision must first seek redress from the objections unit of the Namibia Revenue Agency. If dissatisfied with the outcomes of the objection, the matter must be filed with the Tax Tribunal. Appeals from the Tax Tribunal are handled by the High Court, and thereafter the decision of the High Court can be appealed to the Supreme Court. In practice, no EOI matters have progressed to objections or to the court system in Namibia.

## Tax system

24. Broadly, Namibia has a territorial tax system where mainly only income from Namibian sources is taxable, although in a wide range of circumstances, income earned from a non-Namibian source is deemed as if it is from a Namibian source and is also taxable. The Income Tax Act (ITA) provides two broad principles for deeming foreign earned income as income from a Namibian source, i.e. i) based on residence (e.g. any interest or management fee from a source outside Namibia received or accrued to a Namibia resident person) and ii) based on any connection with Namibia (e.g. an income received or accrued from an agreement made in Namibia of the sale of goods, whether such goods have been delivered or to be delivered in or out of Namibia) (s. 15, ITA).

25. The main taxes levied in Namibia are corporate and personal income taxes with progressive rates that range from 18% to 55%. The highest tax rates are applied to mining income. Incomes from diamond mining and related services are taxed at 55%, while incomes generated from non-diamond mining are taxed at 37.5%. The rate for Value Added Tax is 15%. Other duties and levies include export levies (especially on minerals, gas and crude oil, fish and forestry products), stamp duties and duties on transfer of properties (including shares in a company).

26. The concept of residence is not explicitly defined in Namibia's tax laws. The terms “domestic entity”, to mean a Namibian entity or entity controlled or managed in Namibia, and “ordinarily resident”, in reference to natural persons, are used. Non-residents who earn income from Namibian source are taxed through withholding tax mechanisms executed by the Namibian payers.

27. The Namibia Revenue Agency (NamRA) was established in 2017 as an autonomous agency responsible for administering Namibian tax laws and customs and excise services. Regarding EOI matters, the Minister of Finance is the competent authority for all exchange of information. However, the powers have been delegated to the Commissioner of NamRA, who in turn has delegated the powers to an operational office. Apart from the Exchange of Information on Request (EOIR), Namibia engages in spontaneous exchanges, and it does not engage in Automatic Exchange of Information.

## Financial services sector

28. Namibia's financial sector is moderately developed and is mainly focused on the domestic market. The Namibian Statistics Agency estimated that in 2025, the financial sector's contribution to the GDP ranged between $7\%$ to $8\%$ . The combined banking and non-banking financial institutions' assets totalled approximately NAD 660.7 billion (EUR 29.9 billion) in 2025, representing about $254\%$ of Namibia's GDP.

29. The banking sector is the most developed and comprises five commercial banks, a branch of a foreign bank and one representative office. $^{4}$ The five commercial banks are classified as “domestic systemically important banks” while the foreign branch and the representative office are classified as “non-domestic systemically important banks”. As of June 2025, the total banking assets were NAD 179.1 billion (EUR 8.1 billion). The main contributors were negotiable securities, mortgage lending and consumer credit. The banking sector is regulated by the Bank of Namibia.

30. The non-banking sector comprises 1 126 regulated entities and 14 761 intermediaries, accounting for 72% of the total financial sector assets. Main sub-sectors include insurance, retirement funds, microlenders, collective investment schemes and capital markets. The non-banking financial sector is regulated by the Namibia Financial Institutions Supervisory Authority (NAMFISA).

## Anti-money laundering framework

31. The Financial Intelligence Act (FIA) is the primary law providing anti-money laundering (AML) requirements in Namibia. Section 2 and schedule 1 of the FIA create AML obligations to different categories of obliged persons termed as accountable institutions, who include financial institutions and a wide range of professional service providers where they perform specified functions, such as legal practitioners and trust company service providers, attorneys at law, notaries, accountants, and independent legal professionals. The regulation of AML obligations is carried out by the Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC). The FIA also mandates the FIC and the NAMFISA, as supervisory authorities towards combating money laundering. The FIA is complemented by regulations and binding guidance.

32. Namibia is a member of the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) style regional body, the Eastern and Southern Africa Anti-Money Laundering Group. Namibia's last Mutual Evaluation Report on the implementation of the FATF standard, conducted by this FATF style regional body, was published in September 2022. $^{5}$ The report rated Recommendation 10 (financial institutions customer due diligence) as Largely Compliant, while Recommendations 22 (Designated Non-Financial Business and Professions customer due diligence), 24 and 25 (transparency of legal persons and arrangements) were rated as Partially Compliant. The level of effectiveness on Immediate Outcomes 3 (Supervision), 4 (Preventive Measures) and 5 (Legal persons and arrangements) was rated as Low. Owing to the identified deficiencies, Namibia was given a 12-month observation period to address those deficiencies. Despite the progress made in addressing legal and regulatory framework gaps, in February 2024, Namibia was added to the FATF list of jurisdictions with strategic deficiencies (grey list).

33. Namibia continues to address the deficiencies identified, and in Namibia's latest enhanced follow-up and technical re-rating report in April 2025, Recommendations 22 and 25 have been upgraded to Largely Compliant. $^{6}$ The major pending gaps relate to demonstrating a sustained increase in investigations of money laundering and demonstrating the competencies of the authorities to identify and investigate financing of terrorism.

## Recent developments

34. Key recent developments in Namibia relate to the availability of beneficial ownership information. Since 2023, companies, close corporations and trusts are required to report beneficial ownership information to their respective registrars. Consequently, the reporting of beneficial ownership information is ongoing, and the authorities have already started implementing various oversight activities to ensure that the reported information is accurate, adequate and up to date.

35. In 2025, the Financial Intelligence Centre replaced the Industry Guidance Note No. 01 of 2015 on Identification and verification of beneficial ownership information with a new and enhanced version. The 2025 guidance note references the definition and methods of identification of beneficial owners contained in the Financial Intelligence Act and improves the guidance on the identification of beneficial owner(s) by entities and AML-obliged persons. The authorities reported that inspections to ascertain whether AML-obliged persons aligned their internal policies and procedures accordingly are being conducted and where non-compliance is detected, sanctions are imposed accordingly.

36. Regarding the progress being made to address the deficiencies in its mutual evaluation report, Namibia has made significant progress in addressing its deficiencies. In this regard, the FATF Plenary held in Mexico during February 2026 recommended Namibia for an on-site assessment which was conducted in April 2026 to verify the progress achieved. The resultant report was discussed during the FATF meetings in June 2026, following which Namibia was removed from the list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring.

37. Sections A.1, A.2 and A.3 evaluate the availability of ownership and identity information for relevant entities and arrangements, the availability of accounting information and the availability of banking information.

## A.1 Legal and beneficial ownership and identity information

Jurisdictions should ensure that legal and beneficial ownership and identity information for all relevant entities and arrangements is available to their competent authorities.

38. The availability of legal and beneficial ownership information in Namibia is broadly ensured through a combination of commercial, tax and anti-money laundering laws for all relevant entities and arrangements (companies, close corporations, partnerships, trusts and co-operatives), although there is some scope for improvement, particularly in respect of beneficial ownership information.

39. Companies and close corporations must be registered by the registrar at the Business and Intellectual Property Authority before they come into existence. Co-operatives are registered by the Registrar of Co-operatives, while trusts must register with the Master of the High Court. Partnerships are registered with the Namibia Revenue Agency for tax purposes. The information identifying the legal owners of these entities and participants in these arrangements is maintained by the public registration authorities, the entities themselves or a combination of both.

40. Regarding the availability of beneficial ownership information, Namibia has adopted a multi-pronged approach to ensure the availability of beneficial ownership information, requiring companies, close corporations and trusts to maintain and report beneficial ownership information to their relevant registration authorities and requiring AML-obliged persons to keep beneficial ownership information on their customers. Some gaps exist in both the legal and regulatory framework and in practice.

41. For partnerships and co-operatives, the anti-money laundering (AML) framework is the only source of beneficial ownership information. While co-operatives have an obligation to have a local bank account, partnerships have no obligation to engage an AML-obliged person in an ongoing business relationship. Namibia is recommended to address this gap.

42. Namibia permits the use of nominee arrangements to hold shares in companies. The Companies Act does not impose obligations on nominee shareholders to disclose their nominee status or to provide information on the persons on whose behalf they act, either to the company or to any public authorities. Therefore, the availability of information identifying the persons for whom nominee shareholders act is not always ensured. A recommendation is made in this regard.

43. The industry guidance on the identification and verification of beneficial ownership information was revised in 2025. The updates clarified several elements related to identifying beneficial owners of legal entities and legal arrangements, including providing more detailed instructions for entities on determining beneficial owners, aligning the ownership threshold with the 25% threshold set under the Financial Intelligence Act, and offering additional explanations on customer due diligence obligations under the

AML framework. Authorities indicated that they have already begun supervising compliance with the revised guidance. Nevertheless, given the recent nature of these amendments, continued supervisory follow-up is required to ensure their effective implementation.

44. Namibia has introduced obligations requiring all companies, close corporations and trusts to identify their beneficial owners, maintain internal beneficial ownership registers, and submit this information to their respective registrars. The registrars have conducted awareness-raising, sensitisation and enforcement activities to support compliance. These efforts have led to 54% of companies, 44% of close corporations and 63% of trusts submitting their beneficial ownership information. While the registrars currently conduct checks on the quality and completeness of the information submitted, these checks are limited and recent and Namibia is planning to expand on these verification processes and implement additional sanctioning measures to strengthen compliance going forward. Namibia should strengthen its supervision and enforcement activities.

45. Namibia received 13 requests for identity, legal ownership and beneficial information on companies and trusts, and the requested information was provided in all cases.

## Practical Implementation of the Standard: Largely Compliant


## A.1.1 Availability of legal and beneficial ownership information for companies

Types of legal entities - companies and close corporations

47. The legal framework in Namibia provides for the creation of two main categories of legal entities, i.e. companies and close corporations. The formation and regulation of companies is governed by the Companies Act, while the formation and regulation of close corporations is governed by the Close Corporations Act (CC Act).

48. The Companies Act enables the creation of three types of companies.

\- Companies with share capital. A company having share capital may either be a public company or a private company having shares of par value or shares of no-par value. If it is a public company, it must have seven or more members, while a private company may have two or more members unless it is a single-member company. There were 28 033 companies with share capital registered in Namibia as of 30 September 2025.

\- Companies limited by guarantee. These are companies that do not have a share capital and have the liability of their members limited by the memorandum. All companies limited by guarantee are deemed to be public companies. There were 3 642 companies limited by guarantee registered in Namibia as of 30 September 2025.

\- Non-profit companies. These are companies whose objectives include charitable purposes and the promotion of arts and culture. The company must intend to apply its income to a charitable cause and should prohibit the payment of any dividend to its members. Such a company is incorporated as a company limited by guarantee. There were 3590 non-profit companies registered in Namibia as of 30 September 2025.

49. Close corporations formed under the CC Act are entities with legal personality that are formed by a minimum of one and a maximum of 10 natural persons. Each member contributes either money, property or services to the corporation and receives a certificate stating the percentage of such member's interest in the corporation. A close corporation has simplified formation and compliance requirements and is usually used for small and medium enterprises. The members have limited liability. There were 206 736 close corporations registered in Namibia as of 30 September 2025.

50. Foreign companies can carry on business in Namibia after being registered in the Commercial Registry as external companies. The Companies Act defines external companies as a company or other association of persons, incorporated outside Namibia, which has a place of business in Namibia. There were 428 external companies registered in Namibia as of 30 September 2025 (see detailed discussion on foreign companies below).

## Commercial Company law requirements – information held by the commercial registrar and the entities

52. Companies and close corporations must be incorporated and registered with the Business and Intellectual Property Authority (BIPA). The chief executive officer of BIPA serves as their registrar (s.1, BIPA Act). BIPA is mandated to supervise all commercial registration processes and to regulate legal entities in Namibia, through administering the Companies Act and the CC Act.

53. The information identifying the members of a company or a close corporation must be submitted to BIPA at the time of incorporation and periodically or maintained by the entities themselves. The sections below analyse the obligations for the different categories of entities.

## Companies and the commercial registrar

54. Chapter 4 of the Companies Act regulates the incorporation of companies. The process of incorporation requires the interested person(s) to submit a memorandum and articles of association to BIPA, using prescribed forms.

55. The memorandum of association sets out the proposed name of the company, a relevant statement if the company is to have share capital, the amount and division of the share capital and the number of shares. Further, the memorandum states the name of each subscriber and the number of shares allotted to each of the subscribers. For companies limited by guarantee, the memorandum contains the names of the members and the amount each member undertakes to contribute to the assets of the company. The Companies Administrative Regulations prescribe the registration forms, which require for each subscribing member, to record their full names, occupation, residential address, business address, postal address, and email address (Regulation 8 and annexure 2).

56. A company is considered established upon the registration of the memorandum and articles of association with BIPA. The company is then issued a registration number and entered onto the register of companies. Therefore, the information identifying the founding members of a company is submitted to BIPA at the time of registration of a newly incorporated company.

57. Further, Section 112 of the Companies Act obliges all companies to maintain a register of their members. The register should contain, in respect of each member, i) the name and address of the member, ii) the shares issued to each member (for companies with share capital), and iii) the date on which the person became/ceased to be a member. The register of members of a company must be kept at the company's registered office in Namibia or at the office of the company's agent in Namibia (s. 117, Companies Act). If the register is kept at the office of the company's agent, the company is required to notify BIPA of the place where the register is kept.

58. Any changes in the ownership of a company must be registered with the company by entering it in the register of members. The instrument of transfer is required to be signed by both the transferor and transferee. The transferor is deemed to be the holder of the share until the name of the transferee has been added to the register of members. Therefore, ownership rights in a company are only triggered once the name of the acquiring member has been entered onto the register of members. Thus, only the persons entered into the register of members can exercise their rights as shareholders, including participating in the annual general meeting of the company or receiving dividends. These obligations imply that a person acquiring shares in a company only has title of ownership once the transfer has been completed and accordingly, it is incumbent upon the member acquiring shares in a company, to ensure that their name has been entered onto the register of members in a timely manner. The Companies Act allows any aggrieved person to petition the court if the person's name is not entered into the register of members or if there are undue delays. The court may issue penalties against the company and its directors. These self-enforcing mechanisms ensure that the register of members is kept up to date.

59. The changes in ownership of a company are reported to BIPA through annual returns (s. 181 and 182, Companies Act). The Companies Administrative Regulations (Regulation 39) mandate that the annual returns should be made using a prescribed form, that requires the reporting of shareholder details that include i) the full name of the shareholder, ii) date of birth or identity number or passport number or entity registration number, iii) residential, business and postal addresses, iv) telephone contacts, and v) nationality or place of incorporation.

60. Consequently, the obligations of the Companies Act, as complemented by the Companies Administrative Regulations, provide for the availability of accurate, adequate and up-to-date legal ownership information on companies established under the Companies Act.

## Close corporations and the commercial registrar

61. A close corporation is a simplified structure whose members have an equal right to manage and represent the corporation, unless there is an association agreement concluded that states otherwise. There is no joint holding of members' interests. As discussed in paragraph 49, a close corporation can only be formed by 1 to 10 natural persons. $^{9}$

62. The CC Act provides that persons intending to establish a close corporation must draw up a founding statement which contains the name of the corporation, its postal and physical address. The founding statement must also contain the full names, residential address and identity number of each member. If the person does not have an identity number, his/her date of birth is captured (s. 12, CC Act).

The founding statement is then filed with the registrar at BIPA, following which a certificate of incorporation is issued. Therefore, the information identifying the founding members of a close corporation is available at BIPA.

63. A close corporation is mandated to keep a copy of its founding statement at its registered office in Namibia. Any changes to the founding statement must be filed with the registrar at BIPA before such changes take any effect. Section 15 of the CC Act states that a change in the founding statement (except for the residential address) must be reported within 28 days of any change. Therefore, a change in the membership of a close corporation takes effect only if registered at BIPA. Moreover, all close corporations must confirm their membership information during the process of paying their annual fees.

64. Consequently, the information identifying the members of a close corporation held by BIPA is always accurate, adequate and up to date.

## Retention period and entities that cease to exist

65. The Companies Act and CC Act do not explicitly indicate the period for which the legal ownership information submitted to BIPA should be maintained. Namibian authorities explained that in practice, BIPA keeps any information, including the ownership information received from companies and close corporations, as long as the related entity exists and for 15 years if the entity ceases to exist, and thereafter transfers it to a government archive, where it is maintained indefinitely.

66. Companies and close corporations keep their ownership information throughout their existence since such information confers ownership rights to the legal owners of the companies and members of the close corporation. Namibia's legal and regulatory framework provides for specific arrangements when such entities cease to exist.

67. Companies cease to exist through i) deregistration and ii) winding up and dissolution processes.

68. The registration of a company may be cancelled where the company fails to lodge annual returns for more than two years or where there is reasonable cause to believe that a company has ceased operations (s. 74, Companies Act). The registrar is obliged to contact the company and inquire whether such a company is in business or not. If the company does not respond or responds indicating that it is not conducting any business and does not have any assets or liabilities, the registrar will deregister the company. Additionally, entities may be deregistered for failing to comply with statutory obligations (see paragraph 175). The memorandum and articles of a deregistered company are cancelled, and the company ceases to exist.

69. Regarding the records of a de-registered company, the registrar may direct the way such records shall be disposed of. Namibian authorities explained that the directors of the company are usually appointed to be the custodians of the records. The person appointed must maintain the records for at least five years.

70. The winding up process is conducted either voluntarily (initiated by the creditors or members) or through liquidation procedures initiated by a Court. In all cases, liquidators are formally appointed by the Court. For a voluntary winding-up process, the members or creditors are obliged to nominate a liquidator who is then formally appointed by the Court (s. 375 and 376, Companies Act). Liquidators must be individuals who reside in Namibia (s. 379), and they are issued certificates of appointment by the Master of the High Court.

71. During the liquidation process, the liquidator is obliged to maintain a record of the process through keeping books and accounts of the company, which are subject to supervision by the Master of the High Court. When the affairs of a company have been completely wound up, the liquidator must submit a return of the audited account of the winding-up process to the Master of the High Court. Thereafter, the Master will send a certificate to the registrar, who records the dissolution of the company.

72. Regarding the records of a company that has been wound up and is about to be dissolved, section 428 of the Companies Act requires that, depending on the type of winding up, the Master of the High Court or the company or the creditors will appoint the liquidator or any other person to maintain the records of the company for five years. Namibian authorities explained that in practice, the ownership records are usually maintained by liquidators or former directors of the company in Namibia.

73. The CC Act (s. 66) states that the provisions of the Companies Act, regarding winding up, also apply to close corporations with the requisite modifications. Therefore, the retention of ownership records of a close corporation that ceases to exist shall be maintained accordingly. In any case, the legal ownership information of a close corporation held by BIPA, which is maintained indefinitely, must be up-to-date as discussed in paragraphs 62 and 63.

## Foreign companies

74. Foreign companies (termed as external companies in the Companies Act) that establish a place of business in Namibia must register with BIPA within 21 days.

75. The company is required to submit a certified copy of its memorandum, notice of its address in Namibia, the name and consent of its Namibian auditor, and notice of its financial year. The company must also submit detailed information about its directors, local manager and secretary, including their full names, nationalities, occupations, addresses and dates of appointment (Companies Act, s. 328).

76. Whereas there is no explicit requirement for the external company to submit its ownership information to BIPA at the time of registration, to the extent that the information identifying the legal owners of a company is contained in the memorandum of the company, the ownership information is submitted to BIPA. In addition, Section 334 of the Companies Act requires that any change in the memorandum of the external company be reported to BIPA. The availability of legal ownership information through the registration of the memorandum of the company does not ensure that such information would be available for all external companies, since the inclusion of ownership information in the memorandum is not a uniform requirement in all jurisdictions where such companies may be incorporated.

77. However, the potential gap is fully mitigated by the requirement for all external companies to submit to BIPA an annual return that contains adequate information on their legal owners. The return must be filed using Form CM 23, which captures details of members of the company (see paragraph 59). As of December 2025, 387 foreign companies (90%) were compliant with filing their annual returns and Nambian authorities are following up with the remaining 10%.

78. Consequently, the information identifying the owners of an external company is available at BIPA. This is complemented by the tax obligations on such companies to register for taxes and undergo an audit.

## Tax law requirements

79. Entities established under Namibian law or otherwise having taxable income in Namibia are required to register with the Namibia Revenue Agency (NamRA). Pursuant to these requirements, NamRA maintains some identity and legal ownership information of companies and close corporations.

80. Persons may register through the electronic Integrated Tax Administration System by directly using the online application form or submitting manual forms at a NamRA office that are then captured into the System.

81. At the time of registration, companies must submit copies of their memorandum of association, and close corporations must submit copies of their founding statements. These documents contain information identifying the members of the company or the close corporation, respectively. While there is no obligation for entities to file changes in their legal ownership information with NamRA, Namibian authorities explained that when a taxpayer is under audit or investigation, the legal ownership and identity information is verified for correctness. Further, as part of NamRA's general compliance procedures, tax officers are also required to review and update taxpayer details, including the ownership information held in the Integrated Tax Administration System, during every interaction with a taxpayer.

82. The tax laws oblige taxpayers to retain all records and documents for five years from the date of first creation of the records (s. 65, ITA) or for five years after the end of the tax period to which the records relate (s. 48, VAT Act).

## Nominees

83. The standard requires that identity information be available on persons who act as legal owners on behalf of other persons as nominees or under similar arrangements, including the identity information of the persons on whose behalf such arrangements have been established.

84. The CC Act prohibits nominee arrangements. The Companies Act does not explicitly provide for nominee arrangements, although there are conditions under which the voting rights associated with the shares may be exercised by nominees. The Companies Act provides that voting rights held by a person as nominee for another person are treated as if they were held by that other person, and voting rights must be deemed to be held by a nominee for another person if they are exercisable only on the instructions or with the consent or concurrence of that other person (nominator) (s. 2(4)(c)).

85. There is no specific legal requirement for a shareholder to disclose its status as a nominee and the identity of the nominator(s) to the company or any public authority. However, under the beneficial ownership obligations contained in the Companies Act, for each of the shareholders who is a nominee, the information identifying that shareholder must be recorded in the same manner as the information identifying beneficial owners of the company (see paragraph 127). Additionally, Trust and Company Service Providers (TCSPs) are obliged to conduct CDD for AML purposes when they act as nominees or when they arrange for another person to act as a nominee shareholder.

86. The FIC guidance note (see paragraph 117) stipulates that nominators should be considered as beneficial owners. The guidance note contains extensive explanations and examples on how to look through both formal and informal nominee arrangements. Taken together, the beneficial ownership obligations contained in the Companies Act, FIA and FIC guidance should lead to the identification of nominees and nominators, where such arrangements are used in formal or informal (non-professional) arrangements.

87. However, the above-mentioned requirements relate to the identification of beneficial ownership in companies and thus they may not cover situations where shareholding is below the ownership threshold of 25%. As such, there may be instances where the information identifying persons acting in a nominee capacity and those for whom they act (nominators) will not be available. Consequently, Namibia is recommended to ensure that nominees declare their status to the company and that identity information on the nominators is available to the company and/or any relevant public authority in all cases, in line with the standard.

## Legal ownership information – available sanctions

88. The self-enforcing mechanisms contained in the Companies Act and the CC Act (see paragraphs 57 and 63) ensure the availability of accurate, adequate and up-to-date legal ownership information on close corporations in all cases and for companies that comply with their annual filing obligations, at BIPA.

89. Moreover, the legal and regulatory framework contains sanctions that complement these self-enforcing mechanisms. Any company that fails to maintain a register of its members in the proper form commits an offence and is liable to a fine which does not exceed NAD 800 (EUR 39.8). Where a company refuses to register a transfer of shares, it is required to notify both the transferor and transferee within

30 days of the lodgement of the transfer instrument. Failure to issue such notification constitutes an offence, subject to a monetary penalty of NAD 40 (EUR 2) for every day that the non-compliance continues (s. 146, Companies Act). A similar offence exists for close corporations where a sanction of NAD 10 (EUR 0.5) is applicable for every day that the non-compliance remains unrectified (s. 15(5), CC Act). The duty to execute transfers, or to justify refusal within a prescribed timeframe, helps prevent undue delays or obstructions in updating ownership records. Whereas the associated penalties are of low value, the application of those sanctions daily, complemented by the possibility for the transferee to seek Court intervention, becomes dissuasive.

90. Regarding companies, the annual filing of prescribed returns is the primary mechanism through which changes in ownership information become available to BIPA. Any company that does not submit its annual return is liable to a fine not exceeding NAD 40 (EUR 2) for every day during which the non-compliance continues (s. 181(6)), Companies Act). Additionally, the Companies Administrative Regulations (Annexure 1) prescribe a penalty of NAD 300 (EUR 13.6) for each return form that is delayed or not lodged. The annual return is also linked with the payment of annual fees and, consequently, some of BIPA's key monitoring and oversight activities to ensure compliance with the obligations relating to the availability of legal ownership information for companies, derive primarily from the monitoring of payment of the annual fees.

## Oversight activities by the Business and Intellectual Property Authority

91. To strengthen compliance with the obligations to file annual returns and payment of annual fees, BIPA conducts various activities that are mainly focused on creating awareness and supporting entities to meet their obligations. Additionally, a penalty waiver program has been implemented to encourage voluntary compliance. Since the expiry of the penalty waiver programme, BIPA has also imposed monetary fines.

## Awareness, outreach and the annual fees penalty waiver programme

92. Owing to low compliance levels in the past, BIPA has implemented targeted communication campaigns to improve awareness on the obligations related to payment of annual fees and filing of annual returns. Between 2022 and 2025, a total of 184 radio campaigns, 4 television campaigns and 73 newspaper publications were conducted. Moreover, entities that delay submitting their returns are sent reminders through direct emails and SMS notifications and are prompted to take corrective action.

93. In addition, BIPA conducted outreach programmes and regional engagements in different parts of Namibia, where BIPA officials sensitise different categories of persons and supported such persons to file any missing information.


94. Through activations, BIPA officials offer direct support to persons wishing to file the required returns and information. Namibian authorities reported that on average, between 40 to 50 entities, are assisted per day. Further, the authorities reported that on average, regional engagements attracted between 220-300 participants. $^{10}$

95. A penalty waiver programme was instituted covering the period from 2012 to 2022. This waiver applied to all entities that had accumulated penalties arising from failure to pay their annual fees and to submit annual returns. To benefit from the waiver, the entity concerned was required to submit the detailed annual report forms for all the missing years that contain legal ownership information. Although the penalties were waived, the entity was still liable to pay the principal fees.

96. Namibian authorities reported that since the inception of the waiver program, 8 002 companies and 35 000 close corporations applied to benefit from the waiver, and have all updated their records by filing annual returns at BIPA.

## Inactivity and improving compliance

97. There is no legal definition of inactive entities. However, entities that have not submitted their annual returns or paid their annual fees or that are not conducting any business activities are deemed to be inactive. As explained in paragraph 68, where it has come to the attention of the registrar in the normal course of business that certain entities were not conducting business and when contacted, such entities have confirmed that they are no longer conducting business activities, the entities were deregistered. Between April 2022 and March 2025, 2324 entities were deregistered. In addition, other entities are in the process of being deregistered owing to non-compliance with submitting beneficial ownership information (see paragraph 175).

98. Further, entities that are inactive due to non-compliance are liable to monetary penalties for delayed filing (see paragraph 90). Historically, most of the inactive entities (due to non-compliance) have been close corporations.

99. Close Corporations make up to 85% of all registered entities in Namibia. Namibian authorities explained that the registration of close corporations has largely been influenced by prevailing social and economic trends. Periodically, the Namibian government adopts financial empowerment initiatives where citizens are required to form organised legal structures to benefit. As a result, many of the entities appearing on the register are corporations that never became operational and were created merely in anticipation of potential opportunities, often with the hope of securing tenders or benefiting from emerging government initiatives. Major developments such as the allocation of fishing quotas, the introduction of youth development fund grants, green hydrogen initiatives, and oil and gas sector initiatives have all driven increases in new close corporation registrations. Traditionally, these trends have contributed significantly to the high volume of entities currently recorded and not complying with their annual obligations (hence inactive).

100. Even though the deregistration process engaged by BIPA led to the deregistration of only 1% of entities, the awareness-raising campaigns have improved compliance and by December 2025, the number of close corporations that have filed their annual returns for each year continues to improve.


101. Concerning the availability of legal ownership information, there is no risk posed by inactive close corporations since only the members registered with BIPA have any ownership rights in the corporation (see paragraph 63). Therefore, the membership information registered with BIPA is always accurate and up to date.

102. Regarding companies, on the other hand, the availability of up-to-date legal ownership information on inactive companies may be affected, since the information available at BIPA may not always be up to date. Although the up-to-date legal ownership information is maintained by the companies in their registers of members, the availability of such information to the public authorities may be compromised in some cases, for example, where inactive companies change addresses and cannot be easily contacted by the authorities (see paragraph 106).


104. Namibia explained that the main driver of the improved compliance was the penalty waiver programme that has seen many entities update the records at BIPA.

105. Currently, no monetary sanctions have been applied due to the ongoing penalty waiver program. However, entities that have not complied with the submission of annual returns and payment of annual fees face restrictions at BIPA, including the rejection of amendments or any filing of resolutions until the required information is submitted. Additionally, BIPA does not issue certificates of good standing for such companies.

106. In conclusion, the awareness and outreach programs coupled with the penalty waiver scheme and restrictions have improved the rate of filing of annual returns for entities, as a source of legal ownership information available to the authorities. Namibia should continue to implement oversight activities and monitor the compliance of companies with their annual reporting obligations with BIPA (see Annex 1).

## Availability of legal ownership information in EOIR practice

107. Namibia received nine requests for legal ownership information of companies and successfully provided the requested information. Peers were satisfied with the responses obtained.

## Availability of beneficial ownership information

108. The standard requires that beneficial ownership information be available on companies. In Namibia, this aspect of the standard was initially partially met through the AML requirements contained primarily in the Financial Intelligence Act (FIA), complemented by regulations and binding guidance. Further, in 2023, Namibia introduced obligations in the Companies Act and the CC Act requiring all companies and close corporations to obtain and report beneficial ownership information to BIPA. The beneficial ownership information submitted to BIPA is now the primary source of beneficial ownership information for the competent authority, although the AML framework remains an important source too. Each of these legal regimes is analysed below.

Companies covered by legislation regulating beneficial ownership information


## Definition and methodology for identification of beneficial owners

109. The definition of beneficial owner applicable under the AML requirements and the entity obligations (under the Companies Act and the CC Act) is the same. The definition is contained in Section 1 of the FIA and states as follows:

"beneficial owner" means a natural person referred to in Schedule 5 who -

(a) for the purposes of this Act, ultimately owns or controls -

(i) a client or natural person; or

(ii) a natural person on whose behalf a transaction is being concluded; or

(b) exercises ultimate effective ownership or control over -

(i) a legal person;

where such ultimate ownership or ultimate effective control may be exercised directly or indirectly or through a chain of ownership or control other than direct control.

110. The definition requires the identification of beneficial owners based on direct or indirect ownership or control, in compliance with the standard.

111. Schedule 5 of FIA elaborates on categories of natural persons that can be regarded as beneficial owners. Through this process, the schedule provides additional explanations on the application of the method of identification of beneficial owner(s).

112. The FIC guidance note (see paragraph 117) has clarified that a simultaneous approach should be used to identify beneficial owners using the test for control through both ownership and means other than ownership.

113. On control through ownership, schedule 5 explains that the threshold for ownership is set at 25% of the shares, voting rights or any other ownership interest in a legal person, whether held directly or indirectly. It further explains that a natural person who holds a combination of 25% of ownership through several legal persons should be identified as a beneficial owner, further cementing the need to look out for direct or indirect ownership.

114. On control through means other than ownership, schedule 5 lists aspects such as i) the right, whether directly or indirectly, to appoint or remove majority of the board in the legal person, ii) power to materially influence the decision making or policy of the legal person, and iii) deriving substantive economic benefit from the legal person, including through dividends, profits or enjoyment of assets, even if the person has no formal link to the company, and iv) having influence or ultimate control on the legal person, through any other means.

115. The schedule notes that beneficial owners are natural persons who jointly control a legal person either directly or indirectly through a chain of other legal persons or arrangements.

116. Lastly, the schedule explains that where beneficial owner cannot be identified using the criteria of control through ownership or control through other means, then a natural person who holds a position of senior management should be recorded, and the record should also reflect that the person has been identified as holding such a senior management position. The legal and regulatory framework contains appropriate guidance and mechanism on the application of this backstop option. The FIC guidance note gives examples under which circumstances legal entities may not identify beneficial owners using ownership and control criteria, such as in the cases of public companies. On the other hand, the FIA notes that for CDD purposes, if an AML-obliged person cannot complete CDD, the relationship cannot be created or transaction completed (see paragraph 181).

117. The FIA provisions are further explained in a binding Guidance Note No. 01 of 2015 on identification and verification of beneficial ownership information, issued by the Namibian Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC) – hereafter referred to as the FIC guidance note. $^{12}$ The note has been in operation since 2015 and was replaced by a more comprehensive version in 2025. The revisions have improved the explanations on the identification of beneficial owner(s) by entities and AML-obliged persons. Most importantly, the revised guidance clarifies that for legal persons, the beneficial owner(s) are the natural person(s) who have control through ownership or other means. This clarificatory emphasis avoids any potential confusion that legal persons may face whilst identifying their beneficial owners due to the use of the term “client” in the main definition as discussed in the paragraph 109. The guidance further confirms that a simultaneous approach should be taken to identify beneficial owners taking the criteria for ownership and control together, thereby increasing the number of persons that can be identified as beneficial owners.

118. The revised FIC guidance note also provides a diagrammatic example of scenarios where beneficial ownership may be exercised through interposed legal persons and arrangements and through indirect control. It also provides additional examples of control through other means, such as using debt instruments and personal connections. The guidance note further explains the practical terms of joint control through ownership, using an example of family relationships. It explains that if no one family member owns or controls 25% of the legal person, then, where applicable, ownership interests of individual family members should be evaluated together. The FIC guidance note requires that the entities or AML-obliged persons should undertake efforts to understand the ownership and control structure of the entity.

119. Additionally, the 2015 version of the guidance note, while explaining control through ownership, used a 20% threshold contrary to the 25% threshold indicated in the FIA. While a 20% threshold does not contravene the standard definition of beneficial owner, the disparity between the FIA and the guidance note may have brought confusion. The 2025 revision has now aligned the threshold to 25%.

120. Another important example is that the 2025 revision explains that where a senior managing official is recorded (see paragraph 116), the CDD files or the register data should clearly specify the actions taken, why it was decided to identify the senior manager and that the individual identified is not a real beneficial owner, but a senior manager.

121. In conclusion, the definition and methods for identifying beneficial owner as contained in the FIA and explained further in the FIC guidance note are comprehensive enough to facilitate the identification of beneficial owner(s) by legal persons and AML-obliged persons.

## Implementation of the methods of identifying beneficial owners

122. Regarding the practical implementation of the definition and method to identify beneficial owners, the provisions of the FIA and the FIC guidance note provide direction to companies and close corporations for the identification of their beneficial owners and AML-obliged persons for conducting CDD.

123. The FIC guidance note of 2025 updates the guidance hitherto issued for CDD purposes, while the note provides first time guidance for companies and close corporations.

124. Namibian authorities charged with supervision of AML obligations reported that they have supervised the implementation of the amended guidance. The authorities explained that after the change in guidance, the supervisory authorities held industry engagements to promote the common understanding of the revisions. Further inspections were conducted to ascertain whether AML-obliged persons aligned their internal policies and procedures accordingly, and whether there was effective implementation of these policies and procedures. Where non-compliance was detected, sanctions were imposed accordingly. Further, the representatives of the AML-obliged persons (mainly banks) interviewed during the on-site visit demonstrated clear understanding of the concept of beneficial owner for each type of legal person and explained that the new amendments to the guidance note merely highlighted aspects that are already elaborated in the FIA and are being practiced.

125. However, the AML-obliged persons that are not banks did not express the same level of comfort. Moreover, for companies and close corporations the 2025 note contains the first detailed guidance to explain beneficial ownership identification in more detail. Considering the references added for companies and close corporations, clarification of the method to identify beneficial ownership and the alignment of the threshold of 25% with the FIA, there is a need to continue monitoring the implementation of the changes in the guidance note. Consequently, Namibia is recommended to continue to monitor the implementation of recent changes in the guidance for identification of beneficial owners to ensure that adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information is available in all cases.

## Obligations for legal entities to maintain a register of beneficial owners

126. Since August 2023, both the Companies Act (s. 122A) and the CC Act (s. 16A) require all companies (including foreign companies registered in Namibia) and close corporations to maintain an accurate and up-to-date register of their beneficial owners, in Namibia. The beneficial ownership register should be kept at the same place where the register of members or the founding statement (in the case of close corporations) is kept.

127. The information to be maintained in the register of beneficial owners, for each beneficial owner includes:

• the first name and surname, and any former first names and surnames

\- the date of birth and identification number appearing on the identity document

• full particulars of residential address, business address, email address and postal address

nationality

• the nature and extent of beneficial ownership.

128. Where a shareholder of a company or member of a close corporation $^{13}$ is holding the ownership interest as a nominee, then the identity details (as explained in the preceding paragraph) of the nominee must also be recorded.

129. The Companies Act (s. 122A(5)) and the CC Act (s. 16A(5)) mandate that the legal entity appoint a person residing in Namibia who is responsible for the safekeeping of its register of beneficial owners and responsible for making such information available upon request by competent authorities that include NamRA.

130. Companies and close corporations must keep the information contained in the register of beneficial owners, including the nature and extent of beneficial ownership, for at least five years from the date of creation of the record. This obligation is transferred to the administrator or liquidator of a company or close corporation after the entity is dissolved or otherwise ceases to exist.

## Submission of beneficial ownership information to the registrar and updates


132. The information submitted to BIPA can be accessed by members of the public upon request. The information shared with the public is limited to the full names of the beneficial owner(s) and the extent of beneficial ownership held.

133. To facilitate the reporting of beneficial ownership information, BIPA has prescribed the use of a beneficial ownership declaration form. In addition to the information described at paragraphs 127 and 128, the declaration form requires the submission of the date when beneficial ownership commenced or ceased.

134. Regarding updates to the submitted information, entities are obliged to report any changes to beneficial ownership information within seven days (s. 122A(3), Companies Act and s. 16A(3), CC Act) (see paragraph 135). If the registrar has any reasonable grounds to believe that an entity has not updated its beneficial ownership information within this prescribed time frame, the registrar may issue a directive for the correction of the non-compliance, failure of which would lead to imposition of administrative penalties on the entity (see paragraph 141).

135. Additionally, Section 4 of the FIA obliges entities to collect the beneficial ownership information annually. Accordingly, BIPA prescribed an annual beneficial ownership confirmatory form in 2024. Through this form, entities are obliged to declare that the information previously submitted “remains the same and is accurate, adequate and up to date”, or otherwise file changes to the information.

136. Intermediate entities in the ownership chain or natural persons that do not facilitate the reporting of changes, including responding to the entity when asked for purposes of reporting changes in beneficial ownership or submitting the annual confirmatory form, are liable to the penalties listed at paragraph 144.

137. From 1 April 2024 to 31 March 2025, 8 360 entities submitted changes to their beneficial ownership information.

138. The framework for entities to submit beneficial ownership information at the time of registration, directives issued for existing entities and the requirements to update beneficial ownership information have put in place a system that would enable the availability of accurate, adequate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information, if implemented fully.

## Beneficial ownership obligations on entities – enforcement measures and oversight

139. BIPA is the main supervisory agency tasked with the oversight and enforcement of the obligations on companies and close corporations. In practice, BIPA collaborates with the FIC and other government agencies to execute its supervisory mandate. Despite this collaboration, there are no formal or informal discrepancy reporting requirements on AML-obliged persons, regarding the beneficial ownership information reported to BIPA.

140. BIPA has a multi-faceted approach to oversight and enforcement. Firstly, they have put inplace a mechanism to conduct verification and quality checks on the submitted information. Secondly, the other category of activities is geared towards improving compliance with the reporting obligations. This second category involves awareness, outreach programmes to promote compliance, generating and publishing lists of non-compliant entities and collaborating with law enforcement agencies. Thirdly, the last category involves the issuance of sanctions against non-compliance.

## Applicable sanctions

141. If the registrar has reasonable grounds to believe that an entity or person (including the person appointed as explained at paragraph 129) has failed to maintain a register of beneficial owners or has not complied with the statutory deadlines for submitting beneficial ownership information, the registrar is required to issue a written directive instructing the entity to rectify the non-compliance within seven days. If the entity does not comply with this directive, the registrar may impose administrative sanctions. In determining the appropriate sanction, the registrar considers several factors, including the seriousness, duration and extent of the non-compliance, any history of previous breaches, and any remedial steps taken to prevent recurrence (s. 122A(11-14), Companies Act and s. 16A(11-14), CC Act).

142. Following this assessment, the registrar may impose a financial penalty of up to NAD 50 000 (EUR 2 263) against an entity for failure to maintain its register of beneficial owners or meet reporting deadlines, and, where a directive has been ignored, an additional penalty of up to NAD 1 000 (EUR 45.3) per day of continued non-compliance.

143. The registrar must notify sanctioned entities in writing, outlining the decision, reasons, penalty amount, applicable interest, and payment timelines, with all penalties payable directly to the registrar.

144. In addition to these administrative sanctions, there is criminal liability for entities or individuals who fail to comply with beneficial ownership requirements, provide false or misleading information, withhold required information that should have been entered into the entity's register of beneficial owners, or make false entries in the register. These offences carry penalties of up to NAD 10 million (EUR 452 571), imprisonment of up to 10 years upon conviction, or both. Administrative penalties may be imposed irrespective of criminal proceedings, although any criminal fines already imposed must be considered when assessing administrative sanctions.

145. Lastly, entities that persistently fail to comply are placed on an inactive list and subject to deregistration (see paragraph 173).

## Verification and quality checks

146. BIPA has 8 to 12 staff allocated to manage beneficial ownership-related workload. The staff were initially deployed in project format to manage the initial stages of the obligations, and they have now been mainstreamed to manage all related tasks, ensuring that adequate resources are deployed to manage associated workloads including oversight functions related with beneficial ownership submissions.

147. All submissions are made by physically submitting the completed beneficial ownership declaration forms and supporting identity documents to client service officials at BIPA. All copies of identity documents submitted must be certified by the issuing agency or notarised. The submitted information undergoes two levels of verification and checks. In the first one, the client service official checks the completeness of the form and that supporting identity documents are certified or notarised.

148. If the form is complete and the identity documents are in order, the client service official enters the submitted information into an electronic database. If there are any deficiencies in the submitted information, the form is rejected, and the submitting person is asked to correct any identified anomalies. BIPA officials explained that the common anomalies identified include uncertified identity documents, incomplete addresses, and failure to capture the extent and nature of beneficial ownership.

149. Once the submitted form has been successfully uploaded onto the electronic database, such a form is marked as “submitted pending approval” and must undergo the next category of verification checks.

150. Under this second category of checks, the verification focuses on the accuracy of the information submitted. The main verification carried out is a comparison with the legal ownership information of the entity. For companies, this involves determining whether the ownership structure of the entity conforms to the information submitted, such as ownership percentages of individual shareholders. The majority of companies have simple ownership structures. For close corporations, the checks involve verification against the founding statement.

151. If any information recorded on the form clearly deviates from the legal ownership information without a plausible explanation, a query is sent to the submitting person. Namibian authorities explained that for example if a shareholder (individual) of a company or a member of a close corporation who meets the requisite threshold of ownership is not included on the beneficial ownership form, such a gap would be queried. Only the forms that have no queries or where the queries have been successfully answered are approved. On average, 3% of the submissions are rejected owing to all the verification checks and must be corrected before full submission is achieved.

152. Further, in March 2026, BIPA started sectoral based field on-site inspections to verify compliance with beneficial ownership obligations. The inspections are guided by a risk-based approach that considers the national risk assessment (see paragraph 162). This initial phase of inspections covered 57 entities from the mining and fishing sectors, and covered persons who are appointed to keep beneficial ownership registers on behalf of entities (see paragraph 129). During these visits, BIPA officials physically verify the beneficial ownership records and the adequacy, accuracy and up-to-datedness of the beneficial ownership information maintained. Namibia reported that all the entities inspected exhibited a clear understanding of the beneficial ownership obligations and were maintaining adequate, accurate and up-to-date information.

153. The Namibian authorities also reported that BIPA is in the process of further strengthening their verification and qualitative inspections in collaboration with the FIC, Bank of Namibia, and NamRA.

## Directives and outreach programmes

154. To date, BIPA's approach to improving compliance with the obligations on entities to submit beneficial ownership information mainly includes issuance of directives to entities, conducting outreach programmes and collaborating with other government agencies to enforce compliance.

155. Following the gazetting of the amendments in the Companies Act and CC Act in July 2023, BIPA issued its first directive on 24 July 2023. The directive broadly explained the obligations on entities and required all existing entities to submit their beneficial ownership information to BIPA by 7 September 2024. It also mentioned that BIPA would visit all regions to engage with businesses, and it provided links to relevant submission forms.

156. Initially, compliance was very low. Only a few entities had filed beneficial ownership information for tax year 2023/2024. BIPA thus conducted outreach programmes and regional engagements in different parts of Namibia, where BIPA officials sensitised different entities and supported them to file their beneficial ownership information (see paragraph 93).

157. In most cases, these outreach programmes cover a comprehensive list of obligations that entities must fulfil, including annual reports, payment of annual fees and submission of beneficial ownership information. Namibian authorities reported that on average, between 40 to 50 entities per day are assisted during the outreach programmes and that regional engagements attracted between 220-300 participants.

158. In addition, BIPA sent out bulk emails and ran social media campaigns to sensitise entities and communicate the new obligations in the Companies Act and CC Act concerning the availability of beneficial ownership information.

159. In 2024, BIPA issued additional directives whereby the deadlines for submission were extended and the obligations of entities explained further. On 7 January 2024 and 1 February 2024, the registrar issued new directives explaining the obligations and requiring the submission of beneficial ownership information. The February directive required all existing entities to submit beneficial ownership information by 29 February 2024, requiring all entities to submit their beneficial ownership information within seven days. Following delayed compliance (see paragraph 169), the directives also communicated that all entities that had not submitted their beneficial ownership information would be placed on an inactive list (see paragraphs 173 to 179). The directive also mentioned the possibility of administrative and criminal sanctions. Compliance rate slowly by steadily raised over years.

160. These directives and outreach programmes, complemented by enforcement action have improved the rate of compliance to 45% of entities, with the obligations to submit beneficial ownership information by entities, although the submission rate is still average (see paragraphs 170 and 171).

## Enforcement action undertaken and results

161. As discussed in paragraphs 141 to 144, the Companies Act and CC Act contain administrative (monetary penalties, publishing of non-compliant entities and deregistration) and criminal sanctions to target non-compliance with the obligations to keep registers of beneficial ownership information and to submit such information to BIPA.

162. Concerning monetary and criminal sanctions, BIPA is in the process of finalising a sanctions matrix to be relied upon in the issuance of sanctions. This is because the sanctions contained in the legal and regulatory framework must be implemented in varying forms depending on the severity of the offence or related considerations. The authorities explained that the sanctions matrix explores the application of monetary sanctions in line with Namibia's national risk assessment. This is intended to guide BIPA to vary the value of monetary penalties with the possibility of issuing higher sanctions against entities that operate in sectors that present higher AML risks. To this end, BIPA, in collaboration with the FIC, has conducted a risk assessment of legal persons in Namibia and identified the key sectors where potential risks are prevalent (see paragraph 152). BIPA has not yet started utilising the sanctions matrix or issued any monetary or criminal sanctions yet.

163. So far, Namibia has used the approach of denial and restriction of services, including banking services and publication of inactive entities, to compel compliance.

## Denial and restriction of services

164. Entities that have not submitted their beneficial ownership information are restricted from accessing services such as filing of amendments or any resolutions at BIPA. In addition, such entities cannot be issued certificates of good standing.

165. Secondly, BIPA has collaborated with the Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC) and Bank of Namibia to restrict banking services to non-compliant entities.

166. In April 2024, the FIC issued Directive 01 of 2024, under its powers contained in the FIA, to strengthen national efforts to increase the submission of beneficial ownership information to BIPA. This intervention followed concerns regarding the low rate of beneficial ownership filings with BIPA. The FIC directive was to complement the efforts being undertaken by BIPA. Further, the FIC directive specifically sought to leverage the banking sector as part of a broader, multifaceted national strategy to increase beneficial ownership compliance.

167. The aim of this collaborative measure was to incentivise compliance by linking beneficial ownership compliance to continued access to banking services. Banks were instructed to obtain the periodically updated beneficial ownership-inactive list published by BIPA (see paragraph 173) and identify any of their clients appearing on this list. Banks were required to notify and remind such clients to submit their outstanding beneficial ownership information to BIPA and provide proof of filing to their bank. In cases where companies or close corporations failed to update their beneficial ownership information as required by law, banks were directed to restrict access to banking services, including withdrawals and transfers of funds or assets, until compliance was achieved.

168. Namibia reported that these enforcement actions have improved the rate of compliance. Regarding the restriction on banking services, the FIC conducted targeted inspections of all seven banks in 2025 to assess compliance with its directive. The inspections found that banks had largely implemented the required measures, although the authorities did not confirm that any banks were sanctioned for failing to implement the directive. The representatives of banks interviewed during the on-site visit confirmed that they had implemented FIC's directive and asked their customers to present letters of good standing from BIPA, prior to accessing banking services.

Namibian authorities further reported that between January and March 2026, the rate of compliance with beneficial ownership obligations continued to improve owing to the supervisory and enforcement activities conducted. About 45% of all entities have submitted their beneficial ownership information.

## Publishing of inactive entities

173. Companies and close corporations that persistently fail to comply with the beneficial ownership requirements are placed on an inactive list and are subsequently subject to deregistration after six months (s. 122A(15), Companies Act and s. 16A(15), CC Act). This list is published on the BIPA website. $^{14}$ The list published as of 6 February 2025 contains 144 576 entities, most of which are close corporations. The risk posed to availability of beneficial ownership information by inactive close corporations is of reduced materiality (see paragraph 178).

174. The Namibian authorities explained that the inactive list is useful for reference purposes by the competent authority, in EOIR cases. When a request for beneficial ownership information is received, the competent authority first checks to determine whether the entity subject to the request is published on the inactive list, before proceeding to send the notice for collection of the requested information to BIPA or to an AML-obliged person (see further discussions under Element B.1).

175. Namibia has categorised such listed entities for deregistration, although this has not been carried out yet. BIPA has issued notices to the effect that such entities would be de-registered starting 16 May 2025, although this deadline was further extended until early 2026. De-registrations are now set to commence. As of 16 February 2026, BIPA issued a notice of deregistration for the first tranche of 699 close corporations that have since been deregistered. Deregistered entities cease to exist (see paragraphs 67 and 68).

176. Namibian authorities explained that most of these entities are close corporations that have not yet commenced any business operations. As discussed in the paragraph 99, the Namibian authorities explained that the government of Namibia periodically introduces financial empowerment initiatives that encourage citizens to create formal legal entities. However, many of these entities never become operational and were established to position themselves to secure tenders or other government-supported opportunities.

177. The authorities further reported that during the inspections on the implementation of FIC Directive 01 of 2024 (see paragraph 168), an estimated 14 384 entities on the inactive list (representing about 10% of the inactive population) were identified as having bank accounts in Namibia and the majority of these have since complied. It was further established that the remaining 130 782 entities (around 90%), most of which are close corporations, did not have bank accounts in Namibian banks. BIPA additionally noted that 70 to 80% of these non-compliant entities did not have any recent filings with the registrar, further confirming the possibility that such entities may not have ongoing business transactions. However, there is still the possibility that such entities may conduct business outside of Namibia.

178. The risk to the availability of beneficial ownership information posed by inactive close corporations is mitigated by the fact that, as per the provisions of the CC Act, all members of the close corporation must be natural persons, joint holding of members' interests is prohibited, and all members have equal management and representation rights, unless otherwise specified. All these aspects are contained in the legal ownership information submitted to BIPA (see paragraphs 61 to 64).

179. In conclusion, since the introduction of obligations on companies and close corporations to keep a register of their beneficial owners and to report beneficial ownership information to BIPA in 2023, Namibia has conducted outreach and awareness programmes and applied some administrative sanctions. These activities have improved the rate of filing of beneficial ownership information to BIPA. Moreover, Namibia conducts completeness and qualitative checks on the submitted information and has commenced field inspections to verify the beneficial ownership information maintained by the entities. Nevertheless, the number of entities that have submitted their beneficial ownership is about 45%. Moreover, Namibia is in the process of increasing the range of sanctions (monetary penalties and deregistration) and conducting further quality checks to increase the submission rate and further ensure quality information. Namibia is recommended to strengthen its supervision and enforcement actions to ensure the availability of adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information of legal entities (including inactive ones) in all cases, in line with the standard.

## Anti-money laundering law – Accountable institutions (AML-obliged persons) and customer due diligence

180. The AML framework (see paragraph 31) requires AML-obliged persons, termed as accountable institutions, to identify and verify the identity of their clients, identify the beneficial owners of such clients and take reasonable measures to verify the identity of the beneficial owners using reliable source documents (FIA, s. 21(2)). This obligation applies before establishing a business relationship and before concluding certain transactions.

181. If the AML-obliged person is unable to verify the customer's identity or complete the CDD process, the business relationship cannot be established. If there is an already established and ongoing business relationship, the AML-obliged person must terminate the relationship and file a suspicious transaction report to the FIC.

182. The Financial Intelligence Regulations, hereafter termed as the FI Regulations, require that for the identification of beneficial owners, the AML-obliged persons must obtain the following information: i) full name and previous names if any, ii) nationality, iii) national identity number, iv) passport number or v) date of birth in case of a minor (Regulation 6). The representatives of AML-obliged persons interviewed during the on-site visit explained that the identities of individuals are cross-checked against submitted identity documents.

183. AML-obliged persons are also obliged to conduct CDD that is aligned to the risk profile of the client. Section 20A(4) of the FIA requires AML-obliged persons to develop, adopt and implement internal policies to effectively manage and mitigate risks.

184. The beneficial ownership guidance note (see paragraph 117) explains that the risk assessment of a client with regard to money laundering or terrorism financing is the starting point before CDD is conducted and that it should be documented in the client's file or in the relevant client onboarding systems. Depending on the risk profile of the client, the AML-obliged person may conduct simplified, standard or enhanced due diligence. Simplified CDD can only be conducted in circumstances where there is no suspicion of money laundering or terrorism financing. Regardless of the category of due diligence conducted, the AML-obliged person must identify the client, its beneficial owners and maintain such information (s. 24, FIA).

185. AML-obliged persons must continuously review any complex, unusually large, or otherwise unusual transactions that have no clear lawful purpose. They must also apply enhanced due diligence to clients and transactions linked to higher-risk countries identified by institutional, national Financial Action Task Force (FATF) assessments.

186. Regarding the updating of CDD and beneficial ownership information on existing clients, AML-obliged persons are required to conduct ongoing due diligence throughout the business relationship, including maintaining up-to-date client and beneficial ownership information, and monitoring transactions to ensure they align with the customer's profile. Further, enhanced CDD should be conducted where there are doubts on the veracity of the previously obtained customer identification data or if there is suspicion of money laundering or terrorism financing, regardless of the risk posed by the client. To facilitate this approach, AML-obliged persons must develop and implement internal policies that are consistent with the national or sectoral money laundering or terrorism financing risk assessments (s. 20A and 24, FIA). The FIA only requires that for high-risk clients, the CDD measures should be applied more frequently to ensure that information is up to date (see paragraph 353).

187. Guidance note no. 4 of 2009 on the Implementation of a Compliance Regime requires AML-obliged persons to review CDD at least once every year for high-risk clients and at least once in two years for all other clients. This guidance is binding and enforceable by the authorities. Namibian authorities explained that in practice AML-obliged persons have adopted specified frequencies of at least every year for high-risk clients, at least every two years and at least every three to five years for medium and low risk clients, respectively. The authorities nonetheless consider the range of these frequencies to be appropriate. Moreover, the authorities can sanction an AML-obliged person if the internal policy set is deemed inappropriate. The Nambian authorities explained that in practice, all the seven banks operating in Namibia have set appropriate frequencies to update CDD and beneficial ownership information. In other cases, AML-obliged persons (mainly TCSPs) have been sanctioned for setting inappropriate frequencies or failing to adhere to the frequencies set in their internal policies (see paragraph 205). Since AML-obliged persons may set frequencies that depart from what is included in the binding guidance, there is a potential risk depending on what type of frequencies all AML-obliged persons will set.

188. Regarding the availability of beneficial ownership information on companies and close corporations, any potential gap is mitigated by the requirements to update the beneficial ownership information submitted to BIPA, at least annually. Considering that AML-obliged persons may set frequencies in their internal policies that differ from those stated in the guidance, Namibia should align the provisions of the binding guidance and the practice by AML-obliged persons to ensure that CDD and beneficial ownership information are updated appropriately for all entities and arrangements (see Annex 1).

189. AML-obliged persons are obliged to maintain detailed information concerning the CDD activities conducted, including any document obtained to verify the identity of the client or beneficial owner and the nature of transactions conducted. The records related to the establishment of a business relationship must be kept throughout the course of the business relationship and thereafter, for five years from the date the business relationship is terminated, or longer if required by the authorities.

190. If an AML-obliged person ceases to exist, the records will be maintained by the liquidator as discussed in the paragraphs 72 to 73. If a liquidator who is a natural person dies, the rules governing the administration of deceased persons as governed by the MOHC would apply in ensuring that the estate of the deceased person is properly managed.

191. An AML-obliged person may rely on a third party to carry out CDD and to introduce business to them (Regulation 5, FI regulations). In those circumstances, the AML-obliged person must satisfy itself that the third party or introducer is subject to regulation, supervision, monitoring or equivalent compliance measures relating to CDD and record-keeping and is sufficiently knowledgeable and resourced to ensure compliance. Further, the AML-obliged person is obliged to immediately obtain the identification information of the client, which includes, at a minimum, the beneficial owners of the client. The AML-obliged person must also satisfy themselves that the third party can provide copies of all identification data and documentation without delay upon request. When an AML-obliged person relies on another institution or a third party for customer identification and verification, the ultimate responsibility for ensuring that proper CDD is conducted remains with the AML-obliged person. The representatives of the AML-obliged persons met during the on-site visit, and the supervisory agencies explained that where reliance is placed on third parties, these requirements were fully met. These conditions are in line with the standard.

## Anti-money laundering law requirements – oversight and enforcement

192. The FIA contains both administrative and criminal sanctions that can be applied against non-compliant AML-obliged persons or any other persons. Any person has failed to comply with the provisions of the FIA, any regulation or directive, is liable to administrative sanctions that comprise, i) caution not to repeat the conduct, ii) reprimand, iii) directive to take remedial action, iv) restriction or suspension of certain business activities, v) suspension of licence or a financial penalty not exceeding NAD 10 million (EUR 452 570) (s. 56(3), FIA).

193. Further, an AML-obliged person who does not perform CDD and identify the beneficial owners of its clients as prescribed by the AML framework commits an offence and is liable to a fine not exceeding NAD 100 million (EUR 4.5 million), and if the commission of the offence is attributable to a representative of the AML-obliged person, then such representative is liable to a fine or imprisonment not exceeding 30 years, or to both (s. 24(3), FIA). The same penalty applies to an AML-obliged person who does not maintain CDD and beneficial ownership records as required (s. 26(4), FIA).

194. The FIC and the Namibia Financial Institutions Supervisory Authority (NAMFISA) are mandated to supervise the implementation of AML requirements by AML-obliged persons. The FIC supervises the banking sector and non-financial AML-obliged persons, while NAMFISA supervises the non-banking financial sector.

195. In executing their supervisory mandates, both the FIC and NAMFISA employ risk-based approaches. Both institutions have developed supervision methodologies that are relied upon to conduct oversight, supervision and enforcement.

## Supervision by the Namibia Financial Intelligence Centre

196. As discussed at paragraphs 32 and 33, Namibia has been working to address the deficiencies identified in its 2022 FATF Mutual Evaluation Report, and to this end, the FIC has updated its supervision methodology and enhanced its supervision and sanctioning since 2023. The FIC conducts regular compliance monitoring activities, which include reviewing periodic reports submitted by AML-obliged persons, considering information received from external sources that is likely to affect the compliance behaviour of AML-obliged persons, and continuously assessing risks at the sectoral and national levels. The FIC also provides training to AML-obliged persons to ensure proper understanding and implementation of all AML obligations.

197. The FIC's compliance assessment and supervision methodology was first developed in 2016 and is regularly updated. Based on its supervision methodology, the FIC prepares an annual plan that prioritises sectors and entities with a high risk of non-compliance. The annual plan identifies sectors and AML-obliged persons scheduled for supervisory activities in the given year and outlines the type and depth of planned examinations. Coordination with other regulatory and supervisory bodies is incorporated into the planning process to avoid duplication. For example, the FIC conducts joint assessments with other prudential regulatory authorities.

198. Supervisory actions undertaken by the FIC include off-site and on-site inspections and can be thematic reviews that are focused on addressing specific risks. Off-site inspections involve desk-based assessments conducted primarily by FIC officials on relevant data and information submitted or otherwise made available to the FIC. On the other hand, on-site inspections involve the FIC officials visiting the premises of the AML-obliged person under supervision. In many cases, both types of monitoring are conducted simultaneously.

199. AML-obliged persons identified as high-risk undergo more frequent and intensive supervision. During the on-site inspections, the FIC evaluates the AML-obliged person's internal procedures, the training and competencies of relevant officials and reviews selected client files, to assess the level of compliance. The selection of client files is informed by the prevalent or determined risks. Further, the FIC follows up on findings from previous supervisory cycles and assesses whether institutions have implemented required remedial measures, including improvements in internal controls and customer due diligence measures. Regarding identification of customers and beneficial owners, the examinations assess whether AML-obliged persons implement adequate measures, including timely and reliable identification and verification of customers and beneficial owners, understanding the purpose of relationships, and applying enhanced measures for higher-risk scenarios.

200. The representatives of the AML-obliged persons met during the on-site visit reported that during on-site inspections, authorities examine the entity's internal policies, procedures followed to onboard clients and to conduct CDD. The authorities select and review sample client files. The representatives of the AML-obliged persons further stated that where deficiencies are identified, sanctions have been issued.

201. During the period 2023 to March 2026, the FIC conducted a wide range of supervisory activities targeting AML-obliged persons. A total of 480 off-site inspections targeting AML-obliged persons that are relevant for EOIR purposes were conducted.


202. Moreover, the off-site inspections were supplemented with 103 on-site inspections. Over the three-year period, all the banks operating in Namibia were covered by on-site inspections at least once (see additional discussions under Element A.3).


203. Comparing the different types of inspections, accountants and Trust Company Service Providers (TCSPs) had more off-site inspections compared to on-site inspections, while banks and legal practitioners were mainly covered through on-site inspections.

204. In 2024, the inspections mainly focused on non-financial professionals, especially accountants and legal practitioners. This followed amendments made to the definition of beneficial owner in July 2023. The inspections established whether CDD measures were well implemented, ongoing CDD was conducted based on risk, beneficial owners were identified, and records were well maintained. The representatives of the non-financial professions met during the on-site visit reported that the FIC inspections have enabled them to enhance their CDD practices.

205. Any deficiencies identified are communicated to the AML-obliged person for remediation. The FIC mainly applies the administrative sanctions elaborated in paragraph 192. During the period 2023 to 2025, the FIC cautioned and directed AML-obliged persons to rectify any shortcomings identified and these were promptly addressed. Further, the licences of 139 TCSPs were suspended for continued non-compliance, including inadequate CDD measures.

206. Monetary sanctions amounting to NAD 6.55 million (EUR 296 433) were also issued, mainly to banks. The authorities explained that whereas in 2022 and 2023 they frequently identified inadequate CDD measures and failure to properly identify beneficial owners as deficiencies, by 2024 and 2025, these have not been identified (see detailed discussion on supervision of banks under Element A.3). In all instances, where deficiencies have been identified, the obliged person must also present periodic reports on the progress made to address these deficiencies.

207. NAMFISA follows a similar approach to conduct supervision of the non-banking financial sector AML-obliged persons. AML-obliged persons and sectors assessed as high risk undergo more frequent supervision (at least once annually, based on past compliance behaviour).

208. Some of the key categories of AML-obliged persons supervised by NAMFISA include unit trusts, microlenders, life insurers and asset managers. From 2023 to 2025, NAMFISA conducted various off-site and on-site inspections targeting these AML-obliged persons. Some of the inspections conducted were follow-up and themed inspections focused on CDD measures and identification of beneficial owners.

In 2023, NAMFISA issued monetary penalties amounting to NAD 6.6 million (EUR 298 696), and only NAD 24 024 (EUR 1 087) in 2024.

The activities cover banks and non-financial AML-obliged persons that are relevant for EOIR purposes. In terms of examinations conducted, the authorities evaluated the AML-obliged persons' CDD and beneficial ownership identification and record-keeping practices. Where deficiencies are identified, AML-obliged persons are required to address those deficiencies and report periodic progress. Moreover, sanctions that include the suspension of licences and monetary penalties have been issued. There is an indication that the supervisory and enforcement interventions have improved compliance. ## A.1.2 Bearer shares

213. Prior to July 2023, companies could issue share warrants to bearer with respect to a registered share in the register of members. The holders of such shares could only exercise their rights in the company upon depositing the share warrant at the company's premises. On 21 July 2023, amendments to the Companies Act abolished the issuance of share warrants to bearer.

214. Section 108(4) of the Companies Act provides that where a company had issued a share warrant to a bearer before 21 July 2023, the bearer must, within 30 days, convert the share warrant into an ordinary par value share. Further, for any share warrants not converted within the prescribed timeframe, the bearer would be liable to a fine not exceeding NAD 40 (EUR 1.8) for every day that the non-compliance continues.

215. Eventually, on 7 November 2023, the registrar issued a directive indicating that any share warrant to bearer not converted by 30 November 2023 would be considered null and void and all associated rights with such warrants lost. Further, there is no possibility to revive any of such rights or otherwise seek compensation. In addition, the bearer that still holds such shares would still be liable to the daily monetary penalty stated in paragraph 214 above. The directive further instructed all financial institutions, legal practitioners and stockbrokers not to conduct any transactions that involve share warrants to bearer.

216. Since a share warrant could only be issued in respect of an already registered share, Namibian authorities reported that the data stored at BIPA does not facilitate an effective check; however, the authorities are not aware of any companies that could have issued share warrants to bearer in the past. In any case, the issuance of share warrants to bearer is now prohibited. Any share warrants to bearer that were not converted are now lost and their holders cannot exercise any rights with the company. Further, owing to the self-enforcing nature of legal ownership rights in a company, it is incumbent upon the holder of the share warrant to ensure that its right to a share has been captured in the register of members, otherwise such a right would revert to the shareholder previously listed in the register. The registers of members of companies identify all the legal owners of the company except in situations of nominee arrangements (see paragraphs 83 to 87).

## A.1.3 Partnerships

## Types of partnerships

217. Partnerships in Namibia are legal arrangements without legal personality. There is no legislation governing the incorporation, registration or formation of partnerships in Namibia. As such, only general partnerships created by a contractual agreement between the partners in accordance with common law principles can be formed.

218. The income of partnerships is taxed at the level of the partners, while partnerships themselves must account for VAT and/or pay as you earn deductions made on employee remunerations. Accordingly, all partnerships carrying on business in Namibia must be registered with NamRA for tax purposes. There were 1 219 partnerships registered with NamRA as of 30 September 2025. Namibia further reported that 1 098 partnerships were identified as having active bank accounts with Namibian banks. Most of the legal practice work in Namibia is conducted under partnerships. The representatives of banks and lawyers confirmed that every legal practitioner who holds or receives money on behalf of another person is obliged to open a trust banking account. This requirement has also contributed to the high prevalence of bank accounts of partnerships in Namibia. Namibian authorities explained that the remaining 121 partnerships (10%) were related to an ongoing register cleaning exercise at NamRA.

## Identity information

219. Identity information of partnerships is mainly available through tax and the AML laws. Further, although partnerships themselves have no legal obligations to maintain identity information of their partners, since partnerships are contractual arrangements between two or more persons or entities involved together in a business venture, the identity of partners to the arrangement should be known to the other partner(s), through the partnership agreement or otherwise.

220. Regarding tax law obligations, persons operating under a partnership that carry on business in Namibia or otherwise become liable for any tax, must register and submit returns to NamRA. Section 56(16) of the Income Tax Act (ITA) requires partners to submit separate income tax returns for each of the partners. Moreover, to register as an individual taxpayer, copies of the Namibian national identity card, Namibian passport and a copy of the driver's licence must be submitted. For non-Namibian citizens, a certified copy of the passport or asylum permit must be submitted. Consequently, the identity information on participants in a partnership is available with NamRA.

221. Additionally, as explained in paragraph 214, 90% of the partnerships registered with NamRA have bank accounts in Namibia. The representatives of the banks met during the on-site visit explained that in practice, banks strictly require partnerships to provide copies of partnership agreements before bank accounts are opened. Therefore, the identity information of a partnership shall also be available to banks (see additional discussion on beneficial ownership).

## Beneficial ownership information

222. The AML framework is the only source of beneficial ownership information on partnerships in Namibia. However, there is no obligation in the legal and regulatory framework requiring all partnerships to engage an AML-obliged person in an ongoing business relationship. In practice, most partnerships have a bank account with a Namibian bank (see paragraph 217).

223. The FIA definition of beneficial owner as discussed in paragraph 109 applies to partnerships as well, with the exception that reference to “a legal person” in clause (b)(i) is replaced with “a partnership”.

"beneficial owner" means a natural person referred to in Schedule 5 who -

(a) for the purposes of this Act, ultimately owns or controls -

(i) a client or natural person; or

(ii) a natural person on whose behalf a transaction is being concluded; or

(b) exercises ultimate effective ownership or control over -

(i) a partnership;

where such ultimate ownership or ultimate effective control may be exercised directly or indirectly or through a chain of ownership or control other than direct control.

224. Further, schedule 5 of the FIA and the guidance note issued by the FIC explain the methods for the identification of beneficial owners of a partnership:

In relation to partnership, a natural person who ultimately owns or controls the partnership by -

(a) holding 25 percent or more of the ownership interest in the partnership; or

(b) being able to exercise, directly or indirectly, 25 percent or more of the votes in decision-making on amending the agreement on which the partnership is based or regarding the performance of that agreement otherwise than by acts of management, in so far as in that agreement decision-making by majority of votes is required, and where -

(i) there is doubt as to whether the natural person identified is the beneficial owner or the natural person on whose behalf a transaction is conducted; or

(ii) a natural person cannot be identified as the beneficial owner,

the person who holds the position of senior management in the partnership and record that the person has been identified as holding that position.

225. The determination of beneficial owners for partnerships must consider the specificities of their different forms and structures. In Namibia, partnerships are legal arrangements and in such a case, the standard requires that all the parties to the partnership and any natural person(s) who exercise control over the partnership be identified. Moreover, all the partners may be involved in decision making and management of the partnership, regardless of the percentage of their capital contributions or percentage of participation in the partnership.

226. While the definition refers to a 25% threshold of ownership interest, the main part of the definition as stated in paragraph 223 requires a simultaneous application of the criteria of ownership and control, and as such, would lead to the identification of all the partners in a partnership. Additionally, the FIC guidance note explains that for general partnerships, all partners should be considered as beneficial owners due to their direct involvement in the management and control of the partnership. Consequently, the definition and method contained in the FIA and the FIC guidance note facilitate the identification of beneficial owners in line with the standard.

227. As discussed in paragraph 222, the AML-framework is the only source of beneficial ownership information for partnerships, although not all partnerships are required to engage an AML-obliged person in an ongoing business relationship. Therefore, the beneficial ownership information of partnerships may not be available in some cases. Consequently, Namibia is recommended to ensure that adequate accurate, and up-to-date beneficial ownership of partnerships is available in all cases, in line with the standard.

228. AML-obliged persons are required to update beneficial ownership information whenever there is a change and/or to review and update CDD and beneficial ownership information at least once every year for high-risk clients and at least one in two years for all other clients. In practice, AML-obliged persons have set frequencies in their internal policies. Namibian authorities have sanctioned AML-obliged persons for not setting appropriate policies or not following them (see paragraphs 185 and 186). Considering that AML-obliged persons may set frequencies in their internal policies that differ from those stated in the guidance, Namibia should align the provisions of the binding guidance and the practice by AML-obliged persons to ensure that CDD and beneficial ownership information are updated appropriately for all partnerships (see Annex 1).

229. The identity information of partnerships is maintained by NamRA indefinitely, while the beneficial ownership information of partnerships that cease to exist is maintained by AML-obliged persons, if applicable, or liquidators, as discussed in the paragraphs 189 and 190.

## Oversight and enforcement

230. The obligations to maintain identity information are mainly supervised through tax audits.

231. The oversight and enforcement mechanism under the AML framework and supervisory activities by the FIC and NAMFISA discussed under section A.1.1 also apply to partnerships.

232. The FIC guidance note that was revised in 2025 contains new and more detailed guidance on the determination of beneficial ownership information of partnerships. The authorities explained that the changes in the guidance note have already been integrated in their oversight and supervisory activities; however, since the changes are recent, their implementation could not be fully assessed during this review. Namibia is recommended to continue to monitor the implementation of recent changes in the guidance for identification of beneficial owners to ensure that adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information of all partnerships is available in all cases.

Availability of partnership information in EOIR practice

233. Namibia did not receive any requests for identity or beneficial ownership in relation to partnerships during the review period, and peers have not reported any issues regarding the same.

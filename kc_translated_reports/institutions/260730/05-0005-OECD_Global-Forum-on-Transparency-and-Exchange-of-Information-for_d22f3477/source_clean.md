# Executive summary

1. This report presents the Global Forum's analysis of the Cook Islands' compliance with the standard on transparency and exchange of information on request (the standard) and concludes that the Cook Islands continues to be rated as overall largely Compliant with the standard. This conclusion is based on an assessment of the Cook Islands' legal and regulatory framework in force as on 7 May 2026 and its practical implementation, including in respect of exchange of information (EOI) requests sent and received during the review period from 1 April 2022 to 31 March 2025 (see Annex 3 for details).

Comparison of determinations and ratings for First Round Report and Second Round Report


Note: The three-scale determinations for the legal and regulatory framework are In place, In place but certain aspects of the legal implementation of the element need improvement (needs improvement), and Not in place. The four-scale ratings on compliance with the standard (capturing both the legal framework and practice) are Compliant, Largely Compliant, Partially Compliant, and Non-Compliant.

## Progress made since previous review

2. The 2015 Report concluded that the legal and regulatory framework of the Cook Islands was in place but needed improvement, with a few recommendations made in relation to the availability of accounting information, which have not been addressed yet. There remains a lack of an obligation on limited liability companies, international trusts and foundations to maintain all underlying source documentation of accounting records and a lack of penalty for failure of a foundation to maintain reliable accounting records for at least five years.

3. The standard was strengthened in 2016 to add requirements in respect of the availability of beneficial ownership information. The Cook Islands strengthened its transparency framework in 2019 with the introduction of the obligation for all domestic companies to keep a register of their beneficial owners.

This added to requirements already present in AML law which covers other types of legal entities and arrangements that must have a relationship with an AML obliged person. In 2024, the Cook Islands made further changes to both company law and AML law that strengthened and clarified requirements around the availability of beneficial ownership information. However, some gaps remain for some types of partnerships and trusts that do not have a relationship with an AML-obliged person, and foundations. Gaps also remain in relation to nominees and specifying any frequency with which AML-obliged persons must update their customer due diligence.

4. The Cook Islands signed the Multilateral Convention on Mutual Administrative Assistance in Tax Matters on 28 October 2016 and ratified it on 29 May 2017 with entry into force date on 1 September 2017. The Multilateral Convention significantly extended the EOI network of the Cook Islands.

## Key recommendations on transparency

5. As noted above, the recommendations related to the maintenance of all underlying documentation for accounting records for limited liability companies, international trusts and foundations have not been addressed. There is no penalty for failure to maintain accounting records and underlying documentation for foundations. The recommendations continue to apply. In addition, the accounting records retention requirements are unclear and incomplete in the case of domestic companies that cease to exist. While a company that is liquidated will have records available for at least one year after liquidation (with the liquidator), it is not clear who is the person responsible for keeping the accounting records and the underlying documentation of liquidated companies between one and five years. Companies that cease to exist without undergoing a liquidation process have no specified person responsible for their records after ceasing to exist.

6. Also as indicated above, the AML framework is the main information source relied upon for beneficial ownership information for some types of partnerships and trusts, specifically domestic partnerships and trusts that are not international trusts, however these do not necessarily have a relationship with an AML-obliged person in the Cook Islands and therefore availability of information is not ensured. These gaps are the subject of recommendations. More generally, there is no specified frequency for updating the CDD information (including beneficial ownership information) by AML-obliged persons when no event triggers an update. Therefore, beneficial ownership information of relevant legal entities and arrangements kept by AML-obliged persons may not always be adequate, accurate and up to date (Elements A.1 and A.3).

7. International companies may be restored after being struck off without limit of time, and there is no explicit obligation to maintain and provide ownership information during the entire period. The Cook Islands is recommended to ensure the availability of ownership information upon the restoration of an international company following the strike off from the register, as well as establishing a time limit for the revival of international companies following their dissolution. In practice, international companies that have been struck off and subsequently restored were almost always restored with the elapse of less than a year after strike off and this is well within the period in which the trustee company at the time of strike off is required to retain records on the client. This gap has therefore been of low impact so far.

8. Nominee shareholding is allowed in the Cook Islands, but the Companies Act does not set any specific obligations on nominees and nominators that would ensure that companies that have nominee shareholders obtain information on the nominee arrangements. The tax and anti-money laundering requirements also do not ensure that information on nominees and nominators would be available. The Cook Islands should ensure that ownership and identity information is available in respect of nominee shareholdings.

9. In terms of effective implementation, there are significant gaps in oversight and enforcement of domestic companies by both the Registrar of Companies and the tax authority, that may affect the availability of legal and beneficial ownership for these entities. The tax authority's oversight and enforcement of filing obligations for trusts is also insufficient to ensure availability of identity and ownership information for these arrangements. A recommendation has been made on these aspects.

10. Finally, the monitoring and enforcement of record keeping obligations by the tax authority is insufficient to ensure the availability of accounting information for some types of legal entities and arrangements and a recommendation has been made to strengthen this.

## Exchange of information in practice

11. The Cook Islands received seven exchange of information (EOI) requests from four partners between 1 April 2022 and 31 March 2025. Responses were provided, and all partners were satisfied with the information. During the same period, the Cook Islands made six EOI requests.

## Overall rating

12. The Cook Islands is rated Compliant for Elements B.1, B.2, C.1, C.2, C.3, C.4 and C.5, and Largely Compliant for Elements A.1, A.2 and A.3. Overall, the Cook Islands is rated Largely Compliant.

# Summary of determinations, ratings and recommendations


# Overview of the Cook Islands

14. This overview provides some basic information about the Cook Islands that serves as context for understanding the analysis in the main body of the report. The Cook Islands consist of 15 islands scattered over 2.2 million square kilometres of the Pacific Ocean, Northeast of New Zealand. The total resident population of the Cook Islands, as at June 2025, was 17300. $^{1}$ It is estimated that an additional 80 000 Cook Islanders live in New Zealand (2020 figures). $^{2}$

15. The Cook Islands' GDP is derived principally from tourism, with financial services making a contribution amounting to approximately $7.7\%$ of GDP as per June 2025. $^{3}$ The Cook Islands' main trading partners are Australia, People's Republic of China, Fiji, Japan, New Zealand and the United States. $^{4}$

## Legal system

16. The Cook Islands' Government is both a Constitutional Monarchy and a Parliamentary Democracy. The Head of State is the King in Right of New Zealand, King Charles the Third. The system of Government is based on the Westminster model, which provides for a separation of powers between the Legislature, the Executive and the Judiciary.

17. Prior to 1965 (when the Cook Islands was a dependent territory of New Zealand), the legal system was established by means of a New Zealand enactment, the Cook Islands Act 1915, which provided that the English system of common law was to apply. The Act also listed a select number of New Zealand statutes that, suitably modified, were to apply in the Cook Islands.

18. On independence in 1965, the Constitution conferred full law-making powers on the Cook Islands, but also provided that existing law was to continue to apply. Hence, the Cook Islands Act 1915 remained in effect, as did common law and those New Zealand enactments specified in the Cook Islands Act, such as the Partnership Act 1908 and the Trustee Act 1956. Since 1965, many provisions of the Cook Islands Act have been progressively repealed as the Cook Islands has developed its own statute law. For instance, company law was reformed to streamline and customise for a jurisdiction the size of the Cook Islands.

19. The Legislature (the Parliament of the Cook Islands) is unicameral and consists of 24 Members elected every four years. The Executive initiates and administers the law by deciding policy, drafting Bills and administering Acts. It is exercised on behalf of the King by the King's Representative who, in turn, appoints a Cabinet comprising the Prime Minister and Ministers.

20. The Cook Islands Constitution Act 1964 is the supreme law of the Cook Islands. The hierarchy of the laws is, in decreasing order of rank: (i) the Constitution, (ii) legislation enacted by Parliament, (iii) subsidiary legislation, (iv) common law in accordance with section 615 of the Cook Islands Act 1915 and as declared by the Courts from time to time, and (v) Cook Islands custom in relation to customary land, titles and succession. Under section 68 of the Cook Islands Constitution, taxation may only be imposed by law (that is, by or under an Act of Parliament). Once an EOI agreement has entered into force, its provisions have effect “according to their tenor”, i.e. meaning and content, and prevail over any enactments (Income Tax Act, s. 86(1)).

21. The judiciary consists of the High Court and a Court of Appeal. Appeals from the Court of Appeal may be made to the Judicial Committee of the (British) Privy Council. The High Court has Civil, Criminal and Land Divisions. Prosecutions for tax offences are heard at first instance by the High Court.

## Tax system

22. Taxes in the Cook Islands are all levied at a national level. They consist of an income tax, a value added tax (VAT $^{5}$ ), customs duties, import levies and departure tax, all of which are administered by the Revenue Management Division of the Ministry of Finance and Economic Management. The Cook Islands generally does not tax capital gains. However, some capital gains may be taxed under ordinary income tax rules (such as income from property purchased for the purpose of resale). There are no export incentives or investment holidays in the Cook Islands tax system. Tax credits are allowed for foreign taxes paid (Income Tax Act, s. 85) and charitable donations (Income Tax Act, s. 70).

23. All income tax is imposed under the Income Tax Act 1997. The rules for determining taxable income are generally the same for both individuals and companies. Residents are taxed on their worldwide income, but non-residents are taxable only on their Cook Islands sourced income (Income Tax Act, s. 80). An individual is resident in the Cook Islands for tax purposes if the individual's home is in the Cook Islands and the person is personally present in the Cook Islands for 183 days in a 12-month period (Income Tax Act, s. 82).

24. In 2019, an amendment was made to the International Companies Act 1981-82 which removed the tax exemption for international companies, beginning 1 January 2022. The company residence rules in the Income Tax Act were also amended so that a company is resident in the Cook Islands for tax purposes if: a) the directors exercise control of the company from within the Cook Islands, even if the directors' decision-making also occurs outside the Cook Islands; $^{6}$ or b) the place of effective management is in the Cook Islands; or c) the company is a Cook Islands company and, at any moment in time during the income year, three or more of its directors are resident in the Cook Islands. In short, a company (including an international company) is now only a taxpayer if there is an economic or management nexus with the Cook Islands or it has Cook Islands source income. Progressive tax rates are applied for individuals: 0% to 30% for residents and non-residents (Income Tax Act, First Schedule). The company tax rate is 20% for resident companies and 28% for non-resident companies. Partnerships are treated as transparent for tax purposes (Income Tax Act, s. 11). Trustees are taxed on trust income at 30% (Income

Tax Act, First Schedule). Foundations, international partnerships, international trusts and limited liability companies are exempt from Cook Islands income tax.

25. Final withholding tax at 15% rate is generally imposed on certain income (dividends, interest and royalties) derived from the Cook Islands by a non-resident (Income Tax Act, s 100). Otherwise, the requirements for non-residents to file tax returns are the same as for residents. Withholding tax applies for a resident only if banking interest is paid to the resident and they have not registered their Revenue Management Division (RMD) number with the bank. The RMD number is a unique number issued to all taxpayers (whether individual or non-individual). The number applies for VAT as well as income tax purposes.

## Domestic and international financial services sector

26. The Cook Islands has no Central Bank, and it uses the New Zealand dollar as its official currency. $^{7}$

27. The Cook Islands' financial sector is divided into two parts: domestic and international. The financial sector is relatively small compared to international standards.

28. There are three domestically oriented commercial banks (one of which is a government-owned bank), one private investment bank with an international client orientation, one insurer, four approved external insurers, six insurance intermediaries (agents or brokers), $^{8}$ six captive insurance companies, three Money or Value Transfer Services providers (which provide money changing and remittance services), seven trustee companies and one national superannuation fund. They are all licensed and supervised by the Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) except for the national superannuation (pension) fund which is supervised by a Board established under the Cook Islands National Superannuation Fund Act 2000.

29. The primary business of the three commercial banks is retail services for the domestic market, including deposit taking, savings and lending services. While three banks hold an international banking licence, since mid-2014 only the private investment bank utilises its international banking licence to service international clients and operate in the international sector. It provides custodian and asset management services for international clients. The other participants in the international finance sector are the trustee companies which provide trust and corporate services to international clients and captive insurance companies which provide insurance products to their own parent or related companies. Total assets held by banks is approximately NZD 1 920 million (EUR 968 million) as per 31 December 2025.

30. Trustee companies are licensed and supervised by the FSC in accordance with the Trustee Companies Act 2014. Trustee companies must be licensed to provide services relating to foundations, international companies, international trusts, international partnerships, and limited liability companies. The primary market for trustee companies (90%) remains asset protection trusts for high-net-worth clients from the United States.

31. Money changing (forex) and remittance businesses is a small sector in the Cook Islands with five licensees, most cross border transactions being conducted through the formal banking sector.

32. Captive insurers and insurance managers are licensed and supervised by the FSC under the Captive Insurance Act 2013. Captive insurers can be either foreign companies or domestic companies. There are 24 licensed captive insurers with the majority based in New Zealand. $^{9}$

33. Cook Islands practising lawyers are administered by the Cook Islands Law Society pursuant to the Law Practitioners Act 1993/94. They serve mainly the domestic market for legal advice and representation in land, commercial and criminal matters. A number of lawyers work in the trust and corporate service providers; however, they are captured as employees of a licensed financial entity and are not treated separately. There are currently no entry requirements for accountants. They provide most accountancy services to local businesses, and some provide auditing services to licensed financial institutions.

## Anti-Money Laundering framework

34. The primary legislation for anti-money laundering and countering the financing of terrorism (AML/CFT) in the Cook Islands is the Financial Transactions Reporting Act (FTRA) 2017 and regulations along with the Financial Intelligence Unit Act 2015 (FIUA 2015). The FIU is an administrative part of the FSC; it is operationally independent and is responsible for the administration and enforcement of the FTRA 2017.


36. On transparency of legal persons and arrangements, it was concluded that competent authorities are able to obtain access to basic and beneficial ownership information on almost all legal persons and arrangements in a timely manner through customer due diligence (CDD) collected by reporting institutions. However, understanding of beneficial ownership varied across competent authorities and reporting institutions (Recommendations 24 and 25).

## Recent developments

37. The Cook Islands amended the Companies Act 2017 and the Financial Transactions Reporting Act and its Regulations in December 2024 to clarify the definition of ultimate principal (beneficial owner) and the requirements to identify such persons applicable in each law. The Banking Act was also amended at the same time to provide for record retention by specified persons in the event that a licensed bank is wound up. These developments are analysed in this report.

38. Sections A.1, A.2 and A.3 evaluate the availability of ownership and identity information for relevant entities and arrangements, the availability of accounting information and the availability of banking information.

## A.1 Legal and beneficial ownership and identity information

Jurisdictions should ensure that legal and beneficial ownership and identity information for all relevant entities and arrangements is available to their competent authorities.

39. The 2015 Report had found that information on the legal ownership of legal entities and arrangements in the Cook Islands was generally available. There were no recommendations issued on this aspect.

40. Not discussed in the 2015 Report, but now an integral part of the standard as strengthened in 2016, is the availability of beneficial ownership information on all relevant entities and arrangements. In 2017, the Cook Islands introduced requirements on domestic companies to maintain records of their beneficial ownership. Domestic companies are generally not required to provide this information to the Registrar of Companies, and therefore there is no register of this information maintained by the Registrar. At the same time, the AML law was also amended to include requirements on relevant persons to maintain beneficial ownership information of clients, and this obligation applies to certain persons providing services to international companies, limited liability companies, international and limited partnerships, and international trusts. The AML framework is the only comprehensive source of beneficial ownership information on these international entities and arrangements. They are always required to have a relationship with an AML obliged person. Until 4 December 2024 some definitional gaps existed in both company law and AML law that could have caused the availability of beneficial ownership for companies to fall short of the standard, however amendments in effect from that date have fully resolved those issues. The same amendments to AML law have also improved the availability of beneficial ownership information for domestic partnerships, domestic trusts and foreign trusts with a resident trustee, however there are no requirements that would cause these legal arrangements to always have a relationship with an AML-obliged person. Tax law requirements will only partially mitigate the gap. Additionally, there is no specified frequency for updating the CDD information (including beneficial ownership information) by AML-obliged persons when no event triggers an update. Therefore, beneficial ownership information of relevant legal entities and arrangements kept by AML-obliged persons may not always be adequate, accurate and up to date.

41. This report also analyses the ways in which a company ceases to exist. International companies may be restored after being struck off without limit of time, and there is no explicit obligation to maintain and provide ownership information for relevant prior years in line with the standard.

42. Finally, nominee shareholding is allowed in the Cook Islands, but the Companies Act does not set any specific obligations on nominees or nominators to provide information to the authorities or the company in which they have an interest. The tax and anti-money laundering requirements do not ensure that information on nominees would be available.

43. With respect to exchange of information in practice, the requests that the Cook Islands receive are consistently in connection with the international sector and are generally recognisable as such on receipt. It has been able to respond to these requests seeking legal and beneficial ownership and identity information. There were no requests made or identified as being in connection with the domestic sector during the review period. If this were to happen, the Cook Islands would rely on filed tax returns for identity and ownership information for partnerships and trusts that are not created under the relevant international partnership and trust laws of the Cook Islands. Alternatively, it may rely on availability with AML-obliged persons (specifically banks) to the extent that the relevant legal arrangement in question has a relationship with an AML-obliged person, which is not required in all cases. Company tax returns may be used as a source of information on domestic companies that have ceased to exist. However, while requests for information on these entities and legal arrangements have not been received in recent years, if ever, doubts arise on whether the information would be available in practice if requested as there is a lack of verification and enforcement activity to ensure compliance with each of the filing obligations, leading to low rates of filing in practice. In relation to beneficial ownership information, if this was to be requested in connection with the domestic sector, the Cook Islands may rely on company law obligations on companies to hold such information, but the lack of supervision of these obligations leaves a lack of assurance that it would be available in practice. The Cook Islands could also resort to the AML framework as a source of information for companies and would solely rely on the AML framework in the case of legal arrangements, but a relationship with an AML-obliged person is not required in all cases.

## 44. The Cook Islands is recommended to remedy these gaps.

45. During the review period, the Cook Islands successfully answered seven requests for legal and beneficial ownership information, all of which were in relation to international companies and international trusts.

## A.1.1 Availability of legal and beneficial ownership information for companies

## Types of Companies

47. There are three types of companies which may be formed in the Cook Islands: (i) domestic companies; (ii) international companies, and (iii) limited liability companies.

48. Domestic companies are essentially companies which trade locally or that have local shareholders. They must be incorporated under the Companies Act (CA). A company must have one or more shares; one or more shareholders, having limited or unlimited liability for the obligations of the company; and one or more directors, of whom at least one must (i) live in the Cook Islands or (ii) live in New Zealand and be a director of a company that is registered (except as the equivalent of a foreign company) in New Zealand (CA 2017, s. 6).

49. International companies may be incorporated for any lawful purpose under the International Companies Act 1981-82 (ICA). Natural persons who are residents or citizens of, or domiciled in, the Cook Islands are prohibited from holding a beneficial interest in an international company. Section 13(3) of the ICA provides for the incorporation of the following types of international company:

• company limited by shares

• no-liability company $^{11}$

• company limited by guarantee

• company limited by both shares and by guarantee

• unlimited company or

\- mutual company.

50. Limited liability companies may be incorporated for any lawful purpose under the Limited Liability Companies Act 2008 (LLCA, s. 6). A limited liability company can only be formed through a trustee company acting on behalf of persons who do not reside in the Cook Islands. The person or persons on whose behalf the trustee company acts need not be members of the limited liability company after the company is formed (LLCA, s. 11). The trustee company is always recorded as an initial member at the time of formation (alone or together with other members). The membership is then transferred to the client or a representative of the client. The client may choose to keep the trustee company as a nominee member, similar to a nominee shareholder. This form of corporate vehicle offers simpler provisions for determining rights between members, which are allocated a share of company profits, losses or distributions on the basis of the value of their contributions. The limited liability company legislation also contains asset protection features. The Cook Islands authorities explain that limited liability companies are typically set up to limit the ability of creditors to access the assets held within the structures.

51. In this report, a foreign company means a company incorporated outside of the Cook Islands. If a foreign company (also defined by the CA 2017 as an “overseas company”) is resident in the Cook Islands for tax purposes by virtue of its centre of management and control, it is subject to the general return filing requirement for resident companies. Furthermore, foreign companies deriving income from the Cook Islands (whether resident there or elsewhere) are assessable for income tax in the Cook Islands (Income Tax Act 1997, ss. 80(2) and 83).

## Company law requirements on domestic companies

54. Domestic companies are required to maintain various records including the company's certificate of incorporation, certificate of re-registration (in case a company was registered prior the Companies Act 2017 implementation), the constitution of the company, the share register, the full names and residential and postal addresses of the current directors, and details of the company's registered office and postal address (CA 2017, s. 144). The share register must be maintained for seven years (see para. 56). The minutes of all meetings and resolutions of shareholders and directors, copies of all written communications to all shareholders, copies of all financial statements, accounting records and other documentation occurring or relevant to the last seven years must also be kept (CA 2017, s. 144).

55. A company must keep the company records at (i) its registered office in the Cook Islands $^{13}$ or (ii) another place in the Cook Islands, provided the company has filed with the Registrar a notice of the location of the records within 10 working days after the records are first kept there (CA, s. 145). It is an offence if company records (including the share register) are not kept in the Cook Islands (CA 2017, s. 145).

56. The entry of the name of a person in the share register as holder of a share is evidence that legal title to the share vests in that person (CA 2017, s. 50). The share register must include (i) the names and the last known address of each person, who is, or has within the last seven years been, a shareholder; (ii) the number of shares of each class held by each shareholder within the last seven years; (iii) the date of each transaction (the issue of shares, the repurchase or redemption of shares from a shareholder, the transfer of shares) within the last seven years; (iv) in relation to the transfer of shares by or to a shareholder, the name of the person to or from whom the shares were transferred (CA 2017, s. 49). The share register may be maintained by an agent on behalf of the company (CA 2017, s. 49). Although there is no explicit requirement for the agent to be in the Cook Islands, the registered office of the agent must be in the Cook Islands (CA 2017, s. 142(1)).

57. Any person may apply for the registration of a company under the Companies Act (CA 2017, s. 7). To file an application to incorporate a new company, first a person must have a client account with the Cook Islands Companies and Incorporated Societies Registry. $^{14}$ Then, the person can complete an online application to incorporate a company or to register a foreign company. The forms have tabs for the types of information (company name and contact details, the names of company's directors and shareholders, company constitution, primary business activity, etc. $^{15}$ ). Upon review and approval of the application, the

Registrar issues a Certificate of Incorporation stating that all the requirements under the Companies Act have been complied with and, from the date of incorporation stated on the certificate, the company is incorporated under the Companies Act (CA 2017, s. 9).

58. Companies must also disclose ownership information to the Registrar of Companies upon registration and then on an annual basis in an annual return. The annual return requires, in respect of each shareholder, their name, gender, nationality, date of birth, residential address and the number of shares held. There is no requirement to inform the Registrar of any changes in these details between the filing of annual returns, although the company may choose to do so through the online facility. The Registrar is part of the Ministry of Justice (MoJ).

## Company law requirements on international companies

59. All international companies created in accordance with the ICA are registered with the Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC).

60. Registrations of international companies with the FSC must be conducted through a trustee company $^{16}$ (ICA, s. 9). An international company must have a resident secretary, who is responsible for compliance by the company with lodging all documents required to be lodged with the FSC and the maintenance of the company's records at the registered office of the company. The secretary must be a trustee company or an officer of a trustee company. The international company must keep at its registered office a register of its directors, resident directors and secretaries (ICA, s. 91). The registered office of the international company must be the principal office of the trustee company acting as secretary (ICA, s. 81). The international company must, within one month of incorporation, file details of the directors, resident directors and secretaries with the Registrar. If any person ceases or commences to hold any of these roles, the international company must file a notification to inform the Registrar of the updated details.

61. International companies are required to record their members in a register to be kept at the registered office of the company in the Cook Islands (ICA, s. 106). Only the name and address of the members, the number of shares held, and the date acquired or allotted must be recorded. These details must be held for seven years after ceasing to be a member (ICA, s. 105).

62. As explained in paragraphs 76 to 83, trustee companies have AML obligations under the FTRA. In practice, the trustee company's obligations under the FTRA to ensure that its CDD is up to date and appropriate, and the client international company's obligation to maintain a register of members with a trustee company fulfilling these obligations for it, are self-reinforcing and, in some respects the AML requirements add to the requirements of the ICA. For example, CDD obligations require collection of additional identity information and documents on beneficial owners of the company (see paragraph 142). A common structure in practice is that all shares in an international company are held by the trustee of an international trust that is also serviced by the same trustee company, so that CDD requirements in relation to the international trust would cover the shareholder in the international company. Alternatively, the structure commonly involves one or two shareholders that are either an individual who is an ultimate principal or a corporate entity to which ultimate principal(s) are related, and the CDD requirements under the FTRA will also be applied. The FSC has not identified any instances in its inspections where a shareholder of an international company would not be subject to this CDD, so that the additional identity and documentation required under the FTRA is always held in practice.

63. The FSC is the Registrar under the ICA and also supervises compliance with the FTRA. Consequently, the verification and enforcement framework for the FTRA over trustee companies assists in ensuring the compliance of international companies with the ICA.

## Company law requirements on limited liability companies

64. Limited liability companies are required to keep at their registered office (which is the office of the trustee company acting as registered agent, LLCA, ss. 2 and 18) a current list of the full name and business, residence or mailing address of each member and manager, including copies of the articles of association and operating agreement (LLCA, s. 32). These records must be maintained by the trustee company for six years after dissolution of the limited liability company.

65. In addition, these entities must be established through a Cook Islands trustee company, which is subject to comprehensive FTRA obligations, including customer due diligence requirements.

66. All limited liability companies are registered with the Registrar of Limited Liability Companies (LLCA, s. 3), which is part of the FSC. Limited liability companies must file with the Registrar articles of association which include: (i) the name of the limited liability company; (ii) the name and business address of the registered agent; (iii) the period of the duration of the limited liability company, which may be perpetual (LLCA, s. 12). No identification of members or owners is required as part of the registration process, or when the company files its annual report (LLCA, ss. 11(1) and 20).

67. As explained in paragraphs 76 to 83, trustee companies have AML obligations under the FTRA. In practice, the trustee company's obligations under the FTRA to ensure that its CDD information is up to date, appropriate and retained for six years will assist in ensuring that a client limited liability company's records of members is available and is kept up to date. Consequently, the verification and enforcement framework for the FTRA over trustee companies also assists in ensuring the compliance of limited liability companies with the LLCA. For example, CDD obligations require collection of additional identity information and documents on beneficial owners of the company (see paragraph 142). According to the FSC, the same observations described in paragraph 63 on structures for international companies apply to limited liability companies and it has not identified any instances in its inspections where a shareholder of a limited liability company would not be subject to CDD under the FTRA, so that the additional identity and documentation required under the FTRA is always held in practice.

## Company law requirements on foreign companies

68. Foreign companies (described as overseas companies in the Companies Act) carrying on business in the Cook Islands are required, within 20 working days of commencing business in Cook Islands, to provide the Registrar of Companies with a certified copy of the instrument constituting or defining the constitution of the company (CA 2017, s. 358). There is no legal requirement for the registration of a foreign company through a service provider or a legal practitioner. Therefore, in such instances, the availability of ownership information will depend on the law of the jurisdiction in which the company is incorporated (but see tax law requirements below). Nevertheless, upon registration, they must disclose the full name and address of each director, as well as of one or more persons in the Cook Islands authorised to accept service of documents on behalf of the company (CA 2017, s. 364). The Registrar of Companies is to be notified of any change in the directors of the company (name, residential address or postal address). In the case of change of director, the notice must be filed within 20 days of the appointment or resignation. In the case of death of a director or change in name or address, it must be filed within 20 days of the company first becoming aware of the change. Sanctions apply for non-compliance (a late filing fee to the Registrar and a fine not exceeding NZD 4 000 (EUR 2 016) (CA 2017, s. 364)).

69. Under section 18 of the Development Investment Act, any foreign enterprise (whether or not a company) with more than one third foreign ownership and wishing to invest in or carry on a business in the

Cook Islands must register with the Business Trade and Investment Board. Carrying on a business does not include the passive holding of assets such as real estate. The information to be filed on registration, and annually thereafter, includes detailed ownership information, such as the names, addresses and passport numbers of all legal owners of the shares (Development Investment Act, s. 34 and Regulation 3(d) of the Development Investment Regulations 1996) and the beneficial owners of the shares. The same details are required in the annual return. These filing requirements are independent of any requirements under tax law, so that tax resident and non-resident companies are required to file annual returns with the BTIB containing legal ownership information. There were nine foreign companies registered with the Registrar of Companies as of 31 March 2025 and all nine were also registered with the Business Trade and Investment Board (as they were all more than one third foreign owned).

## Tax law requirements

70. There are no direct registration requirements for companies to register with the RMD. The Income Tax Act requires that every taxpayer has an RMD number (tax identification number, s. 218), which may be seen as fulfilling the function of a registration system. The wording of the requirement is unclear on whether each taxpayer must apply for such a number or only that the Collector (the head of the RMD) must issue a number to each taxpayer. In general, every person deriving assessable income in the Cook Islands must file an annual tax return, so that the end result is that such persons are required to have an RMD number before or through filing a first return. Not having an RMD number may be an impediment to conducting some activities, such as importing goods to the Cook Islands (as the RMD is also the customs authority), but other than this and when filing a tax return, no comprehensive compulsion has been identified to apply for an RMD number. No penalty has been identified in the Income Tax Act that would be available in the event that a taxpayer fails to apply for an RMD number, but there are penalties for failing to file a tax return (see paragraph 100).

71. The RMD has two forms available for taxpayers that can result in the issuance of an RMD number, one for individuals not in business, which is clearly titled as an application for an RMD number. The second form is for businesses and is more generally titled as a business application to be used by companies and other persons or arrangements. The form can serve multiple purposes, including registering for VAT and as an employer for employment related tax obligations. The term "business" is not defined on this application form but is taken to mean deriving any income for which a tax return would ultimately need to be filed (i.e. deriving assessable income, including passive income). In the case of a company, the business form requires a copy of the certificate of incorporation. Instructions require that all directors be identified, but shareholders and executive office holders need only be identified if the company wishes such persons to be additional contacts for the company. Each director and, if relevant, shareholder and executive office holder listed must be verified with identity documents being either a passport, a Cook Islands driver's licence or a birth certificate in the case of individuals, or a certificate of incorporation or trust deed in the case of companies and trusts, respectively. There are 1847 domestic companies registered with the RMD as of December 2025 (see paragraph 101 on the difficulty in reconciling this to registrations under company law). With respect to international companies, 256 have registered with the RMD as on 18 February 2026, out of 413 registered with the Registrar of the Financial Supervisory Commission. The discrepancy has not been explained by the RMD, other than to indicate that its registrations would include some companies automatically struck off under the clean-up process following enactment of the CA 2017 (see paragraph 96).

72. In practice, the legal ownership information is often collected by the RMD as part of the application for an RMD number; with supporting evidence, as most companies are closely held and the directors and office holders will also be shareholders. However, it is not ensured. The prescribed company tax return nominally requires the shareholders to be listed with names and addresses and an RMD number if available so that shareholders may be identified at the filing of the first tax return, however the RMD advises that return processing procedures do not compel the provision of the RMD number for shareholders and it is therefore not provided in all cases.

73. Limited liability companies are not subject to Cook Islands taxation and are therefore under no requirement to register or furnish returns of income (LLCA, s. 76(1)). International companies are subject to Cook Islands taxation if tax resident or deriving income with a Cook Islands source. In practice, this is rarely the case. International companies are subject to withholding tax on dividends and interest paid to non-residents, subject to tax treaties. They are also required to register with the RMD and file tax returns.

74. If a foreign company is resident in the Cook Islands for tax purposes by virtue of its management and control, it is subject to the same general return filing requirement for resident companies including providing in the annual tax return the full name, address and RMD number of each shareholder. On 15 May 2026, there were no foreign companies registered with the RMD that were considered to be resident. In addition, all foreign companies deriving income from the Cook Islands (whether resident there or elsewhere) are assessable for income tax in the Cook Islands (Income Tax Act 1997, ss. 80(2), and 83). Such foreign companies are also required to furnish an annual return of income including the full name, address and RMD number of each shareholder (Income Tax Act, ss. 2 and 8).

75. There is no explicit obligation on the RMD to retain these records for a specified period. It has for some time considered the six-year period in the Public Records Act as strong guidance, if not directly applicable. The time bar limit on standard tax assessments of four years for non-criminal matters and ten years for criminal matters would also be a consideration. However, in practice the RMD keeps all paper records for at least 20 years and electronic records indefinitely.

## Obligations under anti-money laundering legislation

76. The AML framework is primarily provided by the FTRA 2017 and the regulations made under that Act. Section 25 of the FTRA requires reporting institutions to establish, maintain and operate procedures to ensure that they conduct customer due diligence (CDD) before entering into an ongoing business relationship or an isolated transaction with or on behalf of a customer.

77. A reporting institution is defined (in FTRA 2017, s. 5) to mean:

• a licensed financial institution

\- a person who, in the course of carrying on business, enters into transactions with or on behalf of customers in respect of one or more specified activity

• any other person that may be prescribed. $^{17}$

78. The FTRA Regulations prescribe a range of specified activities that will cause a person to be a reporting institution. It includes acting as a trust and company service provider and therefore brings into scope all seven licensed trustee companies mentioned in paragraphs 61 to 66.

79. Reporting institutions are required to have an AML/CFT compliance programme overseen by a Money Laundering Reporting Officer. It needs to set out in detail how the reporting institution complies with the FTRA 2017, and internal policies relating to auditing of their AML/CFT compliance programme, vetting of new staff and training staff for their AML/CFT compliance programme.

80. Reporting institutions are required to undertake risk assessments for their business as a whole, and for each of their customers prior to establishing an ongoing relationship, and a technology risk assessment when implementing new technology in relation to their operations. In addition, reporting institutions are required to have procedures in place to undertake customer due diligence prior to establishing a relationship or conducting a one-off transaction.

81. When dealing with a customer that is not a natural person, reporting institutions must verify the legal status of the customer, using relevant information obtained from a reliable independent source, and obtain sufficient information to understand the nature of the customer's business and its ownership and control structure. Reporting institutions must carry out ongoing and effective monitoring, including review of information held for the purpose of CDD to ensure that it is up to date and appropriate (FTRA 2017, s. 32).

82. The AML framework covers all international companies and limited liability companies as a result of the requirement for every such company to have a relationship with a reporting institution, specifically a trustee company. As explained in paragraphs 62 and 67, the AML framework and the company law requirements governing international companies and limited liability companies have overlapping requirements so that the companies are required to maintain ownership information with a trustee company and the trustee company is required to hold that information. However, in the case of domestic companies there is no requirement to have a relationship with an AML-obliged person in all cases. A domestic company may have an account with a Cook Islands bank but is not required to.


## Companies that cease to exist

84. A domestic company ceases to exist when removed from the Cook Islands register. A domestic company in the Cook Islands may be removed from the register due to: (i) liquidation; (ii) request of a shareholder or another authorised person if the company has ceased business; $^{18}$ or (iii) failing to file its annual return or to meet other specified requirements. Liquidation of a domestic company begins with the appointment of a liquidator (CA, s. 202). The Companies Act details how liquidation must proceed and the periodic filings that a liquidator must make with the court (CA, ss. 200 to 295). The liquidator must prepare an initial report soon after appointment and subsequently prepare six-month reports advising interested parties of the progress of the liquidation and file these with the Registrar.

85. A company can be automatically removed from the register for failure to file an annual return (CA 2017, s. 338). No one may object to this removal. Any third party seeking to know the status of a company can see if it is registered or struck off. There is no intermediary status as “inactive”. The Registrar did not strike off any companies during the review period, despite substantial numbers of companies failing to file annual returns – see paragraph 97.

86. The company's historical ownership information filed through the online Registry is kept indefinitely, whatever the reason that led to formal removal of the company from the register.

87. A domestic company may be restored to the register if it was removed therefrom for failing to file an annual return. In case of removal for a failure to file an annual return, the application for restoration must be filed with the Registrar within two years after its removal, together with overdue annual returns and all pending and late filing fees (CA, s. 350). Thus, the latest ownership information is available with the Registrar within the two-year period after the company's removal from the register.

88. If the removal was for a reason other than the failure to file an annual return, or if it has been more than two years since a removal for failure to file an annual return, then the party seeking restoration must seek a court order (CA 2017, s. 351). The court can order to restore the company to the register for any reason that is just and equitable (e.g. at the time of removal the company was still carrying on business, there are other reasons for it to continue its existence, the company was a party to a legal procedure) (CA 2017, ss. 350 to 351). The court may make restoration conditional on compliance with any provisions of the Companies Act or of regulations made under the Companies Act if the company had failed to comply with those provisions before it was removed from the register (CA 2017, s. 351 (3)). In these cases, the filing of outstanding returns is not prescribed by law, although it is open to the court to require this. A company is restored to the register when the Registrar receives a notice stating that the company is restored to the register. The restored company is treated as if it has not been removed from the register and as having continued in existence (CA 2017, s. 352). In practice, no applications for restoration have been made and so there are no cases from which court practices can be observed.

89. An international company may be struck off the register at the request of the company. It may also be struck off if the company or an officer of the company informs the Registrar that it does not intend to renew its annual certificate of incorporation. Alternatively, the Registrar may, of their own motion and following a procedure that requires at least two months' notice, strike a company off if the Registrar has reason to believe that the company does not hold a current certificate of incorporation. An international company that has been struck off no longer has legal personality. When an international company is struck off the register, any remaining assets vest with the Registrar. Where an international company is struck off, the resident secretary must ensure that the books and papers of the company – including its register of members – are retained by a trustee company for a period of six years from the striking off (ICA, s. 197(6)).

90. An international company may be restored to the register if the Registrar is satisfied that no person will be prejudiced and that due cause has been shown (ICA, s. 197(3)). The restored international company is deemed to have continued in existence as if its name had not been struck off, and unless the Registrar at the time of restoration orders to the contrary, the international company and all other persons are deemed to be in the same position as nearly as may be as if the international company had not been struck off (ICA, s. 197(3A)).

91. There is no time limit for the restoration of an international company after being struck off the register by the Registrar. In the case of struck off companies, there is a risk that an adequate retention of ownership information will not be ensured as there is no explicit obligation to maintain and provide ownership information at that time. Since the retention period after strike-off is six years, at which time the Act allows the resident secretary to destroy the records, any reinstatement after that date does not provide for the availability of ownership information for years prior to restoration in line with the standard. Therefore, the Cook Islands is recommended to ensure that ownership information is available for restored international companies in line with the standard.

92. During the review period, there were 32 international companies that had been deregistered and were restored to the register through this process. In all cases but one, the period of deregistration was less than one year. In the one case that was more than one year the period of deregistration was five years and seven months, thus within the retention period applicable to the trustee company. A gap nonetheless remains in the legal framework.

93. A limited liability company can be dissolved upon the occurrence of any of the following events: (i) by the unanimous written agreement of all members and managers, (ii) at the time or upon the occurrence of events specified in writing in its articles of organisation or operating agreement (LLCA, s. 49), or (iii) automatically by operation of law, one year after failing to pay fees or file appropriate reports. Irrespective of the cause for dissolution, all records of the limited liability company must be kept at the registered office (the office of the last registered agent in the Cook Islands) for six years following the dissolution (LLCA, s. 32). There are no legal provisions in the Cook Islands for restoration of a dissolved limited liability company.

94. A foreign company can be revoked and removed from the register as a result of a successful prosecution of the company for breaching the law, failing to comply with the conditions of the Business Trade and Investment Board's (BTIB) approval, going into liquidation or bankruptcy or ceasing to do business in the Cook Islands. Deregistration also occurs where the foreign enterprise is no longer a foreign enterprise because the foreign investors have become Cook Islanders (granted Permanent Residence status) or Cook Islanders beneficially own and control more than two thirds of the enterprise (Development Investment Act, s. 26). A foreign company may apply or be required to be re-registered if it subsequently meets the conditions required to do so. The availability of legal ownership information for foreign companies that no longer have a presence in the Cook Islands relies on the tax returns filed with the RMD which retains these for at least 20 years, see paragraph 75.

## Legal ownership information – Enforcement measures and oversight

95. The authorities ensuring compliance with the requirements to keep legal ownership information for all domestic, international, limited liability and foreign companies are: i) the Registrar of Companies through the Ministry of Justice (MoJ), ii) the RMD, iii) the FSC and iv) the BTIB. The MoJ oversees the application of company law to domestic companies. The RMD is responsible for monitoring all companies for their tax obligations except for limited liability companies, which are not subject to taxation in the Cook Islands. The BTIB oversees foreign companies if more than one third owned or controlled by non-Cook Islanders. The FSC is responsible for the ongoing monitoring or supervision of international companies and limited liability companies, as well as supervising the reporting institutions with whom by law they must have a relationship (i.e. trustee companies).

## Domestic and foreign companies

96. After the implementation of the Companies Act in 2017, all previously registered domestic and foreign companies were obliged to re-register latest by 9 December 2020. The re-registration process required the submission of the same data elements as would be required for a new company formed under the new law. In this way, all active existing companies had both the opportunity and the requirement to provide legal ownership information into the registry that was up to date and in compliance with the new law. At the time the deadline passed, 887 companies had re-registered. This deadline was extended to 31 March 2022, by an amendment to the Act in November 2021 because the COVID-19 pandemic slowed down registrations; this allowed an additional 176 companies to re-register. This process also had the positive effect of aging-off companies that were no longer active as failure to re-register resulted in the company being struck off the registry, although the number that were struck off as a consequence is not available. $^{19}$ No further extension was provided and no companies deregistered in this process have been restored to the register.

97. On 31 March 2025, there were 1 613 domestic companies registered. However, the number of companies filing annual returns (which is a requirement for all domestic companies) has declined from 832 in 2022, to 604 in 2024. This raises the possibility that economically inactive companies may again be accumulating on the register. The MoJ states that all registered companies are presumed to be active, although no objective basis for this presumption has been provided and it may simply reflect an absence of knowledge to the contrary. There is no formal status of “inactive” in the Companies Act. The Registrar has the power to strike off companies for failing to file annual returns (see paragraph 85) but did not use this power during the review period. No routine inspections or audits were conducted during the review period. Overall, the only verification and enforcement activities stated to have been carried out by the MoJ are some reviews of annual returns when filed, with between 5% and 15% of returns rejected for incomplete information. It appears that at least some of these rejections are automatic validations on whether fields have been completed rather than an evaluation of the content of the returns. Penalties have been applied for late filing, but no statistics have been provided on the total cases or amounts, or whether any of the penalties were not simply automatically imposed when companies voluntarily filed, without prompting action from the MoJ.

98. Domestic companies are required to keep a register of their shareholders (CA, s. 49, see paragraph 56). A company that fails to comply with this requirement commits an offence and is liable on conviction to a fine not exceeding NZD 4 000 (EUR 2 016) (CA 2017, s. 49(4)). However, during the review period, the Registrar of Companies carried out little to no activities relevant to ensuring compliance by domestic companies with this obligation.

99. Nine foreign companies were registered with the Registrar of Companies as of 31 March 2025. Four of these are banks that have been operating in the Cook Islands for some time. The balance of foreign companies tends to have a relatively short-term presence in the Cook Islands, being for various projects that come to a conclusion in due course.

100. In practice, there is reliance on the RMD to ensure the availability of legal ownership information on domestic companies and foreign companies through initial registration and then subsequently through the information filed in annual tax returns, albeit that this information may not reflect changes that may occur in the period between filing of tax returns. Under tax law, a person who fails to file a tax return is liable to a fine not exceeding NZD 10 000 (EUR 5 040) and not less than NZD 500 (EUR 252) (ITA, s. 206). Wilfully or negligently providing false information may on conviction be subject to imprisonment for a term not exceeding 2 years or a fine not exceeding NZD 100 000 (EUR 50 400), or both.

101. The RMD has 1 847 companies registered for tax purposes and this is not reconcilable to those registered with the Registrar of Companies (1 613, see paragraph 51) even after accounting for timing differences. The RMD is aware that many of the registrations are likely to include companies that have been struck off or are now economically inactive, although it is unable to categorise these in any detail. Only 407 of the companies registered with the RMD (22%) filed an income tax return for the most recent tax year (2024). While the RMD advises that it carries out a range of compliance activities, it has not provided information on whether these activities relate to ensuring that tax returns are filed as required. The RMD notes that the imposition of sanctions for late filing currently can only be done following prosecution and conviction by the court, which is administratively burdensome and limits enforcement in practice. Precise statistics on audits of taxpayers that do file tax returns and the extent to which these audits verify information are not available, however the RMD advises that 397 tax related audits were conducted during the review period and based on a material sample of these cases, around a quarter would have reviewed the correctness of ownership information filed. The RMD has also stated that company tax returns are sometimes filed and accepted without the schedule of ownership information having been completed. While it notes that ownership information would instead be included in the financial statements, it is also not always the case that financial statements are filed with the tax return (see Element A.2). No penalties or other sanctions were imposed for any non-compliance.

102. No requests for ownership information were made during the review period relating to domestic or foreign companies. In the event that such a request is received, the source of information would be either the RMD's tax return records, the company itself if the company remains in existence, or the domestic company Registrar. However, the low filing rates of annual returns for both company law and tax law purposes causes uncertainty over whether registered companies remain active or would have current contact details available. There is a lack of verification and enforcement activity to ensure compliance with each of these filing obligations. In the case of filing of ownership information with the RMD, while some activity is carried out to ensure that this information is complete and accurate, there is a lack of enforcement when filings are incomplete. This casts doubt on whether the information held by the RMD can ensure the availability of legal ownership information for domestic companies and relevant foreign companies with nexus to the Cook Islands. The Cook Islands is recommended to implement effective oversight and enforcement measures to ensure the availability of legal ownership information for domestic and relevant foreign companies.

## International companies and limited liability companies

103. International companies are required to keep at their registered office a share register (ICA, ss. 105 and 106, see paragraph 61). Failure to maintain the share register in accordance with the legislative requirements is an offence and, on conviction, a fine of NZD 500 (EUR 252) will apply (ICA, ss. 105(3) and 219). A NZD 29 (EUR 15) penalty per month applies for a late filing of the annual renewal of an international company's registration, and a NZD 29 (EUR 15) penalty per month applies for a late notification to the Registrar of a change of registered agent or registered office (International Companies (Prescribed Fees) (Amendment) Regulations 2014).

104. Limited liability companies are also required to keep at their registered office a current list of members (LLCA, s. 32(1), see paragraph 64). Failure to comply with any legal requirement imposed by the LLCA is an offence and, on conviction, punishable with a fine not exceeding NZD 10 000 (EUR 5 040) or imprisonment for a term not exceeding one year, or to both (LLCA, s. 78). A NZD 37 (EUR 18) penalty per month applies for a late filing of the annual renewal of a LLCA's registration.

105. The FSC has the legal mandate for supervising compliance with requirements of the ICA and the LLCA. It also has the legal mandate for AML supervision, together with the Financial Intelligence Unit. In practice, and insofar as there is overlap between the obligations on international companies and limited liability companies on the one hand and trustee companies that service them on the other hand (see paragraphs 59 to 66), the compliance activities carried out by the FSC are intended to verify compliance on both sides. The FSC conducts desk-based reviews and onsite inspections of reporting institutions. Most relevantly, these include the seven licensed trustee companies and the four licensed banks. The primary focus of the inspections is on compliance with the FTRA, including that they have complied with their CDD obligations. In the case of inspections conducted on trustee companies, these also cover compliance by their clients and/or the trustee company's provision of services to those clients, for which all international companies and limited liability companies are ultimately in scope.

106. Prior to July 2022, the trustee companies were on an annual cycle of onsite inspections by the FSC, although some of these were carried out virtually in 2020 and 2021 due to the Covid-19 pandemic. From July 2022, the cycle moved to an onsite inspection every two years. During the review period, every trustee company was subject to this activity at least once, and most were reviewed twice.

107. The FSC has provided statistics for the onsite inspections carried out during the review period. On average 20-25 clients are selected for review. The base minimum file review always includes the CDD requirements, include obtaining and verifying identity information for both legal and beneficial owners of the customer, purpose of relationship, documentation of control and management (corporate documents, trust deeds, letters of wishes, application forms), source of wealth and validation of wealth sources. Services provided by a trustee company for a client may include services in connection with one or more relevant companies (or none). ## Nominees

110. Nominee shareholding is allowed in the Cook Islands but the Companies Act, ICA or LLCA do not set any specific obligations on nominees or nominators to inform the relevant company of the existence of the nominee arrangement. Prior to 4 December 2024, there were also no specific obligations on the companies themselves to obtain information on nominees holding interests in the company. That remains the case for the ICA and LLCA, however the Companies Act was amended with effect from 4 December 2024 to add an obligation on domestic companies to:

obtain and maintain sufficient information on any nominee arrangements including the beneficial owner of the nominator (s. 52(2) (aa))

111. As this obligation is not matched by any corresponding obligation on the nominees to inform the company of their status, availability of information with the company is not ensured.

112. Under the AML framework, trustee companies are obliged to conduct CDD on international companies and limited liability companies to identify, verify and maintain information on the customer and any person acting on behalf of a customer (FTRA, s. 25). Customer is defined to mean a person with whom the reporting institution enters into a transaction. Section 56 also imposes an obligation on any person dealing with a reporting institution to disclose their nominee or trustee status to the reporting institution (trustee companies are reporting institutions). A person or reporting institution that breaches section 56 commits an offence and is liable on conviction to a fine up to NZD 250 000 (EUR 126 000) or imprisonment up to five years, or both.

113. A trustee company that acts as a nominee in respect to a shareholding or as a member of the international company or limited liability company does so as part of their services to the client and therefore is in a position to know the nominator that they act for. In practice, a trustee company acting as a nominee is often only temporary at the initial registration of the company and the ownership is transferred to the intended owner soon after the creation of the company.

114. However, a third party could act as a nominee holding an interest in the international company or limited liability company on a more ongoing basis and in these circumstances the third party would not in all cases be under an obligation to disclose their status to the trustee company or the company in which they hold their interest. The third-party nominee shareholder or member of the company will only be required to disclose their status to the trustee company if that person is also directly “dealing” with the trustee company, which might not be the case. According to the FSC, the trustee company will usually know if a third-party nominee holds an interest in an international company or limited liability company as a result of being closely involved in advising the ultimate client and setting up the structure.

115. The ITA considers the nominee and nominator indistinctly. $^{20}$ There are requirements for international companies, domestic companies and foreign companies which are resident for tax purposes in the Cook Islands to furnish an annual return of income in the prescribed form, including the full names and addresses of shareholders and, if a shareholder holds their shares as a nominee, trustee or the shareholder is otherwise not the beneficial owner, the company must also provide the full name and address of the beneficial owners of the shares $^{21}$ (Income Tax Act, s. 8, company tax return RM 6). There are no further instructions in the form on how to comply with this. While completing the income tax return may cause the company to provide information on a nominator in respect of a shareholder that is known by the company to be a nominee, there is nothing in the Income Tax Act or other laws of the Cook Islands that would require a nominee to disclose their status as nominee to the company or to provide information on the nominator to the company. Availability of complete information with the RMD on nominee arrangements is therefore not ensured.

116. In summary, some information may be available for each type of company on nominees and their nominators through requirements in the Companies Act, the FTRA and tax law. However, there are no requirements that would comprehensively require nominees to disclose their status to a Cook Islands company and provide information to the company on their nominator, so it is not ensured that the information available will be complete. The Cook Islands is recommended to ensure that ownership and identity information is available in respect of nominee shareholdings.

117. As described in the 2015 Report, nominee arrangements are very rare for domestic companies, many of which are small and owned by a husband and wife, and this remains the case. In contrast, nominee arrangements are common in limited liability companies and international companies (see paragraphs 50 and 60).

Beneficial ownership information in the Cook Islands is collected primarily through the AML framework. ## Company law requirements

121. Prior to 4 December 2024, domestic companies were required to obtain and maintain sufficient information to identify the beneficial owner of each share issued by the company. Companies were not generally required to file this information with the Registrar, they were only required to disclose this information if required to do so in a written notice issued by the Registrar (CA 2017, s. 52(2)). While the Companies Act 2017 required all companies to reregister on a new online system by a deadline ultimately extended to 31 March 2022, this process did not require any information on beneficial owners to be added to the system.

122. Formerly, section 52(3) of the Companies Act defined a beneficial owner as:

the person who ultimately owns or controls the share

123. This definition and related obligation departed from the standard. The Cook Islands legislation did not require domestic companies to keep information about their beneficial ownership but about the beneficial ownership of each share.

124. While the definition of beneficial owner referred to “person”, it was not clear if that meant a natural person or could also be a legal person. There was also no default position where an individual in a senior managerial position would be identified. Individuals having either ultimate ownership or control of the share appeared to be covered; however, persons having ultimate control of the company through means other than ownership would not be identified. It was also not clear whether “ultimately” would be interpreted as meaning any person who controls the share acting directly or indirectly, or acting individually or jointly. The beneficial ownership information was therefore not required to be maintained in line with the standard under the company law requirements. $^{24}$

125. Most importantly, the requirement was not to obtain and maintain information on the identity of the beneficial owners, it was to obtain and maintain sufficient information to identify the beneficial owners. This falls short of actually identifying the beneficial owners and, even if there might be sufficient information assembled under which it could be done if required, the adequacy of the assembled information is not tested by doing so. Furthermore, there was no specification of identity information on the beneficial owners that should be covered by this – such as name, address, date of birth, etc.

126. The Companies Act was amended with effect from 4 December 2024, so that the requirement is now to obtain and maintain sufficient information to identify the beneficial owners of the company. While the amendment improved the definition of beneficial owners as further discussed below, it did not remove the concern over only requiring sufficient information to identify the beneficial owners instead of clearly requiring that there is a process carried out to actually identify them. It also remains the case that there is no specification of the identity information on the beneficial owners that should be available (for example, name, address, date of birth, other details). These gaps are discussed further at paragraph 136).

127. The definition of beneficial owner was also amended to have the same meaning as “ultimate principal” provided by the FTRA. At the same time, the definition of ultimate principal in the FTRA was amended so that beneficial owner (ultimate principal) now means, for both the Companies Act and the FTRA:

(1) ...the 1 or more natural persons -

(a) who exercise ultimate effective control over a legal person or legal arrangement...

(2) In this section, exercise ultimate effective control includes where ownership or control is exercised through a chain of ownership or by any means of control other than direct control; and as further provided in regulations.

128. Regulation 6 of the FTRA regulations was also amended to elaborate on the meaning of “ultimate principal” in the Act. The part relevant to ultimate principals of legal persons is as follows:

(1) This regulation sets out further detail for identifying ultimate principals who exercise ultimate effective control over a legal person or legal arrangement.

(2) (a) the natural persons who

(i) ultimately hold directly or indirectly, jointly or otherwise, 25% or more of the shares in the capital or voting rights of the legal person; or

(ii) are jointly and severally liable for the liabilities, regardless of their percentage of the capital or voting rights in the legal person

(b) if, under subclause (1)(a), no natural person is identified or there are doubts about whether the natural persons identified are ultimate principals, the natural persons who control the legal person by any other means, de facto or de jure

(c) if no natural person is identified under subclause (1)(a) or (b), the natural persons who hold the position of senior managing official.

(3) A natural person who meets 1 or more of the specified conditions under subclause (2) is deemed to be an ultimate principal unless that person operates solely in the capacity of a professional advisor or professional manager.

(4) If no natural person meets any of the specified conditions under subclause (2) and the trustees of a trust meet one or more of those specified conditions, the trustees of the trust are deemed to be the ultimate principals unless they are exercising ultimate effective control over the activities of the trust solely in the capacity of a professional adviser, professional manager or trustee company.

(5) If no natural person is identified as an ultimate principal under subclauses (2) to (4), any senior managing official is deemed to be the ultimate principal.

129. These amendments have resolved most of the previous deficiencies in the definition of beneficial owner in the Companies Act by ensuring that the relevant persons to be identified are natural persons, the relevant control is over the company and not each share, that control may be direct or indirect, acting jointly with other persons, and that a senior managing official is identified in the event that no other beneficial owners are identified.

130. Some observations may be made on the new definition and cascading approach to identify beneficial owners. Firstly, cross-reference errors appear to have been made in clause (2), by referring to subclause (1)(a) and again subclauses (1)(a) and (b) when no subclauses of clause (1) exist. It is relatively clear that the intention was to refer to subclauses (2)(a) and (b). The relevant Cook Islands' supervisory authority for the FTRA confirms that this was the intention and expects this to be enforceable despite the error.

131. Secondly, clause (2) closely follows the standard and would meet the standard. However, it is subject to clauses (3) to (5) which must also be examined to assess the full outcome.

132. Clause (3) excludes a person who is operating solely in the capacity of professional advisor. Professional advisor is defined in the regulations and includes a lawyer, an accountant or a financial advisor acting in a professional capacity. Clause (3) also excludes professional managers, and these are defined to include liquidators, receivers and officers of the FSC exercising their statutory functions. While these exclusions could be appropriate in relation to persons who would otherwise be in scope under subclauses (2)(a) and (b), the exclusion would not always be appropriate in the event that it became necessary to apply the default mechanism of subclause (2)(c) – the identification of a senior managing official. It is noted that clause (5) reintroduces the requirement to identify senior managing officials if no natural person has been identified under clauses (2) to (4), which should bring back into scope any relevant professional advisor or professional manager that meets the description of being a senior managing official when no other ultimate principals have been identified. It remains that the interplay of the clauses is complex and may lead to difficulties in implementation.

133. If no natural person is identified by clause (2), clause (4) will deem the trustees of a trust to be ultimate principals if they meet any of the conditions in clause (2). It goes on to then exclude a trustee of a trust from being an ultimate principal if they are a professional advisor, professional manager or trustee company. Again, these exclusions could be appropriate in relation to persons otherwise in scope under subclauses (2)(a) and (b), however the exclusion would not always be appropriate in the event that it became necessary to apply the default mechanism of subclause (2)(c) – the identification of a senior managing official. Furthermore, under the client structures and arrangements under which trustee companies in the Cook Islands commonly provide services, it seems very unlikely that no other persons would have been identified as ultimate principals by reason of their roles in relation to the trust or as natural persons to whom the professional advisor, professional manager or trustee company is in substance providing their services. The reference to a trustee company potentially needing the benefit of the exclusion in clause (4) adds some doubt over the intention or operation of the clause, as such entities should never be identified as ultimate principals under the standard as they are not natural persons.

134. No guidance has been issued by the authorities on the operation of these clauses in the new definition for either the Companies Act or the FTRA. A representative of a trustee company indicated that it was not aware of the purpose of clauses (3) and (4) in the new definition and in practice it did not apply these to modify the operation of clause (2). According to the FSC, almost all trustee company business is referred through professional intermediaries and typically there will be a variety of advisors (lawyers, investment advisors, etc.) that may have some role in the structure. The trustee companies are required to identify and verify these intermediaries, however the purpose of clauses (3) and (4) is to ensure that they do not stop at that point and must continue further to the client individual(s) ultimately behind the structure. If they can only identify the professional intermediaries or advisors in their CDD processes (i.e. not the client that is providing the instructions or the assets to the structure) then the FSC expects the reporting institution not to proceed at all with the relationship.

135. While the definition of beneficial owner (ultimate principal) now applicable under the Companies Act and the FTRA may be interpreted with a meaning that is in line with the standard, there is some complexity to the exclusions for professional advisors and managers and it would benefit from guidance from the authorities to ensure these do not cause the definition to depart from the standard in practice. The Cook Islands should clarify in guidance the operation of the definition of beneficial owner in relation to professional advisors and managers (see Annex 1).

136. While there is an obligation on a domestic company to obtain and maintain beneficial ownership information, there are several deficiencies that could result in a failure to have available all information that would meet the standard. As mentioned at paragraphs 125 and 126, previous deficiencies around completing the process of identifying the beneficial owners and the lack of specification of the identity details that need to be obtained remain the case and were not resolved by the amendment in December 2024. The Companies Act also does not provide for any mechanism under which the company might compel the provision of information from shareholders or beneficial owners. It does not impose any onus on a shareholder or a beneficial owner to disclose information to the company or to inform the company of any changes. No sanctions are envisaged in case a shareholder or beneficial owner refuses to co-operate with the company. The legal framework therefore lacks mechanisms under which domestic companies can obtain and update beneficial ownership information. Furthermore, even if a company has the ability to comply, no timeframes have been specified within which they must do so. No timeframe was originally given under which companies could transition to compliance under the original requirements introduced in 2017. The amendments effective from 4 December 2024 also did not specify any timeframe within which companies would be required to obtain and maintain any new information to comply with the amended requirements. In this context, the only sanction available if a company fails to comply is an offence provision requiring conviction by the court (see paragraph 151). This has never been tested, and doubts arise over whether a court would sanction a failure when what would constitute a failure (in terms of missing identity information) has not been specified and in any case no deadline for compliance has been provided. In summary, there is an absence of mechanisms under which domestic companies can obtain and update beneficial ownership information and even if it is feasible for a company to do so, a failure to comply may not be enforceable. The deficiency is not mitigated by the AML framework as domestic companies are not required to engage an AML-obliged person in all cases (see paragraph 140). The Cook Islands is recommended to ensure that adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information is available for domestic companies in line with the standard.

137. No information or guidance has been issued by the Registrar of Companies to inform companies of the new requirements or to provide any transitional period under which they may be permitted to come into compliance. These circumstances and the lack of any monitoring and verification activities by the Registrar of Companies in practice (see paragraph 97), lead to significant doubts on the extent to which domestic companies have implemented the original or revised beneficial ownership information requirements, if at all. The Cook Islands is recommended to implement effective oversight and enforcement measures to ensure the availability of beneficial ownership information for domestic companies.

## Anti-money laundering law requirements

138. Registration of international companies and limited liability companies must be conducted through a licensed trustee company in the Cook Islands, $^{25}$ each of which are reporting institutions under the AML framework (see paragraph 77). The trustee company is required to conduct CDD procedures to identify and verify the beneficial owners (FTRA 2017, ss. 25 to 30) before establishing the relationship (prior to registration of the prospective client entity). Each international company and limited liability company must continue to have an ongoing relationship with a trustee company. Thus, information on beneficial ownership of these companies is required to be kept with the licensed trustee companies.

139. International companies and limited liability companies will rarely if ever have a bank account with the domestic banks. These entities sometimes have a bank account with the international bank operating in the Cook Islands. Banks are therefore at best a secondary source for beneficial ownership information on these entities.

140. For domestic companies, beneficial ownership under the AML framework will be available only if they have an ongoing relationship with an AML-obliged person, for instance a bank. There is no legal obligation that requires or would cause a domestic company to have an ongoing relationship with an AML-obliged person, which means that the AML framework does not provide for a complete source of information on beneficial ownership of domestic companies. The Cook Islands understands that domestic companies would commonly have a bank account, although this is not assured, nor is it assured that such bank accounts would be held with a Cook Islands bank. To date, there has been no requirement to obtain beneficial ownership information on domestic companies for EOI purposes, so there is no experience in practice.

141. Pursuant to FTRA 2017, when dealing with a customer that is not a natural person, reporting institutions are required to do the following:

\- identify and verify any “ultimate principal” of the customer

• verify the status of the customer

\- obtain sufficient information to understand the customer's business and its ownership and control structure and

\- obtain information concerning the person(s) by whom, and the method by which, binding obligations may be imposed on the customer $^{26}$ (FTRA 2017, s. 25).

142. The types, kinds or categories of identity information to be obtained or verified for meeting the requirements above and any additional due diligence requirements may be prescribed in Regulations, and the Cook Islands has done so. In the case of a natural person, the identifying information to be obtained includes the full name, residential address, date and place of birth, nationality and an official personal identification number or other unique identifier in a valid official document. In the case of legal persons and legal arrangements, various identification information is also prescribed, and it includes the same identification requirements mentioned for natural persons that are ultimate principals and other natural persons acting on behalf of the legal person or legal arrangement. The primary Act requires, and the

Regulations reiterate, that verification of identity and information to be based on documents, data or information issued by a reliable and independent source.

143. A reporting institution that is unable to meet the customer due diligence requirements of the FTRA must terminate the relationship and consider whether to submit a suspicious activity report to the FSC. If the reporting institution reasonably believes that undertaking the customer due diligence or terminating the relationship would tip off the customer, it must submit a suspicious activity report within 24 hours.

144. Reporting institutions are required to maintain up-to-date and current CDD information on their clients (FTRA 2017, s. 32). The retention period is six years from the end of the relationship or the one-off transaction (FTRA 2017, s. 44). A reporting institution must carry out ongoing and effective monitoring of any ongoing business relationship, including the review of information held for the purpose of CDD to ensure that it is up to date and appropriate. The reporting institution must make sure that the extent and frequency of the monitoring appropriately reflect: (i) the type of CDD conducted when the ongoing business relationship with the customer was established, and (ii) the customer's level of risk (FTRA 2017, s. 32(2)(3)). Practice Guidelines issued by the FSC also explain that the reporting institution should take note of any changes in the nature of the relationship with the customer, such as changes in activities, turnover or transactions and when significant, reassess the customer's risk profile and the basis of the ongoing relationship.

145. There is no specified frequency for updating CDD. Practice Guidelines issued by the FSC advise that CDD information must be reviewed periodically, and monitoring frequencies are “suggested” as at least every three years for standard risk customers and annually for high-risk customers. In practice, only some reporting institutions follow this suggestion with a specified default cycle time and some others instead only apply a trigger-based system, or their system is trigger based for some clients. These trigger-based approaches have been considered by the FSC as acceptable under its Guidelines. In the case of trustee companies, CDD is in practice is always updated at least annually when preparing the annual returns required to be filed for international companies and limited liability companies. However, representatives of the domestic banks stated that they classify clients in terms of risk as high, medium or low. High risk clients, a category that includes every client with indications of foreign residence, are reviewed on a cycle of 6 to 12 months. Medium and low risk client reviews are trigger based, with re-evaluation triggered by any change in information held on the client that may affect their risk rating. The international bank stated that it has no cycle time for re-assessment of clients based on their risk classification, with re-assessment based on triggers similar to those applied by domestic banks but applied for all clients. An exception to this is for politically exposed persons who are subject to enhanced ongoing monitoring and must be reviewed annually.

146. The definition of “ultimate principal” was amended towards the end of the review period (see paragraph 127), so it is necessary to examine both the previous and the current definitions. Prior to 4 December 2024, the definition of “ultimate principal” was defined as (FTRA 2017, s. 8):

one or more natural persons who ultimately own or effectively control the customer, or on whose behalf a transaction or activity is being conducted and includes:

(i) in the case of a legal person, other than a company whose securities are listed on a recognised stock exchange, any natural person who ultimately owns or effectively controls (whether through direct or indirect ownership or control, including through bear share holdings) 25% or more of the shares or voting rights in the legal person;

(ii) In the case of any legal person, it includes any natural person who otherwise exercises effective control over the management of the legal person;

147. Section 8 therefore operated to provide the definition of beneficial owner in the Act, including providing a threshold for ownership or control. It also made it clear that ownership or control may be direct or indirect. While the definition contained the principal elements required by the standard, it was not clear that control will include a person acting individually or jointly, although this could have been captured by the term “otherwise”. There was a simultaneous approach to the definition of beneficial owner in the case of a legal person which was in line with the standard.

148. However, the definition did not contemplate the identification of the individuals holding a senior managerial position in cases where a beneficial owner cannot be identified. The Companies Act did not compensate for this absence, as it did not require the names of senior managers to be registered with the Registrar. This allowed the possibility that no beneficial owner would be identified in some cases.

149. The definition of ultimate principal in the FTRA and in regulations made under that law was amended with effect from 4 December 2024. For legal persons, this has been analysed in paragraphs 126 to 133 in relation to the use of this definition in the Companies Act and the same observations apply for its application under the FTRA. Specifically, the definition of ultimate principal and cascading method for their identification now applicable under the FTRA may be interpreted in line with the standard, however it would benefit from guidance from the authorities to ensure correct application in practice. The Cook Islands should clarify in guidance the operation of the definition of ultimate principal in relation to professional advisors and managers (see Annex 1). Additionally, as noted in paragraph 136, the amendments did not specify any timeframe within which reporting institutions would be required to redetermine the ultimate principals of account holders or clients under the amended requirements. In practice though, the change in definition of ultimate principal in the FTRA was less substantial than the change in definition of beneficial owner in the Companies Act, and the FSC states that the amendment did not require any change to their pre-existing practices which were in effect already aligned to the amended definition. Representatives of the reporting institutions concurred with this assessment, noting that it was already their practice to identify senior managing officials.

## Conclusion

150. The availability of beneficial ownership information for companies other than domestic companies is dependent on and ensured by the AML framework. However, there is no specified frequency for updating beneficial ownership information under the AML framework. In the case of domestic companies, beneficial ownership information is required to be maintained by the companies themselves. This information source is supported by the AML framework as a potential secondary source of information to the extent that a domestic company has a relationship with an AML obliged person. Some beneficial ownership information may also be available under tax law, although those requirements are not in line with the standard. The Cook Islands is recommended to ensure that adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information is available for all relevant legal entities and arrangements in line with the standard.

## Beneficial ownership information – Enforcement measures and oversight

151. If a domestic company fails to obtain and maintain sufficient information to identify the beneficial owner a penalty is applicable to the company and every director, but only if the company fails to provide that information to the Registrar when requested to do so by written notice. In the event of such failure, (i) the company commits an offence and is liable on conviction to a fine not exceeding NZD 10 000 (EUR 5 040), and (ii) every director commits an offence and is liable on conviction to a fine not exceeding NZD 10 000 (EUR 5 040) or to a term of imprisonment not exceeding 12 months, or both (CA 2017, s. 52).

152. The Registrar of Companies, and the Ministry of Justice of which it is part, is generally responsible for Compliance with the Companies Act. There is no requirement to file beneficial ownership information with the Registrar, and so whether companies are complying with their obligation to obtain and maintain information sufficient to identify their beneficial owners would depend on the Registrar requesting this information or otherwise examining the records held by companies. No routine inspections or audits of any kind were conducted during the review period. No penalty would be applicable in any case if such activities were to be conducted and identify non-compliance. Enforcement would be possible if the Registrar was to formally request these records, however no requests for records relevant to the beneficial ownership subject are known to have been issued during the review period or at any time before or since. The extent to which domestic companies are complying with their obligations under the Companies Act on beneficial owners is therefore unknown.

153. For the AML framework, there are two oversight bodies – the Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) and the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU). The FIU was merged with the FSC in 2012. Despite being merged, each body has its own responsibilities and focuses on specific areas. The FSC is responsible for the overall supervision of regulated financial entities and financial services. The FIU's function is to facilitate the prevention, detection, investigation and prosecution of money laundering, the financing of terrorism and other serious offences (any offence with a term of imprisonment of 12 months or more). To do this, the FIU is empowered under the Financial Intelligence Unit Act 2015 (FIUA 2015) to examine their compliance with the FTRA and the Regulations. The FIU may request information from reporting institutions, conduct on-site examinations and provide reports for this purpose.


155. Prior to July 2022, every bank and trustee company had at least one onsite inspection each year, (except for 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic when only the three largest trustee companies were inspected). From July 2022, the cycle time remains annual for banks but changed for trustee companies which moved to a two-year inspection cycle. Inspections are primarily onsite and include, among other things, verifying compliance with the FTRA. Compliance with CDD obligations is a key focus. For trustee companies, these are the same inspections as described in paragraphs 105 to 108 that are combined exercises by the FSC to verify compliance by trustee companies with the FTRA, the ICA and the LLCA. As noted in those paragraphs, files on 20-25 clients of a trustee company are selected for review and it can be the case that there are multiple entities in the structure provided for the client. Every entity connected with a client is reviewed and the statistics can be found in the table at paragraph 106.

156. Insofar as banks could be a source of information on beneficial ownership of companies under the AML framework, the FSC's inspections of banks provide assurance over the information they hold. The base minimum file review always includes the CDD requirements related to obtaining and verifying identity information for both legal and beneficial owners of the customer, purpose of relationship, documentation of control and management (corporate documents, trust deeds, letters of wishes, application forms), source of wealth and validation of wealth sources. Between 20 and 25 customers are selected for review, the mix of which will tend to reflect the client base of the bank. The representation of companies and company type in these reviews therefore varies according to the bank's client base. For example, any bank accounts maintained in the Cook Islands for international companies, limited liability companies, international trusts and foundations are concentrated with the international bank, as these generally do not have a relationship with any of the domestic banks. Both legal and beneficial ownership information of a customer is subject to review in these inspections. In summary, an appropriate supervisory framework is in place for the AML framework which assures the availability of beneficial ownership information for international companies and limited liability companies. However, the company law framework which is relied upon for the availability of beneficial ownership on domestic companies has had little or no relevant supervision. The AML framework may mitigate this gap, but domestic companies are not required to have a relationship with an AML-obliged person. Tax law may also provide some mitigation, but the monitoring and enforcement of the relevant obligations under tax law also has gaps. ## A.1.2 Bearer shares

159. Bearer shares are prohibited under the Companies Act and, in the event that any were to be issued, such shares are a nullity (CA, s. 19). Limited liability companies established under the LLCA do not have their capital divided into shares and therefore cannot issue bearer shares.

160. In contrast, international companies are permitted to issue bearer shares under the ICA. Despite this, the FSC runs a policy (in place until such time as the legislation is amended to extinguish bearer shares, see paragraph 164) which prohibits the registration of any company with bearer shares.

161. In 2016 and 2017, the FSC monitored international companies with bearer shares that were managed by licensed trustee companies, reviewing the nature and purpose of both the company and the structures with which they were placed, the records relating to beneficial ownership and bearers, and conducted independent checks on those recorded.

162. The FSC released a policy (non-binding) on 15 November 2017 encouraging the Cook Islands' legal persons to phase out bearer instruments by 1 July 2018 for conversion, redemption or surrender of existing bearer instruments. There were four companies with bearer shares in 2018. Although there is still a provision for issuance of bearer shares in the ICA, the Cook Islands reports that the last company with bearer shares converted these in 2019 and no companies have been registered with bearer shares since then. The FSC reviews the share registers of international companies as part of its inspections of trustee companies and also requires any company to notify it if bearer shares have been issued when renewing their annual certificates.

163. In the event that any bearer share was to be issued, section 35A of the ICA requires that it must be delivered to and held by a custodian and the custodian cannot hold the share until it has received satisfactory evidence of the identity of the person for whom they will hold the share in custody. $^{28}$ The custodian must be a licensed financial institution in the Cook Islands and will therefore be subject to the CDD requirements in the FTRA, which now also add to the requirements of the Regulations issued in 2004.

164. According to the FSC, trustee companies are under an expectation that in the event that any client company issued bearer shares, this would prompt legislation to be put forward to extinguish such shares. Furthermore, the relevant trustee company would be put on an enhanced monitoring program in the interim. There is therefore no appetite among international companies and their trustee companies to reintroduce bearer shares, which has also limited the priority given to amend the law to permanently remove the possibility. In any event, there is currently no impediment to transparency related to bearer shares in the Cook Islands. Nevertheless, it remains legally possible for international companies to issue bearer shares in the Cook Islands. The Cook Islands should ensure that ownership information is available in the event that bearer shares are issued (see Annex 1).

## A.1.3 Partnerships

## Types of Partnerships

165. The following types of partnerships may be established under the Cook Islands' laws: (i) domestic partnerships; (ii) international partnerships and (iii) limited partnerships. In addition, this section also covers foreign partnerships. None of the forms of partnership is a legal entity separate from the individual partners that comprise the partnership.

166. Partnership law in the Cook Islands for domestic entities is governed primarily by a New Zealand enactment, the Partnership Act 1908 (PA). That Act defines a partnership as “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view to profit” (PA, s. 4(1)). A partnership is a distinct commercial entity for accounting purposes, with each partner jointly and severally liable for the liabilities of the partnership. These partnerships are sometimes referred to as general partnerships. In practice, about 100 domestic partnerships are commonly operating small businesses owned by and comprised of partners who are local tax residents. The partnerships are usually operating small grocery, food or short-term accommodation businesses.

167. The International Partnership Act 1984 (IPA 1984) provides for the creation of two types of partnerships (see also 2015 Report, paragraph 111):

i) international partnership – every partner is jointly and severally liable for the liabilities of the partnership (IPA, s. 22)

ii) limited partnership – every general partner is jointly and severally liable for the liabilities of the partnership, but a limited partner is generally only liable to contribute money or money's worth to the common stock, as capital (IPA, s. 62).

168. At registration, every partner in an international or limited partnership must be non-resident in the Cook Islands. As at 31 March 2025, there were three international partnerships and no limited partnerships in the Cook Islands.

## Identity Information Requirements

169. The creation of an international or limited partnership in the Cook Islands requires registration with the Registrar of Partnerships within the FSC. The registration of these partnerships must be arranged through a trustee company. At registration, one of the partners of an international partnership or limited partnership must be a trustee company or an international company of the kind described at paragraphs 59 to 61. After registration and before commencement of business, details of all partners of limited partnerships (but not international partnerships) must be submitted to the Registrar. Both types of partnership must have a registered office, which must be the registered office in the Cook Islands of the partner that is a trustee company or international company (which, if the latter, will also be the registered office of a trustee company). A trustee company is therefore always engaged at creation and on an ongoing basis for international and limited partnerships and is required to conduct CDD on the partnership and keep this up to date as has been described for companies from paragraph 142.

170. In addition, the conduct of onshore business by an international partnership or limited partnership is governed by the Development Investment Act and is subject to registration with the Business Trade and Investment Board. The information to be filed on registration, and annually thereafter, includes detailed ownership information, with the names, addresses and passport numbers of all legal owners of the shares of the partnership income (Development Investment Act, s. 34 and Regulation 3(d) of the Development Investment Regulations 1996). None of the currently registered international partnerships are carrying on business in the Cook Islands.

171. All accounts and records of an international partnership or limited partnership must be retained for a period of not less than six years following the completion of the transaction to which the records and underlying documentation relate (IPA, s. 7). International partnerships and limited partnerships are not subject to Cook Islands taxation and therefore there are no requirements for them to furnish returns on income (IPA, s. 72). Consequently, no international partnership or limited partnership has reason to register with the RMD or apply for an RMD number, and none have done so.

172. There are no direct registration requirements for domestic partnerships. As explained at paragraphs 70 and 71, taxpayers with assessable income generally must file a tax return and administratively a tax return is not processed until an RMD number (tax identification number, ITA s. 218) is issued, which may be seen as fulfilling the function of a registration system. However, there is no comprehensive requirement to apply for an RMD number. In the case of partnerships, a further layer of uncertainty arises in that these legal arrangements are not “taxpayers” as the Cook Islands levies income tax only on the partners and not the partnership. No penalty has been identified in the Income Tax Act that would be available where a partnership fails to apply for an RMD number. Nevertheless, partnerships may apply for an RMD number for various reasons, such as for importing goods, registering for VAT or to meet obligations as an employer.

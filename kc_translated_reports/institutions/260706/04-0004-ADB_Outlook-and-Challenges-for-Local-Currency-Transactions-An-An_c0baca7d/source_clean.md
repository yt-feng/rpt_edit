## Abstract

Since the late 2010s, rising geopolitical tensions—particularly between the United States and the People's Republic of China (PRC)—have begun to affect currency choices in trade and investment across Asia. Although the US dollar remains the dominant international currency, emerging economies are increasingly promoting local currency-denominated transactions. The PRC has advanced the internationalization of the renminbi (RMB); at the same time, its global payment share remains limited in SWIFT statistics, and RMB usage has expanded through alternative infrastructures such as the Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS). In Southeast Asia, Thailand has experienced a notable increase in local currency transactions following regulatory easing. This paper argues that the sustainable expansion of local currency trade invoicing requires well-developed foreign exchange markets and credible bilateral and multilateral policy frameworks. However, excessive currency and capital controls may hinder long-term progress. The analysis highlights both the potential and the constraints of ongoing efforts to diversify settlement currencies beyond the US dollar.

Keywords: Dominant Currency Paradigm, internationalization of the RMB, settlement currency, cross-border transactions, direct exchange market

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Since the late 2010s, the PRC and some Southeast Asian economies have set the promotion of trade and investment as their goal, implementing policies to encourage transactions invoiced in their respective (local) currencies and settlements using local currencies rather than the US dollar. Against the backdrop of rising geopolitical risks stemming from political and security issues between the United States (US) and the PRC, changes in trade and investment flows are evident, including increasing trade dependence between the PRC and ASEAN economies. On the other hand, the US dollar remains dominant in trade transactions within Asian supply chains, particularly those involving Japanese companies (Ito et al. 2018; Shimizu et al 2021). In contrast to the Dominant Currency Paradigm (DCP), where the US dollar continues to hold a dominant position in global trade, a phenomenon that could be described as “dedollarization” (also written “de-dollarization”) is progressing gradually. However, its pace has tended to accelerate or slow down in response to President Trump’s tariff policies since 2024, and to new developments in geopolitical risk.

These policies aimed at promoting transactions invoiced in national currencies, centered on emerging economies, are not limited to individual economies' initiatives but are being proposed and implemented under various bilateral and multilateral frameworks (such as BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organization). The expansion of RMB use in the PRC (its internationalization) is one such example. Although the PRC government has been promoting the internationalization of the RMB since 2009, the share of the RMB used in the private and public sectors as a unit of account, a medium of exchange, and a store of value remains limited. According to figures from SWIFT's RMB Tracker (February 2026), the RMB's share of international payments stands at $2.74\%$ , making it difficult to characterize it as a true international settlement currency at this stage.

On the other hand, SWIFT's statistics are limited to transactions conducted through the SWIFT network; therefore, it is impossible to assess the internationalization of the RMB without verifying the extent to which its use has increased through the Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS), an RMB international settlement system established under the leadership of the People's Bank of China. While the RMB cannot be considered a fully convertible currency, as various regulations regarding currency and foreign exchange transactions remain in place, its share in trade and other transactions has increased rapidly since the 2010s. In terms of the pathways of this expansion, within trade, usage initially focused on RMB-invoiced imports of goods, then expanded to RMB-invoiced exports of goods, and later increased in capital transactions, including trade in services, RMB-denominated direct investment, and securities investment. Geographically, while RMB-invoiced transactions were initially concentrated between the PRC and Hong Kong, China, the proportion of RMB-invoiced trade with regions such as Southeast Asia and Europe has been gradually increasing. This trend is supported by statistical data from trading partners, which have gradually been made public in recent years. Specifically, there has been greater growth in cases where economies such as Thailand, Indonesia, and Australia import in RMB (i.e., pay in RMB) rather than export to the PRC in RMB. Furthermore, in recent years, data suggest that RMB-invoiced transactions are taking place not only in trade and other transactions between the PRC and specific bilateral partners but also in trade among third economies (for example, between Brazil and the Russian Federation).

Even if an economy actively promotes domestic currency-invoiced transactions as a matter of policy, the results of this trend—such as an increase in such transactions—are not necessarily uniform across economies with differing economic scales and levels of economic openness. For example, Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia—three geographically close economies—are implementing the Local Currency Settlement Framework to increase local currency-invoiced transactions and settlement in trade and investment. However, as of now, progress shows that the share of the Thai baht has risen significantly across the three economies. At the same time, it is difficult to say whether transaction volumes in the Malaysian ringgit and Indonesian rupiah are increasing steadily. This situation is thought to stem from the fact that, among these three economies, Thailand has made relatively greater progress in relaxing currency and foreign exchange regulations, in contrast to Malaysia and Indonesia, which have actually tightened various regulations on nonresidents' use and holding of currency, as well as foreign exchange transactions, since the latter half of the 2010s. The differences in the values and composition of each economy's foreign investment positions reflect these differences in capital flows, including capital transactions. They may serve as a useful hint for increasing the number of transactions invoiced in local currencies.

The growth in the value of local currency-invoiced transactions (in the home or partner economy) in these nations is believed to stem in part from usage driven by actual demand. Specifically, as reported by the Bank of Thailand, when exports are carried out, the exporting economy receives the partner economy's currency, and it has become clear that a portion of this is held in the partner economy's currency (foreign currency) and used to make future external payments. Furthermore, when making payments to overseas counterparties, foreign currency borrowing from financial institutions may also be used, in addition to the aforementioned foreign currency holdings. Between the PRC and some Southeast Asian economies, efforts are underway to establish and foster “direct exchange markets” where exchange and settlement are conducted using local currency pairs, without involving the US dollar, to underpin local currency-denominated trade and investment transactions. From the perspective of increasing local currency-denominated transactions, the formation of foreign exchange markets—in either the home or partner economy—where multiple currencies, including emerging-market currencies, can be traded is considered necessary (Tomizawa 2023). Furthermore, the existence of such exchange markets creates the possibility of local currency transactions and settlements even between emerging economies (and their currencies) where capital controls remain in place.

While past research has shown that the increase in local currency-invoiced transactions is influenced by factors such as an economy's growing trade share and the reduction of transaction costs for that economy's currency due to deregulation, this paper clarifies that the use of the RMB expands when the government, like that of the PRC, takes the lead in promoting RMB-invoiced transactions through policy measures—namely, the existence of bilateral or multilateral political memorandums of understanding (MOUs) or initiatives, swap arrangements, and clearing and settlement systems, as well as the expansion of local subsidiary/branch networks by the PRC's five major banks, and that there is a new dimension to the expansion of RMB usage: the PRC taking a leadership role in trade among emerging economies where foreign exchange regulations remain in place.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on currency choice in international trade, with a particular emphasis on the Dominant Currency Paradigm and recent debates on nondollar currency usage in emerging economies. Section 3 conducts an extensive analysis based on economy-level trade and settlement data, covering the PRC, major ASEAN economies, and selected advanced and emerging economies. Section 4 summarizes the current situation, discusses the reasons that regional and multilateral initiatives promote local currency-invoiced transactions, focusing on Asia and the Global South, examines the role of policy coordination and financial infrastructure, and concludes by summarizing the main findings and drawing policy implications for currency diversification.

## 2. THE DOMINANT CURRENCY PARADIGM AND DEBATES ON CURRENCY CHOICE IN PREVIOUS RESEARCH

Gopinath et al. (2016) and Casas et al. (2017) have pointed out that the Dominant Currency Paradigm (DCP) is a new framework that replaces the traditional approaches to currency selection in international trade, namely Producer Currency Pricing (PCP) and Local Currency Pricing (LCP). This paradigm posits that while there are 180 currencies in the world, only a very small number are used in trade, finance, and reserves; many economies set their import and export prices in US dollars, and the US dollar plays an overwhelming role in international trade. It is particularly well suited to providing a more realistic explanation of the relationship between trade and exchange rates in small open economies. Gopinath et al. constructed a dataset of prices and volumes for over 2,500 bilateral trade relationships covering 91% of world trade using economy-specific six-digit HS commodity data from UN Comtrade and invoice currency shares from Gopinath (2015). Using Colombian firm-level data, they confirmed that the US dollar is used as the dominant currency and plays a crucial role in pricing and trade responses.

Georgiadis et al. (2021, ECB) acknowledged that the fact that approximately 40% of global trade is invoiced in dollars—a share that far exceeds the US’s 10% share of global trade—is indicative of DCP. However, they pointed out that this is due to strategic complementarity (the incentive to price in the same currency as competitors) and integration into global value chains (GVCs), which reinforce the use of the dollar, concluding that the dollar holds a dominant position as a “reserve currency” even in transactions with third economies. With regard to the euro, they noted that while it is frequently used in intra-European and Europe—third-economy transactions, its role as a vehicle currency in third-economy transactions is weaker than that of the dollar, and that policy support from European authorities is limited. In contrast, they demonstrated that the PRC government authorities are promoting various policies to internationalize the RMB, which is having a positive impact on its use.

Perez-Saiz, Zhang, and Iyer (2023) conducted a broader examination of the DCP, using SWIFT data to analyze patterns of currency use in cross-border payments within the International Monetary System (IMS) and the factors driving them $^{1}$ . Their findings confirm that, as in the previous studies, cross-border payments are primarily concentrated in the US dollar and the euro. While the share of the RMB has expanded in recent years, its usage rate remains low due to strong inertia in currency usage, driven by network effects and switching costs. The study identified the currency's legal-tender status, as well as geographical and political distance, as the main factors influencing currency choice; however, these factors proved relatively unimportant. On the other hand, the results indicated that trade and financial linkages are more important for emerging currencies than for existing major currencies. The study also points out that recent rapid changes in geopolitical risks may accelerate the multipolarization of the current international monetary system.

Furthermore, Perez-Saiz and Zhang (2023) analyzed international usage of the RMB, noting that while its use as an international settlement currency is still in its early stages—accounting for only 2% of global cross-border transactions—its usage varies significantly by region, with its adoption expanding in Asia and certain emerging market economies (such as Mongolia, the Lao PDR, and Argentina). They utilize SWIFT data, and in addition to the standard MT103 and MT202 messages, they include MT700 and MT400. Furthermore, by incorporating swap lines and the establishment of offshore settlement banks—as explanatory variables promoted as part of the RMB's internationalization—they confirmed that these policies contribute to the expansion of RMB usage. Geographical and political distance, which had little impact in the previous empirical analysis, are also shown to be important factors influencing the use of RMB, with its usage in the Asian region being particularly pronounced. In conclusion, while the internationalization of the RMB is still in its early stages, its role as a regional currency is expanding, and it may play a more significant role in the international monetary system in the future.

Research focusing on Asia is also progressing. In their study on the Asia and the Pacific region, Mercado, Jr., Jacildo, and Basu Das (2022) analyzed the reasons that the US dollar dominates as the invoicing currency for international trade in the region, demonstrating that participation in global value chains (GVCs) and the presence of multinational corporations (MNCs) have a significant influence on the use of the US dollar. They point out that key factors in the Asia and the Pacific region include the absence of a strong regional currency and limited regional trade integration. In particular, they note that the numerous GVCs and MNCs in the region engage in both import and export transactions; imports of intermediate goods, such as raw materials, are invoiced in dollars; and since the United States is the primary destination for exports of finished products, it has become customary to conduct all transactions in US dollars. A comparison between the Asia and Pacific region and other regions, including Europe, suggests that in Europe, the euro is widely used as the invoice currency, indicating that the existence of a regional currency significantly influences the choice of invoice currency. Therefore, to reduce the dependence of the Asia and Pacific region on the US dollar, it is essential to develop financial infrastructure—including cultivating foreign exchange hedging markets that promote the use of regional currencies—and to advance intraregional financial cooperation to facilitate trade settlements in regional currencies.

An analysis of invoice currency selection using customs data from individual economies can be found in Shimizu et al. (2022). As regards the currency selection for invoices by Japanese firms, studies such as Shimizu et al. (2021) and Ito et al. (2018) have shown that, despite being a developed economy, Japan relies heavily on the US dollar rather than the yen in trade with emerging Asian nations. This has been attributed to foreign exchange risk management considerations: As supply chains with overseas subsidiaries expand, companies standardize intragroup trade in US dollars to allow the headquarters finance department to manage foreign exchange risk centrally. This indicates that GVC factors are at play. However, when Shimizu et al. (2022) analyzed invoice currency shares by trading partner using individual-level data from the Ministry of Finance's customs records, they confirmed that, particularly in exports to Asia in recent years, the share of US dollar-invoiced transactions has decreased, while the shares of yen-invoiced and partner currency-invoiced transactions have increased. For example, regarding the Thai baht, while previous data disclosures showed a share of only around 1% for exports to Asia, 2020 data indicate that it accounts for 15.5% of exports to Thailand by value and 17.1% by number of transactions. Compared to 2014 data, the share of transactions invoiced in the partner economy's currency has more than doubled for exports to the PRC (10.4%), the Republic of Korea (6.0%), and India (9.6%). In all these economies except India, the share of dollar-invoiced exports has decreased. The currency choices observed in these bilateral transactions indicate a growing trend toward using local currencies, which does not necessarily align with the DCP identified by the IMF.

While the DCP has taken hold, the use of emerging-market currencies, led by the RMB, is expanding across a wide range of economies, albeit with a low market share. Emerging economies, having witnessed the intensification of the US–PRC trade war and financial sanctions during the Russian invasion of Ukraine, are also pursuing policy initiatives to break away from excessive dependence on the US dollar amid the recent rise in geopolitical risks and to promote the use of their own currencies, at least in intraregional trade. Whether such promotional measures are effective will require further analysis based on a close examination of data from each economy.

## 3. CURRENT STATUS AND ANALYSIS OF LOCAL CURRENCY TRANSACTIONS AND SETTLEMENTS IN VARIOUS ECONOMIES

Emerging economies, led by the PRC, have been on high alert about excessive fluctuations in exchange rates against the US dollar and their impact on domestic economies and capital flows. Coupled with the implementation of policies aimed at increasing trade and capital transactions invoiced in local currencies, shifts in the share of local currency denomination and settlement in trade with other countries are becoming apparent in some cases.

In this section, we analyze changes in the use of local currencies for trade pricing and settlement across countries, based on published data. Referring to the Chinese RMB (3.1), the Thai baht (3.2), the Indonesian rupiah (3.3), the Malaysian ringgit (3.4), the Australian dollar (3.5), the NT dollar (3.6), the euro and other EU currencies (3.7), the British pound (3.8), and the Brazilian real (3.9), we will clarify how trade (and investment) denominations and settlements have changed and how the use of domestic currencies has progressed in each region.

## 3.1 Chinese Yuan (RMB)

In the PRC, since 2009, the People's Bank of China has designated pilot enterprises in certain cities such as Shanghai and in parts of Guangdong Province and began testing RMB-invoiced trade transactions with Hong Kong, China and ASEAN economies, gradually expanding the scope to cover the entire economy and extending to partner economies and regions worldwide. A key feature of the RMB's internationalization process is that it began with the use of the RMB in such trade transactions; however, initially, it was also intended to help Chinese exporters avoid fluctuations in the RMB exchange rate following the Lehman Shock (Sekine 2010). Furthermore, early government documents suggest that the process of RMB internationalization should proceed in the order of “peripheralization” (use in border trade, etc.), “regionalization”

(use in trade and investment with neighboring economies), and “globalization” (Ba 2012).

Looking back at the PRC's economic and financial environment at the time, following its accession to the WTO, the 2000s saw massive inflows of foreign currency as hot money, driven particularly by trade surpluses and the expectation of a rising yuan. The government frequently intervened in the foreign exchange market but was unable to sufficiently sterilize (recoup) the RMB released into the market. Amid a period when excess liquidity in financial markets flowed into the stock market and real estate transactions, the economy exhibited signs of a bubble. With capital controls and foreign exchange management regarding the RMB still in place, a major challenge was how to promote the liberalization of the capital account in a manageable and orderly manner while ensuring stable economic growth.

To support “RMB-invoiced trade transactions” launched under this macroeconomic environment, the People’s Bank of China (PBOC) not only concluded bilateral local currency swap agreements with central banks of various economies to supply RMB liquidity but also designated RMB clearing banks in each economy. Many of these currency swap agreements were designed to promote trade and investment, and the number of counterparties and the scale of transactions have expanded in line with the growth of RMB settlements with partner economies and the development of offshore RMB markets. Furthermore, in 2011, RMB-denominated inward and outward foreign direct investment and the RMB Qualified Foreign Institutional Investor (RQFII) program were launched, thereby broadening the scope for the use of the RMB in capital account transactions and settlements. In 2015, the Cross-border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) was established as a financial infrastructure to facilitate RMB settlements. The China Foreign Exchange Trade System (CFETS) worked to expand the direct exchange (trading) market between the RMB and currencies other than the US dollar (without the use of the US dollar as an intermediary).

The proportion of transactions settled in RMB in the PRC's external trade is reflected in statistics released by the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE) (covering receipts and payments from external transactions conducted by banks on behalf of their customers). From the inception of these initiatives until around 2015, the volume of RMB usage grew primarily through receipts and payments in trade (goods) transactions (Figure 1).

In the PRC's foreign exchange market spot transactions, the US dollar continues to account for over 95% of all RMB trading (exchange) counterpart currencies. Even as of May 2025, while tariff negotiations with US President Trump were underway, approximately 97% of foreign exchange transactions traded with RMB were in US dollars. Among currencies other than the US dollar, the euro, the Japanese yen, and the HK dollar account for relatively high proportions. Furthermore, the People's Bank of China refers to the 17 currencies of economies with which it has established cooperative relationships under the Belt and Road Initiative as “Belt and Road currencies.” By developing foreign exchange transactions between the RMB and these Belt and Road currencies, the bank aims to reduce corporate foreign exchange transaction costs and play a positive role in improving trade convenience and facilitating financing within the Belt and Road region (RMB Internationalization Report 2024).

Tomizawa (2023) analyzes the effects of these direct exchange markets. The People's Bank of China's Monetary Policy Report for the fourth quarter of 2014 (Column 2) notes that the introduction of direct exchange transactions has led to narrower spreads and lower transaction costs, improved exchange rate transparency and flexibility through the optimization of the market maker structure, and enhanced services to the real economy through an increased variety of foreign exchange products offered to customers. It is reported that for currencies where direct exchange was initiated, the average daily trading volume increased to 8.4 times the pre-implementation level.

With regard to the Russian Federation, reports indicated that RMB-invoiced transactions, including resource trades, increased around 2023. $^{3}$ While the Russian government has released limited specific data, the Bank of Russia's 2023 Annual Report indicates that, in cross-border settlements, Russian banks have expanded their correspondent banking networks with major partner economies and increased settlements in their own currency (the ruble). In particular, as of the end of 2023, it is reported that the RMB's share of the Russian Federation's export receipts reached $35.8\%$ , while its share of import settlements reached $37.0\%$ . While trading in currencies of friendly nations, such as the RMB, is said to be increasing in the Russian Federation's foreign exchange exchanges and over-the-counter (OTC) markets, CFETS data do not necessarily show an increase in ruble transactions on the Chinese side. Furthermore, there are reports that since 2024, Chinese banks, fearing secondary sanctions, have become cautious about using the RMB in transactions with the Russian Federation. $^{4}$

Furthermore, while Chinese statistics currently do not provide numerical evidence that the PRC is increasing RMB-invoiced and settlement transactions in its overseas resource trade (except for the increase in RMB-invoiced transactions for Indonesia's resource imports, which is discussed later), if signs emerge that energy and primary commodity transactions with resource-rich economies are shifting to RMB-invoiced terms, $^{5}$ this could be interpreted as a sign that non-US dollar-invoiced transactions will increase in the future for resource trade among emerging economies.

## 3.2 Thai Baht

In Southeast Asian economies, there is a lack of harmonization of foreign exchange regulations, and access to local currencies and domestic assets is limited. To address these issues, a framework for promoting bilateral transactions invoiced in local currencies was established in 2016 between the Bank of Thailand and the Bank Negara Malaysia (central bank). This initiative was expanded in 2018 to include Bank Indonesia, creating the Local Currency Settlement Framework (LCSF) among Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia to increase local currency-invoiced transactions and settlement in trade and investment. Bearing in mind the specific circumstances of each economy, a more harmonized and flexible foreign exchange regime has been adopted. Specifically, flexibility has been introduced into foreign exchange regulations to enable participating banks to offer competitive financial services for cross-border settlements invoiced in local currencies. In 2021, additional participating banks were designated, and in 2023, the framework was expanded to cover all cross-border transactions, not just trade and investment.

Looking at Thailand's trading partners, while Southeast Asia has consistently accounted for about $25\%$ of total exports (Figure 10), the share of exports to the United States has been on the rise since 2019 (18% in 2024), suggesting that the supply chain may have shifted following the establishment of the first Trump administration, which altered the flow of goods within Asia. Goods produced in Thailand, as well as those manufactured domestically using parts imported from other economies in the region, have been redirected for export to the United States.

## 3.4 Malaysian Ringgit

According to the explanation in the Bank Negara Malaysia's 2023 Annual Report, as part of multilateral efforts, an ASEAN Task Force was established in 2009 to promote the use of local ASEAN currencies for trade settlements within ASEAN. Also in the same year, as part of an initiative for local currency settlements with the PRC, in addition to bilateral local currency swaps and the granting of investment quotas to qualified foreign investors, direct trading between the RMB and the ringgit began at the China Foreign Exchange Trade System. With the PRC, a bilateral cross-border collateral arrangement was concluded in 2010, an RMB liquidity facility was established (by Malaysia) in 2013, and the Bank of China (Malaysia) was designated as an RMB clearing bank in 2015, thereby establishing a mechanism for supplying local currency liquidity.

Meanwhile, as regards ASEAN economies, in 2015, the Bank Negara Malaysia proposed developing a regional framework for the cross-currency market. Subsequently, Local Currency Settlement Frameworks (LCSFs) were established with the Bank of Thailand in 2016 and Bank Indonesia in 2018. Since then, the scope of transactions has been expanded to include capital transactions and other types, and the number of designated banks has increased. In 2023, the three economies agreed to expand the framework to cover all cross-border transactions.

Since Prime Minister Anwar took office, he has made statements suggesting an expansion of local currency use, including in April 2023, when he said there was no reason to continue relying on the US dollar. Unlike Thailand and Indonesia, the central bank does not regularly publish statistics on local currency transactions; however, looking at the specific figures presented in the aforementioned Annual Report, the share of US dollar-settled transactions in Malaysia's total trade stood at 82% (2023). While the share of ringgit-settled transactions has increased, it remains at 3.6%, whereas that of RMB-settled transactions has risen to 2.9%. ## 3.5 Australian Dollar

Australia was said to be growing closer economically to the PRC, as evidenced by the PRC's President Xi's 2014 visit to Australia, during which a summit was held, the bilateral relationship was elevated to a “comprehensive strategic partnership,” and the Australia–PRC FTA entered into force in 2015. ## 3.6 Taipei, China/NT Dollar

Like Australia, Taipei, China is one of the regions whose foreign and economic relations with the PRC have changed significantly over the past five to ten years. Taipei, China conglomerates such as Hon Hai, which have expanded into the PRC, have become manufacturers there and drive the PRC's export trends. Against this backdrop, under the Chinese Nationalist Party, the Cross-Strait Economic Cooperation Framework Agreement (ECFA) was established in 2010. In 2012, the Bank of China branch in Taipei, China was designated as an RMB clearing bank on the Taipei, China side. In 2013, the two sides agreed to expand securities investments by companies from both sides, denominated in RMB and other currencies. They were moving to strengthen relations in various areas, including trade, investment, and people-to-people exchanges. However, when the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) returned to power in 2016, a shadow was cast over these relations, and the situation deteriorated to the point where concerns arose about a contingency in the waters separating the PRC and Taipei, China.

Looking at Taipei, China's export and import destinations, exports are led by the United States; the PRC; and Hong Kong, China; which together account for over $50\%$ of the total (Figure 36). In terms of imports, imports from the PRC account for over $20\%$ of the total, nearly double the volume of those from the United States. Notably, perhaps in part due to the success of the New Southbound Policy promoted by the DPP administration, Southeast Asian economies—the policy's primary targets—rank among the top ten.

During this visit, 18 agreements were reportedly signed covering railway lines, the development of electric vehicle (EV) charging networks, and energy-related cooperation. In addition to the fact that trade between the PRC and Hungary has quadrupled over the past 12 years, direct investment by Chinese companies in Hungary is also progressing, making the PRC Hungary's largest source of foreign direct investment (with over 13 billion euros committed in 2023, approximately double the amount from the previous year).

CATL, a major manufacturer of automotive batteries, announced in August 2022 that it would build a new battery factory for EVs in Debrecen, eastern Hungary (with an investment of 7.34 billion euros), and the factory is scheduled to begin operations in 2026. Furthermore, in December 2023, EV giant BYD announced plans to build its first European EV assembly plant in Szeged, in the southeast of the economy. This marks the company's third investment in Hungary, following the electric bus plant opened in Komárom (northwest) in 2017 and the battery assembly plant announced in June 2023 in Fót (north).

Hungary was the first European country to sign a memorandum of understanding (MOU) on Belt and Road cooperation with the PRC in June 2015. In the financial sphere, a local currency swap agreement (RMB100 billion/HUF375 billion) was concluded in 2013, which was renewed four times through September 2025, with its size expanding to RMB400 billion/HUF1.9 trillion (approximately USD5.5 billion). In 2015, Hungary also became the first country in central and eastern Europe to establish an RMB clearing bank with the opening of the Bank of China's Hungary branch. Furthermore, in 2016, Hungary became the first country in central and eastern Europe to issue Hungarian government bonds in the PRC in the form of RMB-denominated Panda bonds; such issuances have since been conducted repeatedly, including green bonds. In addition, since 2018, the Hungarian forint (HUF) has been added as a trading currency pair against the renminbi on the China Foreign Exchange Trade System (CFETS), enabling direct exchange transactions in that market.

In November 2024, a meeting was held in Beijing between President Xi and Slovak Prime Minister Fico, during which an MOU was agreed upon regarding the commencement of investments by Chinese companies in EV battery plants in Slovakia starting in 2025, the joint planning and implementation of railway and road infrastructure projects, and water resource management projects.

Thus, it is completely plausible that not only Chinese firms' direct investment itself in Europe but also transactions within and beyond Europe related to those firms' manufacturing facilities—whether among Chinese firms or along their supply chains—are already being, or will increasingly be, conducted in RMB.

## 3.8 United Kingdom/Pound Sterling

In the United Kingdom, as part of the so-called “Brexit” process, 51.9% of voters chose to leave the EU following the results of the June 2016 referendum. Negotiations for the UK’s withdrawal from the EU began in June 2017, with the initial target set for March 2019. However, after repeated delays, legislation implementing the UK’s withdrawal from the European Union was enacted on 23 January 2020, and the withdrawal procedures were completed. On 29 January, the European Parliament approved the withdrawal agreement, and the UK’s departure from the EU was finalized at midnight on 1 February (Belgian time).

Against this complex backdrop, data from 2024 show that, in terms of the UK's global trade, exports were invoiced in US dollars (38%), British pounds (approximately 24%), and euros (14%), and imports in US dollars (39%), British pounds (29%), and euros (21%) (Figure 48).

Next, trade with the EU and with economies and regions outside the EU exhibits different characteristics. In 2024, for exports to the 27 EU member states, the British pound accounted for 27.5% and the euro for 26.5% (totaling 54%); for imports, the British pound accounted for 39% and the euro for 39% (totaling 78%), with the British pound and the euro continuing to dominate.

In contrast, for the UK's trade with non-EU economies and regions (where trends since 2013 can be confirmed in published data), the share of the British pound in UK exports to non-EU economies has declined significantly (from $55\%$ in 2013 to $21\%$ in 2024). Conversely, the share of the US dollar has increased significantly (from $31\%$ to $58\%$ for the same period), reversing the relative usage of the British pound and the US dollar. This trend appears to have continued even after the UK's withdrawal from the EU.

For UK imports from outside the EU, the US dollar remained at around 60% and the British pound at around 20% almost consistently from 2013 to 2024, with no major changes observed.

Since 2014, RMB-invoiced settlements have accounted for approximately 1%–2% of the UK's exports to economies "outside" the EU (however, the RMB is not used for exports from the UK to economies "within" the EU). Furthermore, in terms of imports from economies "outside" the EU, the figures for RMB-invoiced transactions have not reached a level sufficient to be tracked or published accurately. While RMB-invoiced transactions account for 1% of all UK exports, the proportion is higher at 2.2% for machinery and transport equipment. According to data published by the People's Bank of China, the PRC's RMB settlement volume with the UK (including capital transactions) ranks third among economies and regions, following Hong Kong, China and Singapore. This figure has been increasing year by year, in line with UK trade statistics. This assessment is broadly consistent with the fact that, in the UK statistics cited above, the share of RMB invoicing in extra-EU exports stands at 1.8% (2024). At the same time, however, it is also possible that part of this figure is supported by securities transactions settled in renminbi that are intermediated through London.

## 4. CONCLUSION

This paper summarizes the current usage and notable changes in currencies other than the US dollar by scrutinizing data on the share of currencies used in trade settlements published by central banks and other institutions. The status of each currency is as follows:

## RMB

\- In the PRC, RMB settlement transactions are increasing, with a slightly larger increase observed in import payments.

\- Looking at data from other economies, such as those in the EU, there is a possibility that RMB-invoiced transactions are occurring in some third-country trade, particularly in eastern European economies (especially those along the Belt and Road route).

## Thai baht

\- In Thailand, the share of baht-settled transactions has increased to 15%–16% for exports and around 8% for imports.

\- In trade with ASEAN economies, the baht is widely used, accounting for over 20% of exports and more than 10% of imports.

## Indonesian rupiah

\- In Indonesia, the proportion of imports invoiced in RMB has increased to 8.8%.

\- For oil and gas imports, transactions with the PRC have almost entirely shifted to RMB.

## Malaysian ringgit

\- In Malaysia's trade, the share settled in ringgit rose to 3.6%, while that settled in RMB increased to 2.9%.

\- In trade with the PRC, transactions settled in RMB or ringgit account for 24.4%.

## Australian dollar

\- For Australian exports, 88.9% are invoiced in US dollars and 8.8% in Australian dollars.

\- The Australian dollar accounts for a high proportion of sales of specific items, such as beverages and medical products.

## NT dollar

\- In Taipei, China, the US dollar dominates both exports and imports; the RMB accounts for 1.4% of exports and 1.5% of imports, and the NT dollar is not yet widely used.

## Euro

\- In trade with economies outside the EU, 51.7% of exports and 39.7% of imports are invoiced in euros.

\- US dollar-invoiced transactions account for 31.4% of exports and 51.1% of imports.

## British pound

\- In the UK's trade with non-EU economies, 58% of exports are invoiced in US dollars and 21% in pounds. For imports, US dollar-invoiced transactions remain stable at around 60%.

\- The share of pounds in UK exports to non-EU economies has declined significantly (from 55% in 2013 to 21% in 2024), while its share in imports has remained largely unchanged at around 20%.

## Brazilian real

\- For Brazilian exports, 96% are invoiced in US dollars and 1.3% in Brazilian reals.

\- For imports, the share invoiced in Brazilian reals stands at 7.2%, while that invoiced in RMB has increased to 0.4%. RMB is also used for Brazil's imports from third economies other than the PRC.

## ASEAN currencies

\- Within the ASEAN region, transactions invoiced in local currencies are increasing, with the Thai baht being particularly widely used.

Based on the above data, it has been confirmed that the use of currencies other than the US dollar has been increasing in recent years due to regional economic blocs and policy initiatives. With regard to the RMB in particular, under the PRC government's RMB internationalization policy launched in 2009, its use in trade and capital transactions has been promoted; initially limited to import payments, it has since expanded to include export transactions, trade in services, direct investment, and securities investment. RMB-invoiced trade transactions were initially centered on trade between the PRC and Hong Kong, China, but usage has increased, particularly for import payments. RMB-invoiced and settlement transactions are expanding not only in Asian economies such as Japan, the Republic of Korea, and ASEAN nations, but also in Australia, Brazil, and European economies. It has become evident that the use of the renminbi has increased markedly in the EU for import transactions of specific goods. This expansion in the use of the RMB is largely influenced by the People's Bank of China's establishment of local currency swap agreements and clearing banks, as well as the construction of the Cross-border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) to streamline settlements—a trend that is consistent with previous research. Furthermore, the growing popularity of third-party payment services such as Alipay and WeChat Pay, driven by the increase in the number of Chinese tourists, is also considered a contributing factor.

The RMB is still not a fully free international settlement currency. Previous IMF studies using SWIFT data indicate that the US dollar continues to play a dominant role in international settlements, and the RMB's share as an international settlement currency remains limited. However, data published by individual economies firmly confirm that transactions in RMB and other currencies are gradually increasing outside the United States. The following seven factors are cited as reasons for the increase in transactions in currencies other than the US dollar.

## 1. Economic factors

\- Hedging against exchange rate risk: mitigating the impact of exchange rate fluctuations against the US dollar on the domestic economy

\- Reduction of trade costs: reducing foreign exchange fees and spreads by conducting transactions in the domestic currency

\- Avoiding economic sanctions: avoiding the risk of sanctions associated with US dollar settlements

## 2. Policy factors

\- Policies to promote transactions invoiced in local currencies: policies encouraging intraregional trade and investment using local currencies are being promoted, particularly among ASEAN economies

\- Currency swap agreements: securing local currency liquidity through bilateral currency swap agreements

\- Establishment of clearing banks: development of financial infrastructure to support local currency settlements

## 3. Regional factors

\- Expansion of regional economic blocs: increased use of local currencies within regions such as ASEAN and BRICS

\- Creation of direct exchange markets: development of local currency trading markets that do not rely on the US dollar

## 4. Geopolitical factors

\- US–PRC tensions: US tariff policies and economic sanctions are driving efforts to reduce dependence on the dollar.

\- Rise of emerging economies: Economies in the Global South are promoting the use of their own currencies.

## 5. Technological factors

\- The spread of digital payments: Third-party payment services such as Alipay and WeChat Pay are driving the use of local currencies.

\- Introduction of digital currencies: consideration of Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs)

(In addition, the movement of people constitutes a factor specific to the Asian region.)

6. Development of financial infrastructure

\- Cross-border payment systems: Systems such as CIPS support the use of nondollar currencies like the RMB.

\- Formation of multicurrency trading markets: expansion of multicurrency trading markets, including currencies of emerging economies in the Global South, such as the BRICS Cross-Border Payment Initiative (BCBPI)

7. Changes in trade structure

\- Expansion of the PRC's trade: Trade between the PRC and other economies is increasing, leading to expanded use of the RMB.

\- Changes in resource transactions: increased use of the RMB and local currencies in resource imports

Before World War I, the international monetary system was based on the classical gold standard, under which exchange rates were effectively fixed. The British pound served as a de facto reserve currency; however, its position weakened following the destabilization of the gold standard during World War I and the Great Depression. Under the Bretton Woods system (1944), the US dollar became the core currency by being pegged to gold (1 ounce = 35 dollars). Although gold convertibility ended in 1971, the dollar retained its dominance in international payments, foreign exchange reserves, and financial transactions; even in the post-1995 multipolarization phase following the establishment of the WTO, the monetary order can be characterized as one in which partial diversification proceeded on the premise of dollar dominance.

Since the late 2010s, amid changes in the political and security environment surrounding US–PRC relations, currency choice has gradually shifted in East and Southeast Asia. While the US dollar remains dominant in invoicing and settlement, and transactional inertia persists, emerging economies have strengthened policies to expand local currency transactions denominated in their own currencies, or in those of their counterparties, with the aim of mitigating spillovers from dollar exchange rate fluctuations and reducing transaction costs and sanctions-related risks. At the same time, “dedollarization” has not progressed linearly; rather, it is observed as a multidirectional change—including, in some economies, “redollarization” phases.

Although the renminbi remains subject to various controls, its use in trade has expanded since the mid-2010s alongside improvements in transaction and settlement infrastructure, spreading from imports to exports and services trade and further to capital account transactions such as foreign direct investment and portfolio investment. Further expansion of cross-border use requires not only institutional changes on the PRC's side but also regulatory easing in partner economies, interauthority cooperation, and arrangements that enable a “supply–recovery–circulation” mechanism through offshore markets and settlement functions. Nevertheless, international use remains limited: According to the “Dollar Dominance Monitor” (as of March 2026), the US dollar accounts for 54% of export invoicing, 89% of foreign exchange trading (on a 200% gross basis), and 57% of foreign reserves, whereas the renminbi accounts for 4%, 9%, and 2%, respectively. In terms of monetary function, the renminbi has expanded as a unit of account and means of payment, while it has lagged as a store of value (foreign reserves); renminbi-denominated reserves have been on a declining trend since peaking around 2021.

In this paper, we identify the following three factors: (1) underdevelopment of the PRC's financial sector and remaining controls on renminbi and capital account transactions; (2) concerns regarding the credibility of monetary policy and information disclosure, the PRC's macroeconomic outlook, and fiscal and financial management, and uncertainty in the real estate sector; and (3) perceived risks of sanctions and asset freezes associated with renminbi assets. As for regional cases, Thailand has increased baht-denominated trade and settlement with neighboring ASEAN economies through, inter alia, regulatory easing, and Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia have introduced the Local Currency Settlement Framework (LCSF). Indonesia has also relaxed constraints on the use of the rupiah and, in cooperation with the PRC, expanded bilateral local currency transactions in 2025 to cover all transactions, including the capital account, while also extending cooperation to QR code instant payments. These cases suggest that even in emerging economies where regulations remain, renminbi and local currency transactions can expand to some extent when appropriate financial infrastructure and policy coordination are in place (see Shimizu and Tomizawa (2026) for an empirical analysis). However, in multilateral initiatives such as BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO), the expansion and diversification of participants are likely to raise coordination costs and lengthen the time required.

Overall, this study indicates that renminbi internationalization has expanded in a complementary manner under a dollar-based system and is shaped more by institutional and relational factors than by trade alone. The PRC's experience cannot necessarily be generalized directly to developing economies, and avoiding the “middle-income trap” requires orderly liberalization of exchange and capital controls during the growth phase, stable macroeconomic management, and financial market development. As geopolitical fragmentation deepens, firms are increasingly compelled to secure transaction routes while factoring in financial sanctions and asset freezes, and expanding settlement in one's own currency may serve as a defensive strategy to reduce risk. Even advanced economies with limited scope for deregulation, such as Japan, may promote local currency-denominated trade and investment by deepening domestic financial markets, enhancing their attractiveness, and strengthening cooperation with emerging economies, while considering currency digitalization and changes in cross-border payments (including the introduction of CBDCs and the spread of dollar-denominated stablecoins). Against the backdrop of rising geopolitical risk and accelerating regional fragmentation, the trajectory of the dollar-based monetary order warrants close and sustained scrutiny.

# Executive summary

Papua New Guinea embarked on the Tariff Reduction Programme (TRP) as part of a broader economic recovery and reform agenda that aimed to improve economic efficiency and integrate with global markets. By 2015, Papua New Guinea's tariffs were at historic lows, around 3.2 per cent on average, making it one of the most open economies in the region. After further tariff reductions, the government suspended the final stage of tariff cuts in 2018. It progressively raised tariffs on some products, citing the need to protect infant industries and boost revenue. As Papua New Guinea implements new national development frameworks, including the Papua New Guinea National Trade Policy 2017–2032, the Medium-Term Development Plan IV (2023–2027), and the Medium-Term Revenue Strategy (2023–2027), as well as other sector-specific plans in fisheries, forestry, agriculture, and manufacturing, there is a need for a structured and evidence-based assessment of the TRP.


On economic outcomes and sectoral impacts, the TRP coincided with a period of reduced import demand. Therefore, Papua New Guinea did not experience an import surge, linked to the lowering of tariffs. Instead, it benefited from strong export earnings coming from mining and petroleum. When Papua New Guinea reversed course and raised tariffs in 2018, the tariff hikes had a limited additional dampening effect on import levels, which had already been low and stable. Overall, Papua New Guinea's economy overall exhibited remarkable gross domestic product (GDP) growth averaging 6.3 per cent annually during the active TRP period, compared to the post-suspension period growth of two per cent. On tariff revenue, tariff policy plays an important but diminishing role in Papua New Guinea's fiscal revenue. The tax-to-GDP ratio declined to 15.8 per cent in 2023, even as tax revenue grew in absolute terms. Import duties rose from \$71.5 million in 2010 to \$112 million in 2023, $^{1}$ yet their share in total revenue fell as the Global Sales Tax and other taxes became more prominent. Consumer goods remain the largest source of tariff revenue because of high import volumes, especially for fuel, vehicles, alcohol, and tobacco. In contrast, raw materials and intermediates generate little revenue despite some high nominal rates, due to low import volumes. Simulations indicate tariff cuts during the TRP (2012–2017) reduced revenue by only about \$7.8 million annually, or just one per cent of tariff revenue, a negligible effect. After 2018, tariff hikes were expected to boost revenue, but collections were about \$15.6 million lower than they would have been under 2018 baseline tariffs. This outcome was driven largely by the sharp cut in tobacco duties, one of Papua New Guinea's top revenue sources, alongside reduced import volumes and likely evasion incentives. The experience showed tariff liberalization cost Papua New Guinea little in revenue terms.


Innovation under this report is considered broadly via the ability to introduce new products to the market. During the TRP, Papua New Guinea experienced a notable surge in export diversification, followed by a decline once tariffs were raised. Between 2010 and 2018, the number of distinct export products expanded significantly, with 591 new products entering Papua New Guinea's export portfolio, representing 45 per cent of the total basket. However, the majority of these exported items were of low value, with only 44 new products exceeding \$100,000 in value in 2018. This latest export drive was supported by cheaper imported inputs and machinery, which lowered production costs and enabled firms to test new product lines in international markets. However, sustainability was limited, with only 465 products (35 per cent) surviving through the period, underscoring the importance of addressing structural constraints beyond tariff reductions.

Consumer welfare was affected by the radical change in tariffs of a few product groups. During 2010–2018, simulations show a net welfare loss of around \$5.9 million for Papua New Guinea consumers. While tariffs fell on many consumer goods, Papua New Guinea retained or raised very high tariffs on beverages and tobacco, which alone caused losses of about \$6.5 million. Beyond beverages and tobacco products, Papua New Guinea increased tariffs on about \$390 million of other goods, lifting average rates from 5 to 14 per cent which contributes a welfare loss of \$1 million. The total consumer welfare loss, therefore, outweighed the \$1.6 million gain consumers enjoyed from all the other tariff reductions.

The evidence reveals that tariff policy alone cannot determine economic development outcomes in contexts where structural constraints fundamentally limit competitiveness. Papua New Guinea's businesses face transport costs, energy expenses, and regulatory complexity that often dwarf the effects of tariff modifications, suggesting that successful development strategies must also address these underlying constraints to deliver more results from trade policy instruments like tariffs.

Yet trade policy remains an important component of the government policy toolbox for a comprehensive development strategy. The TRP period's correlation with stronger economic growth, export diversification, and increased business dynamism suggests that trade openness can contribute to economic vitality when implemented within supportive policy frameworks. Conversely, contraction in exports and GDP following TRP suspension appears to show some association between the two.

The study of the TRP reveals both potential benefits and limitations of trade policy as a development tool, which provide valuable lessons for future policy design and implementation. It is suggested that to yield results, trade policy requires consistent efforts to address fundamental constraints while maintaining openness to international opportunities that can accelerate development progress. Moving forward, Papua New Guinea should aim to formulate a tariff policy that balances development objectives with fiscal revenue needs and a realistic dependence on outside sourcing for its industry.

## 1. Restore gradual tariff rationalization

Papua New Guinea should adopt a predictable, phased tariff reduction path to allow industries time to adjust. A simpler, more uniform structure with low rates would reduce distortions, improve certainty, and avoid counterproductive high tariffs that harm consumers, encourage smuggling, and undermine industrial growth.

## 2. Focus on competitive business environment

Policy should prioritize reducing the high cost of doing business rather than shielding firms with tariffs. Investments in infrastructure, power supply, and streamlined customs can lower costs, while reducing tariffs on key inputs will directly enhance competitiveness and help firms produce goods that can compete globally.

## 3. Use targeted support for infant industries

Support to priority sectors should be time-bound, specific, and performance-based rather than relying on broad tariff protection. Targeted measures like subsidies, tax relief, or technical assistance will boost productivity and encourage industries to mature into competitive sectors, while public-private dialogue can ensure support meets actual business needs.

## 4. Safeguard consumer welfare and food security

Tariff policy must protect low-income households by keeping tariffs on essential goods like food, fuel, and medicine at low or zero rates. Excise taxes, rather than tariffs, should be used to regulate socially harmful goods such as alcohol and tobacco without distorting trade or raising consumer costs unfairly.

## 5. Enhance regional integration efforts

Beyond lowering tariffs, Papua New Guinea should reduce non-tariff barriers and strengthen customs efficiency to lower trade costs. Greater engagement in regional and multilateral agreements, such as PACER Plus, MSG, ASEAN+1, and WTO initiatives, can expand market access, improve input sourcing, and support diversification of export opportunities.

## 6. Institutionalize evidence-based review mechanisms

Tariff policy should follow a transparent, institutionalized review process rather than ad hoc budget announcements. Establishing a Tariff Advisory Board and conducting regular impact assessments would help ensure tariff measures are evidence-based, predictable, and phased out when no longer needed, boosting investor confidence and policy credibility.

## 7. Strengthen trade defence mechanisms

Papua New Guinea should build institutional capacity to monitor, investigate, and respond to unfair trade practices such as dumping or subsidised exports. Enacting anti-dumping and safeguard laws, supported by a specialised trade remedies unit, would help protect domestic industries, maintain a level playing field, and align with WTO rules.

Introduction


Papua New Guinea rolled out the Tariff Reduction Programme (TRP) in 1999 as part of a broader economic recovery and reform agenda. After a phase of deep tariff cuts, which started in 2012, the TRP was suspended in 2018 with the reversing of rates for some tariff lines. As Papua New Guinea implement new national development frameworks, including the Papua New Guinea National Trade Policy 2017–2032, the Medium-Term Development Plan IV (2023–2027), and the Medium-Term Revenue Strategy (2023–2027), as well as other sector-specific plans in fisheries, forestry, agriculture, and manufacturing, there is a need for a structured and evidence-based assessment of the TRP.

This TRP review is intended to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the cost and benefits of the TRP to date, assess the economic implications of its suspension, and provide well-informed policy options for the future. This includes determining the TRP's impact on domestic industries, consumers, trade competitiveness, and alignment with international agreements such as the Interim Economic Partnership Agreement (iEPA) with the European Union and commitments under regional and multilateral frameworks.

This study meets several specific objectives as follows:

\- To evaluate the impact of tariff changes on the private sector, including determining effects on competitiveness, efficiency and productivity by surveying companies to understand their response to the policy.

• To analyse the benefits and costs of tariff changes on different stakeholders, including consumers (through choice/variety of products and price reductions), businesses (competitiveness), and trade participants (importers/exporters).

\- To determine the impact of tariff changes supporting downstream processing and value addition in sectors such as manufacturing, fisheries, and agriculture.

\- To evaluate whether certain exogenous factors explain any unintended outcomes arising from the tariff changes.

The review provides a set of conclusions and recommendations in order to guide the development of a future coherent, transparent, and development-friendly import tariff policy that supports the government's overarching objectives of promoting value addition, import substitution, and economic resilience in a globally competitive context.

The concentration in low- to moderate-complexity commodities gives Papua New Guinea certain characteristics –namely, vulnerability to commodity price swings, but also strong export revenues when prices rise. However, this structure limits Papua New Guinea's ability to move up the value chain, develop export products with greater technology, branding, or processing content, and thus capture more value. Diversification is similarly constrained by the geography of Papua New Guinea's product space: many products that are adjacent (i.e. “nearby” in the product space) to what Papua New Guinea currently exports are still commodities or low-processed goods (e.g. further processing of minerals or agricultural goods). # B. Papua New Guinea's Tariff Reduction Programme: a chronology

## 1. Initiation of the Tariff Reduction Programme (late 1990s – early 2000s)

Papua New Guinea embarked on a significant Tariff Reduction Programme (TRP) in 1999 as part of a broader economic recovery and reform agenda. The TRP was introduced in exchange for support from international financial institutions such as the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank, and was aimed at making Papua New Guinea's trade regime more open and competitive (Scollay, 2007). A new Value Added Tax (VAT) and Government Sales Tax (GST) were simultaneously implemented to offset the revenue losses from cutting tariffs. The government's 1997 Tariff Reform White Paper, inspired by a 1996 World Bank review, argued that high import duties were hindering efficiency, raising business costs, and protecting uncompetitive industries. Lowering tariffs was seen as a way to encourage a more productive private sector by exposing firms to international competition. At the same time, the new VAT would replace tariffs as a revenue source (Scollay, 2007). This policy shift came at a time of economic difficulty, following the Asian financial crisis and commodity price slumps of the late 1990s, but reflected Papua New Guinea's commitment to outward-oriented reforms under World Trade Organisation (WTO) membership and the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) goals.

## 2. Phased tariff reductions (1999–2006)


The TRP legislated stepwise reductions of 5 percentage points for each band at set intervals. In practice, tariffs fell in stages on January 1 of 1999, 2000, 2001, 2003, and finally 2006. By the end of 2006, the intermediate rate had been reduced to 15 per cent, the protective rate to 25 per cent, and the prohibitive rate to 40 per cent. $^{2}$ These cuts effectively halved Papua New Guinea's average applied tariff, with the unweighted average tariff dropping from about 20 per cent in the late 1990s to roughly 5 per cent by 2006. The new structure dramatically increased the share of duty-free lines from around 3 per cent before the cuts, to about 77 per cent of tariff lines by 2006. Only about 15 per cent of tariff lines (mainly for domestically produced foods and manufactures) still faced import duties at the protective 25 per cent or prohibitive 40 per cent rates by 2006 (UNCTAD, 2006). A few sensitive products, such as sugar, canned fish, salt, tobacco, alcohol, and plywood, had been granted special high protection due to prior government commitments or industry


lobbying, and these were exempted from the standard TRP timetable.


As a result of these findings, the government rejected the moratorium proposal and decided to complete the TRP as planned (Fox et al., 2018). By 2006, the final step of the initial program was implemented, bringing tariffs to their new low bands of 40, 25 and 15 per cent, as mentioned above. The 2003 review even recommended extending the tariff

cuts beyond 2006, envisioning further 5 per cent reductions every few years such that all tariffs would eventually converge at 15 per cent (Scollay, 2007; UNCTAD, 2006). However, this suggestion was not adopted at the time, and trade policy liberalization essentially paused once the first TRP was completed in 2006 (WTO, 2010). The mid-2000s debate underscored the balance Papua New Guinea sought between fostering infant industries and pursuing an open, competitive economy. While the government remained committed to outward-looking reform, it became clear that complementary measures (improving infrastructure, good governance, and tax compliance) were needed to maximize the benefits of tariff reform (Scollay, 2007).

While an independent review of the tariff policy suggested that lower tariffs were not causing damage to national industries and could support broader economic growth, many stakeholders remained unconvinced about the overall benefits arising from the tariff reductions. As a result, further tariff reforms were suspended.

## 3. Continued tariff reductions (2007–2017)

After a brief pause in tariff changes, Papua New Guinea revisited its tariff policy to achieve further liberalisation in the late 2000s. The Tariff Review in 2007 (Scollay) assessed the post-TRP landscape and paved the way for a second phase of tariff reductions. The government's development strategies, such as the Medium-Term Development Strategy and Vision 2050/Development Strategic Plan, emphasizing improving competitiveness and integrating Papua New Guinea's economy with global and regional markets. In line with this, the 2007 review proposed additional gradual cuts to move Papua New Guinea toward a more uniform and lower tariff structure over the next decade (Fox et al., 2018). These recommendations were eventually codified in the Import and Export Customs Tariff Act of 2012, which legislated a schedule of tariff changes: some reductions took effect immediately in 2012, followed by further 5 per cent cuts planned on January 1, 2015, and again planned on January 1, 2018. The ultimate goal was to reduce the remaining protective tariffs to around 10 per cent, flattening the tariff bands at a low rate across all products by 2018 (Laveil, 2020). For example, under this plan, the standard protective duty rate would drop from 25 per cent (post-2006) down to 15 per cent by 2015 and 10 per cent by 2018, while the highest “prohibitive” rate would fall from 40 to 30 per cent by 2015 and eventually 25 per cent by 2018. By the mid-2010s, Papua New Guinea

had implemented most of these scheduled cuts: the average of the three tariff bands declined from about 42 in 1999 to 18 per cent by 2015. Papua New Guinea's trade policy in this era remained outward-oriented, influenced by international commitments. As a member of the WTO, Papua New Guinea had bound many of its tariffs at ceilings (often around 40-45 per cent), and its applied rates after the TRP were well below these bindings (UNCTAD, 2006). This gave Papua New Guinea flexibility to liberalise and policy space to raise tariffs up to the bound rates if deemed necessary.

## Tariffs in the context of free trade agreements

Papua New Guinea was also mindful of its regional and bilateral trade agreements. It joined the Pacific Island Countries Trade Agreement (effective mid-2000s) and the Melanesian Spearhead Group (MSG) agreement, which progressively eliminated tariffs on trade with neighbouring Pacific states, though these had a limited impact on overall tariff policy since they apply only to regional partners. Under an interim Economic Partnership Agreement (EPA) with the European Union, signed in 2009 and ratified in 2011, Papua New Guinea committed to gradually fully liberalize 88 per cent of its imports from the European Union, with sensitive items excluded (European Commission, n.d.). Papua New Guinea chose to liberalise its trade with the European Union almost immediately, reflecting that many of those imports already faced low or zero most-favoured-nations (MFN) tariffs as a result of the TRP.

Meanwhile, as an APEC member, Papua New Guinea had endorsed the Bogor Goals of free and open trade by 2020 for developing economies. $^{3}$ All these international frameworks reinforced Papua New Guinea's tariff reduction trajectory and the government's objective of using low, uniform tariffs to promote efficiency and investment. By 2015, tariffs were at historically low levels, and the country was more integrated into global trade networks, with few import restrictions and relatively low protective barriers. $^{4}$

Average tariff rates on affected goods were raised by about seven percentage points in 2018.

## 4. Reversal and shift towards protectionism (2017–present)

In the late 2010s, Papua New Guinea's tariff policy took a significant turn as a result of economic challenges. Facing economic headwinds such as foreign exchange shortages, fiscal pressures, and a push to develop domestic industries, the government began reconsidering its open trade stance. A notable precursor was a brief import ban on select fruits and vegetables from Australia in late 2015, an attempt to protect local farmers (Fox et al., 2018).

In August 2017, the government formally suspended the TRP before the final 2018 cuts could be implemented. Instead of reducing the remaining tariffs to 10 per cent as planned, Papua New Guinea moved to raise many tariffs in order to shelter domestic producers. In the 2018 Budget (enacted in late 2017), authorities announced increases on roughly 250 tariff lines and simultaneously scrapped or deferred over 600 scheduled tariff reductions. Average tariff rates on affected goods were raised by about seven percentage points in 2018. This policy reversal accelerated in the following year: by 2019, Papua New Guinea had introduced a total of 323

tariff line increases over two years, with an average hike of around 14 percentage points in the second round (Laveil, 2020).

Many of these changes targeted goods that compete with nascent or existing local industries, in line with the government's renewed emphasis on import substitution and industrialization. For example, a 25 per cent import duty on milk was imposed (milk had previously been duty-free) to support Papua New Guinea's first domestic dairy venture, Ilimo Dairy Farm. Tariffs on items like frozen meats, packaged fruits, sugar and confectionery, flour and cereals, certain textiles and apparel, furniture, and household goods (soaps, detergents, etc.) were also raised significantly. The highest tariffs faced were for tobacco products with an ad valorem equivalent (AVE) of 155 per cent, followed by 65 per cent for beverages, 34 per cent for edible vegetables, 27 per cent for meat and meat products, 26 per cent for pearls and diamonds, 23 per cent for sugars, 20 per cent for furniture and 20 per cent for coffee (See Figure 5). The Customs Tariff Amendment Act of 2018 explicitly stated its intent was to “provide relief to local pioneer industries and existing local manufacturers from cheap imports” (Laveil, 2020). In practice, this meant reinstating protective barriers for a range of products to give local businesses a price advantage over imports.

This reversal of past policies marked a new protectionist chapter in Papua New Guinea's trade policy. After nearly two decades of falling tariffs, Papua New Guinea's rates on some goods began climbing for the first time since the late 1990s. The tariff schedule became more complex, with new 12.5 per cent, 20 per cent, and 25 per cent bands added. The change sparked debate. Proponents argued this protection would nurture infant industries such as dairy, poultry, and textiles. Critics noted mixed past results from similar policies: Ramu Sugar struggled for decades despite high protection, while Halla Cement only became competitive after privatisation and lower tariffs (Fox et al., 2018). Critics of the 2018–19 tariff hikes pointed to these, arguing that prolonged protection can lead to inefficiency and high consumer prices without guaranteeing a globally competitive industry (Laveil, 2020). They also warned that reversing the TRP could harm Papua New Guinea's export sectors, as higher tariffs raise input costs for Papua New Guinea's exporters and risk retaliatory measures or losing credibility in trade negotiations.

By 2020, the government signalled a pause on further tariff changes. The

2020 budget contained no new increases, but the protective measures remained in place with no further tariff reduced. As of the early 2020s, Papua New Guinea's applied tariff rates remained higher than the lows achieved under the TRP, especially for products deemed important to “import-replacement” industries. Papua New Guinea stayed within its WTO bindings, avoiding breaches, but the reversal marked a clear shift from the liberalisation path followed since 1999.

This evolution from aggressive tariff cuts in the early 2000s to consolidation and commitment to free trade agreements and then to a resurgence of protectionist measures highlights how economic conditions, government objectives, and international influences have continually reshaped Papua New Guinea's tariff landscape over the past twenty years. Each phase of the Tariff Reduction Programme and decisions to partial reverse changes were driven by a mix of economic rationale and political debate, underscoring the challenge of balancing development goals with global trade commitments in Papua New Guinea.

## Government policies on trade and industry


Papua New Guinea implemented several sectoral and horizontal plans to support industry and trade. While tariff policies are typically decided by a Ministry of Finance (or Treasury in the case of Papua New Guinea), their objectives are often multiple and span several policy domains. These may include raising government revenue to ensure fiscal sustainability, restricting products harmful to the environment, protecting emerging industries, supporting industrial or agricultural development, and addressing external imbalances. Because of these diverse objectives, coordination across government institutions with different mandates is therefore essential to ensure alignment with the various policies of the Government.

Tariff policy aligned with the goals of the Medium-Term Development Plan (MTDP) IV (2023–2027) to promote diversification. By raising tariffs on processed foods, textiles, wood products and consumer goods, the government created a price advantage for domestic firms engaged in downstream processing, in line with the MTDP IV's focus on job creation, SME promotion, and adding value to Papua New Guinea's natural resources before export.

Sectoral development plans in forestry, fisheries, agriculture, and industry also reveal the way tariff policy was integrated into the drive for diversification. High tariffs on wood and furniture, and the log export ban policy, supported local timber processing. In agriculture, tariffs on dairy, vegetables, and processed foods created space for new agribusiness ventures, while in fisheries, duties on canned and smoked fish reinforced incentives for local processing. While tariffs alone were not always sufficient to stimulate competitive industries, they formed part of a wider policy mix designed to encourage local value addition, support SMEs, and expand Papua New Guinea's industrial base.

## A. National Trade Policy (2017–2032)

Papua New Guinea's National Trade Policy (NTP) 2017–2032 marked a strategic shift in trade policy. At its launch in 2017, the government suspended the TRP. Furthermore, the government declared that Papua New Guinea would review all trade agreements and even consider suspending trade with partners seen as unfair, underscoring a more assertive, Papua New Guinea-first stance. Reflecting this stance, Papua New Guinea declined to join the Pacific Agreement on Closer Economic Relations Plus (PACER-Plus) regional free trade agreement. Instead seeking arrangements with Australia and

New Zealand that yield tangible investment, export earnings and jobs for Papua New Guineans (Post Courrier, 2017).

The subsequent tariff adjustments in 2018–2020 align with the NTP's protective orientation. According to the 2019 Customs Tariff Amendment Act, the rationale for recent tariff increases was “to provide relief to local pioneer industries and existing local manufacturers from cheap imports” (The National, 2020). In other words, the tariff policy has been reoriented to nurture domestic industries – a key goal of the NTP – by sheltering them from import competition.


Papua New Guinea's MTDP IV embodies objectives that relate to trade and tariffs. The MTDP IV targets broad economic growth and explicitly aims to “encourage downstream processing of our natural resources” (Ministry of Treasury, 2024). This priority reflects a desire to add value locally in sectors like agriculture, forestry, fisheries and mining, rather than exporting raw commodities. The tariff adjustments support this goal by making certain imported finished goods relatively more expensive and thus giving an edge to local producers of processed foods, wood products, textiles, and other manufactures. For example, tariffs were raised on a range of food products and consumer goods, including frozen meats, packaged fruits and vegetables, sugar, confectionery, flour, garments, wood and paper products, furniture and others, which is consistent with incentivizing domestic processing in those product

chains. The MTDP IV also emphasizes job creation (one million additional jobs) and promotion of Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (Fox et al., 2018).

However, the MTDP IV simultaneously calls for a “conducive environment to attract Foreign Direct Investment”. This implies that tariff policy must strike a balance, providing reasonable protection to encourage local production, while not creating an overly prohibitive or unpredictable trade regime that could deter investors. Excessive or ad hoc tariff increases could conflict with the objective of a stable, investment-friendly climate. This tension is recognized by policymakers; indeed, observers noted that Papua New Guinea’s abrupt deviation from the TRP in 2018 introduced new tariff rates (12.5, 20, 25 per cent) beyond the previous uniform structure and set a precedent for lobbying by industries for even higher tariffs (Fox et al., 2018).

## C. Medium Term Revenue Strategy (MTRS) (2023–2027)

Papua New Guinea's MTRS 2023–2027 provides another lens through which to view tariff policy. The MTRS seeks to halt the decline in revenue-to-GDP and raise it to about 17.9 per cent by 2027 through improved compliance and a broader tax base (Ministry of Treasury, 2024). It emphasizes shifting toward consumption-based taxes in a fair, efficient manner while minimizing ad-hoc exemptions.

Tariffs play a dual role in this strategy. On one hand, they provide revenue, and the increases in 2018–2019 (averaging 7 per cent and 14 per cent) offered short-term relief to a budget under strain (The National, 2020). On the other hand, the MTRS cautions against measures that undermine efficiency. Higher tariffs can discourage imports, reduce GST collections, or encourage smuggling if rates are excessive (The National, 2020).

The trade-off between revenue and efficiency is acknowledged by analysts – for example, an overvalued exchange rate was identified as a more direct cause of competitiveness issues, suggesting that devaluing the Kina would be a more efficient way to assist both import-competing and export sectors than raising tariffs (Fox et al., 2018). $^{5}$ Thus, to remain consistent with the MTRS, tariff policy should be carefully calibrated: providing needed revenue and industry support, but complemented by broader tax reforms and macroeconomic adjustments to ensure it does not undermine productivity or compliance.

## Analytical approach employed for the analysis

To evaluate the outcomes and impacts of the TRP, the study relies on two complementary methodologies: an ex-post econometric analysis of trade flows, which assesses the results of the implementation of the TRP on specific economic variables, and an ex-ante partial equilibrium simulation, which assesses the expected changes to occur as a result of tariff changes. All analysis is based on data obtained from international data sets, namely from UN Comtrade, ITC TradeMap, ITC MacMap, and the World Bank WITS. This data was also compared to national data obtained from the Papua New Guinea Customs Service.

Partial equilibrium simulation (ex-ante projections): A partial equilibrium (PE) trade model was employed to simulate the impact of tariff changes on trade, welfare, and revenue. Two periods were examined: (1) TRP Implementation (2010–2018) – comparing outcomes with the tariff reductions against a counterfactual of no tariff change against the baseline (2010); and (2) TRP Suspension (2018–2023), comparing outcomes with the tariff increases against a counterfactual of retaining 2017 tariff rates, which were the lower tariffs. The model uses detailed import data by product and source country, along with import demand elasticities, to estimate how a tariff cut or hike would change import quantities, government tariff revenue, and welfare (measured by consumer surplus). For instance, for normal goods that exhibit a price elasticity, lowering a tariff should increase demand (and import volumes) but reduce per-unit tariff revenue. Raising a tariff would do the opposite, all else being equal. The results from the PE model quantify the effects on a product-by-product basis. Notably, the model captures the largest trading partners (like China and Australia) to see how their exports to Papua New Guinea were affected by tariff changes, and it computes the net welfare impact (consumer surplus change) for Papua New Guinea's consumers from each policy change.

Gravity modelling (ex-post analysis): A standard bilateral gravity model of trade was employed to quantify how changes in tariffs affected Papua New Guinea's import volumes. This model relates Papua New Guinea's observed import values to various factors: (i) the tariff level on each product, (ii) the economic size of trading partners, and (iii) trade frictions. In formal terms, for each HS-6 product and partner country, the specification regresses the log of import value on the applied tariff (trade-weighted average) and controls. The regression includes partner GDP (to capture market size), distance between

Papua New Guinea and the partner (a proxy for transport cost), a dummy for whether the partner has a free trade agreement (FTA) with Papua New Guinea (e.g., Australia under PACER Plus, or other regional agreements), and a measure of the partner's regulatory quality (to proxy overall trade facilitation). Year fixed effects control for global shocks, and product-group fixed effects capture commodity-specific factors. This approach isolates the partial effect of tariff changes on imports, holding other factors constant. The gravity model estimation provides an elasticity of import demand with respect to tariff changes and indicates whether Papua New Guinea's recent tariff hikes or cuts significantly altered import behaviour.

## Impacts by product category

The gravity model analysis shows that tariffs do have a negative relationship with import volumes, but the elasticity is modest. Specifically, a 1 per cent increase in tariffs (ad valorem) is associated with a 2.1 per cent decrease in import value on average, holding other factors constant (see details in Annex 3). This implies that Papua New Guinea's import demand is somewhat price-sensitive, but this effect can be overshadowed by other forces. The results from the gravity model further suggest that tariff increases have a more significant effect in dampening the demand for importing raw materials and consumables. On the other hand, tariffs had little measured impact on intermediate goods imports, possibly because industries had no close substitutes for those inputs. For capital goods, the model found a positive correlation with tariffs, which is not causal but rather reflects that many capital goods imports in Papua New Guinea enjoy duty exemptions (for priority infrastructure or investment projects). This finding aligns with the practice of exempting crucial imports; for example, mining equipment or electricity generators might be exempted, meaning tariff hikes do not reduce those imports.

Import composition showed a strong shift towards consumer goods during the liberalization period. From 2012 to 2017, the share of consumer goods in total imports increased from 28 per cent to 41 per cent during the TRP, eventually reaching 46 per cent by 2023 (Figure 16). This structural change reflects both the direct effects of tariff reductions on consumer goods and the indirect effects of improved purchasing power driving consumption growth. The rising share of consumer goods imports could reflect increased demand for foreign consumer products as tariffs

fell and Papua New Guinea's purchasing power grew modestly. It might also indicate that local manufacturing did not expand to meet consumer needs, so imports filled the gap over time. Intermediate goods showed a gradual expansion from an average 12.5 per cent share during the TRP to 16 per cent following the suspension of the TRP, indicating continued dependence on imported inputs for domestic production processes. On the other hand, raw material imports experienced the opposite trend, with their share in total imports halving from 13 per cent to 7 per cent during the TRP and remaining at these low levels after suspending the TRP (Figure 16). The decline in raw material imports could be due to the expansion of Papua New Guinea's agriculture and resource sectors to supply more raw inputs locally, or due to the stagnating manufacturing (which uses raw materials) which required fewer imported inputs.

## Impact by sector

Examination of agriculture, mining, and manufacturing performance, using the gross value added (GVA) index over the past two decades, reveals that the tariff policy alone did not generate much change to the performance of major sectors of the economy. Imports can contribute to fiscal revenue, but usually through the imposition of GST most directly, while expansion of the economy will lead to higher income tax, corporate tax and GST collection through the expansion of spending.

Lowering tariff rates may reduce tariff revenue collection or increase it, depending on the elasticities of demand. Despite the evidence, holding all factors constant, it is interesting to simulate the expected changes in revenue from tariff changes. During the TRP, as tariff rates declined, revenue from import duties would be expected to drop proportionally. Using the partial equilibrium model, the modelled results estimate that tariff revenue in 2010–2018 would decline only marginally, holding flows of trade constant. Specifically, the partial equilibrium simulation estimated that the tariff reductions under TRP caused about a \$7.8 million annual revenue loss by 2018, relative to what revenue would have been without those cuts. To put this in context, this would present a shortfall in just over 1 per cent of tariff revenue collected, almost insignificant in terms of the overall fiscal revenue stream. This small impact is because many of Papua New Guinea's imports were either zero-rated already or not strongly affected by slight tariff changes (especially given import contraction in those years). Additionally, the GST in Papua New Guinea applies to imports, so as tariffs fell, GST collected on a possibly higher import base could offset some losses (Figure 22).

After 2018, with the TRP suspension, the expectation was that fiscal revenue would increase when tariffs rise. On a static basis, higher rates on the same import volume should yield more revenue. $^{6}$ However, two dynamic effects played out: volume reductions (imports of some items fell because of higher prices) and policy reversals on specific items. The simulation for 2018–2023 shows a somewhat surprising result: tariff revenues were about \$15.6 million lower with the post-2018 tariff increases than they would have been if Papua New Guinea had maintained its baseline 2018 levels of tariffs. In other words, raising tariffs did not raise revenue. A deep dive into the results reveals the

primary reason for this outcome: Papua New Guinea slashed the tariff rate on tobacco soon after raising other tariffs. Tobacco is a top revenue-generating import (due to high volume and previously very high rates), so when its duty was reduced from 159 per cent to 58 per cent, the government forewent a huge amount of potential revenue on that item. Essentially, Papua New Guinea suspended the TRP “to gain revenue,” but then undermined that goal by cutting taxes on one of its most fiscal generating tariff lines (Figure 22).

Overall, tariff liberalization cost Papua New Guinea little in forgone revenue, while the attempt to reverse it did not yield the expected revenue gains. A

reason behind such unexpected results is collection efficiency and behaviour. It is possible that higher tariffs encouraged more evasion or smuggling, meaning the import volumes reported (and taxed) declined disproportionately. For instance, a 150 per cent tariff creates a strong incentive to under-declare, circumvent or smuggle. Papua New Guinea's large informal cross-

border trade (e.g. along the Indonesian border or via sea routes) could become a channel for tariff evasion when rates rise too steeply (Conroy, 2021). This suggests Papua New Guinea's optimal tariff (for revenue purposes) on many goods might be at a relatively low rate, where imports flow without significant distortions.

## F. Consumer welfare

Trade policy has direct consequences for consumer welfare through its impact on prices and product availability. The welfare effects of the TRP can be assessed using consumer surplus, a measure of the benefit consumers obtain from being able to purchase goods at certain prices, derived from partial equilibrium simulations. In theory, consumer welfare outcomes depend on the balance between short-term price pressures caused by tariffs and the longer-terms domestic industry development. In the short term, higher tariffs on finished products could lead to an increase in consumer prices for imported goods, thus reducing purchasing power, especially for lower income groups. Over the longer term, consumers may benefit from improved access to locally produced substitutes if protective tariffs succeed in stimulating domestic industries, they could

contribute to price stabilization, fostering import substitution, and strengthening local manufacturing capacity, thereby reducing dependence on expensive imports. However, there is also a risk that reduced competition from imports may limit product variety and quality in the domestic market, particularly if domestic industries do not simultaneously improve their production standards and capabilities.

The consumer welfare impact of the tariff policies was heavily impacted by the radical change in a few product groups. During the Papua New Guinea TRP implementation period (2010–2018), one would expect consumer welfare to improve because tariffs coming down should lower prices. Indeed, many categories of consumer goods did see tariff reductions (the average tariff on consumer goods

fell from \~23 per cent to \~14 per cent). However, the simulation results show a net welfare loss of around \$5.9 million for Papua New Guinea consumers in this period (Figure 30). This seemingly paradoxical outcome can be explained by the structure of the tariff changes. While hundreds of products had small tariff cuts, a few products retained very high tariffs, which resulted in large negative effects. Specifically, throughout the TRP, Papua New Guinea kept high tariffs on beverages (alcohol, soft drinks) and tobacco. The welfare analysis indicates that this single policy (high tariffs on alcohol and tobacco) caused an estimated \$6.5 million loss in consumer surplus. Beyond beverages and tobacco products, Papua New Guinea increased tariffs on about \$390 million of other goods, lifting average rates from 5 per cent to 14 per cent, which contributed to a welfare loss of \$1 million. The total consumer welfare loss, therefore, outweighed the \$1.6 million gain consumers enjoyed from all the other tariff reductions. This finding is a reminder of the need for consideration of tariff impacts on items of common consumption or high expenditure with overall consumer welfare impact.

A critical observation is that conventional consumer welfare calculations from tariff reduction often assume that greater consumption, driven by lower prices, translates into higher welfare. However, this overlooks the negative externalities of harmful goods. For example, reduced tariffs on tobacco and alcoholic beverages may lower prices and increase consumption, which models interpret as a welfare gain. In reality, such increases can impose significant health costs on individuals and society,

such as higher medical expenditures, reduced productivity, and long-term public health burdens. These adverse effects are not captured in standard surplus-based measures, which focus narrowly on changes in prices and quantities consumed. As a result, the calculated welfare improvement may overstate the true benefits of tariff reductions when the products involved carry detrimental health or social consequences. This highlights the need to integrate broader social cost considerations alongside economic models when evaluating tariff policies.

Another dimension of welfare is the variety and quality of products available. Under a liberal trade regime, consumers enjoy greater choice – more brands, newer products – whereas high tariffs can limit choices to expensive local substitutes. While it is challenging to quantify the impact of increasing variety and choice, survey responses indicate that 55 per cent of businesses believe Papua New Guinea consumers prefer cheaper imported goods to higher-priced local ones (Figure 31), which further highlights the importance of price and variety for consumers. If tariffs force consumers to buy local products that are more expensive or of lower quality, that would result in a welfare loss not fully captured in the model.

Consumer welfare in Papua New Guinea suffered during the tariff reductions phase mainly due to selective high taxation on certain goods, and it improved slightly after 2018 solely because the tobacco tariff was cut. The lesson for policy is that extreme levels of tariff rates on popular consumption goods can cause loss to social and consumer welfare. A more balanced approach involving moderate tariffs across a broad base might achieve protection or revenue goals with less welfare damage.

Complementary measures such as excise taxes are more suitable for addressing public health concerns, for example in the case of tobacco. Stability in tariff policy is also important, as frequent shifts between high and low rates create uncertainty and uneven impacts on households. Continuous monitoring of tariff impacts across different segments of the economy is therefore essential to ensure that fiscal objectives or sectoral protection do not come at the expense of consumer welfare.

# Conclusions and Recommendations


Papua New Guinea embarked on the TRP as part of a broader post-crisis reform agenda, aiming to improve economic efficiency and integrate with global markets. The policy was intended to improve access to a larger variety of imported inputs and final products at lower cost, while exposing Papua New Guinea's domestic industries to international competition, thereby encouraging productivity gains and deliver consumer benefits.

The evidence from this review reveals that tariff policy alone cannot determine economic development outcomes in contexts where structural constraints fundamentally limit competitiveness. Papua New Guinea's businesses face high transport costs, energy expenses, and regulatory complexity that often dwarf the effects of tariff modifications, suggesting that successful development strategies must also address these underlying constraints to deliver more results from trade policy instruments like tariffs.

Nevertheless, trade policy remains an important component of the government policy toolbox for a comprehensive development strategy. Predictability emerges as perhaps the most important characteristic of effective trade policy, with businesses consistently emphasizing the importance of stable policy environments for long-term planning and investment. Whether tariff rates are high or low matters less than whether they are consistent and reliable over the time horizons required for building productive capacity -allowing businesses to predict and plan accordingly.

Tariff policy also operates withing wider regional and global frameworks. Free trade agreements with significant markets nearby, including Australia and New Zealand, and far away – with the European Union – reduce the policy space for tariff increases but provide predictability and the ability to tackle existing non-tariff barriers to trade.

Papua New Guinea's natural competitive advantages in agriculture, forestry, and mineral resources provide a foundation for economic growth and sustainable development. It is, therefore, important that trade policy should support and enhance existing strengths rather than attempting to create artificial advantages in sectors lacking competitiveness.

Moving forward, Papua New Guinea should aim to formulate a tariff policy that balances development objectives with fiscal revenue needs, while taking account of the economy's reliance on imported inputs. The following recommendations are suggested:

## 1. Restore a path of gradual tariff rationalization

The Department of Treasury should consider resuming tariff reductions in a gradual, predictable manner. A phased approach over several years – for example, five to 10 years, similar to an FTA phasing out process – can help industries adjust, prepare for the changes and make necessary investments. The objective should be to establish a simpler, more uniform tariff structure, perhaps with a low single-digit uniform rate for most goods, removing nuisance tariffs, and a limited number of truly sensitive items at a slightly higher rate. This would reduce trade distortions and administrative complexity. Extremely high tariffs – e.g., above 40 per cent – could be counterproductive in both revenue and development terms (as they may encourage under-invoicing, smuggling, and may harm a variety of products for consumers). Generally, specific tariffs (covering nearly 7 per cent of imports for agricultural products and 2.2 per cent of imports for non-agricultural products in Papua New Guinea) are less transparent

## 2. Focus on fostering a competitive business environment

The government should pivot from protecting industries as a framework to enable them to compete. While adding additional layers of costs will undoubtedly provide some shielding from competition, this can have significant implications for consumers, businesses and ultimately growth. Papua New Guinea needs to focus on tackling the high-cost structure to do business on the island. Investments in infrastructure (roads, ports, electricity) are critical – e.g., improving transport could directly address the 94 per cent of firms citing logistics as their top constraint (Figure 32). Similarly, expanding a reliable and affordable power supply would benefit all manufacturers more than a tariff hike ever could. In fact, these are among the top policy recommendations to strengthen the industry's competitiveness as suggested by Papua New Guinea firms during the business surveys. Lowering input costs is another quick win: for instance, eliminate or reduce tariffs on key intermediate inputs and capital goods (many of which are already low or zero). This would make it cheaper to produce “Made in Papua New Guinea” goods and services. With respect to product categorisation, Papua New Guinea should align its classification of goods for tariff purposes with the UNCTAD classification and justify any deviations. For example, products could be grouped by end use (raw materials, intermediary inputs, capital goods, or consumption goods) and further disaggregate into categories such as “import competing”, “environmental concerns”, or “fiscal revenue generating”. These categories should reflect stakeholders’ priorities and trade-offs from tariffs across.

In addition, simplifying customs procedures and reducing red tape can lower the non-tariff barriers that often exceed the tariff in impact. Improving governance, increasing multi-stakeholder engagement, and improving the efficiency of government lie at the heart of such trade facilitation measures.

## 3. Use targeted support for infant industries

If the government identifies certain sectors as priorities for diversification (e.g. agro-processing, light manufacturing, downstream processing of natural resources), it should employ targeted, time-bound support measures rather than blanket import protection. However, empirical evidence often highlights that governments frequently lack both the information and the ability to effectively target industries, leading to prolonged inefficiencies and entrenched protectionism rather than temporary support. $^{7}$ In addition, a usual byproduct of tariffs is rent-seeking (or engaging in nonproductive efforts to extract resources and wealth) and other political economy considerations. $^{8}$ Such political capture can entrench tariffs, reduce overall economic efficiency and complicate subsequent liberalization efforts.

A targeted support needs to be carefully identified and engineered. The types of support could include subsidies on inputs, tax holidays, credit facilities for capital investment, infrastructure investments, or technical assistance programs to improve quality and productivity. For example, instead of a 25 per cent tariff on dairy that raises consumer prices, a targeted program to provide dairy farmers with feed or veterinary services at subsidized cost would directly boost their productivity. Any protection that is provided should come with a clear sunset clause and performance benchmarks (as true infant industry support requires a path to competitiveness, not indefinite shelter). Engaging in public-private dialogues can help identify what specific support is most needed in each

sector, as suggested through the survey results, whereby many firms would prefer specific support over import tariffs.

## 4. Safeguard consumer welfare and food security

Tariff policy should be calibrated to avoid undue burden on consumers, especially for essential goods. Papua New Guinea has a relatively low purchasing power and a high poverty rate, and many households spend a large share of their income on food and essentials. $^{9}$ High tariffs on staple foods or fuel are therefore regressive. It is recommended that essential goods (basic food items, medicine, fuel) be kept at low or zero tariffs to protect consumers' purchasing power. For goods like rice, flour, or tinned fish that are daily necessities, the government could explore buffer stocks or price stabilization schemes rather than high tariffs to support local producers. Where there are health or social reasons to tax a product (e.g. alcohol, tobacco), using excise taxes might achieve the goal without distorting trade since excise duties apply to both imports and local products and can be better targeted for health policy.

## 5. Enhance regional integration efforts

Lowering tariffs is only one aspect of openness – equally important is reducing non-tariff barriers and actively leveraging trade agreements. Papua New Guinea should continue to strengthen its customs service capacity to facilitate trade, for example, by adopting digital customs clearance, risk-based inspections, and

working with neighbours to streamline border processes. This will reduce costs for importers and exporters alike. Papua New Guinea could strengthen its position by leveraging existing agreements such as the European Union interim EPA, MSG arrangements, and PACER Plus, while also considering participation in broader frameworks like the Association of Southeast Asian Nations Plus 1 (ASEAN+1) and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), agreements. This would require deeper commitments from Papua New Guinea, but could yield significant market access. A final avenue to explore for Papua New Guinea is to capitalize on its success in implementing the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement by extending efforts to other plurilateral agreements recently adopted in the framework of the WTO, such as the e-commerce joint statement initiative, the Domestic Regulations of Trade in Services, Investment Facilitation for Development, the older Information Technology Agreements I and II, and the Government Procurement Agreement. Implementing these agreements will help lower the cost of trade, improve the ability to source critical inputs for Papua New Guinea's industry, and raise efficiency for exploring new channels and opportunities in trade. Finally, Papua New Guinea should consider acceding to the Framework Agreement on the Facilitation of Cross-border Paperless Trade in Asia and the Pacific (CPTA) in order to facilitate the trade facilitation digitalization processes, which have been shown to have significant results in reducing trade costs. $^{10}$

## 6. Institutionalize evidence-based review mechanisms

The tariff changes in 2018–2019 were made through budget announcements without extensive public analysis. Going forward, Treasury would benefit from a more institutionalized review process for tariff policy. For instance, a Tariff Advisory Board could be established, including government, private sector, and independent experts, to evaluate requests for tariff changes and periodically assess outcomes. Regular impact assessments (such as the present report) should inform adjustments. Such a body could help gather evidence to inform policy formulation and ensure that any tariff protection is granted based on clear evidence of need and with a plan for phase-out. Transparency in tariff policymaking will also reassure investors that the environment will not shift unpredictably.

## 7. Invest in the capacity to monitor, investigate and deploy trade defense mechanisms

In the survey conducted under this review, the business community raised the view that unfair trade practices from some countries were posing a threat to their competitiveness. Papua New Guinea could adopt pending legislation on anti-dumping, countervailing, and safeguard measures, supported by a transparent legal framework. A specialised trade remedies unit with expertise in economics, law, and data analysis would strengthen monitoring and investigation capacity. Partnerships with regional and international organisations could provide training and technical support, helping ensure that Papua New Guinea can apply WTO-consistent measures when necessary. The last comprehensive call for this came in the UNCTAD Trade Policy Framework of 2006. $^{11}$ According to the Review, a draft Anti-dumping and Countervailing Measures Act and a draft Safeguards Measures Act were to be adopted, but this does not appear to have happened. $^{12}$ By adopting the regulation and strengthening its institutional framework to undertake investigations, Papua New Guinea can protect vulnerable industries, mitigate economic shocks from external dumping, and navigate trade relations with greater confidence and authority.

Papua New Guinea stands at a crossroads in trade policy. The evidence indicates that while protective tariffs may offer short-term relief or revenue, they are not a sustainable strategy for long-term development.
A return to an outward-oriented trade policy, complemented by strong domestic investments in infrastructure, education, and governance, is likely to yield the best

outcomes in terms of growth, diversification, and consumer welfare. Papua New Guinea's own experience from 2010 to 2017 shows that it can successfully integrate with the world while bringing benefits home. The policy reversals of 2018 to 2023, meanwhile, serve as a caution that protectionism can be a costly detour. The recommended path forward is one of open but strategic trade engagement by keeping the economy open to gain efficiency, while using smart policies at home to build the competitiveness of industries so they can thrive in the global marketplace without needing high tariff walls. This balanced approach will support Papua New Guinea's aspirations for diversified, inclusive, and sustainable development.

## Review of the Papua New Guinea Tariff Reduction Program

## A. BACKGROUND

In 1986, the Bogan Review covered Papua New Guinea Tariff Policy and Administration in Papua New Guinea, which resulted in the release of the 1997 White Paper on the Tariff Reform Program. Subsequently, the first Tariff Reduction Program (TRP) began in 1999. The TRP was aimed at encouraging a more efficient and productive private sector through greater exposure to international competition. As part of the reform, duty rates for most goods (three-quarters) were set at zero, with the exception of goods that are produced in Papua New Guinea or could potentially be produced in Papua New Guinea. The duty rates for goods that are produced or could potentially be produced in Papua New Guinea, the duty rates were combined into three categories: intermediate, protective, and prohibitive rates. The three categories of duty rates were reduced in three scheduled steps at a rate of 5 per cent.

The introduction of the Value Added Tax (now the Goods and Services Tax) was designed and introduced to ensure that there would be no concern over revenue losses arising from the TRP. The revenue collection mechanism for GST became a more modern and efficient approach to the collection of indirect taxes.

In the lead-up to the 2003 National Budget, the Manufacturers' Council of Papua New Guinea sought a temporary suspension of the TRP. The government responded by commissioning two reviews of the Tariff Reduction Program, conducted separately in 2003 and 2007, to establish the future direction of tariff reduction policy for Papua New Guinea.

Both reviews confirmed that the TRP had achieved its intended purposes and recommended that the government implement further tariff reduction, aimed at achieving more uniform as well as lower tariff rates for a more uniform effective rate of protection. Following these reviews, the government implemented a scheduled or phased tariff reduction in three-year intervals, which commenced in 2012 and ended in 2017. Prior to 2018, a proposal was made to carry out a review into the last phase of TRP implementation and to recommend the next step going forward. However, the proposal was unsuccessful due to funding issues. Therefore, in the 2018 Budget, the government suspended the TRP and maintained the 2017 TRP rates in 2018 pending the review.

Whilst the TRP is suspended, the government introduced several changes to specific tariff items, mainly to protect infant local manufacturers from cheap imported products. The amendments were made to the Customs Tariff Act 1990 in the 2018, 2019 and 2020 National Budgets. ## Protection measures

A first step consists of preparing the simple average and weighted average tariffs by sector over time. Dispersion rates, maximum and minimum rates will also be presented by sector to illustrate the level of protection, using the published tariff rates at the HS-6-digit level (or a more detailed level, depending on the availability of data provided by Papua New Guinea) $^{17}$ to avoid underestimation of the level of tariff protection.

The simple average tariff is calculated by adding the tariffs on all lines and dividing by the number of those tariff lines. In other words, each tariff line has the same weight irrespective of its economic importance. The simple average tariff provides a simple indication of how much protection is applied by an economy or region, on average. Higher values indicate a more protected economy, lower values a less protected economy.

$$
\bar {\tau} = \frac {\sum_ {i} \tau_ {i}}{n}
$$

Where $\tau$ indicates the tariff applied to specific product i, and n is the number of products.

The weighted average tariff is calculated as the average rate of duty per imported value unit. Like the simple average, the weighted average tariff tells us how much protection is applied by an economy or region, on average. Higher values indicate a more protected economy, lower values a less protected economy. The difference is that the weighted average tariff considers the volume of imports in each product category. $^{18}$ The weighted average tariff is normally lower than the simple average tariff because products with high tariffs are normally imported less, while products with low tariffs are imported more. In other words, high tariffs tend to deflect imports and thus lower the tariff average. $^{19}$

$$
\bar {\tau} = \sum w _ {k} \tau_ {k}
$$

where k indexes imported goods and is the weight given to tariff k in the average, calculated as the share of goods k in the country's overall imports.

Dispersion. Average tariffs (both simple and weighted) can be considered as not truly reflecting the level of protection. $^{20}$ As an alternative, the dispersion of tariffs provides the level of distortion of tariffs to trade: the higher the dispersion, the greater the distortion. The dispersion of tariffs can be captured by using the standard deviation (see below) to show how tariffs are widely spread out (compared to the average tariff). $^{21}$ The standard deviation ( $\sigma$ ) is calculated as below:

$$
\sigma = \sqrt {\frac {1}{N} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {N} (\tau_ {k} - \bar {\tau}) ^ {2}}
$$

## Impact analysis

For the ex-post analysis, a standard bilateral gravity model of trade was applied to quantify how changes in Papua New

Guinea's tariffs affected its import volumes. This model relates Papua New Guinea's import values to various factors: (i) the tariff level on each product, (ii) the economic size of trading partners, and (iii) trade frictions. In formal terms, for each HS-6 product and partner country, the model regresses the log of import value on the applied tariff (trade-weighted average) and relevant controls. The regression includes partner GDP (to capture market size), distance between Papua New Guinea and the partner (a proxy for transport cost), a dummy for whether the partner has a free trade agreement (FTA) with Papua New Guinea (e.g. Australia under PACER Plus, or other regional agreements), and a measure of the partner's regulatory quality (to proxy overall trade facilitation). Year fixed effects control for global shocks, and product-group fixed effects capture commodity-specific factors. This approach isolates the partial effect of tariff changes on imports, holding other factors constant. The gravity model estimation provides an elasticity of import demand with respect to tariff changes and indicates whether Papua New Guinea's recent tariff hikes or cuts significantly altered import behaviour.

For the ex-ante analysis, a Partial Equilibrium Simulation Model was applied to complement the econometric approach. The model is similar to the World Bank's SMART model, which is used to simulate the impact of tariff changes on trade, welfare, and revenue. Two scenarios were examined: (1) TRP Implementation (2010–2017) – comparing outcomes with the tariff reductions against a counterfactual of no tariff change; and (2) TRP Suspension (2018–2023) – comparing outcomes with the tariff increases against a counterfactual of retaining the lower tariffs. The model uses detailed import data by product and source country, along with import demand elasticities, to estimate how a tariff cut or hike would change import quantities, government tariff revenue, and consumer surplus. For instance, lowering a tariff should increase import volume (and consumer surplus) but reduce per-unit revenue, while raising a tariff would do the opposite, all else equal. These simulations draw on data from ITC MacMap for tariff rates and UN Comtrade for trade values. They allow us to quantify effects on a product-by-product basis and aggregate to sector or economy-wide totals. Notably, the model captures the largest trading partners (like China and Australia) to see how their exports to Papua New Guinea were affected, and it computes the net welfare impact (consumer surplus change) for Papua New Guinea's consumers from each policy change.

For the underlying data, Papua New Guinea's official import data was cross-checked against international sources, and some discrepancies were noted. For example, 2014 showed an anomalously large import value in Papua New Guinea's customs data (due to a one-off inclusion of \~\$11.4 billion in petroleum imports), which did not appear in international data sets, nor in mirror statistics. Adjustments were made in the analyses to account for such anomalies, ensuring they did not skew the results. All monetary values are in US dollars at current prices unless stated otherwise. Where averages or changes in “tariffs” are mentioned, these refer to trade-weighted ad valorem tariffs, providing a sense of effective protection.

By combining these methods – regression analysis for observed historical impact and simulation for scenario assessment – a robust picture is obtained of how the TRP influenced Papua New Guinea's trade outcomes and what the effects of reversing it have been. The following sections present the findings on trade flows, sectoral performance, welfare, revenue, and business sentiment, each supported by evidence from the data and the models.

## Economic modelling strategy


The modelling is inevitably influenced by the availability and quality of data provided. As such, the modelling strategy at the inception phase proposes several avenues for investigating the relationships between tariff changes and relevant economic aggregates, with some approaches requiring less ambition underlying data. The analysis begins with less data-intensive models and increases toward more granular results that require more refined data sets.

## Partial equilibrium model

The partial equilibrium (PE) model framework is a useful modelling tool since it demands a minimal amount of data and is relatively easy to understand and use. In this case,

the policy instrument is treated as a tariff, and the underlying assumption is that the country is a small country that has no influence on world prices (influence on world prices would be expected from a large supplier). In this model, where the country is a price taker, the objective will be to estimate the effects of introducing higher tariffs on prices, demand, and supply for the product directly affected by the measure.

In contrast to a general equilibrium model, which considers the whole economy, including potential interactions across all markets, the advantage of partial equilibrium modelling is the level of detail that can be examined. Negotiation of trade agreements rarely encompasses product group level blanket tariff cuts, such found in the general equilibrium context, which takes an average tariff cut for an entire sector, thereby leading to varying degrees of aggregation bias, with potentially generalised conclusions. These biases are minimised in the PE model. It is usually, therefore, helpful to use a PE model to get a better understanding of the product line (and sector trade flow effects), but also to quantify the effects on macroeconomic aggregates and capture cross-sectoral effects arising from liberalisation.

To split imports across different exporters, the well-known Armington equation is used, where the shift parameter, import shares of each country, and the degree of substitution between imports from different countries are the key variables. From the model, it is straightforward to determine the changes in imports, tariff revenue, and welfare following differential changes in bilateral tariffs.

A basic PE model essentially has three equations, specifying demand, supply, and a market-clearing equation that demand and supply must equate (equations (1) to (3)). Additionally, the price linkage equation (4) shows how import prices relate to export prices.

$$
M = \alpha_ {M} P M ^ {\varepsilon}\tag{1}
$$

$$
X = \alpha_ {X} P X ^ {\mu}\tag{2}
$$

$$
X = M\tag{3}
$$

$$
P M = P X \times (1 + t)\tag{4}
$$

$$
T R = P X \times t \times M\tag{5}
$$

$$
\Delta W M = \int_ {P M _ {1}} ^ {P M _ {0}} \alpha_ {M} P M ^ {\varepsilon} d P M = \frac {\alpha_ {M}}{\varepsilon + 1} \left(P M _ {0} ^ {\varepsilon + 1} - P M _ {1} ^ {\varepsilon + 1}\right)\tag{6}
$$

$$
\Delta W X = \int_ {P X _ {0}} ^ {P X _ {1}} \alpha_ {X} P X ^ {\varepsilon} d P X = \frac {\alpha_ {X}}{\mu + 1} \left(P X _ {1} ^ {\mu + 1} - P X _ {0} ^ {\mu + 1}\right)\tag{7}
$$

Where M is imports, X exports, PM price of imports, PX price of exports, $\alpha_{M}>0$ , and $\alpha_{X}>0$ are shift parameters, $\varepsilon<0$ and $\mu>0$ are the elasticities of import demand and export supply, and t is the ad valorem tariff rate. TR is tariff revenue. $\Delta WM$ and $\Delta WX$ are the changes in the gains for importing and exporting countries, respectively.

Given the parameter values $\varepsilon$ , $\mu$ , $\alpha_{M}$ , $\alpha_{X}$ and the policy variable t, equations (1) to (4) are a system of four equations in four unknowns, PM, PX, M, and X. Once the equilibrium prices and quantities are determined, one can determine the components of economic surplus — equations (5), (6) and (7).

This model is a helpful ex-ante check on the direction and estimated trade effects arising from tariff changes. The modelling does not involve significant data challenges and is expected to be possible. A ready-built tool partial equilibrium model, based on John Gilbert (2017) is proposed for us, $^{22}$ which is structurally similar to the SMART model in the World Bank's WITS. The model is quite flexible and can be extended to include imports from different sources as well as allow the tariffs to vary by source of imports using Armington elasticities. Extensions to the model allow for the inclusion of several parameters, e.g. inclusion of Non-Tariff Measures (NTMs) AVE in the simulations.

## Gravity model

A common approach for estimating the impact of tariff changes on export performance is to use a gravity model of trade. Gravity models enjoy a robust theoretical foundation $^{23}$ and remains to this day one of the most popular approaches to estimate the flow of international trade between countries, based on a host of variables which attract or repel countries to trade with each other, such as the size of economies, their geographical distance, common languages and legal systems, etc.

Building on the gravity model framework developed by Anderson & Van Wincoop (2003), $^{24}$ which provides a theoretically sound foundation for analysing trade flows. Extending this by incorporating multilateral resistance terms (MRTs) to correct for third-country effects and extending it by incorporating sectoral-level tariff effects, the following approach used by Liu (2020) is considered, $^{25}$ enabling a more disaggregated analysis of tariff impacts on specific industries.

According to Anderson & Van Wincoop (2003), a gravity model accounts for bilateral trade costs and the global resistance factor:

$$
X _ {i j} = \frac {Y _ {i} Y _ {j}}{Y _ {w}} \bigg (\frac {t _ {i j}}{\pi_ {i} P _ {j}} \bigg) 1 ^ {- \sigma}
$$

Where, $X_{ij} Y_{i} Y_{j}$ are GDP of exporting and importing countries $Y_{w}$ is the world GDP $t_{ij}$ are trade (including tariffs, $\pi_{i} P_{j}$ are Multilateral Resistance terms (MRTs) and is the elasticity of substitution.

Following Liu (2020), the gravity model is extended to a sectoral level, estimating the impact of tariffs on different industries. This extension is presented in the equation below:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {l n X _ {s, i j} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} l n G D P _ {i} + \beta_ {2} l n G D P _ {j} + \beta_ {3} l n D I S T _ {i j} + \beta_ {4} l n T a r i f f _ {i j} + \beta_ {5} l n R T A _ {i j} +} \\ & {\beta_ {6} l n C o n t i g u i t y _ {i j} + \beta_ {7} C o m m o n l a n g u a g e _ {i j} + \beta_ {8} l n P o p l u a t i o n _ {j t} + \beta_ {9} l n P o p u l a t i o n _ {i t} +} \\ & {\beta_ {1 0} l n P o p l u a t i o n _ {j t} + \beta_ {1 1} l n c o n t r o l s _ {i j} + \delta_ {i} + \delta_ {j} + \epsilon_ {s, i j}} \end{array}
$$

$X_{s,ij}$ is Exports of sector s from country i to j, $GDP_{1}$ and $GDP_{j}$ are the GDPs of exporting and importing countries, bilateral Distance between countries and tariff rate applied to sector s in trade between i and j. $InPopulation_{jt}$ is Natural logarithm of an exporter's or importer's population $InContiguity_{ij}$ is a dummy if countries share a boarder and $Common\ language_{ij}$ dummy if countries share a common language

Anderson emphasises the importance of controlling for third-country effects, which is implemented by including $\delta_{i}$ and $\delta_{j}$ which represent country-specific trade barriers, which proxy for unobservable MRTs.

Such a model can be used to measure the effects of tariff change on driving bilateral trade between Papua New Guinea and its trading partners, with the sign of the elasticity of $\beta_{4}$ expected to be negative, as a tariff increase (reduction) will lead to a

decrease (increase) in exports between a trading partner and Papua New Guinea.

The gravity model is helpful to explain changes in trade flows ex-post, but it does not help to identify output, employment or productivity changes from tariffs, which is of fundamental importance for this study. As such, this model is not proposed to be prioritised over others, unless data availability proves to be overly challenging to use other modelling efforts.

## Methodology and data

This section presents the detailed statistics and estimates of the impacts of tariff changes on Papua New Guinea imports.

A log-linear regression model is employed to examine the impact of tariffs on Papua New Guinea imports. The specification is as below:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathrm{In(Imports)} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \mathrm{Tariff} + \sum_ {\mathrm{j}} \beta_ {\mathrm{j}} \mathrm{Tariff} * \mathrm{SoP} _ {\mathrm{k}}} \\ & {\qquad + \alpha_ {2} + \alpha_ {1} \mathrm{In(GDP)} + \alpha_ {3} \mathrm{In(distance)} + \alpha_ {4} \mathrm{FTA} + \alpha_ {5} \mathrm{RQI} + \mu_ {\mathrm{t}} + \varphi_ {\mathrm{k}} + \epsilon} \end{array}
$$

Where

\- “Imports” denotes annual import value into Papua New Guinea at the Harmonised System (HS) 6-digit level;

\- Tariff denotes trade-weighted tariff at the HS 6-digit level;

\- SoP denotes commodity group;

\- GDP denotes the GDP of Papua New Guinea's partner countries;

\- Distance is the geographic distance (in kilometres) between Papua New Guinea and its partner countries;

\- FTA is a dummy variable indicating the existence of a regional trade agreement between Papua New Guinea and its partner countries;

\- RQI is the Regulatory Quality Index of Papua New Guinea's partner countries, capturing the ability of a government to formulate and implement sound policies and regulations that permit and promote private sector development;

\- $\mu_{t}$ denotes year fixed effects and denotes commodity group fixed effects. They are incorporated into the model as different dummy variables.

The model is estimated using annual trade data at the HS 6-digit level over multiple years (details to be discussed later). For simplicity, subscripts for partner country, HS6 product, and year are suppressed in the notation above.

A log-linear model is used for this estimation. The main

reasons for this model selection include:

\- A log-linear specification is preferred over Poisson Pseudo-Maximum Likelihood (PPML) because the dataset includes only imports into Papua New Guinea, rather than bilateral trade flows as in a typical gravity model. Since zero import values are not widespread, the log-linear model is appropriate and directly provides the elasticity of trade with respect to tariffs. By contrast, PPML is generally advantageous in settings with a high prevalence of zero trade flows and heteroskedasticity, as it accommodates zeros without transformation and yields consistent estimates under heteroskedastic errors.

\- The log-linear model is estimated with clustered standard errors at the partner level, allowing for arbitrary correlation of residuals across product categories and years within each partner country. This adjustment accounts for unobserved partner-specific shocks (e.g., macroeconomic or policy changes) that may simultaneously affect multiple trade flows, thereby ensuring consistent inference.

\- SoP dummies are included to capture differences in both tariff responsiveness and import values across commodity groups.

\- Time dummies control for year-specific shocks affecting all trade flows.

\- Given the data availability, partner-specific variables such as GDP, distance, FTA, and RQI are incorporated as they are key determinants of imports.

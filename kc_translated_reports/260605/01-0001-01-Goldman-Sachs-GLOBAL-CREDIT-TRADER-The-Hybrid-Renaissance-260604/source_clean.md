# The Hybrid Renaissance

# Hybrid supply: On pace to set new records, especially in the EUR market

The issuance tailwind behind the hybrid debt market—which began in early 2024 following a change to the Moody’s rating methodology—has accelerated in 2026. As shown in Exhibit 1, both the USD and EUR markets are on pace to set new annual records for hybrid gross supply.

In the USD market, there are now more than a dozen hybrid bonds with \$1 billion or more of notional outstanding—and some of the largest hybrid new issues were launched in recent months. The elevated gross supply of EUR hybrids partly reflects a more mature market, which has had a high share of bonds being called this year. For comparison, while gross supply is up 132% year to date vs. the same period last year, the notional amount outstanding of the EUR hybrid market has increased by a more modest 9% since the end of 2025.

Utilities have been among the most active sectors in both markets, accounting for 50% and 40% of hybrid supply in the USD and EUR markets, respectively, since 2024 (Exhibits 2 and 3).

# The drivers of hybrid issuance and investment

With the broader debt capital markets open to a wide range of borrowers, many market participants have questioned why an issuer might opt for a subordinated (higher coupon) hybrid instrument vs. a more traditional financing option in senior unsecured form.

Issuers in capital intensive industries—which often operate with large debt capital structures—tend to favor hybrid debt for two key reasons. First is the partial equity credit it receives from the rating agencies, which reflects its ‘equity like’ characteristics, including subordination in the capital structure and the ability to defer coupon payments without triggering a default. For a capital structure with limited ‘headroom’ within a targeted leverage or rating band, this partial equity credit can be attractive—especially since it does not involve diluting shareholders.

Second is the favorable tax deductibility of hybrid interest (relative to preferred stock, for example). For a profitable corporate taxpayer, the tax shield can help optimize the weighted average cost of capital—even if the hybrid debt has a moderately higher coupon.

The swift growth in the USD hybrid market has not derailed performance. As Exhibit 10 illustrates, the spread ratio of the USD Hybrid Index vs. both BBBs and BBs has compressed since early 2024—indicating hybrid outperformance.

In the EUR market, the recent performance backdrop for hybrids has been largely range-bound—at least compared to BBBs (Exhibit 11). This likely reflects, in our view, the more mature stage of the market and the significant periods of outperformance in prior years (most recently in 2022, for example).

# Hybrids generally trade structure risk for quality risk

For IG-focused investors searching for ways to capture incremental spread without deviating too far down the quality spectrum, hybrids can be an attractive solution given the spread premium they offer vs. senior debt.

This additional spread premia serves as ‘compensation’ for hybrids’ subordination as well as some issuer-friendly structural features, such as the ability to defer interest payments in certain situations (without triggering a default).

That said, determining whether this compensation is adequate requires a clear understanding of the company-specific capital management priorities for each hybrid issuer. It also requires a thorough analysis of the potential long-term fundamental headwinds facing the issuers' industries to evaluate the risk of financial distress (which may necessitate deferral of interest).

Very few investors expect hybrid issuers to actually utilize the provision to defer interest—especially if that issuer already has a large debt capital structure which requires frequent refinancing. The assumption is that deferring the hybrid’s coupon payments may make it more difficult—due to investor push back from overlapping buyer bases—to refinance the ‘regular’ (non-subordinated) debt when the time comes. In many instances, deferring a hybrid coupon may also prevent an issuer from paying its common equity dividend.

The value of this interest deferral provision to the issuer increases during times of economic weakness, which can often result in hybrids underperforming during risk-off moves (again, Exhibit 12 and 13).

# Quantifying the compensation for subordination

To quantify this compensation premium, we again use the USD and EUR hybrid indices mentioned earlier, comparing index-level average (and current) spreads relative to the broader BBB and BB markets. Leaving the important rating and industry compositional difference caveats aside, we show the index-level absolute spread differentials across regions in Exhibit 14 and 15.

For example, the absolute spread differential between USD hybrids and USD BBBs compressed from 99bp (on average) in 2023 to 61bp, currently (again, Exhibit 14). While this is narrower vs. a few years ago, it is consistent with the directional trend of spread tightening in the USD market. As a result, we view this index-level compensation as moderately attractive considering spreads for many markets are hovering at the tight end of the post-financial crisis history (Exhibits 37 and 38 show spread percentile ranks vs. history). We also expect the hybrid spread premium to compress as the market grows further.

In the EUR market, the hybrid vs. BBB spread differential is currently 53bp (again, Exhibit 15). This tighter spread differential likely reflects, in part, the more mature positioning of the EUR hybrid market, as investors and issuers have become very familiar with the structures over the past decade.

We do not see much scope for further absolute spread tightening (compression) in EUR hybrids, given the more challenging growth-inflation-monetary policy mix stemming from the ongoing energy shock. That said, the additional carry vs. EUR BBBs can be attractive for a range of investor types. Given our more cautious positioning in the EUR market (relative to USD), we would focus on higher-quality hybrid issuers in more economically resilient sectors.

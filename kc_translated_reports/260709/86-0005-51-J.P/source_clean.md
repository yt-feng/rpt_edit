# China monthly data outlook
## Production resilient, demand awaits fiscal execution

We expect some stabilization in June data after the April–May soft patch, but the recovery remains narrow and externally supported. PMIs improved modestly, exports remain helped by high-tech and AI-related demand, and lower oil prices eased cost pressure. Domestic demand weakened further: May retail sales fell 0.6% oya, FAI contracted 10.7% oya, infrastructure FAI dropped 9.4% oya, and real estate FAI declined 24.3% oya. The near-term question is whether fiscal execution can turn available funding into activity before external demand becomes less reliable. PMIs point to a floor in industrial momentum, not a new upswing. Production and orders improved, but employment, inventories, and export orders remain mixed, with little evidence of a broader restocking, hiring, or capex cycle. Lower input prices ease near-term margin pressure, but weak downstream demand still caps pricing power.

The recovery is still two-speed. Manufacturing competitiveness, trade rerouting, and the global IP/AI cycle continue to support exports and high-tech production. High-tech manufacturing rose 15.1% oya in May and contributed nearly 40% of industrial growth in January–May. But strength remains concentrated in electronics, high-tech manufacturing, and selected upstream sectors; consumer-related, housing-linked, and traditional industries remain weak.

External demand remains the main cushion, but there are risks. June exports may remain strong, supported by AI-related equipment, memory modules, new energy products, and trade rerouting. Yet tariffs, anti-subsidy cases, local-content rules, and carbon-related barriers could increasingly limit external absorption. If exports slow before domestic demand recovers, resilient production would translate into inventories, margin compression, and renewed producer-price weakness.

Domestic demand is the binding constraint. Trade-in subsidies appear to have pulled forward durable-goods purchases, while weak income expectations and confidence cap services upside. The latest activity mix points to an absorption problem: without faster fiscal spending and stronger household demand, resilient output risks feeding inventories and price discounting.

Fiscal execution is the key July watch. General budget spending contracted for a second month in May, infrastructure outlays fell 12.0% oya, and fiscal deposits rose to the second-highest May level in a decade. Slow fiscal deployment remains a drag. Special local government bond issuance has picked up, but the growth impact depends on whether proceeds quickly become project starts and physical work.

If June activity and 2Q GDP disappoint, the July Politburo meeting could bring stronger counter-cyclical considerations and targeted measures. The response would likely remain fiscal-led. Rate cuts may be used as a signal, but without faster fiscal execution and stronger credit demand, the growth impulse would be limited. The near-term outlook hinges on whether external demand and fiscal deployment can absorb resilient production. If trade frictions broaden or fiscal projects lag, output resilience could instead translate into inventories, margin compression, and softer output prices. Policy should now shift from supporting production to converting fiscal resources into real demand.

## Real GDP (2Q)

July 15

Following weaker-than-expected April–May activity data, we revised down our 2Q real GDP growth forecast to 3.3% q/q saar (from 4.0%), reflecting softer retail sales, a deeper contraction in fixed asset investment, weaker fiscal deployment, and continued housing-market weakness. While exports remained resilient and high-tech manufacturing continued to outperform, these supports were insufficient to offset broad domestic demand weakness. At the same time, we revised 3Q and 4Q growth forecasts up to 3.5% and 3.7% q/q saar, respectively, on expectations of continued global AI/tech demand, lower energy prices following the easing of Middle East disruptions, and stronger fiscal execution in 2H.

## Industrial production (June) July 15

Industrial production increased 4.5% oya or 0.2% m/m sa in May, after April's sharp contraction. Underlying momentum remained soft, with the trend pace slowing to 1.0% 3m/3m saar. Industrial upgrading continued. High-tech and equipment manufacturing outperformed amid a tech-cycle upswing and policy support, accelerating to 15.1% oya and 9.5%, respectively. Output of 3D printing equipment, lithium-ion batteries, industrial robots and integrated circuits also increased. NEV production rebounded strongly amid higher gasoline prices. We look for IP to rise 0.5% m/m sa, leaving annual growth decent, at 4.6% oya in June.

## Manufacturing PMI (June) June 30

Both the NBS and RatingDog PMIs suggest manufacturing stayed in modest expansion in June, with activity holding up after the April–May soft patch. Output and new orders point to near-term stabilization and a small improvement in order momentum, rather than a clear cyclical upturn. External demand remains mixed: the more private-sector-skewed RatingDog export orders slipped back into contraction territory, implying export resilience is becoming more contested, while the NBS export gauge moved back slightly above the expansion line. Pricing signals easing cost pressures but limited reflation, with downstream pricing power still weak.

## Merchandise trade (June) July 14

China's May exports rose $3.1\% \mathrm{m / m}$ sa after April's jump, lifting annual growth to $19.4\%$ oya. High-tech shipments increased for a seventh consecutive month, led by mobile phones, electronic ICs and ADP. Prices likely provided a meaningful boost. Imports expanded for a sixth straight month, rising $2.9\% \mathrm{m / m}$ sa (27.5% oya), after undershooting for most of 2025. Import strength appears driven more by AI supply chain needs and commodity stockpiling (e.g., coal) than a broad domestic-demand recovery. Crude imports continued to fall, reflecting reduced demand and inventory drawdowns. We expect a $1.3\% \mathrm{m / m}$ sa gain in June exports.

## Retail sales (June)

## July 15

Retail sales softened further in May, falling 0.6% oya on a 0.1%m/m sa decline. Auto retail sales plunged a further 16.4% oya (-1.1%m/m sa). Ex. autos, retail sales rose 0.9% oya, down from 1.7% in April. Several categories recorded sharp declines: spending on household electronics fell 15.6% oya (vs -15.1% April), construction and decoration materials sales fell 13.6%, gold and silver jewelry fell 8.9%, and consumption of oil and related products fell 3.2%.

Services spending continued to outperform: services retail sales grew 5.4% oya ytd in May, led by communications, software & information, leasing and business, financial, and transport and storage services. Online sales expanded further, with services outpacing goods. Urban unemployment rate edged down to 5.1%, although it may not fully reflect the state of the job recovery and underemployment. We expect retail sales to tick up 0.2% m/m sa in June.

## Fixed investment (June)

## July 15

Fixed asset investment (FAI) contracted sharply by 10.7% oya in May (vs -8.0% April), dragging the ytd FAI further into contraction at -4.1% oya ytd (vs +1.7% in 1Q). By sector, real estate FAI (-24.3% oya) fell the most and drove the decline, indicating a contraction stronger than seen in 1Q. Manufacturing (-4.2% oya) and infrastructure (-9.4%) also contracted. Within manufacturing, investment broadly cooled or contracted – led by special purpose equipment, chemicals, and pharmaceuticals – while high-tech manufacturing FAI held up better (electrical machinery and equipment, computers, communications and other electronic equipment). Infrastructure softness was broad-based across environmental management, utilities, and transport

Policy support has somewhat lost steam and become less front-loaded in April and May, despite a stronger 1Q push, especially for “effective investment”, as shown in strong year-start infrastructure investment. The lack of projects in reserve, weak local finances, and the missing private sector enthusiasm may explain the subsequent weaker investment in April and May. Indeed, not only have the intended multiplier effects yet to materialize from fiscal support for investment, but there is also a lack of conviction in providing further consumption stimulus, with waning trade-in subsidies and weak household credit. A possible policy pivot away from boosting consumption to a focus on infrastructure spending, as we were expecting, has yet to be seen.

We expect FAI to decline 8.8% oya in June, up slightly from the 10.7% drop in May.

## Industrial profits (June) July 27

Headline industrial profits growth edged up to 18.8% oya ytd, while the pace of expansion slightly moderated to 21.1% oya in May. Sales revenue rose 5.5% oya ytd, while costs rose more slowly at 4.7%, supporting net profit margin. That said, accounts receivable climbed 7.7% oya and finished goods inventories rose 8.8%, pointing to cash conversion pressure and slower turnover. Profit gains remained broad-based across ownership types, led by shareholding firms (+24.1% oya ytd), followed by SOEs (+19.6%), private (+10.7%) and foreign firms (+4.2%).

Sectoral performance continued to show a two-speed picture. Electronics (+103.9% oya ytd) and high-tech (+44.7%) manufacturing profits outperformed on the global AI upcycle and strong demand for memory chips. Energy-linked industries' profits improved, with petro-processing turning from last year's loss to profit and chemical profits rising by 71.6% oya ytd. In contrast, downstream and consumer-related industries lagged, with deeper declines in furniture (-58.4% oya ytd) and autos (-19.8%), and continued weakness in apparel and cultural/sports/entertainment goods.

## Labor market and consumer confidence (June)

July 15

The survey-based urban unemployment rate and the surveyed unemployment rate in 31 cities both edged down to 5.1% in May from 5.2% in April. Meanwhile, the unemployment rate for the age group 16-24 (excluding students) fell to 15.6% (vs. 16.3 in April).

Consumer confidence ticked up in May, but remains at subdued levels. The headline consumer confidence index edged up to 89.9 in May (vs. 89.0 in April, average monthly reading of 88.7 in 2025). In the breakdown, the employment component ticked up to 74.6 (vs. 73.4 in April, 2025 average 73.6), while the income component rose to 98.8 (vs. 97.9 in April, 2025 average 96.8).

## Consumer prices (June) July 9

Headline CPI inflation stabilized at 1.2% oya, rising 0.1% m/m sa with broadly muted moves across major components. Transport/communication increased 0.2% m/m sa, but passenger cars and vehicle fuel fell on the month, while communication tools jumped as AI-related memory tightness fed through; the NBS highlighted price gains in mobile phones and tablets. Education/culture/entertainment rose on Labor holiday tourism. Food deflation persisted, led by fresh vegetables and pork. Misc. goods/services (including gold jewelry) slipped again, though still added to headline inflation. Core CPI eased to 1.1% oya.

High-frequency tracking indicates that agricultural food prices and pork wholesale prices continued to decline, which will likely weigh on headline CPI. We expect headline CPI to rise 1.2% oya (or 0.1% m/m sa) in June.

## Producer prices (June) July 9

PPI extended its upturn in the May, rising 0.8%m/m sa and 3.9%oya. Relative to last month, the gain reflected industrial upgrading and AI-driven compute demand, lifting prices for metals, electrical machinery and electronics. Seasonal “summer peak” demand also supported coal, cooling appliances and power. Gains were partly capped by global oil price swings that pulled upstream oil and refining prices lower and cooled oil-linked chemicals. Producer-goods inflation strengthened, while consumer-goods PPI remained in deflation amid weak demand and limited pass-through. The manufacturing input price PMIs remained expansionary but slowed in pace. We expect PPI to rise 4.3%oya (or 0.4% m/m sa) in June.

July 15

The NBS 70-city new-home price decline stabilized at $0.20\% \mathrm{m / m}$ nsa, and the secondary home price fall widened modestly to $0.26\% \mathrm{m / m}$ nsa in May. Weakness continued to be concentrated in lower-tier cities, while tier-1 cities posted asequential gain. New-home prices are now down $13.7\%$ from the 2021 peak; secondary prices are down $22.6\%$ . ## Housing activities (June) July 15

JPM's housing activity index ticked down further in May. The downtick reflected a steeper decline in real estate FAI and new home sales, as well as a recent acceleration in land sales contraction. The State Council published the Urban Renewal 15th FYP in early June, positioning urban renewal as a key tool for proving livelihoods, stabilizing the property market, and supporting high-quality urban growth. China's housing market remains in a time-inconsistency trap. Policymakers aim to improve affordability and reduce reliance on property, but have slowed price clearing to protect household wealth and local governments, forcing the adjustment mainly through quantities. As a result, the baseline should not yet be reassessed: current trends look more like narrow, K-shaped stabilization within a prolonged quantity adjustment, not the start of a durable nationwide recovery.

## Money aggregates (June) July 9-15

May new loan creation turned positive after April's contraction at 520bn yuan, but was still the weakest May print since 2009. Corporate short-term loans and bill financing, a tool banks sometimes use to meet credit creation targets, provided the main lift. However, household loans and corporate medium- and long-term loans remained in contraction, signaling general demand weakness. Loan growth slowed to a new low of $5.5\%$ . We expect new loan creation to pick up to 1,912bn yuan in June and M2 growth to edge higher, to $8.7\%$ oya.

## Aggregate financing (June) July 9-15

TSF growth slowed by 0.1%pt to a historical low of 7.7%oya, with TSF flow at Rmb2029bn in May. Government bond issuance accelerated, mainly driven by CGBs, including special CGBs, while special LGB issuance remained slow. ## Interest rates

## Daily release

The policy rate – the seven-day reverse repo rate – was kept unchanged in June. For key market rates, DR007, three-month SHIBOR, and 10-year CGB yield rose 17bps, 3bps, and 3bps, respectively, while the one-year CGB yield ticked down 3bps.

While the PBOC's 1Q26 monetary policy operations report struck a relatively more hawkish tone and points to low odds of a near-term cut, recent weakness, especially the surprisingly soft April activity data and downside risks to 2Q GDP, should keep a rate cut on the table. A more hawkish Fed and other major central banks may complicate the external backdrop, but China's rate decision should remain driven by domestic conditions, particularly with the CNY still resilient and the main pressure being to slow appreciation rather than defend against depreciation. A cut could become more likely in 2H if growth headwinds intensify. As usual, a 10bp move would likely be more of a policy signal than a meaningful easing impulse.

## Daily release

PBOC net injected 583bn yuan via pledged 7-day OMO and 200bn yuan via MLF in June, while withdrawing 300bn yuan via outright OMO. The PBOC also initiated overnight reverse repos at the end of June, in line with Governor Pan's announcement at the Lujiazui forum. ## Fiscal position (June)

## July

Headline general budget expenditure continued to contract for a second month (-1.6% oya), with infrastructure outlays the main drag (-12.0% oya). This helps explain the continued weakness in infrastructure and public FAI in May. General budget revenue maintained solid growth at 6.6% oya, with gains in both tax and non-tax revenues. Outside the general budget, revenue and expenditure in government-managed funds both saw sharp declines, amidst a recent acceleration in land sales contraction, underscoring ongoing drag from the housing/land downturn on local-government fiscal conditions. Fiscal deposits increased further, reaching May levels second only to 2025 over the past decade.

Fiscal delivery remained less front-loaded than we had expected through May, leaving more budgetary room for the rest of the year. Special local government bond issuance picked up to nearly 500bn yuan mtd in June, signaling reactive acceleration in utilizing the remaining fiscal resources, but project deployment is likely to lag. Looking ahead, the fiscal path is seen in two stages: stage 1 is about executing NPC-approved budgets and drawing down deposits to support 2H investment, and stage 2 is conditional, potentially adding more fiscal support if 2Q growth undershoots the 4.5–5% full-year target range, albeit with less conviction due to the flexibility of a range target.

## Monetary conditions (June)

## July 9-15

Our credit impulse estimate, the gap between TSF growth and nominal GDP growth, slipped a further 0.3pt to 3.3%. The continued slide in loan growth underscores broad demand weakness across households and corporates. A durable loan demand rebound requires stronger private-sector confidence. While PPI has ticked up, the improvement is narrow, and margin pressure in mid- and downstream sectors may keep capex and loan demand soft. If the reported 2tr yuan state-led data-center/computing-hub funding plan is implemented, it could bring supplementary loan demand via targeted credit tools or policy banks, but likely over multiple years and not enough to reverse the downturn in overall loan growth.

## Currency exchange rate

Daily release

CFETS CNY has strengthened from 101.3 to 102.8 in June, though USD/CNY gains were partly capped by renewed USD strength. The CFETS basket has more than fully retraced the 2025 depreciation: it was broadly stable through April before jumping higher. Last year's CNY TWI weakness drew pushback from non-US trading partners amid China's record trade surplus, reviving accusations of "intended" depreciation alongside a pronounced decline in the REER. In our view, China's export outperformance is better explained by manufacturing efficiency gains and domestic deflation than by FX undervaluation. A firmer CNY basket could also help ease geopolitical frictions and support RMB confidence.

Banks' FX settlement ratio, a proxy for customers' willingness to convert foreign currency into RMB, rose to $50.9\%$ in May from $50.2\%$ in April. The FX sales ratio (customers' willingness to buy foreign currency) also increased, from $49.8\%$ to $50.0\%$ . Consequently, the net FX settlement ratio (settlement minus sales) widened to $0.9\%$ pt in May from April's $0.4\%$ pts.

## FX reserves (June)

## July 7

May FX reserves surprised to the upside again, rising US\$31.7bn to \$3442.2bn. Exports beat expectations on strong high-tech shipments, with a growing price lift in memory chips/modules, AI datacenter equipment, and new energy. Imports firmed for a sixth month, driven more by AI supply-chain needs and commodity stockpiling than broad domestic-demand recovery. The current account surplus widened to US\$67.9bn in May. Including a US\$12.3bn valuation loss, implied capital outflows widened to US\$24.0bn. For June, we expect FX reserves to have slightly ticked down to US\$3,439bn.

Recent regulatory moves have raised questions about whether China is meaningfully tightening outbound capital flows. The cross-border enforcement aims to shut down illicit solicitation/servicing and re-route flows into supervised pathways, rather than imposing a blanket ban on offshore investing. Against the backdrop of easing near-term capital outflow pressure and CNY strengthening, the State Council's Regulations on Outbound Investment also appear to be less about restricting outbound investment, but to standardize governance and tighten enforcement around activities that already sit within existing rules.

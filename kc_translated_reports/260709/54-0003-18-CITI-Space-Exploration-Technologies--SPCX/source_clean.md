## 1) Existing launch capabilities already market-leading

SPCX is the global leader in orbital launch services. More than any other organization, SPCX has achieved space access at scale, transforming an entire industry. SpaceX designs, manufactures, launches, and operates the world's most advanced rockets and spacecraft. The company safely and reliably transports astronauts, satellites, and other payloads, providing cost-efficient, reliable, and high-cadence access to space for its own purposes as well as for third-party commercial and government customers. As of 1Q26, SPCX has launched a total mass to orbit of approximately 7,400 metric tons with a 99%+ mission success rate across its Falcon rockets. The company has completed approximately 650 orbital space launches, and over 540 of those launches were completed by a flight-proven Falcon rocket. In 2025 alone, SpaceX completed 170 missions across Falcon and Starship vehicles and 159 flight-proven booster launches with a 99%+ success rate on attempted booster recoveries. Specifically, SPCX launched over 2,200 metric tons, representing over 80% of mass to orbit for the world in 2025, continuing a trend that has been in place for multiple years. Importantly SPCX has demonstrated that its rocket technology can be scaled across geographies and launch assets. In 2025, SPCX launched from four primary launch pads in the United States and successfully recovered boosters across seven landing facilities including autonomous drone ships and catch towers based on the vehicle type and mission profile. The company believes that its extensive vertical integration and end-to-end control over the entire value chain, from design to launch to operations, allows it to achieve unprecedented speed and cost efficiency and it is difficult to disagree given the enormity of what SPCX has accomplished in launch. Ultimately, we believe that SPCX's unrivaled launch capabilities represent the foundational competitive advantage that enables all other parts of its business.

## 2) The ‘space disruptor’ disrupting itself through Starship

While many competitors are trying to develop rocket technologies that are roughly Falcon 9-competitive, SPCX is disrupting its own launch business with the fully and rapidly reusable Starship. As discussed in the prior section, SPCX is already the global leader in launch – yet - Starship, a two-stage super heavy-lift launch vehicle that the company has been flight testing since 2023, further enhances its industry-defining launch offerings. Starship is SPCX's next-generation vehicle that the company expects will dramatically expand its launch capability through full and rapid reusability combined with unprecedented mass to orbit capability. The company believes that Starship is the most powerful launch system ever developed, and we find it hard to disagree. We expect that Starship V3 will be able to carry a payload of 100 metric tons and that future generations could reach 200 metric tons, likely as soon as Starship V4. Moreover, we assume that Starship will be the only vehicle with fully reusable first and second stages, which is critical to reducing launch costs and increasing launch cadence, for many years and the only vehicle to operate in this way at a 100+ metric ton scale for over a decade. Once Starship's launch cadence reaches the thousands annually, we expect launch costs to fall by \~90% per metric ton (from \~\$2,700/ton to \$270/ton) and from there SPCX believes that costs could fall 99%+ vs. the Falcon 9 as launch cadence grows, future generations of the Starship are capable of carrying greater payload, and the company expands opportunities for extreme vertical integration.

What is Starship and how does it differ from the Falcon 9? Starship is designed to be a fully reusable, super heavy-lift launch vehicle. Starship V3 is designed to deliver 100 metric tons to Earth's orbit in a fully reusable configuration while enabling rapid turnaround times akin to commercial aviation. Future generations of Starship are being designed to double this payload capacity. To date, SPCX has executed 12 Starship flight tests. We cannot enumerate all of the improvements of Starship vs. the Falcon 9, but we can highlight a number of key differences including:

Full and rapid reusability: Starship's core design innovation is its full and rapid approach to reusability: both stages return to Earth for catch and rapid refurbishment. The Super Heavy booster returns to the launch site following stage separation and is caught mid-air by the launch tower's mechanical arms, also known as “chopsticks” by SPCX, to facilitate immediate inspection, refurbishment, and relaunch. As context, the Falcon 9 already cracked part of the code of rocket reusability by demonstrating that the rocket's first-stage could be launched to space and landed back on earth. One of the major innovations of Starship is that the Starship second-stage (upper), after orbital delivery or missions beyond, is designed to reenter protected by advanced heat shield tiles, execute a propulsive landing burn, and be similarly caught mid-air by the launch tower's mechanical arms. In the fullness of time, SPCX believes that Starship's full and rapid reusability will enable sub-one hour re-flights, causing a paradigm shift in launch cadence.

Massive payload capacity: With a payload bay volume rivaling the pressurized sections of the International Space Station, Starship is designed to deploy structures like space station modules, large telescopes, next-generation V3 satellites, and future AI compute satellites. Starship is powered by 39 Raptor engines, which are full-flow staged combustion cycle rocket engines burning cryogenic liquid methane and liquid oxygen. Raptor engines offer nearly triple the thrust per engine, higher efficiency, and better performance for heavy-lift and deep space missions compared to the Merlin engine used on Falcon 9, according to the company. The company also notes that each Raptor 3 engine in Starship saves nearly a ton of vehicle mass compared to previous generations by removing heat shields and simplifying plumbing.

## 3) Falling launch costs open up new growth markets

SPCX believes that space represents the largest economic frontier in human history – and we agree. The company argues that since Earth has obvious limits, it is inevitable that we must build infrastructure and industries in space, expanding human capabilities to improve life on Earth and to establish life beyond. One example of this is SPCX's space-based ‘Connectivity’ infrastructure, also known as Starlink. Another example is AI infrastructure in space, which can utilize the virtually limitless power of the Sun and thereby enable the use of AI as a transformative force for understanding the universe and improving the daily lives of all humans. SPCX believes the convergence of these areas will enable an unprecedented expansion in the global economy, leading to an age of abundance and its innovations and technological advancements will redefine entire industries on Earth, while also creating new ones on the Moon, Mars, and beyond. In this section we help illustrate the falling launch cost algorithm that drives this vision of the future.

SPCX has already demonstrated a history of driving huge reductions in launch costs through the Falcon 9. With the first successful launch of Falcon 1 in 2008, SPCX became the first private company to successfully launch a liquid-fueled rocket to Earth's orbit. In 2010, the commercial debut of the Falcon 9 rocket revolutionized space access by delivering cost efficiency that was unprecedented at the time. For example, according to NASA, the first version of Falcon 9 in 2010 reduced launch cost to approximately \$2,700 per kilogram, which represented a reduction of approximately 85% compared to the historical average launch cost per kilogram of \$18,500. The first version of Falcon Heavy in 2018 further reduced this cost to \$1,400 per kilogram, a reduction of approximately 92% compared to the historical average. SPCX has also further reduced its internal cost of launch through a combination of engineering improvements, manufacturing efficiencies, and economies of scale, most notably through its ability to drive more frequent reuse of rockets, according to the company. Furthering the point, until the 2000s and the introduction of the Falcon 9 rocket, global commercial launch activity averaged 25 to 35 launches per year. As a result, the space industry remained a niche domain with limited ability to support large commercial markets or scaled space-based infrastructure.

## What drives the Starship unit cost advantage over time?

■ Payload capacity: The transition from SpaceX's Falcon class of rockets to the Starship launch system represents an unprecedented leap in orbital delivery capabilities, driving a massive unit cost advantage over time. While the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy offer payload capacities to Low-Earth Orbit (LEO) of

approximately 23 metric tons and 64 metric tons respectively, Starship V3 is designed to deliver a massive 100 metric tons in a fully reusable configuration. Future iterations, potentially as early as Starship V4, aim to double this capability to a target of 200 metric tons to Earth's orbit. This exponential growth enables orbital scale operations, such as deploying up to 60 V3 Starlink satellites in a single mission to achieve a twenty-fold increase in downlink capacity compared to a Falcon 9 launch. By scaling up payload volume, the company can deploy large-scale orbital AI compute infrastructure and deep-space logistics much more efficiently. Ultimately, combining this massive capacity with full reusability is projected to reduce mass-to-orbit launch costs by 99% or more relative to the historical average of \$18,500 per kilogram.

■ Booster reusability: Central to SpaceX's cost reduction strategy is the reusability of key hardware, most notably first-stage boosters, which dramatically lowers per-launch costs by minimizing hardware replacement expenses. This operational model is thoroughly proven by the Falcon 9 fleet, where individual first-stage boosters have demonstrated the capability to refly up to 34 times as of early 2026. Building upon this established foundation, the Starship system utilizes the massive Super Heavy booster, which is designed to return directly to the launch site following stage separation. Instead of performing traditional landings on drone ships or land pads, the booster is caught mid-air by the launch tower's innovative mechanical arms, also known as "chopsticks". This revolutionary recovery method eliminates weight penalties from onboard landing gear and facilitates immediate inspection, refurbishment, and rapid relaunch. By enabling such an accelerated operational cycle, this technology allows SpaceX to amortize its fixed production costs over highly frequent missions, driving launch costs down to the point where propellant is the primary marginal expense.

■ Starship stage 1 and 2 reusability: Achieving total system reusability across both the Super Heavy booster (Stage 1) and the Starship spacecraft (Stage 2) represents the primary engineering driver of long-term unit cost advantages. The complex technical execution of this architecture requires the Stage 2 spacecraft to perform a controlled atmospheric reentry protected by advanced heat shield tiles, execute a precise propulsive landing burn, and be caught mid-air by the launch tower's mechanical "chopstick" arms. This recovery process is designed to enable rapid refurbishment and potential sub-one hour reflights, mirroring commercial aviation by spreading fixed production costs across frequent repeated uses. By recovering and reflying both stages of the vehicle instead of discarding them, the company minimizes hardware replacement expenses to ensure that propellant becomes the primary marginal cost of each flight. When contrasted against historical launch economics, such as NASA's \$18,500 per kilogram benchmark, full system reusability is projected to reduce the cost to reach orbit by 99% or more to under \$185 per kilogram.

■ Faster turn times on launch infrastructure: To transform space access into routine, airline-like operations, SpaceX is actively evolving its ground infrastructure to achieve a high-frequency launch cadence that dramatically drives down unit costs. At the heart of this strategy is the innovative "chopsticks" catch mechanism on the launch tower, which captures returning vehicles mid-air to facilitate immediate inspections and rapid refurbishments. According to the company, this rapid recovery process will eventually enable sub-one hour reflights, allowing the same hardware to fly multiple times per day. To maximize the utilization rate of these flight and ground assets, SpaceX significantly increased its annual capital expenditures in 2025, prioritizing critical launch site infrastructure. ## 4) Launch risks

Being the world's market-leader in launch is not easy – getting to the point where the Starship can be launched thousands of times per year carries significant execution risk. Below we highlight seven execution risks that we think are particularly important to consider, but not insurmountable:

■ Access to launch facilities: To launch thousands of times a year, SPCX needs access to >10 launchpads, in our view. Sufficient Starship launch infrastructure needs to be in place to ensure rapid reusability of Starship (2029 in Citi Forecast). SPCX is likely to need to develop new greenfield launch facilities beyond its existing facilities and Starbase.

■ Environmental risk factors: Increasing launch frequency requires navigating complex and evolving environmental issues and public safety considerations. SPCX may face regulatory scrutiny from environmental activists and politicians given the potential environmental impact of their launch cadence.

■ FAA regulatory framework: SPCX must obtain necessary regulatory approvals, particularly from the FAA, to support a high launch cadence while addressing safety and environmental concerns. Any significant delay in the establishment of this regulatory framework puts our launch forecast at risk.

■ Reusability of the second stage: Full reusability of Starship (both first and second stage) remains a key factor in achieving the desired cost efficiencies at scale. SPCX has yet to achieve this, and we expect a demonstration of the capability by year-end 2026.

■ Rapid reusability of boosters: To facilitate multiple launches per day, SPCX must be able to rapidly refurbish/reposition the boosters on Starship. However, ensuring the rapid refurbishment and high-rate reusability of engines and other components remains a meaningful challenge to maintaining a high launch cadence.

■ Supply-chain constraints: The Starship growth strategy is dependent on increased access to raw materials and components for Starship and its engines. Supply-chain disruptions or constraints can lead to longer deployment timelines and may force the reallocation of critical resources from other projects.

\- Access to propellant: Achieving a high launch cadence requires the construction of dedicated propellant production facilities, such as air separation units and methane liquification plants. Delays commissioning this fueling infrastructure or the unavailability of sufficient fuel and propellant would materially affect the ability to execute on Citi's launch forecast.

## 2) LEO constellations insight & overview

## A) Broadband Constellation

The global broadband constellation currently services over 10 million subscribers across roughly 9,600 satellites. With the introduction of Starship rockets, Starlink will be launching its enhanced V3 satellites that increase the effective capacity per satellite from roughly 100 Gbps to 1 Tbps. We see three possible paths to create terrestrial coverage to offer end-to-end mobile services:

1. Build organically – Execution requires assembling a layer cake of spectrum, constructing or leasing terrestrial cell sites and establishing a full retail go-to-market operation. Also, enterprise operations could also be of interest to extend its reach into the vertical with

3. MVNO – The lightest-capital option would involve a MVNO agreement that would allow SpaceX to offer wireless services using another operator's network. In the US market, the three national MNOs carriers have publicly indicated (see link, link, link) that they are not interested in an MVNO agreement with SpaceX. Historical cable-industry MVNO arrangements demonstrate that such structures can be unlocked when carriers are externally incentivized. There is also the possibility that one of the carriers sees a differentiated opportunity with SpaceX or simply needs incremental revenue to offset other areas of weakness and decides to partner with SpaceX and/or other LEO constellations. Internationally, particularly in markets where spectrum is difficult to acquire, MVNOs may represent the most attractive path, allowing Starlink to layer its satellite differentiator atop borrowed terrestrial capacity.

We believe the most probable outcome is a hybrid, geography by geography strategy: build or buy in select markets where spectrum and economics support it, MVNO in markets where carrier partnerships are accessible, and pure D2D and wholesale in markets that are too small or restrictive for direct entry.

## 4) Impact to wireless and broadband markets

Consistent with the history of the communications ecosystem, Starlink is likely to receive some degree of cooperation as well as competition from the telecom and cable incumbents. In broadband, Starlink will be competing for subscriptions in both the residential and business markets, while cable and telecom companies may still resell Starlink solutions for connectivity to specific underserved verticals. For example, Comcast offers its enterprise customers to integrate Starlink satellite capabilities into their connectivity solutions. T-Mobile is currently reselling Starlink D2D services under its T-Satellite branded solution, while also entering into a joint venture (for spectrum and IP) with the other carriers to promote additional D2D competition. Starlink also faces direct competition from emerging constellations from AST SpaceMobile and Amazon LEO that are in the process of launching their satellites into orbit, albeit currently well behind Starlink's established constellation.

The wrinkle in the D2D market is that the enthusiasm for monetizing valuable coverage assurance from their customers has faded from the low level of usage being reported by both T-Mobile and independent firms, including Ookla. Although Ookla is reporting a nearly 25% increase in global D2D connections between July 2025 and March 2026 as Starlink has expanded into new geographies, monthly usage was disclosed as generally less than 1% with 0.46% of Speedtest users in the US connecting to D2D satellites. The US market also leads the world in D2D connections at nearly 46% of the usage. As Starlink migrates to its V2 platform with greater continuity of coverage and substantially greater capacity, the D2D category should be able to offer a more compelling case for coverage assurance in markets with well built terrestrial mobile networks, such as in the US, and expand use cases in markets with a lot of rural sprawl and under-built mobile infrastructure.

We anticipate T-Mobile's exclusivity with Starlink is likely to expire over the next few months, if it hasn't already, which can open the door to direct marketing as well as marketing through other partners, such as Boost Mobile. The structural characteristic of a satellite broadband network is that the majority of usable capacity inherently lands over rural and underserved geographies, a function of beam architecture and population density. Even as Starlink dramatically expands constellation size and per-satellite bandwidth, the incremental capacity disproportionately serves low-density areas where terrestrial alternatives are weakest. We believe the model will be that penetration scales aggressively where Starlink has structural advantage and more modestly in dense markets where fiber, cable, and fixed-wireless incumbents remain competitive.

1) Credible yet execution-dependent extension of launch, AI, and connectivity

The company's orbital AI compute vision (SpaceX, Feb. 2026) is a credible yet execution-dependent extension of the company's core advantages in launch, satellite manufacturing, Starlink networking, and vertically integrated hardware. SpaceX will control both the launch vehicles required to deploy AI satellites as well as the manufacturing capabilities needed to build them. Given SpaceX's long lead in launch capabilities and operational proficiency in satellites, SpaceX's owns more of the prerequisite stack for AI satellites than any other likely competitor.

Is space-based AI infrastructure inevitable? Critical to the company's view is a belief that current advances in AI across both training and inference will require an amount of electricity that cannot be met with terrestrial methods. Therefore, in the long term, space-based AI is the solution to the widening gap between compute demand and power availability. In other words, “try getting the permits [to cover Nevada in solar panels]...it’s harder to scale on ground than it is to scale in space” (A Cheeky Pint, Feb. 2026). With this perspective in mind, a key debate becomes not whether the company can successfully put compute in orbit – we view the technological hurdles as either solved or able to be solved in due time – but whether orbital compute can scale to the level of cost, reliability, power density, and utilization that renders AI satellites competitive with terrestrial alternatives before there is material progress in resolving terrestrial infrastructure bottlenecks by hyperscalers, utilities, nuclear / gas generation, or other off-grid architectures. Accordingly, many view timing as the critical variable in the orbital AI investment thesis, the primary determinant of which will be progress against Starship achieving rapid reusability. That said, if demand for compute is truly as insatiable as recent data points suggest, SPCX argument of scalability at the extremes holds merit unto itself.

## We see four linked suppositions that underpin SpaceX's differentiated

argument: (1) launch costs should decline structurally with Starship rapid reusability; (2) orbital solar power removes the largest long-term operating cost for terrestrial solutions (i.e. power); (3) radiative cooling reduces thermal opex; and (4) Starlink's optical mesh provides the networking substrate needed to turn satellites into effective distributed AI clusters. Of these four, we anticipate that launch costs and optical mesh will be the engineering challenges that command the most focus, particularly since the implied launch cadence required to meet management's goal of 100 GW per year necessitates till now unwarranted regulatory approvals, and the downlink speeds required to mitigate latency expected for inference could prove

insufficient. We expect the company to reach 400MW of installed nameplate compute by 2028, growing to 75GW in 2031 under these conditions.

## 2) Addressing constraints in the terrestrial AI market

The constructive case for orbital AI is that it addresses the most constrained parts of the terrestrial data center stack: power, cooling, permitting, grid interconnection, and physical build time. These satellites will be enabled by solar power (free and clean), Starship deployment, Starlink optical networking, and high-volume satellite manufacturing. Early AI satellite power targets of 100 kW per satellite, compute-density targets of 80-120 kW per ton, and an aspirational deployment rate of 100 GW per year would require \~1M metric tons per year to orbit and the company aims to deploy up to a million satellites (FCC, Jan. 2026). Accordingly, Starship is central to the company's ambitions as it will be the only fully reusable system designed to carry 100+ metric tons to orbit, which is the style of launch architecture needed for 200 kW-class satellites and gigawatt-scale deployment.

However, although there are proof points, none of this has been done at a commercial scale. Starcloud, an orbital data center startup, has shown that orbital AI compute is technically possible at demo scale, launching Starcloud-1 with an NVIDIA H100 and claiming the first LLM training/fine-tuning in orbit (LinkedIn, Nov. 2025). Launched by a Falcon 9 and the size of a small refrigerator, the H100 ran inference on a Google Gemma model and trained Andrej Karpathy's nano-GPT on the complete works of Shakespeare (X, Dec. 2025). This technical milestone proved that a data-center-class GPU could survive launch, operate on solar power in orbit, and execute LLM inference and small-scale training workloads. However, scalable power density, thermal rejection, high-bandwidth networking, maintenance/replenishment, and economics remain unproven.

In our view, orbital AI is less about whether it is possible (it is) and more about whether the technology hits the right ratio of performance / cost. The technology building blocks increasingly exist, the question remains whether SPCX can integrate them at data center scale and at a cost that outperforms terrestrial alternatives. Continuing to diligence orbital AI satellite performance as technology and cost curves evolve will be a focal point for some time.

## 3) Economics attractive on the non-chip infrastructure

We view orbital AI as potentially disruptive to the non-chip portion of the AI data center stack first, and to the full cost-per-token stack only later if Terafab materially reduces accelerator costs. In the interim, AI satellite economics are primarily an ex-chip infrastructure advantage, though our analysis demonstrates that the cost curve remains an engineering challenge. The economics can become attractive on the non-chip infrastructure layer if Starship cadence and satellite manufacturing scale materialize. However, the processor layer still remains a critical cost bucket, so orbital AI does not become a full-stack cost advantage unless SpaceX also reduces accelerator cost through Terafab, proprietary chip design, or materially better utilization. The company is targeting 90% value capture through its supply chain derisking effort via Terafab.

We estimate that 30% of terrestrial AI data center costs are dedicated to construction/shell/MEP/grid interconnection, with the remaining 70% dedicated to chips and other critical IT equipment. Therefore, SpaceX's current cost advantage could remain structurally bounded if it only reduces the construction, power, cooling, and deployment layers while still utilizing higher cost, NVIDIA-class silicon. Our work suggests that relatively aggressive assumptions around launch cost reduction, satellite manufacturing learning curves, solar array costs, and thermal simplification are needed to justify the longer-term costs. Our illustrative analysis assumes a 200 kW AI satellite, 2-ton launch mass, 100 kW/ton specific power, 190 GPU-equivalent chips, and 10% chip infant mortality & non-compute power distribution, yielding 180 kW effective delivered power after adjustment. On that basis, the model estimates ex-chip, ex-Terafab total satellite cost of \$8.5M in 2029E, falling to \$6.5M in 2030E and \$5.8M in 2031E, equivalent to \$47/W, \$36/W, and \$32/W upfront, or \$9.4/W/year, \$7.2/W/year, and \$6.4/W/year over a five-year useful life.

## 4) Orbital AI Risks

Given this is a technology that has never been attempted at scale before, there is a possibility that the architecture is economically fragile once real-world losses materialize. These losses could include liquid cooling loop mass, additional shielding, dedicated radiator mass, radiation-hardening premiums, solar cell cost inflation versus terrestrial benchmarks, and failure related derating. Thermal management may become an important technical gating item. SpaceX's AI1 concept describes a deployable liquid radiator with redundant pumping loops, suggesting that the company is already contemplating active thermal systems rather than purely passive radiative cooling – though this could be describing a chip-level heat transport loop which works simultaneously with a radiative rejection mechanism. Finally, we note that there are several orbital governance considerations, including debris, collision-avoidance, deorbit, spectrum, astronomy, and optical brightness.

## 2) Overview of Terafab

Terafab is a multi-year, capital-intensive chip manufacturing initiative led by SpaceX in partnership with Tesla and Intel, announced in March 2026 (Tesla collaboration) and expanded in April 2026 (Intel joining). The vision is to build what could become the world's largest AI chip manufacturing ecosystem, targeting one terawatt of annual compute output. Terafab is architected as a fully vertically integrated, “closed loop” facility that combines lithography mask design, fabrication of logic and eventually memory chips, advanced packaging, and testing within the same plant. This closed-loop approach allows chips to be designed, manufactured, tested, and quickly refined without relying on external feedback cycles, dramatically accelerating design improvements and yield optimization. SpaceX leads chip architecture for orbital AI and deployment and brings prior in-house silicon design experience, Tesla contributes chip architecture for edge inference (Optimus, FSD) and provides the volume demand needed to justify fab economics, and Intel provides core manufacturing technology support for both foundry and packaging. The strategy is to simplify manufacturing by focusing on \~2–3 core chip types, while still complementing – not replacing – continued reliance on third-party suppliers.

## A) Why Terafab?

Terafab functions as both a supply chain de-risking and margin capture strategy, addressing what SPCX sees as one of the key constraints to AI scaling: chip supply, data center infrastructure, and power. By insourcing chip manufacturing, SpaceX can reduce reliance on constrained external suppliers, secure access to significantly larger volumes of compute hardware, and capture value currently spread across foundries, merchant GPU vendors, and memory providers. It also enables custom chip optimization for both space and terrestrial applications, while the closed-loop development model accelerates iteration and performance gains. Although SpaceX will continue to procure chips from third parties, Terafab provides a long-term pathway to scale compute far beyond current supply limits and strengthen control over the entire physical AI stack.

It is well understood by now that the semiconductor industry is facing persistent shortages across logic and memory driven by insatiable AI demand. From the logic perspective, TSMC faces prolonged tightness in advanced nodes (specifically N3 and N2) and advanced packaging (such as CoWoS), as large die-size AI GPUs and accelerators migrate to these cutting-edge processes faster than capacity can be built. Although TSMC is aggressively expanding facilities in Taiwan and Arizona to narrow the gap, the long 2–3 years lead times for fab construction mean supply for leading-edge nodes will remain structurally tight for the foreseeable future. Simultaneously, the memory market is experiencing an unprecedented surge in demand for HBM and advanced DRAM. Memory leaders such as Micron and Samsung have indicated they cannot fully meet broad-based customer demand, leading to strict supply allocations and rising prices across both high-end and conventional memory categories.

## 3) Terafab implications to the semiconductor industry

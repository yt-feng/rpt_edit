# Company highlights

## From retail to wholesale

VNET is a leading carrier-neutral data center service provider in China. It operates one of the largest third-party IDC networks domestically, concentrated around Tier-1 cities, and serves a diversified base of over 7,000 enterprise customers.

The company provides managed hosting services (IDC), which includes colocation, inter-connectivity, and other value-added services, as well as cloud and business VPN services (non-IDC). We summarize its business segments below:

Wholesale IDC: \~40% of 1Q26 net revenues; providing large-scale, customized data center sites for cloud hyperscalers. Introduced in 2019, this business has become the company's key growth driver, with revenues increasing 58.1% YoY in 1Q26. Meanwhile for the first time, wholesale surpassed retail in terms of revenues, marking a significant milestone in the company's dual-core growth strategy.

Retail IDC: \~38% of 1Q26 net revenues; retail IDC offers standard cabinet colocation, either dedicated or shared, as well as inter-connectivity and a suite of VAS such as hybrid IT, bare metal services, and firewall management. This segment serves a diverse customer base, from blue-chip enterprises to SMEs across various industries. VNET has maintained a high retention rate, with core IDC business churn rate consistently below 1%.

Non-IDC business: \~22% of 1Q26 net revenues; this segment consists of cloud services and VPN services. Through a long-term strategic partnership in place since 2013, VNET is Microsoft's partner in mainland China for cloud offerings (Azure, Microsoft 365, Dynamics 365, and Power Platform), operating under a revenue-sharing model with monthly settlement and performance-based incentives. The VPN business is offered under brand DYXnet, covering enterprise-grade network solutions like MPLS and SD-WAN, across Asia.

Strong bookings momentum to support revenue acceleration

We believe VNET is entering a notable revenue expansion phase, underpinned by the rapid scaling of wholesale IDC and the stabilization of retail IDC and Non-IDC businesses.

The order trend in the wholesale market has recovered since mid-2025 due to better chip availability, and VNET has so far displayed exceptional bookings momentum, outpacing its major IDC competitors. It secured 199MW new bookings in 2H25 and 517MW YTD, compared with 90MW and 334MW at GDS during the same period.

510MW of YTD bookings is from ByteDance for two data center campuses in the Greater Beijing Area. The delivery is scheduled in three phases: one-third in 2H26, one-third in 1H27, and the remainder in 2H27-1H28. Given ByteDance typically ramps up quite fast, we anticipate these capacities will quickly translate into revenue and EBITDA contributions for VNET.

With that said, the delivery pattern for the next 12 months is back-end loaded. The company guided for 2026 capacity delivery to add 450-500MW, of which over 80% should be in 2H based on our estimates. Coupled with some time for customer move-in and utilization to pick up, we think the revenue growth for 2027 should be better than 2026. Overall, we estimate revenue growth of 18.2% for 2026, in line with the guided range of 15.6%-18.6%, and 19.5%/19.6% for 2027/28.

## Land reserves as delivery backup

The company holds 1,056MW wholesale capacity reserves for future development as of 1Q26, mostly concentrated in Ulanqab, of which 697MW is for short-term and 359MW for long-term deployment. We highlight in the later section (Economics in the west) that the unit capex for data center construction in Ulanqab is \~10% lower than in Tier-1 cities, while development yield remains similar; and Ulanqab is favored by hyperscalers for lower utility and rental costs and attractive latency.

Management has confirmed that these reserves were either obtained before the "window guidance" came into practice or have already secured power quotas, which allows them to fast-track delivery by commencing construction immediately upon receiving client orders.

Looking ahead, the company plans to further increase its reserves in Inner Mongolia, the Yangtze River Delta, and two or three key nodes along the "East Data, West Compute" route, as part of its long-term expansion plan. Land acquisition accounts for only low-single-digit percentages of the unit capex, so funding the land bank is manageable.

## Favorable mix shift to drive margin expansion

The wholesale business carries an adj.EBITDA margin of 45-50%, compared to 30-35% margin of the retail IDC business (which is lower due to lower utilization and connectivity that is largely passed through), and low-teens margin of the non-IDC segment. As the wholesale segment's share of revenue grows, we expect it to provide a significant tailwind for the company's overall profitability.

While retail data centers command higher rental rates in the market, they typically require a longer period (3-4 years) to reach a mature-level utilization of \~70%. In contrast, wholesale projects, anchored by large-scale orders from clients, usually achieve a higher utilization rate of over 90% within 6-12 months. In 1Q26, VNET's mature wholesale capacity reached a utilization rate of 93.8%, compared with 68.5% for retail.

We therefore estimate VNET's adj.EBITDA margin to reach 32.3%/34.9%/36.3% in 2026/27/28, driven by the higher mix of wholesale and its average utilization ticking up as projects mature. Our estimates for wholesale utilization are to reach 71.6%/73.1%/74.6% for the same period, while retail remains largely stable at 64%.

## Diversified capital access to fund capex

VNET's capital-intensive growth is supported by an increasingly diversified capital structure. The company has guided 2026 capex at RMB10-12bn, for adding 450-500MW capacity.

## Funding sources include:

■ Project debt. Debt remains the primary source, with 70-80% LTV on project level (vs. 60% at GDS). The corresponding interest rates are mainly floating at LPR (3%) minus several dozen basis points, as per the company.

■ REITs. VNET has successfully established an efficient capital recycling model by monetizing its data center assets through REITs. This strategy provides significant cash proceeds for reinvestment. The company so far has launched three private REITs and has another C-REIT (backed by an 18MW project) awaiting approval. Going forward, the company expects to focus more on private REITs due to the structure's better flexibility. The details of the three listed private REITs are as follows.

In November 2025, VNET launched a real estate green ABS with a total offering size of RMB0.86bn, utilizing Tier-1 retail IDCs as the underlying assets. The deal was valued at \~13x EV/EBITDA, and VNET retained a 7% stake (de-consolidated).

In March 2026, the company listed two additional private REIT projects on the Shanghai Stock Exchange, backed by wholesale IDC assets in Ulanqab and Taicang. These had a combined offering size of RMB6.4bn and were valued at 13x-14x EV/EBITDA. VNET retained a 30% stake in these projects (still consolidated) and expects to realize over RMB2bn cash proceeds (with the balance representing debt in the REIT).

\- Operating cash flow of \~RMB2bn per year and cash on hand (incl. short-term investments) of RMB6.6bn as of Dec 2025.

Convertible notes. The company had outstanding CBs with principal amount of RMB5.1bn. This includes the 2027CB (RMB2.2bn, conversion price at US\$11) and the 2030CB (RMB2.9bn, 2.5% coupon rate, conversion price at US\$13.75), which provides long-term capital with cash or equity settlement options.

We think the key is asset monetizations. So long as the market has appetite to buy its mature data centers at double-digit EV/EBITDA multiples, it will have a solid funding channel. Each RMB2bn raised would fund a 100MW build, so allowing for project financing as well, there seems a strong opportunity to build a development model. We are not factoring such a model into our assumptions.

## Strategic investor change and synergies

The company announced a major shareholder change on May 13 $^{th}$ , 2026. The CATL affiliate PJ Millennium Partnership has agreed to purchase 38.1% of the Class A ordinary shares from Shandong Hi-Speed Holdings Group (SDHG) at US\$8.69 per ADS. The transaction is expected to close in 4Q26, pending certain conditions, including approval by the shareholders of SDHG.

The market reacted positively towards the release, with shares bidding up 25% in the subsequent trading day. This removes the overhang of the largest shareholder's sell-down and introduces potential for better synergies. Management has identified several key areas for collaboration, including energy-storage technology, supply chain resources, and global footprint. We do not see why CATL would provide exclusive terms to a single IDC operator, so are skeptical about synergies, but we do take a lot of comfort in future direction that can come from having one of the world's leading companies as its major shareholder.

The company expects that by leveraging CATL's supply chain and expertise in energy storage, particularly its HVDC technology, it can enhance its capabilities in green and high-performance data center construction. CATL's global presence should also complement VNET's overseas expansion strategy, particularly in securing resources and facilitating funding. This strategic alignment reinforces VNET's leading position and its ability to capture the structural opportunities arising from AI demand.

## Industry trends

## Demand: cloud & AI capex fueling the up-cycle

China's data center market has rebounded since 2024, with revenue growth accelerating and capacity utilization ticking up. This upward cycle is primarily driven by a significant expansion in cloud capex to support AI development. Beyond traditional hyperscalers, we are now seeing AI model companies emerging as a new class of tenant, and committing to large-scale standalone projects.

These AI deployments typically require 1) short lead times - with rapid delivery (6-9mths) from contract signing, faster move-in (6mths), and faster second phase initiations (6mths); 2) longer contract duration (\~10yrs); 3) massive scale (50MW+) with high power density and low PUE, usually utilizing green energy and liquid cooling.

We detail three key customer categories and their capex plan below.

■ Hyperscalers. The same companies remain the major source of demand for new capacity, and thus their capex plans serve as a leading indicator for the IDC industry. The consensus for the combined capex of the BATs (Baidu, Alibaba, and Tencent) is RMB263bn in 2026 and RMB276bn in 2027. While ByteDance's budget alone may reach RMB476bn/RMB680bn in 2026/27, according to Bloomberg. Compared with their US peers, the BAT's capex to operating cash flow ratio is still relatively healthy.

Telcos. While cloud and data center businesses have been a focus of telcos' growth strategies, their capex on computing infrastructure is markedly lower than hyperscalers' and largely remained stable over the past few years, although they may lead a government led build out.

■ Government. The government is reported by Bloomberg in early June for a RMB2tn investment push in AI data centers over the next five years, though there hasn't been any official announcement yet. We have estimated that this could lead to a more than doubling of industry capacity and potential oversupply issues if it fully materializes (see our previous note here).

## Supply: market, pricing, and competition

MIIT's figures indicate a clear ramp up of data center supply nationwide in 2025, increasing 53% to 13.72m standard racks or 34GW. If we factor in the RMB2tn investment from government, plus 3-5GW per year from the private sector, then we estimate the industry will grow at \~21% CAGR till 2030 in terms of power capacity, and substantially more rapidly in terms of computing power given improvements in processing capabilities.

The supply-side landscape is highly fragmented. Market participants can be broadly segmented into three main categories: state-owned telecom carriers, third-party IDC providers, and in-house builds by enterprises themselves.

For hyperscalers, while some like Baidu, Alibaba, and Tencent, do build and operate their own data centers, they still rely heavily on third-party players for the majority of their capacity needs. The project management complexity, capital intensity, and regulatory hurdles associated with self-builds make renting space a more efficient option for them.

## Telecom carriers

We estimate that the three telcos (China Telecom, China Mobile, and China Unicom) collectively account for 35-40% of China's data center capacity in service in 2025. While this represents a decline from around 50% five years ago, their massive scale and nationwide presence continue to render them the price leader. Given their bundling and relatively aggressive pricing strategies in 1H this year, we think a significant and across-the-board price hike is not likely to happen in the near term.

Historically, the three telcos dominated the market by capitalizing on their extensive connectivity resources and customer relationships, particularly with government and SOEs. The market has since transitioned to a hybrid structure where services are jointly provided by both telcos and third-party IDC operators.

The telcos skew their portfolio to remote areas (e.g. Guiyang, Ningxia, etc.) versus third-party operators, as part of a national layout. In Tier-1 cities, even telcos rent from third-party players for better economic efficiency.

## Third-party IDC providers

This includes VNET (DBe: 3% market share in 2025) and its most direct competitors, such as GDS, Chindata, Range Intelligence, etc. These specialists have successfully captured market share by offering better flexibility and customization, notably with market-driven arrangements and carrier-neutral value proposition that frees clients from network lock-in.

While there are some scale economies, it is a commoditized business, as seen by the lack of profitability across operators. Most hyperscale customers strategically employ a multi-vendor architecture ("white list") to diversify supply chain risk and to foster a competitive bidding environment. Comparative advantage and profitability hinges on their asset portfolio, customer mix, operational excellence, and capital access, especially in the absence of market rates inflation. Gaining an edge is achieved by acquiring quality land and power allocations during market downturns.

## Policy: national layout and controls

Since the "East Data, West Compute" project was launched four years ago, China's data center market has gradually moved west. The project outlined "eight hubs and ten clusters" to channel more computing resources from the developed east to resource-rich west. It was reinforced in the $15^{\text{th}}$ Five Year Plan, with a nationally integrated computing network as a focus. According to the MIIT, national hubs hosted $72.6\%$ of the country's data center capacity as of mid-2025, and accounted for $80\%$ of the incremental "computility" added during the year.

Meanwhile, the government has been tightening supply in the east, especially in higher-tier markets. Since 2023, a series of directives have been issued, explicitly prohibiting the new build of large-scale data centers (over 7.5MW/3k racks) outside national hubs. The NDRC later enforced this with a stringent "window guidance" pre-approval mechanism for land use and power quotas, which has become the primary gatekeeper for new development. Projects that fail to clear it are effectively blocked from all subsequent development steps.

Based on our talks with VNET's IR team, the approval rate for the first two batches of applications this year remains low (at around 10%). Operator's track record, order backlog, project PUE design, and use of green power are the priorities for selection.

In our view, these regulatory dynamics not only point to the west as the direction for IDC expansion, but at the same time creates a clear advantage for established incumbents who have already secured land banks and power quotas in Tier-1 & satellite areas as well as the strategically important western hubs.

## Economics in the west

Aside from some remote western nodes serving as training centers, there is also a trend for inference demand to spill over from Tier-1 cities to satellite areas (e.g., Kunshan/Changshu in Jiangsu and Langfang/Huailai in Hebei) and subsequently even to some closer western clusters (e.g., Ulanqab/Horinger in Inner Mongolia).

Based on our channel checks, most current inference orders in the Northwest are gravitating towards Inner Mongolia and Ningxia, driven by cheaper land and utility costs, and reduced operating expenses due to cold weather and lower PUE. Latency, which is critical for inference, is favorable particularly in Ulanqab and Horinger, with only 5ms RTT to Beijing. This is sufficient for most inference scenarios including 2C applications, given many are not yet revenue-generating. However, as more latency-sensitive scenarios emerge and 2C monetization begins, we expect more deployments to return to areas surrounding tier-1 cities.

## Key risks

Demand-supply pattern shift. Our thesis of growth and margin expansion at VNET is based on strong demand from hyperscalers and a tightening effective supply of high-quality data centers in core regions. Any significant slowdown in AI demand, a major economic downturn affecting enterprise IT spending, or an overbuild of industry capacity could lead to softer revenue momentum, increased pricing pressure and lower utilization rates.

Regulations and geopolitics. China's regulation on data centers is getting increasingly stringent, particularly concerning land and power approvals. Any major changes to these could impact VNET's competitive advantage, which is partly based on its strategic reserves portfolio. Meanwhile, heightened trade tension and restrictions on technology exports could disrupt the supply for hyperscaler clients, potentially dampening demand for AIDC resources.

Rising funding costs or inability to fund. VNET's growth is largely capex driven, and it has substantial debt on floating basis. Although the company has diversified its financing channels through debt, REITs, convertible bonds, etc., a sustained rise in interest rates would increase borrowing costs and pressure interest coverage ratios. Any difficulty in raising additional funds could force the company to modify or delay its growth plans.

■ Technology upgrades: AI is still in an early stage, and the AI tech stack will therefore change over time. When AI companies adopt a refresh cycle, that may lead to data center requirements changing, devaluing existing space.

\- Execution risk. Delays in securing land and power resources, or obtaining necessary government approvals could hinder VNET's ability to meet customer demand. Also, worse-than-expected operations on contract wins, project delivery, or customer move-ins should lead to a downward estimate revision.

■ VIE structure. The listed entity for VNET's ADRs is a Cayman Islands holding company, which controls the underlying Chinese operating entities through contractual arrangements rather than direct equity ownership. These contracts have not been tested in a PRC court, and there is a risk that PRC authorities could deem the structure non-compliant with local laws.

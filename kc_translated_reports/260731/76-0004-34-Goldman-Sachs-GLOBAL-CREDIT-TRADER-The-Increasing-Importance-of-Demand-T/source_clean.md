# The Increasing Importance of Demand Technicals in Credit

\- USD and EUR investment grade (IG) corporate bond markets are on pace for record amounts of primary market supply this year, and issuance in leveraged finance markets remains brisk as well. While there has been meaningful dispersion across sectors and ratings, most index-level spread valuations are hovering towards the tight end of their (post-financial crisis) historical ranges.

In our view, this reflects a generally supportive demand backdrop, at least in aggregate. It also sparks an obvious question: Can this demand tailwind persist? In this Global Credit Trader, we take stock of several measures of credit market demand to address this question.

At a high level, we expect the aggregate demand technical will be an important offset to the heavy pace of supply, which we anticipate will continue across most markets. That said, the concentrated nature of debt issuance within the AI-related ecosystem is likely to push spreads modestly wider per our existing forecasts, with the risks skewed toward more meaningful widening.

Fund flows show very steady demand for USD IG bonds and leveraged loans so far this year, while flows for USD HY and EUR IG and HY have been more muted (but remain positive). Under the surface, there has been a marked preference for passive ETFs and for shorter duration funds, with inflows into longer duration funds lagging since May.

## The Increasing Importance of Demand Technicals in Credit

The acceleration in new issue activity across the corporate credit market has been closely watched by market participants so far this year. Both the USD and EUR Investment Grade (IG) markets are on pace for new annual gross issuance records, with USD IG issuance approaching \$1.5 trillion year to date (up 31% year over year) and EUR IG volumes at €535 billion (up 4% YoY). Leveraged finance supply has also remained steady, albeit shy of record territory. USD HY gross supply at \$195 billion is up nearly 10% over last year's pace and EUR HY supply at €70 billion is down 13%.

As a result of the heavy pace of primary market activity, investors have been acutely focused on the demand side of the equation. In this Global Credit Trader we take stock of several measures of credit market demand. At a high level, we expect the aggregate demand technical will be an important offset to the heavy pace of supply, which we anticipate will continue across most markets. That said, the concentrated nature of debt issuance within the AI-related ecosystem is likely to push spreads modestly wider per our existing forecasts, with the risks skewed toward more meaningful widening.

## Fund flows: Mostly solid

The most immediate barometer for demand is fund flow data, which provides both real-time and granular insights. According to EPFR, broad USD IG bond ETFs have enjoyed net inflows for 65 consecutive weeks. Exhibit 1 illustrates that USD IG funds have added roughly 8% to their assets under management (AUM) via net inflows this year, while USD bank loan funds (including CLO-focused funds) have added over 6%. The sole laggard has been USD High Yield (HY) funds, which have generated a modest inflow of 0.6% YTD.

In the EUR market, flows have been softer but remain positive. Exhibit 2 highlights YTD cumulative net inflows of 0.4% for pure EUR IG corporate funds and 1.2% for EUR HY funds. While these figures may appear subdued on the surface, it is notable that every major credit market has garnered positive net inflows YTD despite tight valuations and multiple macro headwinds (most prominently, commodity market disruptions from geopolitical conflicts).

Beneath the headline flow data, several structural themes emerge. Exhibit 3 shows cumulative YTD net flows across various vehicles: ETFs (active and passive) and mutual funds. Passive ETFs have dominated YTD flows across most asset classes, while active mutual funds have mostly experienced net outflows. Given the rising dispersion in credit markets, notably around the AI theme, it is likely that investors have migrated to using the ETF wrapper to achieve active manager selection which helps to explain the rise of the smaller, active ETF funds at the expense of mutual funds. The notable exception is in the leveraged loan market, where existing actively managed CLO ETFs have come to dominate loan-related flows over the past few years.


Another important nuance is the recent bifurcation of IG flows across the curve. Exhibit 4 demonstrates that YTD IG corporate fund inflows have been more concentrated in short- and intermediate-duration funds over the last few months, while longer-duration funds have lagged since May (although are still posting inflows). For context, long-duration funds account for 15% of AUM in USD IG corporate funds, with short- and intermediate-duration funds accounting for 36% and 49% of AUM, respectively.

Exhibit 4: Demand for long-end corporate IG bonds has been more tepid than for front-end and intermediate durations
Cumulative year-to-date net flows into USD IG funds as a percentage of AUM, by tenor


[[KC_IMAGE_003]]


While demand from yield-based buyers likely buoyed long duration bonds in the first four months of the year, the demand for long-duration assets has been more tepid as of late. This is likely a dual response to (1) a supply headwind at the long-end of the curve and (2) some residual uncertainty among credit investors related to the path of long-end rates, given the Fed leadership transition and potential changes in the central bank's reaction function and approach to areas such as the balance sheet. Year to date, bonds with a tenor greater than 15 years have accounted for $27.6\%$ of USD IG gross supply, the highest share in five years.

Year-to-date performance also reflects this weakness. USD IG front-end spreads have tightened by 5bp YTD, while spreads on the 25+ year maturity index have widened by 15bp.

## 'Dealer lift' volumes reveal an investor preference for shorter maturities

Another robust metric of corporate credit demand is trade-level data, specifically the “net dealer lift” amount, which measures dealer-to-customer trades reported in TRACE, netting customer buys against customer sells. Aggregate data for both the USD IG and HY bond markets reveals a healthy appetite for corporate bonds, with the IG market on track for record net buying and the HY market pacing for its second-highest year on record after 2020 (Exhibit 5).

Zooming in on the net dealer lift data across the curve corroborates the fund flow narrative: net demand for long-end bonds has underperformed relative to the front end and belly. For instance, bonds with maturities greater than 12 years have accounted for 26% of YTD total trading volumes but account for just 22% of YTD net demand. Conversely, IG bonds with a maturity between 3 and 7 years have represented 27% of aggregate trading volumes this year but have accounted for an outsized 49% of net customer demand.

## Other measures of demand also point to a solid backdrop

To complete our review of corporate bond demand, we look at three additional metrics: foreign net purchases of US bonds, new issue concessions, and passive demand from recycled coupon payments. All three point to a supportive backdrop.

Exhibit 6 shows a 3-month average of net purchases of US corporate bonds using the Treasury TIC data. (Note: this data references the residence of the issuer, not the currency of the bond.) The key takeaway is that foreign demand (with a caveat that the data is lagged by six weeks) has currently reached new post-financial crisis highs. In fact, the top five highest monthly totals for net foreign demand since the end of the financial crisis have all come in the last 18-months. As of May 2026, net long-term purchases by foreign investors totaled \$439 billion over the trailing 12-month period, pushing total foreign holdings to a record \$5.3 trillion. Looking below the surface, about half of the foreign demand so far this year has originated from Europe, with the rest spread across other regions.

## Exhibit 6: TIC data shows near record foreign demand for US credit

3-month moving average of net foreign purchases of US domestic corporate bonds

Second, we monitor new issue concessions to gauge the market's digestion of incremental supply. Despite recent anecdotes of a few large AI-related deals pricing with higher-than-typical concessions, the aggregate data paints a more benign picture—at least so far. Exhibit 7 tracks the 4-week moving average of our estimate of new issue concessions for the USD and EUR IG markets. While both markets experienced upward pressure during the early stages of the Middle East conflict, they have since restabilized in the low- to mid-single digits—a range consistent with the past decade. Given our expectations for additional debt issuance from the AI-related ecosystem, we will be monitoring this metric closely.

None of these demand measures is comprehensive on its own—each has its flaws and potential blind spots. That said, this broad array of metrics points to a supportive aggregate demand technical. We expect this will be an important mitigant to the heavy pace of supply that we expect.

That said, the concentrated nature of debt issuance within the AI-related ecosystem is likely to push spreads modestly wider per our existing forecasts, with the risks skewed toward more meaningful widening.

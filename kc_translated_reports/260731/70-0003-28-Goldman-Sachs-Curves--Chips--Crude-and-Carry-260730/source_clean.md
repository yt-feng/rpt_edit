EM Equities: Tech Unwind Extends, Non-Tech Resilient to Renewed Energy Shock. The EM equity index (MSCI EM) has retraced 12% from its late June peak, reversing about half of its strong 1H gains driven by ongoing unwinds in crowded tech and AI infrastructure stocks, and renewed escalation in the US-Iran conflict. While the tech unwind has extended in recent days, non-tech EM equities have shown a muted response to rising oil prices so far, with MSCI EM ex-tech up 2% in July. This resilience is supported by lower starting valuations and price levels in oil-sensitive markets, which remain meaningfully below pre-war levels. Furthermore, strong micro fundamentals have cushioned the downside as early 2Q results are tracking ahead of consensus estimates, with positive median earnings surprise – and beats outpacing misses – driving further EPS upgrades.

Still Expect EM Broadening on Improving Earnings and Diversification Appeal. While the global de-grossing in tech has accelerated in recent weeks amid investor fears around rising China competition, AI capex sustainability, and a high bar for 2Q tech earnings, we remain fundamentally constructive and expect continued strong profit growth in AI infrastructure stocks. Volatility could stay elevated in the near term, however, as leveraged positions, although reset from highs, have not fully cleared. As long as macro volatility related to oil and rates is contained, we continue to expect a broadening in EM equities beyond tech, supported by underlying earnings accrual and the regional diversification appeal of non-tech markets, as evidenced during past major TMT drawdowns.

EM Sovereign Credit Issuance: Robust in H1, With More to Come in H2. Despite concerns that large amounts of AI-related debt supply may be crowding out other sectors or countries, EM gross sovereign USD bond issuance has been robust so far this year, propelled by the forward spend on security and energy infrastructure by IG GCC sovereigns since the onset of the Iran war. Both EM IG and HY H1 issuance stand at their second-highest levels in a decade. Our core view is still that the drivers of such robust issuance include the prevalence of favourable issuance terms, with sovereigns taking advantage of tight spreads, and elevated investor demand for primary supply. Looking ahead, our updated full-year 2026 issuance forecast stands at \~US\$209bn, with the lion's share from IG (\~US\$138bn, 66%), and HY contributing \~US\$72bn (34%). While our forecasts appear consistent with a bullish risk-on scenario that is not our baseline, we think they reflect a strong technical backdrop and a large idiosyncratic issuance profile from IG GCC sovereigns. This gross supply should translate into elevated net supply by the end of the year, which should in turn contribute to the moderate spread widening we expect over the next 12 months.

Still a path for RV carry between energy and rate risks, watching carry-to-vol closely. The disruptive Fed hike tail looks to be curtailed for now, especially given our economists' expectations for relatively soft upcoming inflation prints, but there are still rate risks from a continued selloff in the long-end of the curve and high energy prices. The easiest way to resolve these risks is a sustained path to lower energy prices, but until that point EM FX carry structures will likely continue to balance risk and reward by choosing both longs and funders carefully, maximising carry-to-vol and monitoring closely local developments that can challenge carry returns. Looking at carry and carry-to-vol ratios, BRL and COP remain the strongest propositions by a wide margin (Exhibit 7) and this is also the case in carry-to-vol terms versus the Dollar (Exhibit 8). Set against this high carry and positive exposure to energy prices, these currencies' high betas to US long-end yields are a key vulnerability. Between the two, the upcoming pick-up in election noise in Brazil and continuation of a rate cutting cycle (whereas BanRep in Colombia is likely to deliver another hike this week) lead us to prefer COP longs over BRL here. Within the next set of currencies with around $3\%$ carry versus the Dollar (INR, IDR, MXN, ZAR), there are importance differences in carry-to-vol dynamics and local developments that lead us to prefer MXN carry followed by INR and ZAR, while maintaining a more cautious view on IDR due to domestic developments. MXN stands out from a carry-to-vol perspective, especially versus the Euro where this ratio has been steadily increasing (Exhibit 9). Moreover, the Peso's relative resilience to energy price shifts and exposure to US cyclical pricing argue for MXN carry longs. USMCA negotiation risks and a dovish Banxico in a hawkish Fed scenario are the main risks to this view, but our base case is for no Fed hikes and for USMCA negotiations to be less contentious for Mexico than for Canada. INR's carry-to-vol has also been rising supported by the RBI's measures to mitigate FX depreciation pressures, though this

also means the scope for spot appreciation is more limited, in our view. Moreover, INR remains vulnerable to a more disruptive energy price scenario, although we think pairing INR longs with THB shorts (where we maintain a bearish view) can help neutralise this exposure to a large extent. ZAR carry-to-vol is significantly lower than for its peers. On one hand, higher volatility means that the Rand can outperform in an energy relief scenario, but equally that it is not the most attractive carry long in a more uncertain backdrop, especially if central bank support is also less certain than before. HUF carry and carry-to-vol has declined substantially since the election as rates have moved lower. We expect this yield convergence to the Euro area to continue, which means HUF longs are increasingly more of a spot than a carry trade. We maintain a constructive HUF outlook over the medium term, and forecast spot appreciation to 350 in EUR/HUF in 6 months. However, with limited central bank support and energy supply, and price risks, we think near-term dynamics look more challenging. Pairing HUF longs with PLN shorts can be an effective way to neutralise HUF's energy exposure while playing for a convergence in FX valuations from both sides. Turning to potential funders, we note that carry for many low-yielders is at its least negative levels in a year (Exhibit 7), including TWD and KRW, but it is at its lowest levels for PLN and ILS, and THB remains one of the lowest carry EM currencies. Overall, our preferred funders in G10 are CHF, EUR, JPY and CAD in roughly that order, and in EM, as mentioned above, we think THB and PLN screen as attractive funders from both a carry and spot asymmetry perspective. Additionally, the Shekel's high beta to tech stocks and Bol pushback against a strong currency make ILS an attractive funder in our view. And, while we do not have an outright bearish view on CLP, its low carry and high beta to risk and energy prices makes it an attractive funder for investors looking to neutralise broader risk exposures. The Peso's underperformance relative to terms of trade through mid-July would argue against CLP shorts tactically, but with the more recent retracement over the last week, USD/CLP looks more closely aligned with fundamentals than a week ago.

## EM Local Rates: Fed up with Energy

EM Local Rates: Still captive to the barrel, but less so than before. Uncertainty around a sustainable resolution to the Iran war and the energy price volatility this generates remains the primary driver of EM and G10 rates (Exhibit 10). But compared to the start of the war, when the sharp moves in rates that followed energy price spikes were somewhat amplified by investor positioning, with consensus receiver trades in anticipation of rate cuts (HUF, BRL and ZAR, and GBP in G10) hit the hardest, positioning has become cleaner. That, combined with an inflation backdrop that has proven to be more benign than initially feared, as well as central bank reaction functions that have turned out to be more dovish relative to

market pricing at the start of the war, particularly in CEEMEA, has helped trim the more adverse tails in local rates pricing despite the recent bouts of energy price increases. By the same token, however, we continue to think that any relief in local rates from the downside inflation surprises is likely to be more limited until there is more visibility on the supply shocks from oil, a possible El Niño effect and natural gas prices heading into the winter. While our bias through this period has been to fade the excessive selloffs, we think more tactical discipline is warranted against a backdrop of still elevated commodity prices and the prospects for core central bank hikes. For now, prospects appear more compelling in markets where rates have backed up and central banks have a dovish tilt, such as PLN, HUF and ILS (Exhibit 11). On the other side of the EM local rates distribution, while MXN front-ends have also backed up and local inflation dynamics remain benign, we remain cautious here on account of low-interest rate differentials to the US and the possibility of Fed hikes (see below).

EM central banks have leaned against the pre-emptive hikes priced at the start of the Iran war, with some delivering rate cuts instead. At the onset of the Iran war, we argued that the knee-jerk reaction and repricing in front-end rates would likely prove to be too hawkish to sustain because the initial conditions differed quite substantially from the energy shock in 2022. Almost 6 months into the war, EM central banks have leaned against the hikes that were priced, with some, including Hungary, Brazil and Mexico, even moving to cut rates over this period (Exhibit 12). More recently, the dovish surprise from the SARB to hold rates at a meeting that was fully priced for a hike, the BI's decision to hold, also against expectations for hike, and the NBP's surprise signal for a September cut suggest that many EM central banks are willing to wait for more clarity even if this comes with near-term FX weakness. That may be a reasonable course for the EM high-yielders that came into the energy shock with real policy rates at restrictive levels, but the same cannot be said for the EM low-yielders in Asia, such as Korea and Philippines, where real policy rates are low, prompting policy makers in both markets to embark on a hiking cycle (Exhibit 14). The easing bias in Brazil also remains intact, with the front-end screening attractive in our view, but realised rates vol is likely to be high heading into the October elections, and we prefer to stay on the sidelines here. In PLN, market pricing has shifted back to ca. 30bp of rate hikes over the next 12 months despite the central bank signalling a September cut. We think these hikes are unlikely to be delivered given inflation dynamics that remain supportive, and we favour receiving the front-end there.

## EM Equities: Non-Tech Resilient; Still Expect Broadening

EM Equities: Tech unwind extends, but muted EM equity response to the recent energy shock. EM equities have remained under pressure over the past month amid ongoing positioning de-grossing in the tech sector and renewed escalation in the US-Iran conflict that led to a decline in oil flows and pushed oil prices higher. Consequently, the MSCI EM index has corrected 12% from its June 22 peak, reversing about half of its strong 1H gains. Looking beneath the headline EM index, the pattern is similar across regions, with most markets except for the AI/tech-heavy North Asia (Korea and Taiwan) seeing muted moves in response to the current oil shock.

EM Sovereign Credit Issuance: Robust in H1, With More to Come in H2
■ EM gross sovereign issuance standing tall amid the H1 vol ... Despite concerns that large amounts of AI-related debt supply may be crowding out other sectors or countries, EM gross sovereign USD bond issuance has been robust so far this year, propelled by the forward spend on security and energy infrastructure by IG GCC sovereigns since the onset of the Iran war. About US\$126bn of gross supply has reached EM markets, with about 63% coming from IG sovereigns (\~US\$79bn), but also a significant amount (\~US\$47bn) from HY (Exhibit 30). Importantly, both IG and HY H1 issuance are at their second-highest levels in a decade (Exhibit 31). This puts total EM gross USD bond issuance on track towards our updated full-year forecast at \~US\$209bn (more below), up from \~US\$190bn at the beginning of the year, with another \~US\$58bn to come in IG and \~US\$25bn in HY, which would make IG

issuance its highest in a decade, and HY issuance in line with its decade-high of 2017 (Exhibit 30).

Zooming in on monthly data, we find that IG issuance was somewhat less seasonal than usual in H1. We had taken stock of the record amount of IG issuance at the start of the year (Exhibit 32). IG issuance was brought to halt in March, as the onset of the Iran war at the end of February coincided with a risk-off shock, before surging in April to almost ten times its historical April median, and stabilising in subsequent months (Exhibit 32). In contrast, HY supply realised closer to its historical monthly medians than IG supply in H1, thanks to oil exporters issuing in the initial phase of the war, and oil importers coming to the market as geopolitical volatility subsequently subsided. Put together, while IG supply was more volatile, both segments of the EM sovereign credit market contributed to the relative resilience of total supply in the first half, with IG supply exceeding its historical monthly median in four of the first six months of the year, and HY supply exceeding its median in five out of six months. July IG issuance is also tracking above its historical median (Exhibit 32).

Our core view is still that the drivers of such robust issuance include the prevalence of favourable issuance terms, with sovereigns taking advantage of tight spreads: spreads quickly retraced their March weakness after the onset of the Iran war and returned to historically tight levels, that is, historically attractive levels from an issuer's point of view. Alongside the solid spread performance post-issuance, strong investor demand is visible when benchmarking the realised cumulative supply so far this year against global market fundamentals. To do so, we regress total EM monthly issuance levels on a set of four global market variables and monthly dummy variables to control for the seasonality of supply. The set of global market variables is obtained by taking the set of four variables that maximises the R-squared of the regression when choosing combinations of four variables from a larger set. We find that the variables that best explain the variation in issuance levels are EMBI Global Diversified spreads, the MOVE index of US rate volatility, Brent prices and the S&P 500 index. We then back out predicted cumulative levels, and compare them to the realised supply, with the results shown in Exhibit 35. For each of the first six months of the year, cumulative EM issuance has realised higher than model predictions, pointing to a positive contribution from technical and non-global or non-seasonal factors, which include solid investor demand. As mentioned above, our updated full-year 2026 issuance forecast stands at \~US\$209bn, with the lion's share from IG (\~US\$138bn, 66%), and HY contributing \~US\$72bn (34%). While our forecasts appear consistent with a bullish risk-on scenario (EM spreads 100bp tighter, MOVE index down 40 points, Brent prices down 15% and US equity prices up 20%, green dotted line in Exhibit 35) that is not our baseline, we think they reflect a strong technical backdrop and a large idiosyncratic issuance profile from IG GCC sovereigns (more below). In particular, our forecast is notably higher than out-of-sample model predictions based on the current values of market variables. Risks to our forecasts are skewed to the downside given our view of moderate spread widening and the possibility of higher-for-longer oil prices and renewed rate volatility, which according to our model would bring total 2026 EM issuance closer to US\$150bn (red dotted line in Exhibit 35).

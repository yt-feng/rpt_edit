# Assessing Microsoft's Infrastructure Investments

Where is Microsoft's Data Center Capacity Actually Going? Following our recent report Infrastructure Monetization – Still Early Days in the AI Cycle (27 May 2026), we took a deeper dive into Microsoft's data center capacity allocations. Microsoft is undertaking one of the largest infrastructure buildouts in the history of technology, with hyperscaler capacity expected to expand dramatically over the coming years. However, not all AI infrastructure serves the same purpose, with capacity supporting various use cases. Specifically, management has been clear that the company needs to 'balance Azure revenue growth with the growing needs across our first-party apps and AI solutions [i.e., Copilot], our own R&D efforts, and the end of life server replacements'. In the below exhibit, we utilize our latest estimates for Microsoft's total installed capacity and attempt to break down a potential capacity mix across four primary buckets: 1) Core Azure, which includes traditional cloud workloads such as compute, storage, databases, analytics and networking; 2) Azure AI, which includes AI infrastructure services, model hosting, training, inference, AI development platforms and AI-native customer workloads; 3) First-Party Applications (Internal Apps), which includes Microsoft's growing portfolio of AI-enabled products such as M365 Copilot, GitHub Copilot, Dynamics, Security and other first-party AI solutions; and 4) Internal R&D, which includes proprietary model development, frontier AI research, experimentation and future platform capabilities. We believe this framework is important because each workload category carries a distinctly different monetization profile. Core Azure remains the most mature and predictable infrastructure business, while Azure AI monetizes primarily through infrastructure and platform consumption. In contrast, Microsoft's 1P Applications represent the highest-value layer of the stack, where Microsoft can leverage its existing distribution, installed base (>450MM users) and software ecosystem to generate substantially higher revenue and profit per MW of compute deployed. While demand is clearly important, we believe investors should increasingly focus on where supply (or capacity) is ultimately being allocated. If an increasing share of incremental capacity is directed toward Azure AI and Microsoft's own AI-enabled applications, the company's infrastructure monetization profile should progressively migrate away from pure infrastructure economics (i.e., bare-metal Neoclouds) and toward higher-value platform/software economics, supporting stronger revenue per MW, gross profit per MW and total return profile over time (see Exhibit 6).

Assessing Microsoft's Revenue Per MW Framework. We estimate Azure AI workloads become the largest consumer of Microsoft's installed data center capacity over the next few years, growing from roughly 11% of capacity in FY24 to \~43% by FY28, as demand for AI training, inference and platform services continues to accelerate. We estimate first-party applications account for \~21% of installed capacity by FY28 (or >4GW of capacity), reflecting the compute requirements associated with Microsoft 365, Dynamics, Security, GitHub, etc., as well as an expanding portfolio of AI-enabled software offerings. While these workloads are less capital intense than Azure AI, they support a larger portion of Microsoft Cloud revenue, resulting in higher revenue per MW of deployed infrastructure. In our view, this highlights an important distinction in Microsoft's AI strategy – although Azure and Azure AI drives much of the company's infrastructure investments, a substantial portion of the value ultimately accrues at the software and application layer. This dynamic clearly impacted the stock reaction following the most recent two

earnings, as the Azure growth beat factor (vs. management guidance) has come down from roughly 2pts+ above the high-end of guide (historically) to \~1pt above or inline with the high-end due to the supply-constrained environment and incremental capacity coming online each quarter. For example, during the F2Q26 earnings call, CFO Amy Hood clarified 'if I had taken the GPUs that just came online in Q1 and Q2 in terms of GPUs and allocated them all to Azure, the KPI would have been over 40 [more inline with buyside expectations at the time vs. reported 38% YoY growth in cc]. And I think the most important thing to realize is that this is about investing in all the layers of the stack that benefit customers.' Bottom line, unlike a traditional AI infrastructure provider that primarily monetizes compute capacity, Azure AI increasingly serves as an entry point into the broader Azure (and Microsoft) ecosystem, driving pull-through demand for databases, storage, developer tools, Azure AI Foundry, and other platform services. As AI workloads become more complex and enterprise deployments scale, we expect a growing portion of Microsoft's AI revenue to come from these higher-value platform capabilities rather than infrastructure consumption alone. Importantly, this pull-through should allow Microsoft to capture value across multiple layers of the tech stack, enabling Azure AI to monetize in a meaningfully more attractive way than a bare-metal AI infrastructure business, which should ultimately drive better revenue monetization and a more attractive margin profile over the long-run.

As shown in Exhibit 6, we estimate Azure AI will become the largest consumer of Microsoft's installed compute capacity over time, reflecting the infrastructure required to support AI training, inference, and platform services. However, despite consuming a smaller share of capacity, we estimate Microsoft's first-party application portfolio continues to generate materially higher revenue per MW of deployed infrastructure. This reflects the ability to monetize AI through software subscriptions, workflow automation, productivity enhancements, and an evolving business model (i.e., Copilot combining SaaS + consumption) rather than purely through infrastructure consumption. As a result, we believe Microsoft's long-term return profile on AI will be determined not only by Azure AI growth, but also by the company's ability to successfully embed AI capabilities across Microsoft 365 (i.e., Copilot), Security, Dynamics, GitHub and other first-party offerings.

For our 1P application revenue estimates, we assume management is referring more broadly to Microsoft's portfolio of cloud-delivered offerings that consume infrastructure capacity alongside Azure-related workloads. This includes a swathe of products such as broader Microsoft 365, Dynamics, Security, GitHub, and more, as well as the growing portfolio of AI-enabled features embedded within these offerings (i.e., Copilot). Under this framework, first-party applications represent a significant and growing allocation of data center capacity, reflecting both the scale of Microsoft's existing core software franchise and the increasing compute requirements associated with delivering AI-enhanced experiences across the product portfolio.

## Azure AI Monetization and Margin Profile: Bare-Metal Neocloud, Core Azure, or Full-Stack AI Platform?

One of the most important questions surrounding Microsoft's AI infrastructure investments is not how much capacity the company ultimately deploys, but rather how effectively that capacity is monetized. While investors increasingly focus on Microsoft's multi-year AI infrastructure buildout, there remains significant debate around the ultimate revenue and profit profile of these assets. To frame the range of potential outcomes, we developed an MS Azure AI Monetization Model - a sensitivity analysis that evaluates revenue, gross profit and operating profit generation across three distinct monetization scenarios. Our analysis begins with Microsoft's estimated Azure AI capacity footprint, which we forecast grows from approximately 3GW in FY26 to \~9GW by FY28. We then evaluate the three scenarios reflecting different levels of monetization, allowing us to estimate potential revenue, gross profit and EBIT per MW of deployed capacity.

Scenario #1: Azure AI Monetizes Like a Bare-Metal Neocloud. In our first scenario, we assume Azure AI behaves similarly to a commodity AI infrastructure provider. Under this framework, customers primarily purchase GPU compute, model hosting and inference capacity, with limited platform differentiation and minimal pull-through into higher-value Microsoft services. Revenue generation remains largely tied to infrastructure consumption and pricing becomes increasingly competitive as AI compute capacity expands across the ecosystem. Under this scenario, Azure AI remains primarily an infrastructure business with economics resembling those of a bare-metal Neocloud provider. While capacity utilization improves over time, revenue per unit of deployed infrastructure remains relatively constrained and margin expansion is limited. In this outcome, Azure AI functions largely as a compute utility rather than a differentiated cloud platform. We think this level of monetization (\$8-10MM/MW) is similar to the assumptions embedded in our estimates and what the market is pricing-in, creating a very strong case for upside revisions and positive surprise.

Scenario #2: Azure AI Monetization Trends Similar to Core Public Cloud – AI IaaS First, then Higher Level AI PaaS. Our second scenario assumes Azure AI ultimately monetizes in a similar trajectory to Microsoft's traditional Azure business. In particular, AI workloads evolve beyond simple compute consumption and increasingly pull through broader cloud services (i.e., storage, databases, analytics, security, developer tools). This mirrors the historical cloud adoption model where infrastructure serves as the foundation for a broader platform ecosystem. Conceptually, Azure AI becomes the next generation of cloud infrastructure. Just as traditional Azure workloads generated demand for higher-value cloud services, AI workloads increasingly drive consumption across Microsoft's broader cloud stack. As a result, Azure AI economics gradually converge closer toward Azure economics over time, producing revenue scale, gross margins and operating margins comparable to those of Core Azure in prior periods, with Azure AI becoming more of a large-scale cloud platform business rather than just purely infrastructure. Historically, Azure IaaS represented a minority of Azure revenue, with PaaS mix >60% for Azure, driving overall gross margins above 60% – in this scenario, trends are similar, with revenue / MW in the high-teens, though margins still lower given higher capital intensity of workloads.

Scenario #3: Azure AI Becomes a Full-Stack AI Platform/Provider. In our third scenario, we assume Azure AI achieves revenue per MW consistent with the combination of Core Azure and 1st-Party Applications (as a proxy for an AI platform), reflecting Microsoft's ability to monetize across the full AI stack with agentic tools and workflows. This case also assumes Microsoft benefits from further efficiency gains (similar to CEO Satya Nadella's recent comments: 'Our Maia 200 AI accelerator, which offers over 30% improved tokens per dollar, compared to the latest silicon in our fleet, is now live in our Iowa and Arizona datacenters'), potential 1st party LLM creation, and increased Microsoft-specific IP within the AI infrastructure ecosystem (i.e., better scale with Maia/Cobalt, 1P silicon, etc.). Beyond that, Azure AI becomes the foundation for a broader AI ecosystem that drives incremental adoption across Microsoft's software portfolio. AI workloads increasingly pull through additional consumption across security, developer tools, data services, workflow automation and future agentic offerings. In particular, Azure AI generates materially higher revenue per MW of deployed capacity than traditional cloud infrastructure. Revenue growth benefits not only from infrastructure consumption but also from platform and application-layer monetization. Gross margins and operating margins expand meaningfully as Microsoft captures a larger portion of the value created by AI workloads, with Azure AI capturing value up the stack beyond cloud infrastructure, becoming a key high-value business within the broader Microsoft platform. In this scenario, overall revenue per MW crosses above \$20MM/MW, and while gross margins are lower than current Azure gross margins, they trend >50% with significant opex leverage, leading to a similar operating margin % in the out years.

Bottom line, our analysis suggests that Microsoft's Azure AI return profile will likely be determined less by the amount of infrastructure deployed and more by the level of monetization achieved on top of that infrastructure. While all three scenarios assume a similar underlying capacity footprint, the revenue outcomes and margin profiles vary materially depend on whether Azure AI ultimately behaves as a commodity compute provider, a cloud platform business comparable to traditional Core Azure, or more like a full-stack AI platform. While we continue to view Scenario #1 as a floor for Azure AI monetization and believe the market remains largely anchored to this outcome currently, our latest revenue estimates increasingly reflect a blend of Scenario #1 and Scenario #2. Specifically, we are increasing our Azure AI and overall Azure revenue estimates to better reflect improving platform monetization, as AI workloads increasingly pull through higher-value Azure services beyond infrastructure consumption alone. While we conservatively do not yet assume Azure AI fully monetizes like a mature cloud platform, we believe the business is gradually moving beyond pure GPU infrastructure economics, supporting a higher long-term revenue opportunity than currently reflected in consensus.

## Copilot Adoption Continues to Accelerate as the Business Model Evolves Beyond Seats

Microsoft 365 Copilot is increasingly emerging as one of the most important application-layer monetization opportunities within Microsoft's AI strategy. Over the past year, the primary debate surrounding Copilot centered on product-market fit and whether enterprises would be willing to deploy AI assistants at scale. Today, however, adoption continues to broaden, usage intensity is increasing, and customers are moving from pilot deployments toward larger enterprise-wide rollouts. As shown below in our MS CIO Survey (see Exhibit 13 - Exhibit 15), 88% of CIOs in our latest survey now expect to deploy M365 Copilot within the next 12 months, up from 80% in 4Q25 and 72% only a year ago, while expected deployment penetration continues to increase on both a near-term and longer-term basis.

Reflecting these trends, as well as the latest management guidance during FQ3, we are increasing our M365 Copilot estimates and now model Copilot revenue of roughly \$4.4bn in FY26, growing to \~\$22.5bn by FY29. Similarly, we now forecast \~26mm Copilot seats in FY26, growing to \~115mm by FY29, reflecting materially stronger adoption assumptions than our prior estimates (see Exhibit 12). Importantly, as mentioned above, Microsoft has historically monetized its core software business primarily through seat expansion and license upgrades. However, the monetization framework has been evolving recently toward a hybrid model that combines users, platform upgrades and consumption. The introduction of M365 E7 represents an important step in this transition. Rather than positioning AI as a standalone add-on, E7 bundles together E5, Copilot, and Agent365 (includes AI-enabled workflow capabilities) into a higher-value suite, creating a new path for enterprise customers to standardize on Microsoft's AI platform. Similar to the transition from E3 to E5, we believe E7 has the potential to drive a new multi-year upgrade cycle, supporting both higher ARPU and broader Copilot adoption. Our latest MS CIO Survey already points to continued migration up the Microsoft stack, with 47% of organizations currently standardized on E5 and 7% on E7, while expectations for next year are expected to reach 50% and 21%, respectively (see Exhibit 16 & Exhibit 17).

## We also believe Microsoft's recent launch of Copilot Cowork represents an

underappreciated evolution in the product strategy. Historically, Copilot functioned primarily as an AI assistant operating alongside users within M365 applications; however, Cowork expands this model by enabling teams to collaborate directly with AI-powered agents that dive deeper into a user's workflows, coordinate tasks and operate across shared business processes. In our view, this further reinforces Microsoft's broader vision of moving beyond chat-based assistants toward agentic systems that increasingly add value. As organizations move from experimenting with AI assistants to deploying AI agents embedded across everyday workflows, the revenue opportunity becomes increasingly tied to usage and business process automation rather than seat count alone.

Bottom line, we believe the Copilot story is increasingly transitioning from a seat adoption story into focusing more on broader AI platform monetization. Between accelerating enterprise adoption, the E7 SKU as a new upgrade vehicle, growing usage intensity, and new agentic capabilities (i.e., Cowork), we see multiple avenues for ARPU expansion across the M365 installed base. Importantly, we believe Copilot-driven ARPU is increasingly becoming a three-headed growth driver: (1) direct Copilot seat adoption, (2) migration toward higher-value M365 E7 subscriptions, and (3) consumption-based monetization tied to agents, orchestration and workflow automation. While also remaining focused on seat count today, we believe the latter two vectors could become increasingly important contributors to growth over time, materially expanding Microsoft's AI monetization opportunity beyond the traditional per-user software model. As a result, we continue to believe Copilot represents one of the most significant long-term revenue and margin opportunities within the broader Microsoft ecosystem, which remains underappreciated today.

## Gross Margin Headwinds, Gross Profit Tailwinds

Investment Cycles Yield. For Microsoft, the pace and scale of this AI infrastructure build-out are more significant than the original cloud infrastructure build / transition, but the over-arching theme on the investment cycle and margin trajectory remains the same: After capex accelerates, data center footprint is built, capacity and revenue increase and over-time, efficiencies drive meaningful margin expansion. Importantly, management has communicated AI gross margins are already higher than they were at this point in the prior cloud transition (unclear if measured by scale or time). Below, we 1) benchmark gross margins vs. similar AI infrastructure businesses, 2) dig into the history of Microsoft's Cloud gross margin improvements, and 3) lay out the main drivers of AI related gross margin expansion.

1. Benchmarking AI Infrastructure Margins. Hyperscaler segment operating margins are imperfect AI infrastructure benchmarks because they include broad mixes of compute, storage, database, software, support, and application-layer services. However, they are still useful as scaled-platform reference points. Neoclouds are more useful for identifying the lower-bound economics of leased GPU capacity, while Oracle is the closest public AI infrastructure benchmark given its disclosed AI delivered-capacity gross margin. Reported neocloud gross margins (CRWV and NBIS) appear high on the surface, but they put the majority of depreciation in opex. Standardized gross margins are lower if depreciation is treated as a cost of revenue (appropriate for comparing to Microsoft). Oracle is useful as an AI infrastructure benchmark because it has disclosed delivered AI capacity gross margin (+32% in 3FQ26). Microsoft primarily owns and operates the infrastructure, but it also monetizes through Azure services, Azure AI Foundry, Copilot, Microsoft 365, GitHub, Dynamics, security, database, and broader software attach. These facts and mix should allow Microsoft to earn materially better margins over time than a pure leased-GPU provider, provided utilization and pricing discipline remain strong, yet it is useful to benchmark against the neoclouds and Oracle to find a likely floor for realistic Azure AI gross margins.

Near-Term Gross Margin % Headwind Reminder: Near-term gross margin pressure is partly mechanical. When new AI capacity comes online, lease, power, labor, and depreciation costs begin before the asset reaches mature revenue run-rate. There is also a lag between powered-shell readiness, server provisioning, testing, customer handoff, and billable consumption. Given the scale of new capacity relative to Microsoft's installed AI footprint, rapid growth can understate normalized gross margin. This is why lower observed margins during a build cycle should not be extrapolated linearly into steady-state economics. The benchmarking work supports a path to Azure AI gross margins above 40% over time in our model, particularly as utilization, pricing, first-party silicon, and software efficiency improve.

2. Historical Analysis – Digging into Microsoft Cloud Gross Margins and the historical analogue of cloud margin improvement at Microsoft during the transition to the cloud. This historical analogue is important because the current AI build is depressing gross margin before the full revenue and utilization benefits accrue. While the AI build is greater in scale and pace, and ultimately the capital intensity of AI workloads is greater, we expect the same trends to occur with the AI revenue and gross profit ramp as revenue scales and gross margins improve from 1) business model changes, 2) operational efficiency, 3) software efficiency, 4) hardware/silicon efficiency, and 5) partnership IP (all detailed below).

The AI Numbers Are Bigger But We Expect Trends to be Similar. We mark the start of the broader Microsoft cloud build out in FY13, where capex jumped from a steady \$2-\$2.5 billion prior by 85% to \$4.2 billion, increasing by more than 4x from FY12 baseline to FY17. It took four years until annual Microsoft cloud revenue surpassed total annual capex (FY16) and it took six years (FY18) until annual cloud gross profit surpassed annual capex. Fast forward to FY23 (scaled cloud but pre-AI build), annual cloud revenue of \$112B was 3.5x capex of \$32B and FY23 cloud gross profit of \$80B was 2.5x capex. Said differently, capital intensity decreased from >200% in FY14 to \~50% by FY18 and <30% by FY22 for Microsoft's cloud business, clearly demonstrating strong yield on investment and ROIC for Microsoft Cloud.

3. Drivers of Microsoft's Azure AI Gross Margin Expansion. Microsoft's CFO Amy Hood has consistently made the case that the AI margin trajectory is structurally superior to what Microsoft experienced during the server-to-cloud transition. In the FY2025 Q3 earnings call (April 2025), Amy stated explicitly: 'Our margins on the AI side of the business are better than they were at this point by far than when we went through the same transition and the server to cloud transition.' This notion was reiterated on the

3FQ26 earnings call in April 2026. From our Microsoft Campus visit (see Microsoft: Redmond Reflections & More (17 Dec 2025)), we know Microsoft has internal teams dedicated to driving efficiencies across data centers and Azure gross margins – including Azure AI. Each team aligns to a set of internal KPIs reflecting the area of margin efficiency in focus. These KPIs likely correspond to the various categories of margin expansion:

\- Business Model – More Consumption Based to Align Usage to Value Capture. While this theme is relevant for Microsoft 365 Commercial Cloud, historically a seat-based model now with consumption elements to align usage with price paid, this theme is also applicable to GitHub Copilot which sits in Azure AI. Microsoft's AI monetization is not limited to fixed-price seats. The company is increasingly aligning AI pricing with usage, consumption, and customer value. Amy Hood highlighted usage-based pricing as one reason AI margins are better than early cloud margins, and Microsoft has already changed GitHub Copilot pricing to better align price with usage and value. Satya Nadella has also described seat-based AI pricing as an entitlement to a certain level of consumption, with overages and larger commitments shifting toward pure consumption economics. This matters for gross margin because fixed-price AI products can face margin pressure when user engagement rises faster than price. A pricing structure with metered usage, overage capture, customer commitments, and discounting tied to volume should allow Microsoft to better match revenue to inference cost. The model becomes especially powerful as AI usage expands from experimentation to production workloads, because Microsoft can monetize both the seat and the incremental compute intensity.

\- Operational – Faster Build to Revenue Conversion. Microsoft is attacking the time gap between capex and revenue. In Q3 FY26, Satya Nadella said Microsoft had reduced dock-to-live times by nearly 20%, brought its Fairwater data center online six weeks early, and added more than one gigawatt of capacity while remaining on track to double its data center footprint in two years. The gross margin implication is straightforward: shorter construction, installation, and commissioning cycles reduce the period in which assets are depreciating or consuming cash before generating revenue. Faster build-to-revenue conversion also reduces the risk of stranded capacity and improves the denominator over which fixed data center costs are spread.

\- Software – More Tokens Per GPU & Lower Cost Per Token. In Q3 FY26, Microsoft said inference throughput improved 40% and highlighted first-party model work that lowered cost of goods sold, including model-specific GPU efficiency gains. If Microsoft can raise token throughput per GPU, improve scheduling and utilization, use smaller or more specialized models where appropriate, and optimize the serving stack, then each dollar of GPU capex can support more billable consumption. Satya's Q3 FY26 framing is that the hyperscale advantage comes from system-wide software optimization, not just owning data centers.

\- Hardware & Silicon – 1st Party & 3rd Party Improvements. Microsoft is increasingly using first-party silicon and system-level optimization to improve cost/performance. In Q3 FY26, the company pointed to custom networking, security, and virtualization silicon through Azure Boost; first-party CPUs and accelerators;

Maia 200 with more than $30\%$ better tokens per dollar versus the latest silicon in its fleet; and Cobalt server CPUs deployed in nearly half of Microsoft's data center regions. The margin benefit is twofold: First, first-party silicon can reduce the profit pool captured by third-party hardware vendors over time. Second, Microsoft can optimize hardware, software, networking, storage, and model serving together. That full-stack control is a major difference between Microsoft and many AI infrastructure providers that are largely reselling merchant GPU capacity.

What Could Go Wrong? The primary risks to this gross margin framework are lower-than-expected utilization, faster GPU obsolescence, power and supply-chain cost inflation, pricing compression in AI infrastructure, and fixed-price AI products where usage grows faster than monetization.

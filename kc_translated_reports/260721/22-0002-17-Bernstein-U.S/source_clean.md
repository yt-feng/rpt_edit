LTAs won't remove memory cyclicality, but they can meaningfully reduce the severity of downturns by raising the earnings floor and improving revenue and profit visibility. LTAs effectiveness ultimately depends on whether the financial guarantees charged are substantial enough to offset the savings customers could realize by purchasing memory at prevailing lower market prices. LTAs can provide meaningful downside protection, but remain vulnerable to abandonment if market prices fall substantially.

While LTAs are unlikely to penetrate into the entire end market, the customers most inclined to enter these agreements - hyperscalers and AI infrastructure providers - are also among the industry's higher-quality counterparties. With strong balance sheets, diversified revenue streams, and strategic incentives to secure long-term supply, these customers may be better positioned to honor their commitments. Micron taking equity stakes of Anthropic is however an example of vendor financing & risk amplification. In addition, unlike prior semi-cycles that were inflated by inventory hoarding or double-ordering, today's demand is more supported by genuine infrastructure deployment for AI.

Although financial guarantees appear modest relative to the massive multi-year revenue streams they are designed to protect, these New Memory LTAs become increasingly protected over time as the guarantees are back-end weighted. As remaining purchase obligations decline, the financial cost of walking away increases relative to contract value. Hence, downside protection can improve materially in the later years of the agreements (when it is needed most!).

On industry fundamentals, we remain constructive on near-to-medium-term memory supply-demand dynamics. We do not expect Chinese entrants to materially disrupt the market over the next two years, with industry supply growth driven primarily by capex decisions at mature incumbents. We also expect HBM pricing to move higher as conventional memory becomes increasingly profitable. Where views diverge is on NAND: Asia team is more cautious, arguing that AI demand disproportionately benefits DRAM and that Chinese competition poses a greater long-term risk in NAND. U.S. team believes AI workloads are becoming increasingly NAND-intensive through larger context windows and growing storage requirements, creating a favorable supply-demand backdrop amid constrained supply.

We highlight a clear divergence in how memory suppliers are approaching LTAs. Sandisk and Micron have been the most proactive in pursuing agreements backed by financial guarantees, while Asia based suppliers have taken a more selective approach. Looking ahead, both teams view further LTA disclosures, memory ASP, and Sandisk's upcoming Analyst Day as key catalysts that could shape investor perception of both LTAs and memory sector valuations.

## PART I: BULL VS BEAR

## THE BEAR THESIS: LTA LIMITATIONS AND CONSTRAINTS

LTAs protect future revenue only when the financial penalty for breaking them exceeds the savings a customer could obtain by purchasing memory elsewhere. Customers will generally honor these agreements when the forfeited guarantee or other penalties make walking away uneconomic. However, if spot prices fall far enough below contract prices, the savings may outweigh the penalty, making abandonment financially rational. The strength of an LTA therefore depends not simply on the existence of a contract, but on whether its economic terms remain sufficiently binding under changing market conditions. The true protective value of LTAs is difficult to quantify. The relevant variables are all changing over time: market prices, contract floor prices, remaining purchase obligations, and the balance of any guarantees. Because investors do not know how these variables evolve throughout the life of each agreement, calculating the exact amount of downside protection becomes more of a conceptual exercise than a precise analytical one.

Not every customer is a natural candidate for a long-term agreement. Hyperscalers and HBM customers have strong incentives to secure supply years in advance, while consumer electronics companies, transactional buyers, and some Chinese customers often prioritize flexibility and lowest-cost sourcing. Even under optimistic adoption assumptions, a meaningful portion of memory demand is therefore likely to remain outside LTAs and exposed to the traditional forces behind the industry's booms and busts.

The amount of protection disclosed so far appears small relative to what it is supposed to protect. Memory suppliers are discussing agreements that span several years and support enormous future revenue streams. Yet the guarantees announced to date represent only a small fraction of that revenue base. The implication is not that LTAs are worthless, but that investors may be overstating how much protection current guarantee levels actually provide. Protecting peak-cycle earnings would require customer commitments on a completely different scale. If the goal is to preserve today's profitability through a severe downturn, the amount of capital that would need to be posted upfront is enormous. Once the numbers are viewed in relation to the industry's multi-year revenue opportunity, the required guarantees start to look measured in hundreds of billions—or potentially even more.

LTAs can soften the cycle, but they cannot replace favorable industry fundamentals. The biggest benefit may simply be reducing the severity of future downturns. Margins that historically might have become deeply negative could instead remain around breakeven. But sustaining elevated earnings for years still requires the same thing the memory industry has always needed: a durable imbalance between supply and demand. LTAs can help at the margins, but they cannot create that imbalance on their own.

## THE BULL THESIS: THE NEW MEMORY LTA'S BENEFITS AND PROTECTIONS

Today's memory LTAs are fundamentally different from many of the failed contract structures investors point to. Past examples often lacked meaningful financial guarantee. In some cases customers could walk away with little consequence; in others, suppliers technically had contractual protection but had to spend years in court trying to recover losses. Those experiences understandably made investors skeptical, but the current agreements are structured quite differently.

The most important difference is the presence of upfront financial commitments. Customers are not simply promising future purchases. They are posting deposits and guarantees that have immediate economic value. That creates a tangible cost to breaking the agreement and gives suppliers protection that many earlier semiconductor contracts never had.

The structure of the guarantees becomes stronger as time passes. A key feature of these agreements is that financial commitments are back-end weighted. As the remaining purchase obligation declines, the relative protection provided by the guarantee increases. By the later years of a contract, a customer may be giving up a significant amount of value to walk away from a much smaller remaining commitment.

The quality of the counterparties is another important distinction. Many of these contracts are being signed with hyperscalers and other large technology companies that have exceptionally strong balance sheets. Unlike some historical examples where customers were financially stressed, these counterparties are viewed as highly creditworthy and accountable. That makes the contracts more credible from the outset, and even if there is any shortfall beyond posted collateral, it is realistically collectible.

The demand backdrop also looks fundamentally different from prior industry examples. In several past cases, long-term agreements unraveled after demand turned out to be artificially inflated by inventory hoarding, double-ordering, or pandemic-era supply chain behavior. However, the current memory demand is being driven by genuine consumption rather than temporary distortions. Moreover, the demand is tied to AI infrastructure and rapidly growing training/inferencing requirements, which means memory has exponential scaling potentials.

Even under very harsh assumptions, LTAs appear capable of providing meaningful downside protection. Our stress tests assume severe memory price declines and, importantly, allow customers to walk away whenever doing so makes economic sense. Despite those assumptions, earnings remain materially better than they would in a world without LTAs. The contracts do not eliminate downside, but they do appear to reduce it significantly, especially in the outer years.

The analysis may actually understate the real-world effectiveness of these agreements. The models generally assume customers behave with perfect economic rationality and ignore most non-financial considerations. In reality, a customer may think about litigation risk, reputational damage, future access to supply, and the importance of maintaining strategic supplier relationships. Those factors could meaningfully raise the threshold for walking away.

None of this means memory cyclicality disappears, but it will be meaningfully dampened. Prices can still fall, earnings can still decline, and contracts can still be broken under the right circumstances. The claim is not that LTAs permanently fix the memory industry. Rather, they change the magnitude of the cycle by making it harder for revenue and earnings to collapse in the way they have historically.

The most balanced conclusion is that LTAs raise the floor and offer robust protection during down cycles. They should not be viewed as a mechanism for locking in peak earnings forever. Instead, their value lies in reducing volatility, increasing visibility, and providing a meaningful buffer against future downturns. The memory business remains cyclical, but potentially less cyclical than it has been in the past.

## EXHIBIT 8: Key Messages

## Key Messages

\- Historically, long-term agreements in semiconductors and memory functioned primarily as a buyer's tool. Customers used LTAs to secure guaranteed volume, while sellers had little structural leverage of their own.

\- Both structures of historical LTA contracts offered sellers little real downside protection, since neither carried an upfront financial commitment to compensate the supplier if a buyer walked away.

\- Two case studies illustrate exactly how previous LTA structures failed in practice: 1. Microchip / Analog Semiconductors; and 2. Hemlock / Polysilicon. Hemlock case bears more resemblance to what we see in the New Memory LTAs.

\- We identify three structural differences between legacy semiconductor LTAs and the New Memory LTAs.

1. Upfront financial commitment: back-end-weighted cash deposits, letters of credit, or third-party guarantees)

2. Better counterparty quality: financially sound, diversified buyers replace the thinly capitalized or diffuse customer bases that undermined Hemlock and Microchip

3. Structural demand: sustained AI-driven buildout replaces the temporary pull-in and double-ordering that ultimately undermined Microchip(sustained AI-driven buildout replaces the temporary pull-in and double-ordering that ultimately undermined Microchip)

\- The New Memory LTAs provide meaningful but not infinite downside protection. We find that these new memory LTAs are structured in a way which provides significantly higher disincentives to customers to walk away - particularly in the latter part of the contract term - than the cases we previously analyzed in our first note.

# Asia's El Niño playbook: Eight lessons from 25 years of shocks

Why oil prices, irrigation and policy can sometimes matter more than rainfall.


Sonal Varma - NSL


\- El Niño has officially formed, with the potential to morph into a “super El Niño” later this year. Looking at past severe El Niño episodes, we find that there is a significant impact on agricultural output (-2.4pp) but a surprisingly limited impact on food inflation.


\- We believe the relationship between El Niño and food prices is mixed because of three factors: 1) the role of oil prices; 2) an improvement in structural resilience (expanded irrigation, better quality seeds); and 3) aggressive/proactive government policies.

## Introduction

El Niño has officially formed, and is projected to be a strong-to-very-strong episode, with the potential to morph into a "super El Niño" later this year. For most Asian economies, El Niño results in below-normal rainfall and heatwaves. This can hurt food production and trigger food price inflation. Will this time be different?

The southern oscillation index (SOI) fell to -24.9 in June, well below the -7 threshold that indicates El Niño conditions and among the lowest readings in over 20 years (Figure 2). The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) predicts weather conditions will transition from moderate to strong-to-very-strong El Niño (i.e., a “super El Niño”) starting in August, with a peak likely only later this year (Figure 3). Although a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) could still emerge as a potential offset, its timing and strength is uncertain, and El Niño conditions are likely to prevail.

## Impact so far in Asia

We are still in the early months, but some initial effects of El Niño conditions are visible. In India, monsoon season rainfall so far (1 June until 8 July) was 15% below normal, and crop sowing data show summer crop plantations are 20.8% below year-ago levels, led by rice (-13.1%) and oilseeds (-39.3%; Figure 4). Malaysia's Economy Minister said rainfall could be 40-60% lower in several states, and that yields in key commodity sectors (such as palm oil) could fall by 8-10% during an El Niño event (source: Malay Mail). The impact is also seen in food prices so far in 2026, with increases in the prices of rice (Thai 5% broken: 16.5%), wheat (US HRW: 17.8%), soybean oil (58%) and palm oil (12.8%), while maize and sugar price changes have been more muted (Figure 5).

## Past El Niño cycles

Over the last 25 years, Asia has experienced seven El Niño episodes, including years of mild, moderate and strong El Niño conditions:

• Mild: 2002–03, 2004–05, 2006–07, 2018-19

• Moderate: 2009-10

• Severe (Strong to very strong): 2015-16, 2023-24

Figure 6 summarizes the impact of El Niño conditions on inflation (food and headline) and growth (agriculture and overall), looking at past mild/moderate and severe El Niño episodes. The detailed country-wise impact for each El Niño episode is given in the Appendix (Figure 14).

During the past mild to moderate El Niño years, we find that the impact on agricultural output has been somewhat negative (-0.2pp), and food inflation picked up by 0.6pp, on average. During the severe El Niño years, there has been a more significant impact on agricultural output (-2.4pp), although food inflation surprisingly declined, a counterintuitive result explained by three key factors.

## Why the relationship between El Niño and food prices is mixed

The linkage from El Niño to rainfall, from rainfall to food output and from food output to food price inflation is not linear (Figure 7), with several other factors equally important in determining the ultimate impact.

First, oil prices matter since they also drive fertilizer and transportation costs. During El Niño years, food inflation has been relatively contained when oil prices are moderate, as was the case during the last two severe episodes: 2015-16 and 2023-24.

Second, the sensitivity of agricultural output to rainfall has moderated over time, due to increased irrigation (use of reservoir water), the use of better-quality drought-resistant seeds and a rising share of non-crop activities. In India, for instance, the share of crops in agricultural gross value output fell to 54.1% in 2023-24 (from 62.4% in 2011-12), while the share of non-crop sectors (livestock, forestry, fishing) now accounts for the remaining 46% of agricultural output.

Third, governments are more proactive with trade and supply-side policies since food drives the cost of living. Most EM Asian countries maintain strategic reserves of staple crops, and these buffer stocks are released to manage price fluctuations. Additionally, crackdowns on hoarding, imports, export controls and price regulation, among others, are actively deployed to keep food price inflation in check.

## Eight lessons from 25 years of shocks

With history as a guide, and keeping current starting conditions in mind, we see eight key implications for the region this time around.

1. 2026 food inflation contained by buffers: Despite strengthening El Niño conditions, food price inflation in Asia may stay relatively contained this year. Input costs – crude oil and urea prices – have moderated from their April peaks. Buffer stocks across key agricultural products are elevated, with stocks-to-use ratios for rice at 44.8%, wheat at 51.6% and palm oil at 11.9% (Figure 8). India’s stock of rice and wheat are 3 times and 2 times buffer norms, respectively, which can be used to smooth price fluctuations (Figures 9 and 10). Water levels across Thailand’s major reservoirs are starting off at much higher levels than during the previous two severe El Niño episodes (Figure 11).

2. 2027 inflation risks rise as buffers deplete: However, the 2026 stability comes at a cost. Once existing buffer stocks are depleted to stabilize prices this year, the vulnerability will be higher next season. Drought-like conditions would make refilling of water reservoirs difficult, weighing on production of irrigation-dependent crops, such as wheat sowing in India (October-December) and second rice plantings in Thailand (January-April). In the case of palm oil, which has a higher water intensity for irrigation, the impact on prices shows up with a lag because of the biological cycle, as water stress has an immediate impact on flower development, but the resulting decline in fresh fruit bunch (FFB) yields won't materialize until the following year (i.e., with an 8+ month lag). The likelihood of governments imposing export restrictions later this year is also much higher, in our view, which would lower global food supply.

3. Rice, palm oil, sugar and pulses face the highest price risk: The crops most at risk of higher prices next year include rice, palm oil and sugar. Rice is a staple in Asia and a rainfed crop, and only 11.3% of global production traded in 2025/26, so domestic shortfalls can have regional spillovers (see Asia: What if rice prices rise sharply?, 30 August 2022). Risks to palm oil production emanate from the lagged biological response discussed earlier, with lower output likely in Malaysia and Indonesia. Pulses (lentil) prices are also likely to rise in India amid lower buffer stocks and as it emerges from a year-long period of deflation (see India: El Niño El Nino may not scorch the economy, 19 May 2026). Heatwaves might also raise vegetable prices due to spoilage.

5. Stagflation threat (inflation up, rural demand down): For advanced Asian economies (Singapore, Korea and Taiwan), the growth impact is insignificant, and food has a smaller share in their CPI baskets, although all three economies are net importers of cereals and meat, and so the costs of these imports could rise. For EM Asian economies (especially the Philippines, India, Indonesia and Thailand), El Niño poses a dual threat: a supply-side inflation shock (widely discussed) and a demand-side growth shock (widely underappreciated). The growth channel operates through two mechanisms. The agriculture sector still accounts for 42% of total employment in India, 29% in Thailand, 27% in Indonesia, and 20% in the Philippines, so weaker agriculture output translates into weaker farm incomes, and hurts rural consumption demand. If food price inflation picks up next year, lower real disposable incomes could weaken consumer demand from low- and middle-income households.

6. Fiscal policy and supply measures as a first defense: Fiscal and supply-side policies are likely to be Asia's first line of defense. Governments in India and Southeast Asia have already begun to take action (see Appendix, Figure 15). Their focus is on drought mitigation, cloud seeding, promotion of less water-intensive crops, expanding crop insurance, establishing seed reserves, promotion of solar pumps etc. If necessary, fiscal support via increased subsidies or income support to farmers could be announced. Food price controls could also be implemented.

7. Trade protectionism is a major risk in coming months: In the past, Asia has often resorted to the imposition of export restrictions or outright export bans to prevent domestic food prices from rising. While this helps contain local prices, it has ripple effects, as prices surge for food importers in the region, amplifying the food price shock (see Special Report: Gauging who is most exposed if food prices surge, 15 May 2026). India has been at the forefront of these restrictions, previously imposed on rice, wheat and sugar. Vietnam banned rice exports in 2008, and Indonesia temporarily banned palm oil exports in 2022 to ease domestic shortages. There are early signs of similar policies being imposed: in mid-May, India banned exports of sugar until 30 September 2026 to protect domestic supplies. Watch for potential rice export restrictions from India (if monsoon shortfalls persist), and palm oil export curbs or duties from Indonesia/Malaysia, if yields decline.

8. A dilemma for central banks: The monetary policy response may not necessarily be hawkish. Food prices (along with fuel prices) are an important driver of inflation expectations. However, for EM Asian economies that are more vulnerable, growth could weaken below the baseline and weak domestic demand should limit second-round effects on core inflation, similar to 2019 (Figure 13). This is particularly the case for Thailand, and to some extent, even India. However, in the Philippines, the BSP is an orthodox inflation targeting central bank and will likely respond to any uptick in headline inflation with policy tightening.

For most of Asia, the El Niño playbook in 2026-27 will prioritize fiscal buffers and trade policy over monetary tightening, but by 2027, as those buffers run dry, the policy calculus may shift.

# Outlooks 2026-27

## Australia: Below trend


Andrew Ticehurst - NAL

We expect sub-trend growth to continue, with the economy weighed down by higher rates and housing-related budget changes.

Forecast revisions: We have trimmed our CPI forecasts due to lower oil prices and our 2027 GDP growth forecast (by 0.1pp) due to a softer housing market outlook.

Activity: The economy recorded modest GDP growth of 0.3% q-o-q in Q1, and recent data suggest modest growth continued in Q2. In terms of the broader outlook, the decline in fuel prices and some (net) easing of tensions in the Middle East are positives for sentiment. However, concern has shifted to housing. Following three interest rate hikes and tax changes for housing investors – announced in the May budget and just passed in the parliament – secondary housing turnover has slowed and dwelling prices are now falling. While we do not expect a housing “crash”, noting that rates are not especially high, affordability improves as prices decline and population growth remains firm (despite some migration-related concerns in some quarters), we have trimmed our dwelling investment forecasts this month and continue to anticipate a modest rise in unemployment.

Inflation: April and May CPI inflation printed below consensus and our expectations. Details of the May data revealed some pass-through from higher fuel prices, but with the overall result dragged lower by sharp declines in volatile fuel and domestic travel costs. With fuel prices now sharply lower and growth moderating, we expect this inflation pass-through to also pass-through, in time. Moreover, with these data, our Q2 CPI tracking has fallen notably; we currently forecast 0.8% q-o-q and 0.9% q-o-q increases in headline and trimmed mean CPI, respectively (was 1.2% and 1.0% q-o-q). We continue to anticipate a slow return of inflation towards target, with an increase in minimum and award wages and the fading of the fuel excise tax discount impeding faster progress.

Monetary policy: The RBA left rates unchanged in June in a unanimous decision, as we expected, following three consecutive 25bp hikes. Communication, including the subsequent meeting minutes, tilted somewhat hawkish, indicating a preparedness to hike again if necessary, and that the cash rate (at 4.35%) was less restrictive than in 2024 (when it reached this level), as the neutral rate had likely shifted higher. With policy now mildly restrictive, the economy cooling, fuel prices lower and upside risk to inflation expectations diminished, we remain of the view that the cash rate has most likely peaked. We do not expect the RBA to indicate this for many months, however, as it would likely prefer to retain policy flexibility amid concerns over financial conditions easing prematurely. As inflation creeps towards target and unemployment drifts higher, we pencil in a rate cut in May 2027.

Risks: Resolution of underlying Middle East tensions remains an uncertainty. A sharper-than-forecast housing downturn and the El Niño event are other risks we are monitoring.

# China: A K-shaped economy is materializing

Domestic demand is exceptionally weak; Beijing will likely step up fiscal spending in coming months.

Forecast change : We raised our 2026 CPI and PPI inflation forecasts in early June to 0.9% and 2.5%, respectively, from 0.6% and 1.0%.

Activity: Growth across major activity data slowed materially in April and May, confirming our view that the growth rebound in Q1 was short-lived, and we expect no major rebound in June. Growth of retail sales and fixed asset investment have turned negative again, as aggregate domestic demand is being weighed on by payback effects from the trade-in program, the prolonged property slump and a worsening of the double K-shapes. We have lowered our 2026 retail sales growth forecast to 2.2% from 3.2%, in view of its sharp slowdown in recent months and the limited room for short-term policy stimulus. The export surge was primarily price-driven and largely concentrated in AI-related sectors that simultaneously drove higher imports, with a limited net contribution to real economic growth. We maintain our forecast for real GDP growth to slow to 4.1% y-o-y in Q2 from 5.0% in Q1. With the renewed sharp declines in investment against Beijing's commitment to stabilize investment, we believe Beijing will likely accelerate bond issuance and step up fiscal spending in coming months.

Inflation: We revised up our 2026 CPI and PPI inflation forecasts in early June to $0.9\%$ and $2.5\%$ , respectively, from $0.6\%$ and $1.0\%$ , in view of the imported inflationary forces from global oil and chip prices. Although Beijing might feel a sense of relief from the end of deflation, it may not take much comfort, as China is a net importer of chips and the world's largest net importer of energy. The worsening terms of trade could shrink its balance of payments at a macro level and squeeze most domestic producers and consumers.

Policy: We believe Beijing will maintain proactive fiscal spending and accommodative monetary policy to address exceptionally weak domestic demand. While Beijing expanded the quota allocation for "new policy financing tools (NPFT)", and pledged a greater portion of LGSBs toward investment in March, actual government bond issuance and fiscal spending has been slow. We expect Beijing to step up fiscal spending over coming months. Considering ample market liquidity and low CGB yields, we maintain our forecast for no RRR or rate cuts this year. That said, if global oil prices continue to retreat and ease pressures on other major central banks to raise rates, the PBoC may become more likely to deliver a moderate rate cut towards the end of the year.

Risks: The evolution of Middle East tensions and US-China relations, the pace of domestic policy stimulus, private sector tech breakthroughs and the evolvement of the property and stock market all pose two-way risks.

# Hong Kong: Trade is benefiting notably from the global AI supercycle

The ongoing global AI supercycle is driving growth of both exports and imports to exceptionally strong levels.

Forecast changes : We lower our 2026 CPI inflation forecast to 1.7% from 1.9%.

Activity: Domestic demand remains resilient. Retail sales growth moderated to 7.9% y-o-y in May from 8.7% in April, while the pace remained robust. On a comparable basis, May's retail sales reached 83.4% of 2018 levels. The labour market continued to stabilise, with the unemployment rate holding steady at 3.7% in May, unchanged from April. Goods exports maintained exceptional strength, growing by 40.8% y-o-y in May (versus 42.9% in April), likely buoyed by sustained global demand for AI-related electronics, as import growth also stayed elevated at 42.0% (April: 44.4%). Re-export growth also stood at 41.0% y-o-y in May, little changed from 43.0% in April, and we expect this strong growth to continue in H2. We maintain our bullish 2026 GDP growth forecast of 4.8%, although we anticipate a moderation to 5.2% y-o-y in Q2 from 5.9% in Q1. Also, Hong Kong's residential property market showed further signs of recovery, with the Centa City Leading Index (CCLI) rising by 1.8% in June. We expect this rebound to persist through 2026, driven by rising home prices and increased residential land transactions. The outlook on private investment may depend on the Fed's policy rate path.

Inflation: CPI inflation came in at 2.0% y-o-y in May, up from 1.7% in April. Notable price increases were recorded in utilities (6.6%, likely caused by the ongoing global energy shock), transport (5.1%), miscellaneous services (5.1%), miscellaneous goods (2.9%), and alcoholic drinks and tobacco (2.5%), and housing (1.1%). Given the recent moderation in crude oil prices, we lower our 2026 CPI inflation forecast to 1.7% from 1.9%.

Policy: Our US economics team still expects the Fed to hold rates steady indefinitely. We believe this is marginally negative for Hong Kong, as a higher interest rate environment – which is tied to Fed policy moves – may weigh on local private investment, which has made a solid contribution to GDP growth since H2 2025.

Risks: Risks to our forecasts are balanced. Downside risks include heightened geopolitical and trade tensions under the Trump administration, the strength of the ongoing housing market recovery and a sudden deterioration in external demand. Upside risks include more rate cuts by the Fed, stronger external demand, a major de-escalation of geopolitical tensions and a stronger recovery in Mainland tourist arrivals.

# India: Improvement in macro conditions

Lower oil prices reduce pressures on growth, inflation and the twin deficits.

Forecast change: For FY27, we lower our CA deficit forecast by 0.7pp to 1.2% of GDP, our fiscal deficit forecast by 0.1pp to 4.5% of GDP and inflation forecast by 0.4pp to 4.6%.

Activity: Consumption remains robust, evidenced by strong auto sales (passenger vehicles, two-wheelers, tractors and MHCVs), diesel sales and GST collections in June. Industrial production data show that the underperformance has been limited to sectors like fuels, non-metallic minerals, apparels and chemicals, while merchandise export and import growth have been strong in May. Overall, we expect the growth momentum to sustain, exhibiting resilience to the Iran war shocks driven by robust consumption and the capex focus of the government. The recent fall in crude oil prices and a restoration of supply chains also bodes well for momentum, with the government reversing energy curbs. A growth-supportive policy stance, the calming of trade tensions with the US and a focus on structural reforms should help. We forecast FY27 (year ending March 2027) GDP growth of 6.6% y-o-y, down from 7.6% in FY26 and in line with RBI forecasts.

Inflation: May CPI inflation rose to 3.9% y-o-y in May from 3.5% in April, but underlying inflation remained weak at \~2.3%. We expect near-term inflation to be impacted by the pending pass-through of higher retail fuel prices and input costs, and by volatile vegetable prices that typically spike during the monsoon months. While below-normal rains are an upside risk, the impact on inflation has been historically weak. The correction in crude oil prices is unlikely to lead to a reversal of the fuel price hikes, but lower industrial energy costs will ease margin pressure on firms. With unfavourable base effects intensifying in H2, inflation could cross the 5% mark by end-2026. We reduce our FY27 inflation forecast by 40bp to 4.6% (FY26: 2.1%), below the RBI's forecast of 5.1%.

Policy: The RBI continued its policy pause at 5.25%, despite higher inflation and lower growth forecast revisions, setting a high bar for any policy tightening. To defend the currency, policymakers have reduced taxes on FPIs, lowered market entry barriers, and subsidised hedging for the public sector to raise external commercial borrowings and for banks to attract offshore "FCNR(B)" deposits. We expect the latter to garner \~USD55bn of inflows, effectively swinging the BoP to a surplus of \~USD15bn in FY27 from the deficit of \~USD70bn we had forecast previously. Fiscal pressures eased in May due to lower spending and higher revenue growth, and the sharp decline in oil and fertiliser prices and stronger growth are tailwinds. We continue to expect slippage from the FY27 fiscal deficit target of 4.3% of GDP, but now forecast a less slippage (0.2pp versus 0.3pp).

Risks: US-Iran engagement and its impact on crude oil prices and supply chains are key uncertainties. A deterioration could reverse growth tailwinds, while sustained low oil prices are an upside risk. Apart from this, downside risks to growth include the impact of El Niño and deficient Southwest Monsoons, and a potential easing of public capex to limit fiscal slippage. Other upside risks include stronger consumption growth, sustained export demand and the lagged impact of structural reforms on the ease of doing business.

## What is driving non-tech export growth?

We see three key drivers behind the recovery in non-tech exports:

1. AI-related investment spillovers: We believe the recovery in machinery exports is driven by spillover effects from the ongoing AI-related capex upcycle. Investment in AI infrastructure, data centres, electrification and industrial automation has generated demand well beyond semiconductor manufacturing. Building AI infrastructure requires substantial investment in electrical equipment, precision instruments, industrial machinery and more. As a result, beneficiaries of the current capex cycle are no longer limited to chip producers. Unsurprisingly, the improvement in capital goods exports, in particular electrical machinery and optical & precision instruments, has been most evident in Malaysia, Taiwan, Singapore and China, reflecting their dominant positions in precision engineering and their ability to capitalize on spillover effects from AI demand.

2. Commodity price effects: Supply disruptions caused by the US-Iran war had led to higher energy and petrochemical prices, lifting the export values of mineral products & fuel (across Asia), chemicals (China, Malaysia, Singapore, South Korea and Thailand), and plastics & rubber (China, Malaysia, Thailand, South Korea and Taiwan), while heightened geopolitical uncertainty has boosted precious metal prices. These gains have been driven primarily by favourable price effects rather than a sustained improvement in underlying demand.

3. Energy security boosts EV exports: The Iran-US war has refocused the policy push towards energy security and greater EV penetration. The benefit of this primarily accrues to China's expanding vehicle exports. ## Will the recovery in non-tech exports sustain?

We believe the outlook for non-tech export growth is mixed.

## The positives

Our NOM Export Leading Index (NELI) continues to signal that Asia's export upcycle should extend into Q3 (Figure 33). Importantly, the recent improvement in the index has become increasingly broad-based, supported not only by tech-related indicators but also by improving manufacturing sentiment and firmer demand from China. This suggests that the recovery is gradually spreading across the wider manufacturing complex.

We remain most positive on capital goods, particularly electrical machinery and precision instruments, where the ongoing AI-driven capex upcycle should continue to generate spillover demand, providing a more durable source of export growth.

Inventory dynamics reinforce this view. Within non-tech manufacturing, electrical equipment has experienced the most notable improvement. Its I-S ratio has fallen to 1.05 in April from an average of 1.14 in 2025, as shipments strengthened while inventories were steadily reduced (Figure 34). Interestingly, production growth in electrical equipment has turned negative (Figure 35), despite stronger exports, which suggests firms are currently fulfilling demand through inventory drawdowns rather than increased production. If export demand remains firm, inventories will eventually become sufficiently lean to necessitate a recovery in production. Basic metals present a similarly constructive picture, with its I-S ratio easing to 1.11 from 1.19, driven by a combination of firmer shipments and declining inventories, pointing to a gradual recovery in industrial activity.

## The negatives

By contrast, the drivers are less supportive for chemicals and autos. In the chemicals sector, inventories have fallen primarily because producers have curtailed output rather than a material strengthening of shipments, which suggests underlying demand remains relatively subdued. Combined with our view that recent export gains have been supported by higher energy and commodity prices owing to the US-Iran war, the recent pickup in chemicals exports appears less likely to prove durable.

Similarly, although regional auto exports have improved, the recovery remains highly concentrated in China. Elsewhere in Asia, inventory-to-shipment ratios for autos continue to rise as inventories accumulate against sluggish shipments, consistent with softer

external demand and growing competitive pressure from Chinese automakers. Unless demand strengthens more broadly or competitive pressures ease, the regional auto export recovery is likely to remain uneven.

Finally, export growth in other sectors that have benefited from temporary geopolitical price effects – including mineral & fuel products, plastics & rubber, and precious metals – are likely to moderate as these price effects unwind, since underlying demand is still soft.

Overall, non-tech export growth has picked up, but its drivers and the country-level dynamics reveal an uneven recovery.

## The US-Iran peace deal: A turning point for Asia?

Following more than three months of conflict in the Middle East and the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, the US and Iran have finally agreed upon an interim peace deal, which is expected to be formalized on 19 June in Switzerland. Brent crude oil prices have fallen sharply in response; they are currently less than USD83/bbl and 25% below month-ago levels. These prices are well below our baseline assumptions. We currently assume Brent crude will average USD93.9/bbl in 2026, but if oil prices settle closer to USD80/bbl, the full-year average would fall to USD85.4/bbl and oil prices in H2 2026 would be \~15% below our current assumptions.

Moreover, the benefits from reopening the Strait of Hormuz extends beyond oil. Other commodity prices – natural gas, urea – are also repricing lower (Figure 36). The peace deal is still fragile, but if it proves durable and commodity prices stay benign, this would positively impact Asia’s economic outlook, as the region is a large net energy importer and has been the most adversely impacted from the Middle East energy shock.

## How the Hormuz closure has impacted Asia so far

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has proven less damaging to Asia's growth than initially feared, thanks to a strong AI-driven growth boost and sufficient availability of oil supplies (inventory drawdown and sourcing from alternate routes). However, the Iran war's true toll has manifested through higher inflation, currency depreciation and fiscal strain on many economies. Central banks accordingly pivoted in a hawkish direction.

## Supply-chain normalization: A gradual process

Even with the peace deal announced, the unwinding of supply-chain disruptions caused by the Strait of Hormuz closure is likely to be a more gradual process. The deal itself remains fragile, due to deep mutual distrust. There are logistical challenges (clearing the mines, clearing the stranded ships), insurance costs remain high and shipping confidence will only return gradually as vessels test the waters. Oil wells shuttered must be restarted, and as oil flows resume, a surge in restocking demand is inevitable. The entire energy supply chain – oil and refined products (jet fuel, diesel, gasoline), petrochemicals (polymers, plastics, naphtha), fertilizers, industrial metals and minerals (aluminum, sulphur, helium) all face a multi-month normalisation timeline.

## A positive economic impact on Asia

Nevertheless, the US-Iran peace deal offers a welcome reprieve for Asia, with the potential for stronger growth, lower inflation and reduced twin current account and fiscal pressures (Figure 37).

## Lower imports

A lower import bill is likely to be the most immediate impact. On average, every 10% fall in the price of oil improves Asia's current account (CA) balance by \~0.3% of GDP. The top three economies that would experience the most visible improvement include Thailand (every 10% oil price fall improves its CA by 0.5pp), India (0.4pp) and South Korea (0.3pp).

## Easing inflation, but gradually

The easing of inflationary pressures will likely be more gradual, given the role of fiscal policy in capping the upside in energy prices, manufacturing margins still under pressure and lags in supply chain normalisation. Every 10% decline in oil prices, on average, lowers Asia's CPI inflation by 0.2-0.3pp, but as pipeline price pressures continued to rise in May, CPI inflation benefits will likely take 2-3 months to materialise. We expect the impact of lower oil prices to be most visible in the Philippines, which has market-determined fuel prices. Lower oil and fertilizer prices can also help keep the lid on food inflation, although with 2026 an El Niño year, medium-term risks to food inflation remain.

## Growth and fiscal benefits

We also expect some benefits to growth and fiscal relief for governments, but this impact should be more marginal in comparison with the current account and inflation benefits. On fiscal balances, we maintain our forecasts for slightly wider deficits in India (to 4.6% of GDP in FY27 versus the 4.3% budget target) and Indonesia (to 3% of GDP in 2026, with increased below-the-line spending).

## Differentiation within Asia is still critical

While lower oil prices provide a broad-based tailwind across Asia, country-specific differentiation will still be important. We remain more cautious on Indonesia and Thailand. Indonesia faces persistent policy uncertainty, concerns over BI independence, disruptions from the state commodity export agency on goods exports and credit rating concerns, while Thailand's structural constraints (ageing population, productivity challenges, China shock) remain intact. ## Asian central banks: Less hawkish at the margin, but divergence persists

The peace deal and the resulting oil price decline have marginally reduced hawkish pressures across Asian central banks, but large divergences across policy paths remain, reflecting heterogeneous domestic growth-inflation dynamics (Figure 40).

\- HOLD (BI, RBI, RBA, BOT, PBoC): The peace deal strengths our view that BI will leave policy rates unchanged this week and beyond, although this is contingent on a stabilisation of IDR. We maintain our forecast for the RBI to stay on hold this year, even as headline inflation inches up, due to low underlying inflation. The RBA appears done with its tightening cycle, and we maintain our view that it may be able to deliver a rate cut around May 2027, as core inflation moves towards target. Amid low inflation and weak domestic demand, we also expect BOT and PBoC to leave rates unchanged this year.

\- DELAYED HIKE (CBC): Easing inflationary pressures are likely to reinforce the CBC's dovish stance, so we push out the timing we assign to the first 12.5bp hike to December from our previous forecast of June. We now expect 12.5bp rate hikes in December and March next year (terminal rate: 2.25%).

\- HIKE (BSP, BNM, MAS, BOK, RBNZ): Despite lower oil prices, we expect rate hikes / policy tightening in a few economies. For BSP, we expect 75bp of cumulative rate hikes (including a 25bp hike this week), as core inflation remains elevated. We also maintain our forecast for 75bp of cumulative hikes by the BOK (25bp in each of July, October and January 2027), as the BOK's May meeting emphasized positive spillovers from the semiconductor upcycle to the broader economy, growing demand-driven inflation pressures and the need to narrow the interest rate differential with the US. In New Zealand, we continue to forecast three 25bp rate hikes, commencing in September, to bring rates closer to neutral, but we see a risk that tightening could commence at the coming meeting on 8 July.

## Overall: Net positive for Asia, but differentiation still key

Overall, the US-Iran peace deal delivers a net positive shock to Asia, amplifying the region's already-strong momentum from the AI-driven export boom, while alleviating the energy-induced headwinds. Lower energy costs will ease inflationary pressures, relieve profit margin compression for manufacturers, narrow current account deficits for energy importers and provide fiscal breathing room for governments. The combination of AI-driven demand strength and energy cost relief should position Asia for better performance. However, differentiation within Asia remains paramount, as the benefits are not uniformly distributed. Critically, this is not the end of rate hikes for all central banks, and an important variable to monitor, going forward, will be Fed policy and its spillovers.

In the sections that follow, our Asia country economists provide more detailed estimates of the impact of lower oil and energy prices on their economies, and our strategy team assesses the implications for FX and rates markets.

## Country-specific comments

## Australia (Andrew Ticehurst)

Australia is a modest net energy exporter, so a lower oil price might be seen as a headwind. However, lower oil prices should also be helpful for trading partner growth and will also boost battered consumer and business sentiment. We therefore see modestly positive net implications for growth (stronger). We also anticipate modestly positive implications for inflation (weaker). We think the combination of slightly stronger growth and lower inflation should be a “wash” for the RBA, and our base case remains one in which the tightening cycle has most likely ended. We also maintain our forecast for the RBA to deliver a rate cut around May 2027, as core inflation moves slowly towards target.

## China (Ting Lu, Jing Wang, Harrington Zhang and Hannah Liu)

A lower global oil price is positive for the economy, as China is the world's largest crude oil importer, with its oil import dependence elevated at around 73%. The reopening of the SoH should partially alleviate supply chain disruptions that have affected oil and chemical industries. However, we maintain our below-consensus full-year growth forecast of 4.5%, due to a limited boost from the price-driven export boom, substantial payback effects from the durable goods trade-in program, the continued property drag and a lack of forceful policy support. On the trade balance, while lower oil prices should theoretically reduce import values and widen the current account surplus, this effect may be partially offset by increased import volumes, as China replenishes its strategic petroleum reserves.

Following the release of May inflation data, we revised up our 2026 CPI and PPI inflation forecasts to 2.5% and 0.9%, respectively, from 1.0% and 0.6%. We maintain our revisions, as the oil price surge has already been transmitted through to domestic prices and the boost from the AI supercycle remains intact. We expect GDP deflator to turn positive for the year, supported by import-led reflation.

On monetary policy, we maintain our baseline forecast for no RRR or rate cuts this year. The combination of potentially softer import-led inflation and exceptionally weak credit growth could strengthen the case for the PBoC to maintain an accommodative monetary policy stance to stimulate domestic demand. However, an ample interbank liquidity environment and falling CGB yields suggest limited scope for high-profile easing measures.

## India (Sonal Varma, Aurodeep Nandi)

Since the Iran war, India has been diversifying some of its oil imports away from the Middle East, with a pivot towards Russia (\~39% of total shipments) and other non-traditional suppliers like Brazil and Venezuela (source: Kpler). India has also ramped up LPG and LNG imports, particularly from the US, to deal with shortages. If the Strait of Hormuz reopens, it could encourage a partial rejig of India's energy acquisition back to its traditional markets in the Middle East. India's growth data for the last three months suggest some evidence of supply-side disruptions from the war, but the impact has been dampened by the government's control of fuel prices, deft handling of supply shortages and the reluctance of companies to pass on higher costs to consumers. That said, we reduced our forecast for India's FY27 GDP growth by 50bp to 6.6%, on the expectation that some of the economic drags could materialize with lags. If the war concludes and supply-side constraints reverse, margin pressures on firms could ease.

The pick-up in CPI inflation has been primarily driven by higher food and fuel prices, with the government raising petrol and diesel prices (from mid-May) and some increase in LPG prices, but underlying pressures remain tepid, with most sub-components of the core basket yet to demonstrate a decisive pick-up in momentum, even as firms face escalating wholesale inflation. While lower oil and input prices should ease pressures, we note that the government had already cut excise duties on petrol/diesel before hiking prices, so any moderation in global crude oil prices could lead to the government reintroducing some of these taxes, while keeping prices unchanged to reduce the fiscal slippage. This would mean that our FY27 CPI inflation outlook of 5.0% remains intact for now.

On monetary policy, the RBI continues to await definitive signs of second-round effects on inflation and is wary of the downside risks to growth, and our outlook remains one of unchanged policy rates this year. On fiscal policy, higher fertilizer and fuel subsidies and uneven tax collections should lead to a 0.3pp slip in the government's fiscal deficit target to 4.6% of GDP, although a fall in energy prices could dial down some of these pressures. Our baseline FY27 CA deficit forecast is 2.4% of GDP, with the pressure on merchandise trade deficit largely buffered by a strong services surplus and remittances. Every 10% fall in crude oil prices (we assume \~USD100/bbl in FY27) should lead to a compression of the CAD by \~0.4% of GDP, although the government could be encouraged to ease some of its restrictions on gold/silver imports if the crisis passes. While we had been concerned about a large balance of payments deficit due to lower foreign investment flows, the raft of policy measures to bolster the capital account including the move to attract deposits from the Indian diaspora abroad should materially cap external pressures.

## Indonesia (Euben Paracuelles, Nabila Amani)

Lower oil prices will help ease pressures on the overall balance of payments, but only partly, given domestic and other related concerns that are contributing to lower capital inflows and widening the current account deficit. As we have discussed, high policy uncertainty of the Prabowo government is keeping FDI inflows flat, contributing to the basic balance turning to a deficit in Q1. In addition, the announcement to centralize commodity exports could lead to short-term disruptions in goods exports. While fiscal risks may also be dissipated via lower fuel subsidy spending, the implementation of priority programs like the MBG are accelerating, and there are still no revenue reform measures. Thus, we still expect the fiscal deficit this year to hit the ceiling of 3% of GDP and see increased below-the-line spending, so the risk of a stealth breach of that ceiling remains significant. In terms of monetary policy, we continue to expect BI to keep its policy rate unchanged on 18 June at 5.50%, as it has frontloaded the 25bp hike in an off-cycle meeting last week, in our view. For the rest of the year, our base case envisages the policy rate remaining at 5.50%, but we have flagged significant risks of additional

hikes in response to FX weakness. With easing external uncertainty, the risk of further hikes has consequently fallen but, as mentioned above, is not yet completely eliminated.

## Malaysia (Euben Paracuelles, Yiru Chen)

Given the subsidy on widely consumed Ron95 petrol, the decline in crude oil prices will ease the government's fiscal burden, and thus, the risk of a modestly higher fiscal deficit than the budgeted 3.5% of GDP may dissipate, although revenues may also decline. The latest estimates from the government suggest fuel subsidies dropped to MYR3.5bn (0.16% of GDP) per month, based on Brent oil prices around USD90/bbl, down from the peak of MYR7.5bn (0.35%) in April. The impact of lower oil prices on headline CPI inflation will likely be modest, given our CPI forecast of 2.3% this year is still underpinned by strong domestic demand and robust labour market conditions. In terms of growth, lower energy prices may appear somewhat negative for GDP growth, as Malaysia is a net oil and gas exporter. That said, we believe the key growth drivers that underpin our above-consensus 2026 GDP growth forecast of 5.2% remain intact, including strong investment spending and a broadening AI-related demand. All of these factors, alongside increasing signs of building financial imbalance risks, support our out-of-consensus forecast for BNM to hike its overnight policy rate by 25bp to a more neutral 3.00% in Q4.

## New Zealand (Andrew Ticehurst)

New Zealand is totally reliant on fuel imports, so a lower global oil price is a clear positive for the economy and reduces the risk of supply-chain disruptions too. However, it should also mean notably lower inflation. New Zealand's economy has material spare capacity, so it should be able to absorb faster growth than otherwise, and the RBNZ has a singular inflation remit, so the lower inflation outlook associated with lower global oil prices should be meaningful for it. Current policy settings are stimulatory, with the cash rate at 2.25%, and the RBNZ has received a nasty upside inflation surprise this year. We think it would feel most comfortable if it were able to return the cash rate towards neutral. Our forecast profile, for 25bp rate hikes in September, December and February would achieve this objective, and this remains our base case. We acknowledge a clear risk that policy tightening could commence at the coming meeting on 8 July (rather than in our base case of September) but at the margin, a lower inflation outlook, if lower global oil prices sustain, could support our call for a first hike in September.

## Philippines (Euben Paracuelles, Nabila Amani)

Absent fiscal subsidies, the immediate impact of lower crude oil prices will show up in a further drop in headline inflation, which already started easing in May. That said, headline inflation remains well above BSP's 2-4% target and, importantly, core inflation is still rising due to lagged pass-through effects from elevated energy prices. This supports our view that BSP's hiking cycle remains intact but it is keeping a measured approach, as the drop in headline CPI inflation precludes the need for a more aggressive monetary tightening. We therefore maintain our forecast for BSP to hike its policy rate by 25bp this week to 4.75%, followed by another two 25bp hikes in each of the next monetary board meetings. As the terms-of-trade shock should become less negative, it implies a less-sharp widening of the current account deficit. However, BSP still maintains a laissez-faire approach to FX policy, so the currency will unlikely derail BSP's hiking cycle. Fiscal expenditures are also starting to turn around, supporting a gradual improvement in the growth outlook. BSP is likely mindful of the significant risks posed by El Niño, particularly on food inflation. However, if headline inflation falls more materially in the near term, due to the drop in oil prices, and core inflation peaks much earlier than our baseline of Q4, we acknowledge the risk that BSP could hike by a lower 50bp instead of another 75bp.

## Singapore (Euben Paracuelles, Yiru Chen)

Lower crude oil prices should help mitigate the near-term upside risks to core inflation. However, core inflation is likely to remain on an upward trajectory in coming months, given the still-positive output gap. We think the eventual upward adjustments to airfares and holiday expenses from elevated jet fuel costs will still materialise, in addition to lagged adjustments to household utility costs. On the growth outlook, the reported US-Iran MOU and the potential end to hostilities reinforce our above-consensus GDP growth forecast of 4.6% this year (Consensus: 2.9%), and the downside risks which the MTI has flagged should diminish. We maintain our view that multiple growth engines will continue to drive near-term growth. The sustained global tech uptrend and broadening AI-related demand should continue to boost electronics output, while a potential recovery in feedstock supply should support chemicals output, which has been posing a small drag. A strong manufacturing sector would also have some spillovers to the services sector, while a buoyant residential property market and the implementation of several major projects will support construction activity. The expansionary FY26 budget and frontloaded fiscal support measures should further bolster domestic demand resilience, particularly as geopolitical headwinds recede. All these factors support our FX strategy team's forecast for another slight S\$NEER slope increase by the MAS in July.

## South Korea (Jeong Woo Park)

We maintain our 2026 GDP growth and inflation forecasts of 2.4% and 2.7%, respectively. While the direct boost to economic growth may be limited, given the current expansion remains heavily concentrated in the semiconductor sector, lower energy prices should help alleviate the downside risks to domestic demand over the second half of the year. From an inflation perspective, lower oil prices would moderate headline CPI pressures by reducing fuel and transportation costs and lowering the pass-through from imported energy prices to consumer prices. As a result, peak headline inflation during the second half of the year could prove lower than previously anticipated. That said, the impact on core inflation is likely to be more gradual, reflecting sticky domestic service prices.

On fiscal policy, easing oil prices would reduce the need for an additional supplementary budget in H2. As a result, we believe the government may have greater flexibility in allocating a portion of any excess tax income toward improving fiscal sustainability. Rather than deploying the entirety of excess tax revenues to additional spending, policymakers may find it increasingly appropriate to use part of these resources to strengthen the fiscal position, including reducing borrowing needs and rebuilding fiscal buffers.

In terms of monetary policy, the latest developments in the Middle East reduce the urgency for front-loaded tightening, in our view, but do not eliminate the need for further rate hikes. We continue to expect the BOK to deliver a 25bp rate hike in July, although the likelihood of a more aggressive tightening path (i.e., a 50bp hike in July or consecutive rate increases in July and August) appears to have diminished meaningfully following the easing of geopolitical tensions and the decline in oil prices.

Nevertheless, we do not believe the MOU and lower oil prices will materially alter the overall policy stance of the BOK. At its May meeting, the BOK justified the need for tightening by highlighting the positive spillovers from the semiconductor upcycle to the broader economy, the growing possibility of demand-driven inflation pressures and the need to narrow the interest rate differential with the US. Given these considerations remain largely intact, the recent decline in oil prices is unlikely to fundamentally change the BOK's reaction function. Accordingly, we maintain our call for three 25bp rate hikes (July and October 2026, and January 2027; terminal rate: $3.25\%$ ).

## Taiwan (Jeong Woo Park, Si Ying Toh)

We maintain our 2026 GDP growth and inflation forecasts of 9.9% and 2.0%, respectively. Lower oil prices would help reduce downside risks to growth while easing upside pressures on inflation. As a net energy importer, Taiwan would benefit from lower import costs and an improvement in real household purchasing power, providing additional support to domestic demand. From an inflation perspective, the decline in energy prices is likely to lower the near-term trajectory of headline CPI and reduce concerns over a broader pass-through from imported inflation. This should help moderate the upside risks to the inflation outlook that had emerged in recent months.

For monetary policy, the easing inflationary pressures is likely to reinforce the Central Bank of the Republic of China's (Taiwan) relatively dovish stance. We maintain our expectation for two 12.5bp rate hikes to a terminal rate of $2.25\%$ , but push out the timing of our rate hike call to December this year and March next year (versus June and December this year). In our view, lower oil prices reduce the urgency for near-term rate hikes, allowing the CBC to wait for clearer evidence that stronger domestic demand is translating into more persistent inflationary pressures before proceeding with further policy normalisation.

## Thailand (Euben Paracuelles, Lattakit Lapudomkarn)

Thailand is one of the economies most exposed to oil price shocks, due to its sizable energy deficit, estimated at around $7\%$ of GDP, and its heavy reliance on tourism, which contributes approximately $10\%$ of GDP. The decline in oil prices should provide support for Thailand's economy by easing the burden on the consumer through lower inflation and improving tourism sentiment, following earlier weakness driven mainly by the decline in European tourists. However, we maintain our view that the overall growth backdrop is

expected to remain subdued, as it is constrained by structural headwinds and weak domestic demand. On the external front, the record-high trade deficit in April is largely driven by elevated oil prices and front-loaded oil imports due to fear of supply shortages. If oil prices start to ease, the trade balance would also likely improve. Against this backdrop, we maintain our baseline view that the Bank of Thailand will leave policy rates unchanged throughout the year. Lower oil prices further reinforce this baseline view through easing inflationary pressures, reducing the probability of any policy tightening.

## Asia FX and rates strategy

The impending reopening of the Strait of Hormuz supports our stance...

## China: Retail sales revisited

In this report, we revisit the sharp slowdown in retail sales growth this year. We find the slowdown was mainly driven by subsidized goods, which were affected by payback effects from the trade-in program. ## A broader picture of consumption beyond headline retail sales

Headline retail sales data do not provide a comprehensive picture of consumption, as it excludes service consumption other than catering. In this section, we estimate the share and performance of this missing component following the NBS's first-ever release of aggregate retail sales growth covering both goods and services in May. We calculate that aggregate retail sales of all goods and services slowed to 2.8% in the first five months of the year from 4.4% in 2025 (Figure 44). Services excluding catering account for 30% of aggregate retail sales, with growth remaining stable at 6.0% y-o-y in the first five months of this year (2025: 6.1%). This broader view of retail sales data further reinforces our conclusion that goods sales were the main drag on consumption growth.

## How did we arrive at this?

The retail sales data (short for “total society-wide retail sales of consumer products”) measures goods and catering services sold directly to domestic households and entities for final consumption. The part of services consumption other than catering is excluded from retail sales. Since 2023, the NBS started to separately report retail sales growth in all services sectors in year-to-date terms. However, the relative size of the services sector consumption remained unknown.

This missing piece was recently addressed when the NBS for the first time reported aggregate retail sales data ("total society-wide retail sales of consumer products including services"). Along with the May activity data release, the NBS reported aggregate retail sales growth of 2.8% y-o-y in the first five months of 2026. With 1.2% y-o-y growth in goods retail sales and 5.4% in services retail sales during the same period, we derive that goods and services accounted for 62% and 38%, respectively, of aggregate retail sales.

Given the 62% share of goods sales, we calculate the catering services share at 8%, as the NBS reports the absolute value of both goods and catering sales. Thus, services excluding catering account for 30%. Using this breakdown of total retail sales of goods and services, we calculate that services excluding catering registered 6.0% y-o-y growth in the first five months of 2026. We find that goods consumption was the largest drag on aggregate retail sales this year, registering 1.2% y-o-y growth in January-May 2026, followed by catering (3.1% y-o-y), while services excluding catering provided some support (6.0% y-o-y).

## K-shaped wealth effects are a drag on overall consumption growth

A recent research report $[1]$ conducted by CF40 Research, a think tank specializing in macroeconomics and policy research, showed that aggregate household balance sheets have been repairing since late 2024. We believe the mechanism through which this repair occurs limits any positive consumption spillover. First, the balance sheet repair occurs exclusively among the wealthiest cohorts who possess the lowest marginal propensity to consume, while most households remain mired in balance sheet deterioration. Second, the balance sheet repair was driven partly by improving net income surpluses, which were a result of aggressive spending cuts, not robust income growth.

## K-shaped wealth effects from property and stock market

As we noted in our previous report, performances across the property market are diverging, with price stabilization and modest gains concentrated in tier-one cities, while smaller cities continue to experience price declines. Second, the AI boom and stock market rally disproportionately benefit high net worth individuals who hold meaningful equity allocations that made gains on the recent AI market rally, whereas the majority of households have minimal stock market exposure and derived little from these asset price increases. This creates a K-shaped wealth effect, where the balance sheets of only the wealthiest households strengthened, while the vast majority face continued balance sheet deterioration due to ongoing real estate asset depreciation and limited participation in financial markets.

## A repair through austerity rather than prosperity

The report from CF40 Research shows that households are building net income surpluses, but this accumulation is the result of aggressive spending cuts and defensive saving behavior, not robust income growth.

In other words, Chinese households are essentially repairing their balance sheets through austerity rather than prosperity, paying down debts and hoarding precautionary savings in response to future income uncertainty, a weak social protection network and a perennial property crisis. This pattern fundamentally differs from organic balance sheet repair driven by rising incomes. In such a situation, a seemingly improving household balance sheet could weigh on consumption growth.

# How is China's AI sector financed?

In our recent AI and double “K-shapes” thematic report, we discussed the US-China AI race and estimated the size of China’s AI capex. A clear distinction in AI capex between the US and China is the role of government. US AI capex is overwhelmingly driven by the private sector, especially the hyperscalers, while China’s AI capex is led and mainly funded by the state. In this note, we illustrate the decisive role of state capital from both the central and local governments, and we will also comment on private AI spending centered on cloud services and LLMs. With the renewed sharp declines in investment in Q2 and Beijing’s commitment to stabilizing investment, we believe Beijing will likely accelerate bond issuance and step up fiscal spending in coming months. The AI sector will likely become an even more strategic policy focus, as China seeks to nurture its own full AI supply chains and pursue technology self-reliance.

## The decisive role of state capital

China's AI capex lags US levels materially, while the structure of its spending differs considerably from that of the US. While the US government offers a package of USD53bn in funding via the CHIPS and Science Act, the US AI ecosystem is overwhelmingly driven by the private sector, with the vast majority of AI capex funded by hyperscalers. Facing restricted access to advanced chips and a substantial technology gap with the US, China has mobilized extensive, multi-tiered state capital to support its own full AI supply chains, in pursuit of technological self-reliance. Although private enterprises dominate LLMs and AI agents, state capital plays a decisive role in the AI industrial chain's most constrained segments, particularly chip design and manufacturing. We illustrate the multi-layered funding schemes from the central government, local governments and SOEs.

## "Big Fund": From Semiconductors to AI

The National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund (commonly known as the “Big Fund”) is a core state-backed investment vehicle for China’s semiconductor sector, with the strategic goal of elevating domestic IC industrial chains to global advanced standards and fostering world-leading enterprises by 2030. Three fund phases have been launched: Phases I, II and III were launched in September 2014, October 2019 and May 2024, with registered capital of RMB139bn, RMB204bn and RMB344bn, respectively. Major shareholders include the Ministry of Finance, China Development Bank, six major state-owned commercial banks and leading central SOEs, such as China National Tobacco and China Mobile.

The focus of investment has evolved progressively across these fund phases. Phase I prioritized downstream chip manufacturing, packaging and testing to support industrial chain leaders. Phase II shifted upward to target high-barrier upstream semiconductor equipment and materials. Phase III has the broadest coverage, focusing comprehensively on core bottleneck areas. Beyond the traditional semiconductor chain, Phase II has expanded its investment scope to printed circuit boards (PCBs) and AI industries.

Phase III has accelerated tangible AI capital deployment. In January 2025, it co-established the RMB60bn National AI Industry Investment Fund with Guozhitou (Shanghai) Private Equity Fund Management. In early May 2026, it was widely reported that the Big Fund was in active negotiations to lead the initial financing of Chinese AI startup DeepSeek. Collectively, the three Big Fund phases provide nearly RMB700bn in state capital support for semiconductors and AI.

## Ultra-long central government special bonds

To boost domestic demand, the central government started to issue ultra-long-term special bonds from 2024, with a large part of the funding designated to support the “Two Majors” (implementing major national strategies and building up security capacity in key areas). In 2026, Beijing allocated RMB800bn out of an overall RMB1.3trn in ultra-long central government special bonds (CGSB) for the “Two Majors”, with the scale the same as in 2025. In 2025, this RMB800bn funding supported 1,459 major projects covering transportation, water conservancy, energy, environmental protection and farmland construction. For 2026, the first batch of the “Two Majors” funding was RMB390bn, still prioritizing transportation and energy infrastructure projects. However, for the second batch of RMB217bn announced in April, AI was explicitly identified as one of the key areas for support, marking a formal shift of the use of CGSBs toward AI-related sectors.

## The “new policy financing tools”

To arrest the sharp decline in investment, Beijing introduced “new policy financing tools (NPFT)”, which were launched in September 2025 with an initial quota of RMB500bn and an additional RMB800bn in the fiscal budget for this year. Like the previous two similar schemes in 2015-17 and 2022, these new tools are designed to channel equity financing from three policy banks to investment projects. Unlike the previous two rounds that focused on conventional infrastructure projects, the current program prioritizes the digital economy, AI and consumer infrastructure construction. The initial RMB500bn funding quota was divided up as follows: RMB250bn for China Development Bank (CDB), RMB150bn for Agricultural Development Bank of China (ADBC) and RMB100bn for the Export-Import Bank of China (CEXIM). Specifically, CDB allocated 37.5% of its funds to the digital economy and AI sectors, while CEXIM designated 40% of its capital for these fields. Acting as a quasi-fiscal tool, policy bank financing clearly demonstrates the shift of focus of governmental spending toward the AI industries.

Crucially, these funds serve as project equity capital, delivering a substantial investment leverage effect. According to the NDRC, the initial RMB500bn fund is expected to drive over RMB7trn in total project investment, and the leverage effect could be similar to the additional quota of RMB800bn announced this year.

## Local government funding for AI sectors has generally been contained

Compared with central government funding, local governments have deployed relatively less of their own funding to promote AI, as the property fallout continues to weigh on local fiscal conditions. In fact, a significant portion of local government bond issuance over the past three years has been allocated to replace local hidden debt or clean up arrears owed to the private sector.

The net financing of local government bonds totaled RMB5.4trn in 2025, including RMB0.8trn in general bonds and RMB4.6trn in special bonds. Nevertheless, only RMB140bn (merely 2.7%) was channelled into new infrastructure and strategic emerging industrial infrastructure. The proportion further declined to 1.0% in Q1 2026, reflecting limited direct local funding for AI investment in general.

That said, some local governments have managed to ramp up their investment significantly to benefit from the global AI boom. For example, amid the unprecedented global memory supercycle, in April 2026, China's central tech hub Wuhan unveiled a funding package of RMB260bn to support memory chips production expansion in 2026, targeting two major companies: YMTC and XMC.

## Favorable financing conditions for AI Firms

In addition to direct state capital, China's financial system has also been supportive for the AI sectors. First, corporate financing costs have trended steadily downward. In 2025, according to the PBoC's Q1 2026 MPR, the overall financing cost of AI-related bond-issuing enterprises fell by about 35bp, outperforming the 24bp decline in average corporate loan rates, even though the 10y CGB yield rose by 18bp, reflecting policy-driven financing cost advantages for AI firms. Second, on funding structure, AI enterprises secured around RMB250bn in direct financing in 2025, with a notable expansion in medium- and long-term bond issuance. This provides stable, long-term capital for core technological R&D and production capacity expansion. Third, innovative financing tools

are rapidly expanding. The issuance scale of tech innovation bonds and other specialized instruments surged by over 71% in 2025, effectively diversifying financing channels and easing capital constraints for AI enterprises of various stages.

## STAR market and Hong Kong stock market are fueling China's AI ambitions

Amid efforts to revive mainland stock markets and restore capital market confidence, overall IPO activity in China's A-share market has been largely suspended since early 2024. However, a steady stream of tech firms have been allowed to be listed and raise capital on the Shanghai Stock Exchange's STAR Market. Specifically, in the past year, leading domestic GPU designers Moore Threads (RMB8bn) and MetaX (RMB3.9bn), alongside advanced packaging leader SJ Semiconductor (RMB4.8bn) have all completed their IPOs, underscoring institutional support for equity financing of the chips and AI sectors.

In contrast with onshore, Hong Kong has deliberately relaxed its IPO regulations to revitalize its capital market and attract mainland companies, particularly in the technology sector. This policy shift has effectively spurred a wave of listings. Key examples include PCB maker Victory Giant Technology, which raised HKD23bn in April, the largest Hong Kong IPO year to date. Computing chip designer Montage Technology completed a dual listing in Hong Kong with an IPO of HKD8bn, while LLMs firms Zhupu AI and MiniMax raised HKD4.4bn and HKD5.5bn, respectively. Together, the onshore STAR Market and the offshore Hong Kong stock market have strategically channelled substantial capital into Chinese tech companies, supporting the rise of China's chips and AI sectors.

## FCNR(B) details announced

At its 6 June policy meeting, the RBI announced several measures to attract net capital inflows, including the “bazooka” Foreign Currency Non-Resident Bank or the FCNR(B) scheme (see India: Policy bazooka for managing currency prudently leaves out policy rate, 6 June 2026). An FCNR(B) account is a fixed-term deposit for Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) that is held in a foreign currency, is protected from exchange rate fluctuations and earns tax-free interest in India. This scheme aims to offer higher interest rate foreign currency deposits to the large Indian diaspora abroad, with the objective of attracting large capital inflows, funding the balance of payments and easing currency depreciation pressures.

The RBI has since announced more details on the FCNR(B) deposit scheme, and banks have started revealing their FCNR(B) deposit rates. In this note, we discuss the operational details of the FCNR(B) scheme, our estimate of the total amount that could be deposited and the impact on India's balance of payments and the banking system.

## Operational details of the FCNR(B) scheme

The operational details of the FCNR(B) scheme and the RBI swap facility are as follows:

\- Eligibility: The scheme applies to all fresh FCNR(B) deposits made between 8 June and 30 September 2026.

\- Tenor: Deposits should have a minimum tenor of 3 years, and a maximum of 5 years (including renewed deposits upon maturity).

\- Currencies: Deposits can be made in any freely convertible currency, but the RBI swap facility will be available in USD only.

\- Swap mechanism: Banks will sell USD to the RBI at spot and simultaneously agree to buy back at the same rate (par swap) at maturity. The swap facility will be open until 16 October for deposits made by 30 September.

\- Lock-in period: Underlying deposits have a 1-year lock-in; premature withdrawal is allowed after 1 year, but RBI swaps cannot be cancelled.

• Regulatory relief:

RBI will bear the full hedging cost until 30 September 2026.

FCNR(B) deposits will be exempt from mandatory reserve ratio requirements (CRR/SLR).

\- Banks can exclude their swap positions when computing their net open position (NOP-INR) limits.

\- Leverage: Provisions under paragraph 402 of RBI credit facilities directions are not applicable. Paragraph 402 states that banks cannot issue a non-fund based facility (guarantee or letter of credit) to any entity assuring redemption. Since this provision is not applicable for FCNR(B), it should enable banks to offer leverage on these deposits.

## NOM's estimate on the likely inflow under FCNR(B) 2026

India has introduced four major diaspora bond schemes in the past:

• 1991: India Development Bond (IDB)

• 1998: The Resurgent India Bonds (RIB) scheme

• 2000: India Millennium Deposit (IMD) scheme

• 2013: FCNR(B) Swap Window

Box 1 and Figure 52 summarize these past four schemes, the coupons offered on USD deposits, the prevailing USD rates at the time and our estimate of the amount that could be raised via FCNR(B) in 2026.

The 2013 FCNR(B) scheme was a big success, due to low USD rates globally, as well as the leverage provided by banks to NRIs, which increased the annual returns to high teens on the invested amount. Today, USD rates are much higher, but with the RBI assuming the full hedging cost, USD deposits will be attractive to banks, provided they can raise money at a lower rate than INR deposits of a similar maturity. Currently, INR rates for a 3-yr fixed deposits are around 6.4% on average, so most banks are offering a FCNR(B) USD deposit rate of \~6% (Figure 53).

India's largest public sector bank, State Bank of India, has offered a 3-year FCNR(B) USD deposit rate of $5.5\%$ (for deposits over USD1mn), private sector lender HDFC Bank will offer $6\%$ and some smaller private sector banks are offering $7\%$ . Depending on the leverage provided, this should boost returns on FCNR(B) deposits into the teens in 2026, which we believe will make them attractive even in a higher USD rate environment.


According to the World Bank's analysis of past diaspora bonds, the Indian diaspora “did not seek as high a country risk premium as markets would have demanded”, likely reflecting a lower risk of default assigned by investors of Indian origin (see Development Finance via Diaspora Bonds, S. Ketkar and D. Ratha, World Bank, August 2007).

Using the annual private remittance inflow and outstanding NRI deposits as a proxy for India's rising diaspora income, we find that each of the past four diaspora schemes raised at least 40% of the total amount of annual remittance inflow (and 30-38% of outstanding NRI deposits). Considering higher USD rates globally, we believe the 2026 FCNR(B) scheme could conservatively garner \~USD55bn, which equates to 34% of annual remittances, 33% of outstanding NRI deposits and 1.4% of GDP.

## Impact on the banking system

India's banking system has been facing the challenge of an elevated credit-deposit ratio in recent years. As banks swap the FCNR(B) USD deposits with the RBI, their INR liquidity positions will improve over the next four months. Improved liquidity conditions should reduce banks' reliance on higher-cost certificates of deposit for funding. That said, liquidity typically tends to be concentrated in the hands of a few banks, so the impact may be uneven.

With FCNR(B) USD inflow swapped into INR via the RBI's swap facility, banks can: 1) lend more (if credit demand exists), benefiting from CRR/SLR exemptions; 2) invest in government securities (beyond mandatory SLR requirements); 3) deploy in other higher-yielding assets (corporate bonds, commercial paper etc.). In a comfortable liquidity environment, banks would likely prioritize lending and higher-yielding investments rather than parking funds in low-yielding overnight markets.

In 2013, after the FCNR(B) scheme was announced, bank deposit growth picked up by 2.9pp between August and December 2013, while credit growth was largely stable, which led to a moderation in the credit-deposit gap (Figure 58). Bank investment in government securities rose sharply by 4pp during the same period. However, despite the liquidity injection via the FCNR(B) swap, banking system liquidity did not improve, reflecting a deliberate policy choice at that time to keep liquidity tight to guard against inflation risks.

Banking conditions are slightly different today (Figure 59). While credit growth is outpacing deposit growth, and growth in bank investment in government securities has moderated over the past year, banking liquidity is already in a surplus. With the quantum of FCNR(B) deposits likely to be much larger in 2026, and recent policy guidance from the RBI to “ensure appropriate liquidity”, there should be a more salutary impact on banking liquidity and a possible pickup in bank investment in government securities and other higher-yielding assets.

## Conclusion

Overall, we expect the FCNR(B) scheme to be a success and, along with other measures to boost net capital inflows, it should help plug a large part of India's balance of payments deficit we had anticipated for this year. Moreover, this scheme should boost banking liquidity and ease onshore funding constraints. Therefore, the FCNR(B) scheme is well-timed and solves two of India's macro problems.

## India: BoP swings – from deficit to surplus

Lower oil prices, gold import restrictions and measures to attract capital inflows could swing the BoP from a deficit to a surplus of over USD40bn in FY27.

\- Phased policy response to external pressures: Since May 2026, India has rolled out policy measures in broadly two waves; the first targets the current account (gold/silver import duty hikes, austerity advocacy) and the second bolsters the capital account through FCNR(B) deposit incentives, concessional External Commercial Borrowing (ECB) swaps and tax relief for FPIs investing in government securities. In this note, we explore the Balance of Payments (BoP) implications of these measures.

\- FCNR(B) scheme could attract \~USD55bn: The RBI is fully absorbing hedging costs for banks raising 3–5 year foreign currency deposits from non-resident Indians (NRIs) who can access leverage, with CRR/SLR exemptions and deposit rates hiked to 6–7%. Based on the growing size and income of the Indian diaspora, inflows could conservatively reach USD55bn (1.4% of GDP), in line with the upper end of past FX mobilisation episodes.

\- ECBs via the public sector may add \~USD20bn: A concessional forex swap at a fixed 1.5% rate could incentivise public sector companies to borrow abroad. Historical evidence shows a mixed impact on inflows from ECB liberalisation, determined by global conditions and domestic investment appetite.

\- Bond index inclusion focus: The removal of capital gains and withholding taxes on FPI GSec investments, expanded FAR access to 15/30/40-year tenors and BIS tax exemptions are aimed at meeting Bloomberg's operational criteria. Inclusion could drive \~USD20–25bn of staggered inflows, though likely materialising only in FY28.

\- Gold/silver duty hikes could trim the current account by \~USD20bn: Based on past experience, we estimate the 9pp increase in gold import duties could reduce gold imports by \~140 tonnes (\~USD20bn). Silver import restrictions should lead to a further compression of \~USD1.5–2bn of imports, with the impact more modest, given silver's smaller share of total imports.

\- BoP outlook swings from deficit to surplus: Taking policy actions into account, we reduce our FY27 current account deficit forecast to 1.9% of GDP from 2.4% and increase our capital account surplus forecast to \~2.3% of GDP (\~USD94bn) from \~0.7% (\~USD31bn). The net result is a BoP surplus of \~USD15bn in FY27, which marks a reversal from our prior forecast for a deficit of \~USD70bn.

\- BoP surplus with Brent at USD80/bbl: Given the recent fall in oil prices, the BoP outlook looks even better. Assuming Brent oil prices average at USD80/bbl in the remaining months of FY27, we estimate India's current account deficit will narrow even further to 1.2% of GDP, and the BOP surplus will expand to USD43.6bn.

Since May, there has been a regular drumbeat of measures announced to address India's widening BoP deficit. The initial measures – like austerity advocacy and import restrictions and duty hikes on gold/silver imports – were primarily aimed at trimming the current account deficit (Figure 60). The second batch of announcements focused on bolstering the capital account (see India: Policy bazooka for managing currency prudently leaves out policy rate, 6 June 2026). The highlight has been the RBI fully bearing the hedging costs for banks seeking FCNR(B) deposits from Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs), who make foreign currency denominated deposits with domestic banks for which the interest is tax-free. A similar, but partial subsidisation of hedging costs has also been extended to public sector companies undertaking external commercial borrowings (ECBs). Additionally, taxes on FPI investment in GSecs have been done away with and their market entry requirements into bond markets has been relaxed. In this note, we discuss the impact of these measures on India's BoP dynamics.

## Impact on balance of payments

India has mobilised FX deposits four times in the past: 1991, 1998, 2000 and 2013, where it managed to raise \~0.6-1.4% of GDP worth of deposits (Figure 63). As we recently elaborated in our past report (see India: Decoding the FCNR(B) scheme, 11 June 2026), both the size and incomes of the Indian diaspora have increased materially since 2013. Consequently, we believe the 2026 FCNR(B) scheme could conservatively garner \~USD55bn (\~1.4% of GDP, Figure 62).

## If FCNR(B) deposits pick up, will remittances fall?

Not necessarily. Figure 65 shows generally that non-resident deposits and remittances inflows have been largely correlated historically, which suggests they may have both been impacted by common factors like incomes and the size of the Indian diaspora. However, remittances have surged over the past decade, even as non-resident deposits have lagged. Remittances may be linked more closely to persistent familial necessities, while falling NRI deposits could reflect the narrowing global interest rate differentials with India and NRIs diversifying their investments beyond Indian bank deposits.

To further investigate, we compare periods following the introduction of FX deposit mobilisation schemes to see if the increase in NRI deposits has been accompanied by a fall in remittances. We do not find strong evidence to suggest this hypothesis and, indeed, during these periods, there has been a slight pick-up in remittances (Figure 66). Consequently, we do not expect a drop in remittances in reaction to the FCNR(B) scheme.

## #2. Attracting ECBs through public sector companies

## Details of the measure

The RBI has offered a concessional forex swap arrangement to incentivise public sector companies to borrow abroad through ECBs for an average maturity of three years. It has also extended this to authorised dealers to access Overseas Foreign Currency Borrowings (OFCBs) for a minimum of three years. The swap tenor will be capped at five years, and the RBI will be undertaking it at a fixed rate of 1.5%.

## Impact on balance of payments

In Figure 64, we consider the gross and net change in ECBs across the years for which we assess a major liberalisation has taken place in the regulations. We find that the measures have had a mixed impact, with the impact in some cases materialising with a lag. This reflects the sensitivity of ECB flows to other factors, like the global environment and the domestic investment appetite.

Reports suggest that PSUs could raise \~USD20bn through ECBs, with entities like Power Finance Corporation (PFC), National Thermal Power Corporation Ltd (NTPC), Indian Oil Companies (IOC), Steel Authority of India Ltd (SAIL) and Housing and Urban Development Corporation (HUDCO) evaluating raising of overseas funds (source: Business Standard, Hindu Businessline).

## #3. Reducing market entry barriers into the bond market

## Details of the measure

Three important steps were taken by policymakers to make India's bond market more attractive to foreign investors:

1. The government did away with the 12.5% long-term capital gains tax and 20% withholding tax on interest income for FPIs investing in GSecs.

2. Capital gains and interest income from the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) will now additionally be exempt from taxation.

3. The scope of the Fully Accessible Route (FAR) – which is an open-access channel for foreign investors to trade in GSecs – has now been expanded to include new issuances of 15-, 30- and 40-year tenor GSecs and sovereign green bonds. We note that the overall investment limits of 6% of outstanding stock of central government securities and 2% of state government securities remains unchanged. Also, the existing “General” and “Long term” categories for FPI investments will be combined into a single investment limit.

## Impact on balance of payments

FPIs have typically underutilised the investment limits of GSecs, with latest data placing their utilisation at \~17-18%. That said, these reforms seem to be squarely aimed at facilitating India's inclusion into the Bloomberg Global Aggregate Index, which tracks \~USD3trn in global assets (source: The Economic Times, Bloomberg). So far, Bloomberg has deferred India's inclusion owing to operational and market infrastructure reasons. We note that India is already part of indices like the JPM GBI-EM Index (see India rates and FX: IGBs are finally set to be included in the JPM GBI-EM indices, 22 September 2023), the Bloomberg EM Local Currency Government Index and the FTSE Russell Emerging Markets Government Bond Index (EMGBI).

A potential inclusion in the Bloomberg Global Aggregate Index would likely lead to staggered inflows of \~USD20-25bn, assuming \~1% weighting allocated to India, but these flows would most likely not materialise until FY28.

## #4. The impact of gold/silver import restrictions

In Figure 67, we examine the impact of higher import duties (15% from 6%) on gold imports (see India: Gold and silver duty hiked to curb imports, 13 May 2026). We find that, in previous episodes of import tightening, there has been a fall in gold imports, both in nominal and volume terms. In the past, a 1pp rise in gold import duties has, on average, coincided with a drop of \~USD2.5bn/40 tonnes in gold imports, although there are significant variations within episodes. In FY23, a \~4pp increase in customs duties led to a \~200 tonne fall in gold imports, while in FY20, a 2.5pp increase led to a \~263 tonne fall. In comparison, we conservatively assume a fall of \~140 tonnes this time around (for a 9pp rise in import duties). Based on forward gold prices, this amounts to a \~USD20bn drop in gold imports.

The impact of silver import restrictions is likely to be more muted, as it accounts for $\sim 1.6\%$ of overall imports. Assuming a $10\%$ drop in silver import volume demand in FY27, we see a $\sim$ USD1.5-2.0bn fall in silver imports.

## Impact on the balance of payments

In the run-up to these measures, we had projected a current account deficit at 2.4% of GDP in FY27. The tepid net FDI and FII flows led us to expect a massive BOP deficit of \~USD70bn.

Based on these dynamics, we reduce our FY27 current account deficit forecast to 1.9% of GDP from 2.4% (Figure 68). We have not changed our remittances forecast for FY27 much, but have pencilled a frugal \~USD21bn drop in gold and silver imports. For now, we have not modified our annual oil price assumption of \~USD95/bbl, but a sustained fall in prices could lead to a lower deficit.

On the capital account, we increase our forecasts significantly to a surplus of around 2.3% of GDP (\~USD94bn) from \~0.7% (\~USD31bn), driven by \~USD55bn in banking capital (owing to the FCNR (B) deposits), \~USD25bn of loans and incrementally higher FPI and FDI flows. Overall, this should leave India's balance of payments in a small surplus of \~USD15bn, which would represent a significant improvement from our previous forecast for a deficit of USD70bn.

## If Brent oil stays at USD80/bbl

Given the recent fall in oil prices, the BoP outlook looks even better. Assuming Brent oil prices average USD80/bbl over the remaining months of FY27, we estimate India's current account deficit would narrow even further to 1.2% of GDP, and the BOP surplus would expand to USD43.6bn.

## ... and low multiplier effects

The fiscal multiplier is also low at around 0.3, based on our estimates of the impulse response of private consumption, which implies a limited impact on GDP growth. Our estimates are based on the average growth in private consumption relative to its usual pace during the six months following the start of “half-half programs” between 2020 and 2022. These estimates are consistent with BOT's assessment that cash-transfer policies generate lower multiplier effects than direct government spending and investment. We note, however, that our estimate is even lower than the estimate of the Office of the Prime Minister at 0.9.

In our view, the low fiscal multiplier likely reflects high household debt and weak sentiment, particularly among lower-income groups, so the transfers are used for debt servicing rather than new spending. Tight financial conditions, with banks raising credit standards, also provide counteracting pressure, generating a vicious cycle of credit risks, negative loan growth and an economic downturn, similar to the implementation of the Digital Wallet scheme in late 2024 (see Thailand: A vicious cycle between credit risks, credit contraction and an economic downturn, 12 June 2025).

We continue to expect Fitch to lower Thailand's credit rating by one-notch to BBB from $\mathrm{BBB + }$ later in the year. Fitch has had a "negative" rating outlook in place since September 2025. # Indonesia: Discussing the implications of the new state export agency (DSI)

Earlier this week, we hosted a MacroBrew@NOM with an expert on trade and industrial policy from one of Indonesia's leading think-tanks to discuss the newly established PT Danantara Sumerdaya Indonesia (DSI). In this report, we summarize his thoughts in a Q&A format. Overall, the takeaways from this discussion support our view that the creation of DSI could raise investor concerns about greater state control, increase policy uncertainty and contribute to downward pressure on credit ratings and the currency (see First Insights - Indonesia: Our initial thoughts on new policies for the government to control commodity exports, 20 May 2026).

## 1. Can you provide us with some basics on the DSI?

On 20 May 2026, President Prabowo announced in parliament that the DSI is being created to centralize exports of strategic commodities, starting with coal, palm oil (CPO) and ferro-alloys, but all other products could be included over time. The DSI will be operated by Danantara and full implementation is targeted by 1 January 2027, when the DSI will have responsibility and authority over export management of these commodities, including the handling of transactions and contracts with overseas buyers. The next few months will be a transition period. The President said that the main rationale for creating the DSI is to address problems of under-invoicing and transfer pricing, as well as to improve repatriation of export receipts. However, we also believe the decision to establish the DSI likely reflects the government's objective to increase state participation in strategic sectors while advancing Indonesia's downstream industrialisation agenda.

## 2. Is this easy to implement across all three sectors?

Implementation challenges are significant and could come from various aspects across CPO, coal and ferro-alloys (nickel) due to their distinct physical characteristics and sector structures:

\- The CPO sector will be most challenging to centralize. This is because of the nature of palm oil production —fresh fruit bunches (FFB) deteriorate rapidly after harvesting, meaning farmers and refineries cannot hold inventory while awaiting administrative clearance. Fragmentation across the sector compounds this issue, with nearly three million smallholders and hundreds of refineries affected by the new arrangements. Additionally, current trading companies provide sophisticated market risk management services, hedging CPO prices through various mechanisms that the DSI may struggle to replicate in the near term.

\- The coal industry operates on long-term contractual relationships, with producers entering multi-year agreements with utilities and industrial users that use price formulas, quality specifications and buyer credit terms. These existing contracts may require renegotiation under the new arrangements, potentially causing export delays and increasing logistical costs, while adding complexity to an already heavily regulated sector with multiple layers of state control.

\- Ferro-alloys are most directly tied to Indonesia's downstreaming policy, with over 90% of nickel production destined for China and most nickel processing plants owned by Chinese companies. This high buyer concentration potentially creates a double-edged sword: it may enhance Indonesia's negotiating leverage and simultaneously exposes the sector to significant risk owing to the extreme dependency on a single export destination.

## 3. Have there been similar policies in the past? If so what were the key lessons?

Yes, similar policies have been implemented in the past, with notable commodities affected including rice, cloves and plywood during the 1970s and 1990s (Figure 94). The evidence is that initiatives created a monopsony, which caused significant market distortions. Specifically, the domestic prices of commodities in question declined, prompting producers to reduce output, while international prices rose. This dynamic temporarily enhanced Indonesia's export pricing power. However, in the longer run, the policy was also undermined the competitiveness of domestic industries and fostered political patronage. Over time, international buyers adapted by diversifying their supply chains and seeking alternative sources, ultimately eroding Indonesia's pricing advantage and reduced exports.

## 4. Despite these lessons, why do you think President Prabowo is pushing for the creation of the DSI, and will it succeed this time?

Prabowo places significant emphasis on state involvement, not just in policy formulation but also in macroeconomic management and business activity. The president has continued to push the view that Indonesia is resource-rich with great potential. The creation of the DSI appears to be in line with his broader policy agenda, and he likely believes it will be successful.

It is unclear whether this idea came from him or his advisors. However, if you examine his writings on economics and governance, he has long advocated greater state control. This suggests he genuinely prefers such policies to prevent leakages from malpractices such as under-invoicing and safeguard national resources.

## 5. Because of his track record of pushing strongly his priority programs, do you think this will be implemented despite the challenges (similar to MBG)?

The critical question here are: what are Danantara's actual objectives and mandates? It is difficult to assess whether this policy will feature strong governance. However, we expect significant difficulties with implementation, particularly in two sectors – CPO and coal – which are already feeling the impact. The challenge is not only merely administrative but also managing the political backlash.

## 6. What can the DSI realistically achieve by January 2027?

The DSI could help curb problems of under-invoicing and transfer pricing but consolidating the exportation of commodities is not the best solution because of the complexities involved, as discussed above. During the transition period leading to 1 January 2027, we are likely to see the DSI focusing more on monitoring and establishing better contractual arrangements with more favorable procedures for the specified commodities. But the DSI is unlikely to be fully ready by January to function as the comprehensive export agency envisioned by the president.

## 7. Will this cause disruptions in exports at a time when the IDR is under pressure?

The timing of this policy is problematic. Indonesia currently needs robust exports to build up foreign exchange reserves and support the currency. Based on our calculations, 30% of Indonesia's exports are at risk due to this policy (Figure 95). Based on discussions with businesses in the coal and palm oil sectors, concerns about this policy are widespread. Some may even reduce production if the direction of policy remains uncertain. In the CPO sector, domestic FFB prices have already dropped by 15-20% since the announcement of the DSI, while international palm oil prices have increased significantly.

## 8. On the under-invoicing issue, is this a more effective approach?

The fundamental problem is enforcement, not policy. Indonesia already has extensive regulations governing export and import controls. For example, in the past customs

procedures were outsourced rather than managed by the customs office, eventually leading to some monitoring issues. Digital technology may address these issues without establishing new mechanisms that might introduce additional risks.

## 9. What response do you expect from Indonesia's main trading partners?

There are three potential categories of responses: (1) the business and commercial response – Indonesia’s main trading partners will seek alternatives and diversify supply away from Indonesia; (2) the legal response – countries could approach the WTO for mediation if they believe their national interests have been significantly harmed; and (3) retaliatory measures – we might see similar policies implemented by Indonesia’s main trading partners to counterbalance the DSI, and/or anti-dumping measures against Indonesian exports of other products. The first measure would be the most probable, at least in the initial stages.

## 10. How about China's response, particularly on processed nickel exports?

Last month, it was surprising that China's Chamber of Commerce in Indonesia issued an open letter to President Prabowo complaining about various business difficulties, including policies related to mining and resource-intensive manufacturing. This complaint is highly unusual for Chinese businesses and therefore demonstrates the seriousness of their concerns. On this specific policy, Chinese firms will likely react negatively to these new restrictions but they may also try to negotiate exemptions.

## 11. What is the ultimate impact on Indonesian export prices and volumes for these commodities?

In the past, domestic prices have declined in the short-run while international prices have increased, though this has typically been temporary, with international prices eventually adjusting. Buyers will likely diversify their supply sources and seek alternative products. Meanwhile, domestic production constraints cause difficulties for producers that cannot be quickly overcome, even after market normalization. They lose competitiveness, as demonstrated in the case of the plywood export restrictions in the 1990s. Similarly, with nickel, Indonesia's export restrictions caused international prices to spike, but eventually slumped as buyers found alternatives. Technology and markets adjust, making it increasingly challenging for Indonesia to exercise market power effectively.

## High cost of living is the top concern

$50\%$ of respondents were satisfied on the overall performance of the federal government (versus $48\%$ who were dissatisfied). This marks a drop from the start of government's term at around $60\%$ , but is an improvement from lows of $40 - 45\%$ amid elevated uncertainty and risks from US tariffs in June-August 2025.

The high cost of living and high oil prices are among the top concerns, despite Ron95 petrol still being subsidized by the government and tranches of cash handouts for all Malaysian adults. Labour market issues, i.e. minimum income and unemployment, were also key concerns.

## A large majority of local clients expect a general election next year

During our visit to Kuala Lumpur this week, we asked local clients whether they expect a general election (due by 17 February 2028) to be held this year or next year, owing to recent developments. A large majority (around 80%) of local clients at our meetings believe a general election will NOT be held until next year (most likely after the budget

announcement but before the year-end monsoon season), consistent with our baseline scenario. The most common rationale appears to be that PM Anwar requires time to increase his approval ratings, as well as focus on dealing with the fallout from the US-Iran conflict. That said, a few in this camp acknowledge that the two state elections to be held in Johor on 11 July and Negeri Sembilan on 1 August could be a deciding factor.

Only a small minority of local clients that we met expect an early general election to be called this year, citing rising pressures from Barisan Nasional (BN) and other parties (e.g. Rafizi's Bersama), as well as some infighting within PM Anwar's Pakatan Harapan (PH). If BN wins decisively in Johor, that could put some pressure on PH. In that scenario, some thought that a window for BN to form a coalition with other parties such as PAS could open up, and possibly force PM Anwar to call an early election. That said, our sense from the discussions is that local clients do not view the recent policy changes to the diesel subsidy as a big concern and/or a sign of an early election soon, partly because of the relatively small share of diesel in household fuel consumption.

## We continue to see limited economic implications

We still believe political headlines do not pose significant risks to the near-term economic outlook. We continue to forecast 2026 GDP growth of 5.2%, driven by multiple growth engines (see Asia Insights - Malaysia: GDP growth continues to hold up in Q1, 15 May 2026). Even if the election is called this year, it is likely to be after the budget in October, keeping the 2026 growth trajectory relatively intact. The upcoming state elections will likely be seen as a barometer for PM Anwar's unity government but are unlikely to affect economic activity. In terms of monetary policy, we reiterate our forecast for BNM to hike its overnight policy rate (OPR) by 25bp to 3.00% in Q4 2026, even if a general election is held this year, because OPR decisions are assessed by evolving economic conditions, in our view.

## Korea: Strong nominal GDP growth shows there are marked imbalances

As we believe tech-led nominal growth has few spillovers, we maintain our below-consensus 2026 GDP growth forecast of 2.4%.

## Fig. 101: Nominal GDP: Tech versus non-tech sector growth


\- Nominal GDP growth jumped to 17.1% y-o-y in Q1 2026 from 6.3% in Q4, the highest growth since Q3 1995. However, the composition of growth shows there are marked imbalances between the growth in the tech and non-tech sectors rather than broad-based strength.

\- Indeed, tech industry growth surged to $169.5\%$ y-o-y in Q1 2026 from $41.1\%$ in Q4, whereas non-tech industry growth remained more modest at $5.5\%$ . \- More importantly, the 169.5% nominal growth in the tech sector was mostly driven by price increases (118.5%), suggesting the transmission of tech cycles is likely to have a smaller impact than previous tech cycles suggest.

\- The tech industry is known for having few spillover effects on domestic consumption, as it is capital-intensive, and depends heavily on imports for capex. Thus, despite the tech-led strong nominal growth, as we only expect a modest recovery in domestic demand, we maintain our below-consensus 2026 GDP growth forecast of 2.4%.

## India: FCNR(B) inflows are off to a healthy start

In our baseline forecast, inflows reach USD55bn in 2026, boosting the balance of payment surplus in Q3 and injecting banking liquidity.

Fig. 103: FCNR(B) monthly flows: 2013 versus 2026


\- To bolster India's external sector, the RBI announced a bazooka “Foreign Currency Non-Resident Bank [FCNR(B)] scheme” to subsidise the hedging costs of banks seeking foreign currency deposits from non-resident Indians, on which they are allowed to provide leverage.

\- The official data, available only until April, show limited flows. However, Moneycontrol reported that banks have booked USD7bn of deposits in June, the first month of the scheme, which lasts until 30 September.

\- This time, bankers are expecting deposits to cross USD50bn in 45 days, well before the deadline. According to Business Standard, the Middle East, Singapore and Hong Kong are reported to be top sources, while initial flows from the US and London could be muted due to buoyant US equity markets and tax disadvantages, respectively.

\- We continue to expect the FCNR(B) scheme to garner a sizeable USD55bn in 2026, boosting BOP inflows in Q3. With oil prices also much lower, the BoP will likely swing from a deficit to a surplus of over USD40bn in FY27.

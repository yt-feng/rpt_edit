# Japan Consumer and Semiconductors
# Physical AI meets the convenience store – from retail king to AI testbed: why SoftBank, KDDI, Itochu, and Mitsubishi all want in

Last Friday, SoftBank Corp (9434.JP, not covered, consolidated by SoftBank Group) came shopping for convenience stores — and the real story is bigger than retail. SoftBank, PayPay and Sumitomo Mitsui Card are reportedly in talks to invest up to ¥300bn (US\$1.9bn) in Seven & i, operator of the world's largest convenience-store network (link). On its face, a telco buying into a mature retailer.

Look closer, and a different question emerges: where does AI actually prove it can do physical work? Not in a demo video, not in a data center — but somewhere with millions of daily transactions, chronic labor shortage, and thousands of identical floor plans on which an experiment can repeat itself. Japan's convenience stores are arguably the best such laboratory on earth: \~55,000 stores, tens of millions of visits a day, and an industry so starved of workers that automation is not a choice but a survival condition. That is why every deal of the past six years — Itochu×FamilyMart, Mitsubishi/KDDI×Lawson, and now the SoftBank coalition×Seven — has brought outside capital into a format everyone agrees is past its prime.

The format war has been decided — and yet ¥1.4tn (c. US\$9bn) has flowed to the losing side. CVS stagnation is structural: real sales flat for a decade, a 36.2-point fault line between the unit-price index (123.7) and the traffic index (87.5), a store network at its \~55,000 ceiling, and the overworked salaryman — the customer who bought time with money — going extinct as a demographic. Capital keeps coming anyway, because what is being bought is not the format but the network: the last physical piece of the economic zone, spanning supply chains (Itochu makes its money through FamilyMart's, not in it), payments traffic, finance (Seven Bank's ATM corner outearns the store around it, roughly 40x per square meter), and retail media (a ¥1.3tn / US\$8.4bn market by 2029).

And on that network, the story stops being about retail. For SoftBank Group, this is the missing bottom layer of the Physical AI stack Masayoshi Son has been assembling. SoftBank Group has invested in the full stack of AI, including infra, model, chip, and physical AI — but the physical AI portion still lacked the one place where AI meets the physical world daily, at scale, and gets graded. Japan's convenience stores are that place: a deployment field, a data engine, and the source of the most every day, falsifiable receipts for the thesis that AI rewrites every industry. Our conclusion: a losing format, a contested network — and survival decided by the execution that carries AI from showcase to chain-wide core systems. Plenty of investors read AI's future in data centers. Part of the answer sits on the shelf of the nearest convenience store...

We rate Seven & i Market-Perform; we remain Outperform on SoftBank Group on our constructive view of AI.

## CONVENIENCE STORES - TOWARD A STRUCTURAL TURNING POINT

The Japanese convenience store has entered the final chapter of a long growth story. Throughout the deflation era, the CVS refined its distinctive Galápagos model of high density and high efficiency into something close to an art form, elevating 24-hour accessibility and a ubiquitous network into social infrastructure, and stood as the undisputed growth engine of domestic retail. But decades of aggressive expansion have ended, and the industry is now moving into a phase of maturity and defense. The transition is visible at the intersection of several structural indicators.

The first indicator is the widening gap between the appearance of top-line growth and its underlying reality. Indexed to 2015 = 100, nominal sales have climbed to around 115 via price hikes (Exhibit 4), while real sales (inflation-adjusted) peaked in 2019 and have since flatlined near 105. Headline numbers suggest a healthy market, but recent "growth" has been driven almost entirely by the pricing lever rather than by organic customer volume. We call this the Inflationary Gap. The wider the nominal-real divergence grows, the less sustainable this "growth" becomes.

The second indicator cuts deeper — the very pricing actions sustaining the top line are eroding the industry's most valuable asset: high-frequency customer traffic. Average spend per customer has climbed for nine consecutive years to offset rising costs, but the metric has become a double-edged sword. The real unit price index has surged to 123.7, while the customer traffic index has contracted to 87.5 — a Real Gap of 36.2 points. In an environment of squeezed real wages, consumers no longer accept the "convenience premium" automatically, and they scrutinize value more carefully than ever. The price hikes meant to protect the top line have, ironically, quietly destroyed the very daily-visit habit the industry depends on.

The third indicator is the physical ceiling on store expansion. The CVS industry thrived for years on a scale model of opening thousands of stores annually to dominate regions. But by 2019, the domestic network hit a saturation point of approximately 55,000 stores (Exhibit 5). This 2019 wall was no coincidence — it marked the simultaneous convergence of three structural constraints. First, the Work Style Reform legislation that came into effect that April rendered the overtime-dependent labor model that underpinned 24-hour operations economically unsustainable. Second, the shrinking working-age population deepened labor shortages further, driving up the cost of night-shift staffing. Third, the last economically viable unclaimed territories — suburban office districts, rural highway corridors, peripheral rural outposts — had been filled, and the geographic frontier for growth effectively closed. COVID-19 only accelerated a structural ceiling that had already

## formed.

These three indicators — top-line distortion, the inversion of traffic (Exhibit 6) and unit price, and the physical ceiling on expansion — are not independent; they show the same underlying fact from different angles. The CVS sector can no longer defer its problems through quantitative expansion. The major chains have entered a phase in which they have no choice but to move into the complex battle of preserving profitability across existing assets.

## The Societal Paradigm Shift: The Death of the "Overworked Salaryman"

The deepest threat to the CVS model is not economic. Not inflation. Not store saturation. Not competitive pressure from adjacent formats. The true cause lies further beneath — the core customer archetype on which the CVS business was built, the "overworked salaryman," is quietly going extinct as a demographic category. The rise of the CVS was inseparable from the lifestyle of the corporate warrior who worked late into the night and relied on the store for energy drinks and solitary dinners — effectively buying time with money. Now, on top of the structural headwinds of inflation and saturation, a deeper pressure is bearing down: the fundamental reordering of Japanese society that is erasing this demographic altogether.

The first shift is the collapse of the commuter economy. The entrenchment of telework in urban centers has dismantled the incidental purchasing behavior that sustained CVS traffic. Triggered by the pandemic, the time spent at home by Japanese aged 15 and over increased by a striking 50 minutes per day (MIC, 2021). The "pick-up-on-the-way" occasion has evaporated — teleworkers have shorter windows for weekday shopping, and the cash-cow demand for morning coffee and midday bento boxes has migrated entirely to residential supermarkets. With the daytime population hollowed out, the CVS has lost its most powerful weapon: the high-frequency physical touchpoint.

The second shift is the erosion of traditional gender roles. The unwinding of fixed domestic roles has reshaped consumption patterns from "solitary survival" to "family-centered dining," further marginalizing the CVS model. MHLW data shows a large-scale behavioral shift, with the male parental leave take-up rate climbing from 1.4% in 2010 to 40.5% in 2024 (Exhibit 8). As dual-income, dual-parenting households spend more time at home, the need for quick, solitary meals has diminished. Consumers now have the bandwidth to shop at supermarkets for fresh ingredients, identifying the societal evolution — from "time-poor solo workers" to "time-rich family units" — as the root cause of the structural decline in CVS traffic.

The third shift is the compression of the customer base itself through generational turnover. What historically sustained CVS unit economics was the late-shift working-age population, and in particular the 30–49 core consumption cohort. According to the National Institute of Population and Social Security Research, the working-age population (15–64) will shrink from approximately 74.1 million in 2025 to approximately 69.1 million in 2035 — a decline of roughly 5.0 million, or -6.8%. The 30–49 core consumption cohort alone will contract by approximately 3.1 million, or -10.3% (Exhibit 9). The decline matters not only in scale but in character — the generations exiting and the generations replacing them do not share consumption habits. Younger cohorts, prioritizing work-life balance, do not build their routines around late-night office hours. A smaller headcount, combined with a lower CVS dependency among those who remain, moves the same trend line downward from two directions simultaneously.

These three shifts are not independent phenomena — they are different facets of the same underlying transformation: the redesign of Japanese lifestyles themselves. The evaporation of the commuter economy, the redefinition of gender roles, the generational turnover — each is a quiet, undramatic process, but the CVS business model was not designed to operate in an environment where all three unfold simultaneously. This is not a cyclical headwind; it is the outcome of an irreversible structural shift in Japanese society. Legally and culturally, consumers are not returning to a lifestyle of late-night overtime and late-night solitary meals.

## Competitors Stealing the "Daily Routine"

In this new environment, the CVS sector finds itself encircled by a "pincer attack" from competitors who are structurally better positioned to serve the modern consumer. Drugstores have emerged as the winner for daily replenishment, using food as a loss leader to offer a value proposition CVS cannot match, prompting a shift in daily shopping habits. Simultaneously, Urban Mini-Supermarkets like Aeon's "My Basket" have neutralized the CVS's geographic advantage by opening next door with fresh produce and cheaper deli items. Squeezed from above by delivery apps, below by discounters, and from the side by mini-supermarkets, the Convenience Store is slowly ceding its throne as the King of Retail.

The stagnation in customer traffic is not a temporary cyclical downturn, but the result of an irreversible structural transformation of Japanese society. The peak-out in numbers is driven by compound factors: Work Style Reform reducing overtime, the generalization of dual-income households, and the technological entrenchment of telework. It is legally and culturally impossible for consumers to revert to a lifestyle of exhausting overtime and late-night solitary meals. With Millennials and Gen Z prioritizing work-life balance, the decline in demand based on the legacy business model—harvesting demand from time-poor workers at premium prices—is a permanent trend.

## The Structural Dilemma of 7-Eleven

Even 7-Eleven — the absolute monarch that has led the convenience store industry — cannot escape the center of this structural crisis. The company that built the prototype of the Japanese CVS model, and that consistently pioneered the innovation defining the industry, embodied the very capacity for adaptation to change. Today, it still stands unrivalled at the top of the industry, operating roughly 21,000 stores domestically — far ahead of FamilyMart's approximately 16,000 and Lawson's approximately 14,000. In Average Daily Sales (ADS), it has long commanded a premium that left rival chains in the dust.

Yet this very source of dominance — the historical gap in ADS versus competitors — has been narrowing quietly (Exhibit 10) and continuously over the long term. In 2000, 7-Eleven's ADS averaged ¥675,000, against a competitor average of ¥465,000, creating a ¥210,000 gap (Exhibit XX: Average Daily Sales trends of major CVS chains). By 2024, 7-Eleven's ADS had risen only to ¥692,000 — a modest 2.5% advance over 24 years — while the competitor average caught up aggressively to ¥576,000, compressing the gap to ¥116,000, roughly 45% lower than in 2000. This is not a cyclical trend: it is a structural convergence playing out over a quarter-century.

For a CVS operator rooted in a franchise business model, this compression of the ADS gap is a matter of life and death — it directly threatens the retention of franchise owners, the very lifeline of the network. In a model where headquarters and franchisees share profits, a high ADS is the sole justification for demanding a high royalty rate. 7-Eleven's charge system employs a sliding scale under which the headquarters' share rises in tiers as gross profit climbs, imposing an extraordinarily high marginal charge rate of 76% on any monthly gross profit exceeding ¥5.5 million. Owners of standard stores have long tolerated an effective headquarters charge rate of 62.7% — only because of the overwhelmingly high ADS and brand power that dwarfed other chains.

As the convenience store format itself stands at a historic crossroads, the industry's reigning king is being driven into an unprecedented internal crisis by the fierce pursuit of competitors. The macro headwind of traffic deceleration, driven by consumer lifestyle change, is colliding simultaneously with the microstructural flaw of franchisee profits being squeezed by a high-cost franchise package. The king's business model is approaching its breaking point, and without a fundamental review of the charge system and a redefinition of the format, it will be difficult to avoid a quiet deterioration of the franchise network.

This deterioration, however, is also the most powerful catalyst for format redesign. It is precisely when internal limits are laid bare that the transformation to realign with the external environment begins. The following section sets out the three strategic shifts that the CVS sector must execute to survive.

## The Paradox: Losing Format, Contested Asset

If the diagnosis so far is correct, the following fact should be inexplicable. Into a format that is structurally saturated, whose real sales have been flat for a decade, and whose core customer is going demographically extinct, some of Japan's most prominent capital has lined up to bid. Itochu, the general trading house, took FamilyMart fully private for c. ¥580bn (US\$3.7bn) in 2020; Mitsubishi and KDDI delisted Lawson for c. ¥500bn (US\$3.2bn) in 2024; and the SoftBank coalition is now in talks to invest up to ¥300bn (US\$1.9bn) in Seven (2026, unconfirmed). A combined ¥1.4tn (c. US\$9bn) — not the sort of money one pays for a declining format. And the buyers are not retail peers but outsiders: trading houses, telecom, payments (Exhibit 11). There is only one answer to this contradiction. They are not buying format growth. They are buying the network. The next section breaks it into four parts.

## The Network Is the Prize: The Four Faces of the Network

The first face: the supply chain — "the profit is downstream." Measuring Itochu's FamilyMart-related earnings by the retail entity alone misreads the structure. Net profit at the 8th Company, the division housing FamilyMart, was ¥65.1bn (US \$420mn, up 82% from ¥35.8bn) — modest next to the Metals division's ¥226.1bn (US\$1.4bn). But for a trading house, the value of a convenience chain sits upstream of that "visible profit": the merchandise flows running through group wholesalers Itochu Shokuhin and Nippon Access, control of private-brand development, and above all the defensive imperative — in an era where power has shifted to the consumer, whoever fails to hold the downstream tap risks being disintermediated across the entire chain. Itochu itself calls this structure "the profit is downstream." Mitsubishi×Lawson replicates the same design through Mitsubishi Shokuhin. For a trading house, a convenience chain is not a retail business — it is the anchor of the entire value chain.

The second face: telecom-payments — the store network as a traffic engine. For a carrier, the convenience store is the physical layer of churn defense and purchase-data capture. The logic is already proven. After taking joint control of Lawson, KDDI relaunched its subscriber perk program au Smart Pass as "Ponta Pass" (October 2024), wiring its member base directly into Lawson — enrollment reached 1.6x pre-relaunch levels, app MAU 1.4x, coupon usage 3x, measurably lifting Lawson's daily sales and traffic. Connect a carrier's member base to a store network and traffic flows in both directions — that is now a quantified fact. The SoftBank coalition's picture at Seven is the same design at greater scale: 74mn registered PayPay users × Japan's largest network of 22,000 stores. The groundwork is laid — SMCC and PayPay signed a comprehensive alliance (May 2025), and V-point/PayPay-point interchange went live (March 2026); the reported investment talks are the final step of fixing these existing connections in place with capital. But this picture cannot be painted on a blank canvas. Seven carries its own payments legacy — the e-money nanaco (issued since 2007) and Seven Mile — and faces a design problem absent from both precedents: how to integrate an existing economy of tens of millions of members with the PayPay/V-point economy. Running parallel point schemes is an operating cost; forced migration is a churn risk. Lawson's comparatively smooth connection with Ponta — which was born a coalition point program — started from very different initial conditions.

The third face: finance — the ATM as the finished article. The most complete monetization of a convenience network sits, in fact, outside the register. Seven Bank's ATM business earns roughly 90% of its revenue from acceptance fees, runs an ROE of c. 14% — nearly double the megabanks — and by one estimate generates roughly 40x the profit per square meter of a Seven-Eleven store. The one tsubo in the corner outearns the store around it. Some 27,000 machines, 99.98% uptime, roughly 2.3 million users a day: this is the standing answer to the question of what happens when financial functions are layered onto a high-frequency physical network. It is why Itochu took a 16.35% stake in Seven Bank and is rolling ATMs into FamilyMart — and why SMCC has a seat in the SoftBank coalition: to replicate and extend the finished article.

The fourth face: data × retail media — selling the screen, not the shelf. Convenience-store purchase data is Japan's highest-frequency, highest-density first-party data, and cookie deprecation has raised its scarcity value. Monetization has begun. FamilyMart's signage network "FamilyMartVision" completed installation across 10,000 stores nationwide in March 2024, carries 300-plus advertisers, and both operating entities — Gate One (Itochu×FamilyMart) and Data One (targeting ads, with Docomo) — are already profitable. FamilyMart targets ¥10bn (US\$64mn) in retail-media operating profit within four years — roughly a tenth of its current operating profit. Japan's retail-media market is forecast to double from ¥606.6bn (US\$3.9bn) in 2025 to ¥1.3tn (US\$8.4bn) by 2029: a storefront that 15 million people walk through every day is being redefined as a medium.

The four faces are not independent. The supply chain feeds private brands and logistics; payments supply the ID; finance supplies a reason to visit; media supplies the exit for the data — each reinforces the others. However much shelf share the drugstores take, no other physical network in Japan combines all four. A losing format; a contested network (Exhibit 12).

## AI Meets the Shop Floor: From Concept to Chain-Wide – Three Layers

And it is AI that will ultimately determine the value of this network. "AI × convenience store," however, is not a monolith. Sorted by implementation maturity into three layers, it becomes clear where the substance is — and where the concept ends. (Exhibit 13

Layer 1: Core-system AI — proven, and unglamorous. This is the only layer with confirmed profit contribution. Lawson's AI ordering system "AI.CO" completed chain-wide implementation in July 2024, recommending order volumes and markdown timing from weather, inventory and cross-product sales linkages. The results are in the numbers — average daily sales per store reached ¥601,000 (c. US\$3,800), a record level, same-store gross profit is said to have been lifted by c. ¥7bn (US\$45mn) a year, and the company targets a 30% reduction in store operating hours by FY2/2031. Nothing flashy — the AI that works lives inside ordering and markdowns.

Layer 2: Physical AI — scaling, with the store network as the training data. For physical work, one company's evolution path summarizes the industry's current position. Telexistence — a robotics company that automates store restocking via teleoperation technology — has beverage-stocking robots deployed across all three major chains: its current-generation TX Ghost works Seven-Eleven stores in Tokyo (trial operation since September 2025) and two Lawson stores at Takanawa Gateway City, after its predecessor spent three and a half years restocking FamilyMart backyards at hundreds of stores, running 24 hours a day, shelving on the order of 1,000 bottles per store per day and replacing one to three hours of human work — with AI executing over 98% of movements autonomously and remote operators intervening only on failure. That fleet has produced the asset that matters: more than 30,000 hours of real-store teleoperation data, the raw material for training robots to act. The trajectory it feeds is explicit. In September 2025, Seven and Telexistence announced joint development of "Astra," a humanoid running a VLA (vision-language-action) foundation model, with store deployment targeted for 2029 — and the stated core of the partnership fits in one line: real-world data from Seven's 20,000+ stores will be used to train the AI. The freshest proof point arrived in June 2026: as the only Japanese company selected for the AWS–NVIDIA–MassRobotics Physical AI Fellowship, Telexistence published a demonstration of a humanoid autonomously bagging items at a convenience-store register — PET bottle, onigiri, barcode scan, bag — on a single VLA policy, with zero human intervention, built in collaboration with NVIDIA's DreamZero world model. The company is candid that speed, smoothness and generalization remain unsolved; the point is the direction of travel. The store network, in other words, is not merely a destination for AI — it is Japan's largest training-data engine for physical AI. The SoftBank coalition's reported concepts — demand forecasting from base-station foot-traffic data, robotics applications with Yaskawa Electric — only make sense placed in this context.

Layer 3: Full automation — the cautionary tale. Precedents also teach us where the ceiling sits. In January 2026, Amazon announced the closure of all 72 Amazon Go and Amazon Fresh stores — the world's best AI company could not make checkout-free retail work at deployable economics. China's Bingo Box shrank from a "5,000 stores in a year" vision to a peak of 400. The lesson is unambiguous: fully automating the customer experience does not pay. Selectively replacing labor does. Japan's approach — ordering AI, stocking robots, avatar service — is deliberately labor-saving and assistive, not because the technology lags, but because the problem was framed correctly. And that problem is the labor shortage: 61% of franchisees report understaffing, the workforce leans heavily on international students capped at 28 hours a week, and convenience stores sit outside the Specified Skilled Worker visa program. Carrying Japan's most acute labor problem and a 55,000-store scale fit for repeatable experiments, this industry is the country's largest testing environment for whether AI can rewrite labor in the physical world.

The convenience store — once the undisputed growth engine of domestic retail — is now being tested for a second reason to exist: as Japan's AI testbed. What separates winners from losers is whether Layer 2's concepts can be carried through with Layer 1's discipline — chain-wide penetration of core systems. ## What this means for SoftBank Group: the enhancement of the Physical AI stack

As indicated in SoftBank, Arm: From GenAI to Agentic AI; Initiating with Outperform Ratings, SoftBank is a single investment holding company to own the entire AI stack that each generation runs on (Exhibit 14-Exhibit 15). SoftBank has deliberately executed towards this vision. Step back from the shop floor, and the Seven investment stops looking like a retail story at all. Masayoshi Son has spent two years assembling a vertically integrated Physical AI stack with startling speed and public conviction — "SoftBank's next frontier is Physical AI," as he put it when announcing the \$5.4n acquisition of ABB's robotics business (October 2025), before elevating ASI and Physical AI to the twin pillars of his new 30-year vision at the June 2026 AGM, where he noted that "in one factory, robots have already begun mass-producing robots." Map the stack and the design is unmistakable: the brain (OpenAI & Skild AI's robot foundation model), the body (ABB, closing expected in mid-to-late 2026; the Yaskawa MOU pairing SoftBank's VLM with Yaskawa's VLA-driven MOTOMAN NEXT), the compute (Arm, Graphcore and Ampere, spanning from cloud to edge), and the network (AI-RAN/AITRAS, commercializing on SoftBank's own grid from 2026, with NVIDIA claiming c. \$5 of AI inference revenue per \$1 of capex). What the stack conspicuously lacks its bottom layer: a place where all of this meets the physical world at scale, every day, and gets graded.

That is what 22,000 convenience stores are. Three functions, in ascending order of strategic value. First, a deployment field — edge inference for ordering and foot-traffic forecasting, robotics for stocking and cleaning, each store a repeatable unit of experiment. Second, a data engine — the Seven×Telexistence "Astra" program states it plainly: real-world data from 20,000-plus stores to train a VLA humanoid for 2029. It is worth noting that SoftBank already sits on both sides of that arrangement, having participated in Telexistence's ¥23bn Series B alongside Foxconn in 2023 — the coalition's equity stake in Seven would close the loop between the robot developer it funds and the environment that trains it. Third, and most valuable to a holding company whose equity story is a bet that AI rewrites every industry: falsifiable proof. SoftBank's evidence today is concentrated upstream — Arm's IP and silicons, OpenAI's models, Stargate's data centers. A beverage shelf restocked by a robot, an order book written by a demand model, a franchise P&L with measurably fewer labor hours: these are the most everyday, most auditable receipts the AI thesis could produce. For a stock that trades on the credibility of a vision, receipts matter.

## Who Executes Wins: Three Camps, Three Survival Strategies

Grading execution starts from an honest premise: the three camps are not playing under the same conditions.

FamilyMart (100% Itochu) has been running the longest. Six years on from full consolidation, it leads implementation of network monetization: vertical integration of the supply chain, a profitable retail-media business in Data One (the data partnership with Docomo), and signage across 10,000 stores.

Lawson (50/50 Mitsubishi×KDDI) is deepest in core-system AI. It combines the proven "AI that works" — chain-wide AI.CO — with the quantified traffic engine of Ponta Pass. The lesson of two years of joint management — experimental stores stopped at four, while core-system reform reached every store and moved profit — is the implementation roadmap for the entire industry.

Its scale — 22,000 stores, PayPay's 74mn users — and its range — the 2029 humanoid program with Telexistence — are the greatest of the three. But the investment remains at the discussion stage; the integration of nanaco and Seven Mile poses a design problem unique to this deal; and above all, this is a restart from the digital defeats that began with 7pay. Escaping an exhausted, old-school convenience model by inviting in outside capital — the latecomer has been handed the largest canvas, and the outcome is still blank.

What to monitor collapses to a single point: whether each camp's AI implementation stops at the showcase (experimental stores, demos) or reaches chain-wide penetration of core systems (ordering, inventory, markdowns, labor scheduling). As Lawson's AI.CO demonstrated, only the latter reaches shareholder value. The survival of what was once domestic retail's growth engine rests not on prolonging the format, but on completing this experiment.

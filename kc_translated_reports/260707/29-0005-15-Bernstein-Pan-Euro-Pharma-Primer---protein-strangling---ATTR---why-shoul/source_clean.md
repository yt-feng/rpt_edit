## INTRODUCTION TO ATTR

"Amyloidosis" is a broad term that refers to the build up of abnormal clumps of proteins (amyloids/fibrils) in various tissues of the body, thereby causing a wide range of potentially life threatening health problems. Amyloids can accumulate due to prolonged inflammation or genetic mutations that lead to the misfolding of diseased proteins thereby causing their aggregation and clumping. In all, there are estimated to be over 36 types of amyloidosis, specific to the type of protein that misfolds or the predisposing gene.

We are focusing specifically on transthyretin amyloidosis, or ATTR. This particular condition involves the misfolding of the transthyretin protein (TTR), which is normally synthesized in the liver and is involved in the transportation of thyroid proteins and vitamin A (essential for metabolism and vision) in the body.

The complex structure of the TTR protein lends itself to being easily misfolded. Therefore ATTR is the second most common cause of amyloidosis, making up around a third of all cases.

Scientists classify ATTR into two subtypes:

\- In hereditary ATTR (hATTR), 1 of over 120 gene variants that lead to protein misfolding is inherited from a biological parent. Those of African descent are known to carry a gene change predisposing to hATTR.

\- In wild-type ATTR (wATTR), the TTR protein starts misfolding spontaneously, either due to an acquired mutation or simply due to age related changes in cell structures responsible for healthy protein formation. As such, wATTR is more common over the age of 65.

Regardless of the type, the disease process ends up being similar, with the amyloid deposits accumulating everywhere in the body (Exhibit 7).

## ATTR - polyneuropathy (ATTR-PN)

In this category, neurological symptoms prevail, with amyloid deposits predominantly affecting the nervous system and joints. In joints the amyloids cause pain, in peripheral nerves they can cause both pain and dysfunction of the nerves leading to muscle weakness. When affecting more centrally located nerves, the amyloids can cause “autonomic” symptoms, which lead to overall frailty and imbalance in the body’s nervous control (Exhibit 8).

The deposits of amyloid on the nerves are usually permanent, and their damage long-lasting. Hence, after diagnosis it's often a race against time to prevent irreversible symptomatic decline.

Polyneuropathy symptoms can be deeply unpleasant, and affect the quality of life of patients. Nevertheless, it is widely accepted by physicians that ATTR-PN is less likely to lead to mortality in patients than the cardiac equivalent of ATTR disease, as there is usually no key organ system involvement.

## ATTR- cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM)

In this category amyloid deposits build up in heart tissue which cause debilitating symptoms. The amyloid fibrils can be toxic to the cardiac tissues. But more commonly the deposits just make it more difficult for the heart to perform its pumping action as the chambers of the heart become blocked with amyloid plaques. This leads to the muscles and walls of the heart enlarging to compensate (dilated cardiomyopathy), further worsening the blockage (Exhibit 9). Ultimately heart failure results, which leads to a well documented storm of symptoms including breathlessness and fluid congestion in the lungs as the heart is unable to perform its circulatory function. ATTR-CM can be more life-threatening than some forms of cancer.

Heart failure can also be caused by a variety of other health issues. Nevertheless, in ATTR-CM the precipitous decline in heart function accelerated by the constantly accumulating amyloid deposits often leads to rapid health deterioration and death. ATTR-CM is widely accepted as the more dangerous category of ATTR. As such, it dominates R&D into ATTR treatments, and is where, in our view, the majority of the potential market for innovative drug treatments exists in ATTR.

## Diagnostics & epidemiology - the story so far

ATTR-CM and ATTR-PN are not always distinct diseases, and generally refer to the constellation of symptoms that predominate or appear initially.

This is especially the case as one category can often progress to the other category, and in some cases the diseases can manifest as a “mixed” phenotype.

As such, diagnostic criteria can be varied and discretionary. Recent research however, confirms that using both narrow and broad definitions, ATTR-CM is the category that is more common by far (Exhibit 10).

When it comes to diagnosing ATTR more broadly, it has historically been difficult to ascertain a definitive diagnosis.

In ATTR-PN, clinical symptoms are relied upon, hence ATTR-PN can often end up being a diagnosis of exclusion.

## HOW IS ATTR TREATED? - CONVENTIONAL (SYMPTOM RELIEF ONLY)

ATTR is generally considered incurable. In the past, treatments have been largely supportive, and revolved around managing the consequences of amyloid deposition once it has occurred. These supportive treatments can include:

\- Heart failure medications like diuretics that ease the burden on the heart through the removal of excess fluid via urine

\- anti-coagulants and anti-arrhythmics that respectively prevent blood clots and abnormal heart rhythms that result from amyloid deposits

\- In some hereditary cases, liver transplantation is offered as the liver is where the faulty TTR protein is misfolded, although this is no longer the standard of care given mixed historical results and cost

Some off-patent medications are used in ATTR with purported benefits such as doxycycline with tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDC). TUDC is a bile acid that protects membranes in heart muscle cells, negating the toxic effects of amyloid fibrils. Doxycycline on the other hand is a widely used antibiotic that is shown to break down larger clumps of TTR in laboratory cell cultures. Although the combination would be a cheap way to target ATTR, phase II studies thus far have been lacklustre, and so the combination remains investigational.

Diflusinal is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication (NSAID), that uniquely binds to the thyroxine receptors on the TTR protein, slowing down folding. A phase III trial in polyneuropathy only has been conducted demonstrating reduced progression in neurological impairment. Nevertheless, the trial was not powered for a high level of statistical significance, and the drug remains a non-standard of care treatment.

## Disease modifying ATTR treatments

More recently, the high unmet clinical need in ATTR has prompted a series of disease modifying treatments to be developed, that in our view have transformed survival and care in ATTR. These treatments are broadly centered around three pillars of disease modification.

## 1/Silencers

These medications inhibit (“silence”) the production of diseased TTR proteins

## 2/Stabilisers

These medications minimise misfolding, and thereby limit the accumulation and aggregation of amyloid fibrils into plaques

## 3/Depleters

These medications are designed to physically remove amyloid deposits that have already been deposited

These three pillars of treatment act on every available target in the ATTR disease process (Exhibit 13) Between them they support a lucrative and, in our view, strongly growing market for ATTR therapy that should be worth upwards of \$30Bn by 2036e.

## WHAT ARE STABILISERS?

Stabilisers in ATTR are essentially medications which assist in maintaining the form of TTR proteins.

Diseased TTR proteins don't have to misfold immediately. But they ultimately always do, and the presence of other misfolded TTR proteins prompts other proteins to misfold in a vicious cycle. Stabilisers help to slow down the misfolding process (Exhibit 14).

To achieve this, stabilisers bind to functional forms of the TTR protein (which are composed of four protein segments - known as tetramers).

Diseased TTR forms break down into their constituent parts, eventually then folding into the diseased fibril shape characteristic of amyloid deposits. By binding to the greater structure and “holding it together”, stabilizers prevent the progression of TTR amyloid disease.

## WHAT ARE SILENCERS?

The second of the three disease modifying pillars, involves stopping the production of diseased TTR proteins that might go on to misfold into amyloids. An already mentioned example of this kind of treatment is to replace the liver entirely where these proteins are made, thereby “silencing” the production of the faulty proteins. With modern gene therapies however, scientists have developed the ability to silence only the genes that produce the faulty proteins, avoiding the need for more invasive measures like surgery. Several key gene therapy approaches have emerged:

Small interfering RNA (siRNA), are small molecules that actually mimic the messenger molecules (mRNA) just described within cells, and actively interfere in the protein expression pathways leading to TTR production. This active sabotage of these pathways again prevents the cells from producing the diseased TTR proteins. Examples of this technology include Onpattro and Amvuttra (Alnylam (Pickering;O)), launched in 2018 and 2022 respectively, with 2025 sales of \$172m and \$2.3Bn respectively.

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASO), are molecules that bind to the genetic code (mRNA) that within cells leads to production of the TTR protein. This binding means the code (mRNA) can no longer produce the protein and so circulating concentration of TTR protein is reduced. Examples of this technology include Tegsedi (Ionis (MP)), and Wainua (AstraZeneca (O)/Ionis (MP)).

CRISPR is a widely used gene editing technique, designed to offer a potentially permanent fix to edit the gene responsible for the production of TTR proteins.

Considering that all of these are gene therapies are effective (Exhibit 17), silencers command high price premia. But these drugs carry stronger side effect profiles in relative terms to conventional therapies such as NSAIDs. In addition, approvals for silencers (until 2025) have, in the majority, been for ATTR-PN only, which is a significantly smaller patient subgroup. Hence silencers are an emerging market opportunity as thus far their approvals have largely been ex-US.

## SIRNA DEEP DIVE

Revusiran, a siRNA developed by Alnylam (Pickering;O), was the first commercial attempt at a silencer developed for the ATTR market. But it failed its phase III ENDEAVOUR program and was discontinued in 2016.

Onpattro (patisiran), was another siRNA developed by Alnylam, requiring three weekly intravenous infusion targeted for ATTR-PN.

In the phase III study APOLLO program in 2017, Onpattro was compared against placebo over 18 months, with the modified neuropathy impairment score +7 (mNIS+7) used to assess symptom severity. In the intervention group there was a 34 score point reduction (28.1, 29.9 p<0.05) in the mNIS+7 score, which was statistically significant (unlikely to be down to chance)- Exhibit 18.

With a favorable safety profile, Onpattro became a pioneering silencer treatment in ATTR-PN. However, despite what seems like a large absolute point reduction in the mNIS+7 score, the overall effect was still modest in our view given the score is out of 340. This may explain its peak sales of just \$557m in 2022. An ATTR-CM phase III trial (APOLLO-B) program was conducted in 2022, and also met statistical significance. But the effect size was even more modest, and Onpattro never achieved FDA approval in ATTR-CM as a result.

Although loss of exclusivity for Onpattro is not expected until the early 2030s, the product has since been superseded by Alnylam's own successor siRNA - Amvuttra.

Amvuttra (vutrisiran), is a siRNA with a largely similar mechanism of action to Onpattro, but whose greater stability permits once quarterly administration through a much less invasive subcutaneous needle (under the skin). A need to supplement vitamin A was noted, but aside from this the drug had a reassuring safety profile, and so was approved worldwide for ATTR-PN in the following year.

The delivery of comparable clinical efficacy with significantly improved convenience has meant that Amvuttra has cannibalized Onpattro sales in recent years. Last year Amvuttra achieved sales of \$2.3Bn across all segments.

## ASO DEEP DIVE

Tegsedi (inotersen) developed by Ionis pharmaceuticals (Will Pickering, MP), was the first ASO silencer technology deployed in ATTR through weekly subcutaneous injection, and had its phase III NEURO-TTR program read out in 2018. It tested the drug against placebo over a similar timescale of 18 months. The trial met its primary endpoint of reduction in mNIS+7 score of 19.7 compared to placebo (p<0.001) (Exhibit 24).

However, there were significant adverse events with low platelet count and kidney disease (some fatal) recorded and several boxed warnings and liver monitoring requirements appear on this drugs package insert.

Tegsedi was hence approved in ATTR-PN. However given the impending arrival of competitors, a cardiomyopathy clinical program was never pursued, and the drug was discontinued in 2024-25 due to lackluster sales.

## DEPLETERS - WHAT ARE THEY?

The third, and most recent emerging class of disease modifying treatments in ATTR are the depleter class. These medications aim to remove the amyloid plaques via faeces that build up in the body. Thus far, two key agents are in development, and both have similar mechanisms of action.

Coramitug (PRX004) was invented by Prothena and licensed to Novo Nordisk (U), and is a humanized monoclonal antibody designed to target misfolded/non-native TTR proteins. It binds to both the soluble form of misfolded TTR proteins, and those already deposited as plaques in the insoluble fibril form. Coramitug is administered through intravenous injection (into the vein) once a month.

This drug prevents misfolded proteins from adding to plaques, and coramitug promotes the clearance of existing plaques as the body's own immune system cells (macro-phages) recognise the antibodies bound to the plaques and then destroys them (Exhibit 29).

Therefore it promotes only the immune removal/degradation of existing plaques rather than diminishing the concentration of circulating TTR protein. Cliramitug is also administered intravenously (into the vein) once a month.

In phase II, cliramitug demonstrated comparable results to coramitug in terms of key biomarker reduction for heart failure.

## 1/ Expanding the market - AI and improving diagnosis

AstraZeneca has long been developing a quantitative continuous scoring (QCS) system algorithm to improve diagnosis of cancerous tissue. By leveraging AI, QCS analyzes have digitized images of immunohistochemically stained tissue samples to provide detailed, quantitative assessments of biomarker expression (Exhibit 32).

Unlike traditional methods that often rely on subjective ('human eye') evaluation, QCS offers precise, objective measurements not only of biomarker presence but also of their intensity and exact localization within subcellular compartments such as the membrane, cytosol, and nucleus.

This comprehensive computational analysis deepens the understanding of tumor biology, enabling more accurate patient selection and personalized treatment strategies for patients.

We believe that this technology can equally be applied to biopsies looking for amyloid disease, increasing diagnosis of patients and therefore the addressable market.

Furthermore, AstraZeneca has also announced a partnership with Roche (O) to deploy its InVision Echocardiography technology for the diagnosis of cardiac amyloid disease. Echocardiography uses sound waves to detect the normal functioning of the heart. But it can be difficult for even the most astute sonographer to detect the faint deposits of protein that characterize amyloid protein disease of the heart.

With AI, it is anticipated that these images will be better stratified, leading to previous false negatives being correctly diagnosed. We believe that AstraZeneca is using AI to materially expand the size of the patient population through better diagnosis. Therefore, even though there are competitors to Wainua and Cliramitug, we believe that market expansion is much more likely than material share shifts.

## 2/ Market expansion and combination therapy

Our channel checks indicate that ATTR remains a disease with no universal treatment guidelines. Down to the individual hospital, treatments can be quite different from each other. Treatments are also often sequential, which means that one is tried, and if it does not work, physicians may move on to another.

We believe that AstraZeneca is challenging this status quo, through its subgroup analysis in the CardioTTRansform trial in patients already on tafamidis (stabiliser). The company is seeking to answer a key question that many scientists have long asked - is a combination of stabiliser and silencer at the same time more effective, with no increase in toxicity, than sequential therapy?

In the event that this subgroup analysis demonstrates superior results, we believe that the standard of care may shift towards this combination therapy of tafamidis and Wainua. This would drive upside to our AZN estimates in ATTR. This would be differentiated as in Amvuttra HELIOS-B phase 3 trial this subgroup wasn't studied. It's also not being studied in the nucesiran phase 3 trial.

## 3/ Limited patient risk

As with any drug, loss of exclusivity can drive aggressive loss of market share to cheaper alternatives. For AZN's cliramitug, the loss of exclusivity lies well beyond our forecast period which ends in 2036. But for Wainua however, the method of use and composition of matter patents are set to expire in 2036. Nevertheless, from channel checks we have conviction that the silencer class is generally well protected from generic entry post 2036. This is largely because, as with any gene modifying therapy, the regulatory hurdles and manufacturing complexity make it economically challenging to produce a generic alternative, a situation that we don't see materially changing any time soon.

Across the broader ATTR market, the most notable loss of exclusivity is Vyndaquel (tafamadis) which is set to lose exclusivity towards 2029. Our implied TRx (total prescription) based US model with script data from IQVIA (reputable, independent data provider), demonstrates to us that the number of patients who are on tafamidis will likely remain stable/decline slightly in the presence of Attruby which in our view has a stronger evidence base. We believe that when high-priced oral (small molecule; easy to copycat) rare disease medications lose patent protection and much cheaper generics become available, the addressable volume opportunity can expand and the total number of patients on the medication does not decline.

To specify, on 27 $^{th}$ April 2026, the Vyndamax US patent was successfully defended, which extends loss of exclusivity well into the 2030s. Vyndaquel, which requires multiple daily administration as opposed to the once daily Vyndamax will still lose exclusivity in 2029. Despite this, Vyndamax sales make up the vast majority ( $>80\%$ ) of prescriptions for tafamidis in the US. Furthermore, our interviews with KOLs suggest that even if there is generic tafamidis on the market, physicians generally will continue to prescribe the leading branded silencer therapies (which will soon include Wainua) rather than feel the need to “cycle through” an off-patent stabiliser first.

Hence, although generic tafamidis will affect the overall ATTR market, we do not believe that this will be a headwind for AstraZeneca.

## THE BULL CASE - COMBINATION, COMBINATION, COMBINATION

In addition to our base case, we identify two upside scenarios for AstraZeneca:

\- Scenario A: if the tafamadis/Wainua subgroup in the CardioTTRansform trial meets statistical significance, we believe that this clinical data will give Wainua a greater competitive advantage than what our base case projects.

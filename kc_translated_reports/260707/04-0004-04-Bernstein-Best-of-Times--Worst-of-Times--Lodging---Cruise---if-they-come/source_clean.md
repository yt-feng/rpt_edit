# Best of Times, Worst of Times: Lodging & Cruise - if they come, will they build them?

"It was the best of times, it was the worst of times". The famous opening line of Charles Dickens' 'A Tale of Two Cities' captures a period of contradiction: the French aristocracy indulging in opulence and privilege, the peasantry experiencing poverty and oppression. While 2026 is thankfully not 18th century France, shifting class dynamics remain more topical than ever, from the US K-shaped economy to the squeezed Chinese middle class. Travel, particularly leisure, has been a key beneficiary of the K-shaped recovery, with spend increasingly concentrated among higher-income cohorts. The sector is also exhibiting its own internal K-shape: from January–May 2026, US hotel revenues grew 9.1% in the luxury segment while declining 2.7% in economy. Similar divergence is evident across cruise and airline markets. In cruise and aviation, supply is responding in line with market equilibrium theory, with high-end capacity expanding to meet demand. Hotel development, however, tells a different story. Despite strong demand signals, supply growth has been more pronounced in Upscale through Midscale than in Luxury. This raises a key question: are developers anticipating a different demand outlook, or is demand just one of several factors guiding decisions? Elevated financing, construction, and labor costs may be constraining the economics of luxury projects, offsetting revenue upside. Two implications follow. First, luxury hotels are likely to see continued tailwinds, given constrained supply relative to demand. Second, operators underexposed to luxury may increasingly turn to M&A to reposition, pointing to potential deal activity ahead.

The K shape is very easy to spot in travel. The K-shape is very evident across recent company reports. In 1Q26, Park Hyatt grew RevPAR 10.3% versus 2.2% at Hyatt Place (mainstream). In cruising, Viking (luxury) delivered 9.5% yield growth, while Norwegian (more mainstream) saw yields decline 0.3%. Delta similarly reported premium revenue growth at roughly twice the pace of total ticket revenue. These are all pre World-Cup, which are likely to supercharge the trend in the US - in the WC June 14, Luxury Chain RevPAR grew 17.4%.

Vertically integrated capital is flowing into luxury, while fragmented models lag. In sectors where operators control growth, investment is clearly targeting high-end demand. Luxury cruise capacity is growing at $>10\%$ in 2026, with the "yacht" segment up $24\%$ , well ahead of overall industry growth $(4\%)$ . Similarly, Air France's premium capacity has outpaced economy by $\sim 10\%$ since 2019. By contrast, where third parties drive development, the opposite holds: Upscale through Midscale continue to outgrow luxury footprints and pipeline data suggests this continues. This likely reflects structural constraints. Luxury hotels require prime locations, whereas mainstream hotels are more flexible and are expanding into new regional demand drivers such as US infrastructure. They are also far cheaper and faster to build, while luxury assets are capital-intensive, operationally complex, and involve multi-year development cycles. Developers prioritize returns and speed. A similar dynamic exists in restaurants, where capital favors \$500k Wingstop builds over \$8m Ruth's Chris, regardless of demand trends. The result should support Luxury Hotel RevPAR for years, while also driving M&A as a faster route to increase luxury exposure. Recent deals (Lefay, Standard, Graduate) reflect this shift.

## SPOTTING THE K-SHAPE IN TRAVEL IS EASY

Travel is one of the clearest examples of the K-shaped in consumer:

\- In hotels the relative position of a chain scale is a good predictor of how it has performed - the higher chain scales have persistently outperformed, led by the Luxury segment, which has persistently outgrown the wider market, and has been the leading chain scale on RevPAR performance every single month since March 2025. Relative to 2023 Luxury hotel revenues are up more than 30% in May, while Economy hotel revenues are down HSD, while Luxury RevPAR is up in the high teens vs Economy RevPAR is down MSD - despite much greater Luxury supply growth.

\- In cruise, we have seen a similar phenomenon - the premium Celebrity brand has seen pricing strengthen (up LDD vs April 2024) despite macroeconomic headwinds to international / high ticket leisure travel, while the mainstream Royal Caribbean has seen pricing fall.

## WHERE DO THE OTAS SIT ON THE K-SHAPE?

OTAs tend to serve the more price and convenience conscious leisure customer. As a result:

\- OTAs (notably Booking.com) are more exposed to lower end segments of the market - in Europe D-Edge has seen 49% of booking revenues for 3 star hotels come from Booking.com, but for 5 star hotels the Booking.com contribution is only 27%.

\- Prices through OTAs are, on average, lower than the wider market, and through direct and GDS channels, in part due to higher price competition, and also driven by greater exposure to lower end hotels.

As a result the OTAs exposure to the K-shape is relatively unfavorable, with little exposure to the best segments of the market (i.e. Luxury), but some upside from strong travel spend overall.

## DOES TRAVEL ADHERE TO THE MARKET EQUILIBRIUM THEORY?

The Law of Supply states that “All else being equal, as the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises; as the price falls, the quantity supplied falls.” We can see that force in Aviation and Cruise, where market participants are investing more in premium products.

\- Airlines have been increasing premium capacity well in excess of economy capacity (Exhibit 12, Exhibit 13) and expect to continue to do so with United expecting to grow its premium seat capacity 2 to 3 percentage points faster next year than its total capacity

\- In cruise, similarly, luxury supply will outpace premium and economy supply growth (Exhibit 14) to match the demand growth. Even within luxury cruise, the fastest growth is in the ultra-high end “yacht” segment (Exhibit 15), a K-shape within the top K.

\- However in the hotel sector, particularly in the US, supply growth is stronger in mainstream segments. The recent growth and pipeline is stronger in Upscale-Midscale segments than in Luxury. This trend is also seen in Asia (Exhibit 20) although in Europe, luxury growth leads (Exhibit 18). We investigate this phenomenon in the next section, but this looks to mean US Luxury RevPAR will have supply-demand tailwinds that will continue in the coming years.

## WHY IS SUPPLY NOT FOLLOWING DEMAND IN HOTELS?

We would set out the following reasons why Luxury hotel development is not matching the rampant demand environment:

1. The high end consumers is not the only game in town. Branded hotels in the US are majority business travel focused and therefore are also chasing the current infrastructure boom in the US, likely seen as a less cyclical trend to invest into.

2. Luxury hotels are low returning. Despite the strong ADR growth, Luxury Hotels such as Intercontinental (5.6%) and JW Marriott (3.4%) offer far lower returns than select-service brands, as the high ADR doesn't offset the high labor intensity. Growth tends to correlate with returns not with simply high prices (Exhibit 23). Notably, this is not so true in Cruise where Viking has a higher return on investment than mainstream operator. Luxury hotels can offset low operating returns through adding high returning residences or through property price appreciation, but this is less certain than a high operating return.

3. Luxury hotel development costs have risen faster than achievable room rates. Construction inflation, higher financing costs and increasingly demanding luxury specifications have pushed replacement costs sharply higher. While ADRs have grown strongly, in many markets they have not increased sufficiently to preserve historical development yields. As a result, investors may prefer lower-cost formats where capital can be deployed more efficiently and recovered more quickly.

4. Luxury Hotels are also slow to build. Luxury hotels also tie up capital for many years, with an average construction time more than double a mainstream hotel. In the US, essentially all the Midscale projects currently under construction will be completed by the end of 2027, but 25% of luxury projects will still have work ongoing (Exhibit 24); this slows down the ability to recycle that capital.

5. Luxury service models require scarce labour. The guest experience depends on high staffing ratios, specialist skills and personalised service. In many markets labour shortages and wage inflation have become significant constraints, making luxury assets more difficult to operate profitably than lower-service hotel formats.

6. Financing increasingly favors shorter-duration projects. Luxury hotels require large upfront capital commitments, lengthy development periods and often generate lower cash-on-cash returns than other hotel segments. In a higher interest rate environment, lenders and equity investors have become more selective, favoring projects with faster completion timelines and more visible returns.

7. Third party /fragmented capital seems to invest differently to integrated capital. We note the vertically integrated businesses (cruise and airlines) look set to invest behind a long term K-shaped trajectory. Those industries that employ a franchise model (hotels, restaurants) look to focus on more short term, quick to build and high ROI formats.

8. Luxury hotels can't simply be built, the product is about location, exclusivity and heritage. Whereas you can build a mainstream hotel at any freeway junction or airport terminal without too much concern, and a luxury cruise ship is a moving product that can seek out the best locations, luxury hotels need

\- Suitable sites are inherently scarce. Luxury demand is concentrated in a limited number of global gateway cities, resort destinations and iconic heritage locations. Unlike mainstream hotels, where supply can be added near highways, airports and suburban business districts, luxury hotels depend on unique locations that cannot be easily replicated. The best hotels in the world overlook Hong Kong harbor, Lake Como or are in $15^{\text{th}}$ Century Medici palaces in Florence. These don't come up every day, and therefore simply the desire or capital to build more luxury hotels won't simply make it so.

\- Heritage - whereas a new mainstream hotel can be best in breed simply by being new; luxury hotels tend to improve with age, with review scores improving as hotels get older (Exhibit 25).

\- Brand growth is intentionally constrained. Luxury brands derive value from curation and selectivity. Excessive expansion risks diluting brand positioning and reducing pricing power. As a result, the most prestigious brands often choose to grow slowly, even when demand conditions appear attractive. The top reviewed hotel brands tend to have a small number of locations. In Exhibit 27 we show the top reviewed brands- Sofitel Legends has just five locations, Alila 19, Regent 6, Waldorf Astoria 35. It is hard to develop new brands organically and over extending existing brands can undermine exclusivity, holding back total growth.

## WATCH OUT FOR M&A:

Taken together, the luxury hotel sector faces constraints that do not exist in most other travel categories. Returns are relatively modest, projects are capital intensive and slow to complete, financing is increasingly selective, suitable locations are scarce, and leading brands deliberately ration growth to preserve exclusivity. As a result, even in a period of exceptionally strong luxury demand, supply is unlikely to respond as quickly or as aggressively as investors might expect. This dynamic also suggests that M&A may become an increasingly important route to gaining/increasing luxury exposure. With organic development constrained by scarce sites, lengthy development timelines and brand curation, acquiring existing luxury assets, brands or management platforms may offer a faster and more certain path to participate in the sector's growth. For companies seeking to increase their luxury mix, consolidation may therefore prove a more effective strategy than attempting to build scale organically.

## Recent Examples include:

\- IHG's acquisition of Regent Hotels & Resorts (2018) – IHG acquired a majority stake in Regent, a highly prestigious luxury brand with a small global footprint, providing a platform to expand its presence at the very top end of the market.

\- IHG's acquisition of Six Senses (2019) – IHG acquired Six Senses for approximately \$300m, gaining one of the industry's most sought-after luxury and wellness brands rather than building a new luxury concept from scratch.

\- Hyatt's acquisition of Standard International (2024) – Hyatt acquired The Standard and Bunkhouse brands to accelerate its exposure to luxury and lifestyle demand through an established global platform rather than organic development.

\- Hyatt's acquisition of Playa Hotels & Resorts (2025) – Hyatt agreed to acquire Playa Hotels & Resorts in a transaction valued at approximately \$2.6bn, materially increasing its exposure to upscale and luxury resort markets across the Caribbean and Mexico

\- Hilton's acquisition of Graduate Hotels (2024) – Hilton paid \$210m for the Graduate brand, securing a differentiated lifestyle platform with more than 35 operating and pipeline hotels and significant room for future expansion.

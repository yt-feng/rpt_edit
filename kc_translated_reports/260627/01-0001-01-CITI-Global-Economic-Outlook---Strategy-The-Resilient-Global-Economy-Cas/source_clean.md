# The Resilient Global Economy—Cascading Supply Shocks


The global economy has remained resilient despite numerous headwinds. Whether measured by PMIs (Figure 1), the performance of equities or surprise indices, global activity metrics have remained broadly consistent with moderate growth not too far below trend. This is despite the propagation of cyclical negative supply shocks from various conflicts, protectionist trade policies and broader structural factors such as ageing populations and low productivity growth. Some of the resilience reflects stronger AI spending, support from fiscal policy and slightly accommodative monetary policy settings. However, a net-balance of the number of central banks Citi covers are starting to normalise monetary policy (Figure 2), while increased scrutiny over rising levels of public debt suggests fiscal policy cannot indefinitely support global growth to the same degree.

The most recent supply-side shock is (hopefully) ending. The performance of risk assets and energy markets in recent days portends a turning point in the Middle East conflict. The confidence of markets was validated with the Presidents of the United States and Iran signing a memorandum of understanding (MoU) aimed at ending the conflict. This document now provides 60 days to find solutions for the issues that have undermined stability in the region. These issues include a number of nuclear, financial and trade sanctions and regional security arrangements. History suggests these issues will not be solved quickly, but with both sides at least at the negotiating table, the health of the global economy no longer appears to be hostage to the price and availability of liquid fuels.

The Strait of Hormuz should re-open... Negotiations between previously warring parties will hopefully lead to sustained higher Strait of Hormuz (SoH) vessel flows after four months of substantially reduced crossing (Figure 3). Even if the recent agreement proves durable and the Strait remains open, we see Brent oil prices averaging roughly \$75/barrel in the second half of the year. This is \$15/barrel higher than we anticipated before the conflict, reflecting damages to production, pipeline, and refining capacity, as well as increased demand for oil to restock inventories. On the other hand, the MOU allows Iran to significantly increase its oil exports, which could be an important source of additional oil supply.

...but this may take some time. A political agreement to reopen the SoH (Figure 5) is not the same thing as restoring confidence that vessels can safely navigate it. First, mines that have been laid will need to be disarmed and removed. Minesweeping and independent verification of safe water could take months not weeks. Second, ship captains, vessel owners and insurers are likely to want to know details of how sea traffic will operate including designated shipping lanes, control systems and who will manage shipping traffic including what role the US Navy and Iran will have in vessel management. Nevertheless, these issues are solvable, meaning tanker and other sea traffic movement through the SoH will eventually return to normal. Symbolic examples of reopening such as news of an LNG tanker and a few very large crude carriers successfully transiting the Strait are, at the very least, an encouraging sign.

## Implications for Macroeconomic Projections

The US-Iran interim agreement has eased key headwinds for the global economy, with Brent oil prices declining towards \$75/barrel. That said, the jury is still out on whether this resolution proves lasting, with thorny issues remaining — particularly regarding Iran's nuclear program and Strait toll rights. The good news is that the resilience of recent years appears to be prevailing yet again. The global economy has absorbed a sustained period of oil prices above \$100/barrel, and we judge that global growth is tracking at 2½%, down from 2.9% prior to the conflict (Figure 6).

The impact on growth has shown differing severity across economies (Figure 7). Several highly exposed oil importers—including the Philippines, Vietnam, and Sweden—have seen the largest markdowns since the conflict erupted, tipping the scales at $\frac{3}{4}$ ppt or greater. We have marked down euro area growth somewhat further, as US tariffs continue to exert downward pressure on growth.

Cumulatively, growth in the euro area has been revised down by 1 ppt since February to 0.3%, but a large share of the markdown reflects volatility in Irish trade data. In contrast, the Big Four euro area economies have fared a notch better, with a downward revision of $\frac{1}{4}$ ppt since February.

Our forecast also highlights that the AI revolution has provided an important source of support for the global economy. This has been seen in buoyant AI-related investment in the United States. During the first quarter, it surged to well over \$400 billion (annual rate), from just \$300 billion last year. Surging AI demand has provided a boost to other countries as well, with a number of Asian economies — including Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore — registering upward revisions to their growth forecasts on the back of soaring tech exports.

Global headline inflation is tracking at 3.4% this year, slightly lower than last month as oil prices have eased (Figure 8), but still up almost a full percentage point since the conflict erupted. That said, we expect the pass-through of elevated oil prices to unwind and headline inflation to fall back to 2.7% next year.

At the country level, the mark-ups in our inflation forecasts have been significant and broad-based (Figure 9). The largest mark-ups have been among Asian economies — including the Philippines, Thailand, and Vietnam — which have all been revised up by 1½ ppt or more. A few European countries, mainly Italy and France, have also seen meaningful mark-ups. Notably, our projections for the US and the euro area are both up 1 ppt from February.

## El Niño: Hello to a Weather-Induced Supply Shock

While one supply shock is abating, another one may be around the corner. The Pacific Ocean's El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is a large driver of climate variability. Consisting of three phases; neutral, La Niña (cooler temperatures) and El Niño (warmer temperatures), the El Niño phase occurs every three to five years and lasts for roughly one year in duration. This weather system tends to develop in Q2, strengthen in Q3 with the full impact occurring in Q4 and subsequent Q1 of the following year. Monitoring of ENSO occurs via the sea surface temperature (SST) in the tropical Pacific Ocean. This can be done via comparing temperature differences against a baseline period (traditional method) or by comparing temperature differences against an average for the broader tropical region (relative index method). The latter method makes it clearer when Pacific Ocean temperature differences are a sign of ENSO activity, rather than part of the long-term warming trend. A strong El Niño can vary temperature and rainfall over the US, Mexico, South America, Australia, Oceania, India and Sri Lanka, South East Asia, southern and equatorial Africa (Figure 10).

[[KC_IMAGE_009]]

Source: Citi, noaa.gov

A super El Niño event is now expected. The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) threshold for an El Niño event is when tropical Pacific Ocean SST anomalies are above +0.5 degrees Celsius for five consecutive overlapping three-month periods. Secondary evidence for El Niño comes from wind pressure patterns as measured by the Southern Oscillation Index (SOI). SST anomalies meant the probability of an El Niño event increased from 20% across April, May and June to 61% in May, June and July. This assessment was issued by NOAA in April this year. Since then, the probability of an El Niño starting in Q2 and lasting until March 2027 has increased above 95% (Figure 11). Furthermore, NOAA estimates that the strength of the El Niño will increase to a very strong or super El Niño (> +2 degrees Celsius). By the end of the year, NOAA ascribes a 63% probability to this occurring. Further support for a 2026 super El Niño comes from the fact that the rate of warming this year is already the fastest since 1943. The Australian Bureau of Meteorology's seasonal model is forecasting a record peak warming greater than 3 degrees Celsius, above the previous post-1900 record of 2.65 degrees Celsius recorded in 1902.

## Potential economic impacts of El Niño

El Niño significantly impacts the global economy by disrupting agricultural output, damaging infrastructure, and reducing labor productivity. Historically, these cycles have resulted in substantial economic losses. The primary economic impacts are observed across the following sectors:

Agriculture and Food Security: El Niño alters rainfall patterns, which can lead to droughts in regions like Southeast Asia, India, and Australia, but excessive rainfall and the risk of flooding in the southern United States and parts of South America (Figure 10 again). This can negatively affect the supply of crops, increasing global food prices and consumer inflation.

■ Energy Generation and Utilities: Countries that use hydro-electric power, such as Brazil, Colombia, Venezuela, Zambia, and Zimbabwe are at risk of reduced water levels due to drier conditions and heatwaves. This places pressure on power grids, increasing reliance on more expensive fossil fuels. Power fluctuations and outages can occur, disrupting commercial and industrial activities.

■ Supply Chain and Trade Logistics: Extreme weather and reduced water levels (e.g., in the Panama Canal) force ships to carry lighter loads and restrict shipping capacity. Flooding can damage critical infrastructure like roads, warehouses, and ports, leading to logistical bottlenecks and delivery delays.

■ Mining and Commodities: Changes in rainfall patterns can hinder natural resource extraction. For example, excessive rain in Chile's mountains affects copper mining, while droughts in Indonesia impact water supply for nickel operations.

■ Fisheries: El Niño disrupts the upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich waters in the Pacific, depriving small fish species (like anchovies and sardines) of food. This can impact the commercial fishing industry and local coastal economies.

■ Labor Productivity and Health: Extreme heatwaves decrease outdoor labor productivity and increase heat-related illnesses, especially in tropical and developing nations.

## Potential policy Impacts of El Niño

Strong El Niño events directly influence fiscal policy, especially when associated with natural disasters. From the perspective of public finances, strong El Niños can represent a contingent liability. The immediate effects include extra social transfers including spending on income and housing support for affected households, subsidies for affected businesses and other disaster relief payments. Medium- and long-term liabilities include recovery and reconstruction costs for infrastructure alongside some ongoing tax and loan concessions for businesses. Countries with recurring strong El Niño events are more likely to require countercyclical buffers to absorb disaster related spending. This can include mitigation spending to decrease future fiscal liabilities. But contingency reserves are not budget-neutral and come with opportunity costs to other policy areas. However, public authorities facing recurring El Niño events are more likely to view these events as real supply shocks that influence allocative efficiency. The role of the public sector is to distribute that loss efficiently, protect vulnerable sectors and households, and preserve the productive capacity of the economy.

For central banks, the response is less clear. As an isolated event, El Niño can be a relative price shock for agricultural commodities. Energy provision can also be affected when this depends on hydro sources. The headline inflation impact can usually be ignored as a temporary influence. From a central banking perspective, the key question is not whether these prices rise, but whether El Niño-induced price shocks become embedded in underlying inflation and inflation expectations. If inflation is already above target and labour markets are tight, El Niño-related price pressures may reinforce concerns about inflation persistence. Central banks may place greater weight on preventing second-round effects. If inflation is near target and demand is weak, policymakers are more likely to accommodate the shock, allowing headline inflation to temporarily overshoot. Thus, identical weather shocks can have different policy implications depending on the broader macroeconomic environment. Emerging economies often confront stronger transmission from food prices into wages and expectations because food accounts for a larger share of household expenditure. Exchange rate depreciation and weaker agricultural exports can amplify these effects. In practice, El Niño is usually not a catalyst for a change in policy direction, but it can materially influence the pace and timing of policy normalization.

## Impact on Global Growth

Historically, there is a larger drag on growth than expected. The economic consequences of strong El Niño events are substantial and can be long-lasting. The most comprehensive cross-country study to date, Callahan and Mankin (2023), analysed GDP trajectories across 147 countries from 1960 to 2019 and found that El Niño's damage to economic output persists well beyond the weather itself, with effects detectable for five years or more in most affected economies and up to 14 years in the most severely impacted tropical nations. $^{1}$ The mechanism is not simply a temporary weather disruption, it is a structural scarring of agricultural systems, infrastructure, and institutional capacity that compounds over time. The dollar cost of that scarring is striking. Callahan and Mankin estimate the 1982–83 event caused approximately 4.1 trillion in cumulative lost output — the 1997–98 event cost an estimated 5.7 trillion, making it the most economically destructive El Niño on record; and the 2015–16 event cost approximately 3.9 trillion, equivalent to around 5% of 2016 global GDP. These figures carry wide confidence intervals and should be treated as order-of-magnitude estimates rather than precise forecasts. The actual losses are sensitive to modelling assumptions and the counterfactual growth path assumed. What the research does establish with confidence is that the damage is real, persistent, and concentrated heavily in tropical, agriculture-dependent economies, with developed markets experiencing a far smaller direct hit.

The current El-Niño event could have damaging effects for years to come. The 2026–27 El Niño now forming presents a potentially more severe shock than the 2023–24 episode, though significant uncertainty remains around its ultimate intensity (Figure 12). NOAA's June 2026 assessment puts the probability of a very strong or super El Niño — sea surface temperature anomalies exceeding +2.0°C — at 63% by year-end, with the Australian Bureau of Meteorology's seasonal model forecasting a record peak above +3.0°C that would surpass the previous post-1900 record of +2.65°C set in 1902 (Figure 13). Applying the Callahan and Mankin framework to the current global economy; approximately \$110 trillion in 2026 (IMF WEO April 2026) $^{2}$ . The damage profile is front-loaded. The sharpest output loss typically arrives in the event year itself, with the drag gradually diminishing over the following four years as economies recover, though never fully catching up to the pre-shock growth path. Under a base case strong event, cumulative five-year losses could reach \$3.0–3.5 trillion, equivalent to roughly 2.7–3.2% of current global GDP. Under a super El Niño scenario, that figure could rise to \$5.0–7.0 trillion, or 4.5–6.4% of 2026 global GDP, approaching the 1997–98 record in absolute dollar terms, though representing a smaller share of a much larger world economy. There is one structural amplifier that makes 2026–27 more dangerous than historical precedent alone suggests: global baseline ocean temperatures are already 0.3–0.4°C warmer than during the 2023–24 event due to long-term climate change, meaning the El Niño anomaly sits on top of an already elevated thermal baseline. Even a "strong" rather than "super" classification could therefore produce real-world disruptions to harvests, hydropower, shipping lanes, and sovereign balance sheets. The IMF's October 2026 World Economic Outlook will be the first major institutional attempt to formally quantify the impact, but the historical record offers little comfort. When El Niño arrives at this intensity, the economic cost is large, persistent, and unevenly distributed, falling hardest on the economies least equipped to absorb it.

# Region-Specific El Niño considerations

## Euro Area

As a large economy far away from the South Pacific Ocean, it would be easy to assume that El Niño-related weather disruption can have only negligible impact on the Europe's economic activity, price developments, and therefore on fiscal or monetary policy. By and large, that conclusion seems correct. In the current circumstances, however, we think it would be a mistake to ignore entirely the risk that disruptions to global food supply and prices reinforce a pre-existing “hawkish” bias of monetary policy in Europe, particularly for the ECB.

Food prices account for a relatively minor contribution to the European price indices (approximately 18 pp for the euro area) similar to other large advanced economies (circa 15 pp in the US) and much less than in economies such as China, or India for instance. This by itself ought to reduce the impact of weather disruption on consumer inflation and by, extension on domestic demand in other sectors. Further, Europe is more than self-sufficient for much of the food it consumes (cereals, meat, dairy products in particular) and only relies on net imports for a comparatively small share of products (e.g. oils). Even countries, such as the UK, which import very large proportion (more than a third) of their food requirements do so primarily from their European neighbours.

So why then would El Niño matter for the European economy? First is the observation that while Europe is self-sufficient in net terms, it also trades food actively with the rest of the world, meaning that domestic prices would not be insensitive to a global shortage, if one were to materialise. Further, the prospective El Niño event seems timed to have its peak impact approximately one year after the current energy price shock. This is relevant because European food prices exhibit sensitivity to energy prices (though gas more than oil) with a lag of up to one year. Even if energy prices were to normalise now, the impact of the recent spike on retail food prices may compound the El Niño effect towards the end of 2026 or early 2027.

Again, none of that would be relevant if central banks adopted the (reasonable) view that gyrations of food prices, in particular if triggered by identifiably temporary factors, can be ignored. The issue however is that it is by no means certain that central banks across Europe will follow that line of thinking. European central bankers seem to us generally more concerned about the risk of de-anchoring of inflation expectations than, say, their peers in the United States. That may make them more alert to a spike in food prices because research (for instance by the Bank of England) shows that consumer inflation expectations are more sensitive to realised food inflation than to any other prices.

It is also noteworthy that some central banks in Europe seem to take an asymmetric view on the risks caused by an event of the type of El Niño. In the communication of the Governing Council of the European Central Bank the statement that “extreme weather events, and the unfolding of climate and nature crises more broadly, could drive up food prices by more than expected” routinely appears. No symmetric risk on the downside is mentioned. This suggests to us that, at the margin ECB governors are less likely than others to ignore a rise in food prices, whatever the causes.

The prospect of a particularly severe El Niño event does not feature in our baseline expectations for monetary policy in Europe. But it may reinforce an existing asymmetry in monetary policy response to price shocks and push upwards the distribution of plausible policy rates at the 2027 horizon.

## Asia

El Niño tends to lead to intense heat and drought conditions in Southeast Asia (Vietnam, Indonesia, the Philippines), below normal monsoons in India, Pakistan and Sri Lanka, while providing a more mixed pattern for China, where we see drought conditions in the North, versus more intense rainfall/flooding in the South. El Niño also tends to lead to warmer weather in South Korea and Taiwan but is not as climatically significant than in South- and Southeast Asia.

The main macroeconomic concern for Asia is through loss of crop yield and agriculture output, leading to higher food inflation. This hurt countries with relatively high share of employment in this sector – the lower income Asian economies (Figure 14)– as well as impacting prices and feed into food inflation. Rice is a particularly important staple for the region, whose prices have historically surged during El Niño episodes due to both its moisture sensitivity and the drought vulnerability of the world’s major producers. In fact, rice prices have already been on a rising trend since 4Q25 (Thai rice benchmark up 22% YoY in June). Overall, this would make the Philippines stand out globally not only because of its relatively high food weight (highest among the Asian countries we cover), but also since rice alone accounts for a disproportionately large, 8.9% CPI weight. Of course, adverse weather events could also impact other cereals, vegetables and pulses, and could still significantly impact inflation of the more food-heavy CPI baskets (Figure 15).

However, we also note reduced agriculture output and food inflation volatility for some countries, which may help mitigate the damage of the upcoming El Niño shock. For example, a recent detailed analysis of India indicates a structural shift in the resilience of Indian agriculture to the vagaries of weather. Food grain production consistently declined YoY during deficient monsoons in the pre-FY2018 period but has rarely ever seen production declines over last 8 years, and ample govt buffer stock may have also helped mitigate cereal prices. Vietnam's rice production is also demonstrating greater resilience than others like Thailand, Indonesia or the Philippines, due to abundance of water management tools, rice fields that are of lower altitude vs. the water surface of the Mekong River, and shorter crop cycles.

Energy demand and supply can also be significantly impacted by El Niño. Energy demand for cooling could rise sharply, especially during intense heat across the summer months (not to mention health hazards), which is problematic for South and Southeast Asia where average temperatures are already very high to begin with. On the supply-side, Sri Lanka (about 35-40% of electricity generation) and Vietnam (\~30%), and to a lesser extent Pakistan (20%), are also more exposed to hydropower that can suffer from deficient rainfall. In the case of both Sri Lanka and Pakistan that are currently in IMF programs and are thus required to facilitate direct cost pass-through to electricity prices as part of its prerequisite to mitigate quasi-fiscal losses, this could further add to inflationary risks.


For now, most Asian central banks are not expected to respond significantly to supply-side inflationary shocks from El Niño. Perhaps countries with inflation already running well ahead of target and with high food-inflation sensitivity – particularly the Philippines and Pakistan – could be at risk of having central banks be more cautious, and keep monetary policy tight for longer, notwithstanding the recent sharp reversal of energy prices following US-Iran peace deal. But for the likes of India where RBI's inflation forecasts are coming from the mid-point of the upper half of the inflation band, our anticipated sharp reversal of energy prices provide significant downside risk to RBI's inflation such that, despite some lingering El Niño risks (which may impact vegetable and pulses), we still think the odds are that RBI can stand pat for longer $^{3}$ . Most other climate affected countries in ASEAN such as Thailand, Indonesia or Malaysia are coming from lower levels of inflation risks, and where it is more likely that central banks will look through supply-side shocks, and respond more through fiscal measures (targeted cash transfers) and administrative policies on rice supply. For the more advanced Asian economies of Korea and Taiwan, El Niño is unlikely to feature prominently in monetary policy decisions, where tightening stance is driven by other factors.

Lastly, we don't think China's economy and policies will be significantly affected by El Niño. China has long pursued policies of food security and will be buffered by strategic (wheat) reserves and more resilient water management infrastructure. We think any inflationary impulse from El Niño could be short-lived and offset by pork prices – the single most important driver of China's food CPI — which remains in a structural downcycle, weighed down by rising production efficiency and softening demand. If anything, El Niño will likely reinforce China's longer-term investment

rationale for climate-resilient infrastructure. The April Politburo meeting has already highlighted “six networks”, including water networks and urban underground pipelines.

## CEEMEA

## Central Europe

Central Europe is relatively unaffected by El Niño, with the central banks of the Czech Republic, Poland and Hungary generally regarding it as a minor factor in their forecasts. There could be a secondary impact via global food prices, however, El Niño is still not a major factor in our forecasts since both Poland, and to some extent Hungary, are producers of food and self-sufficient in terms of vegetables, fruits, meat and dairy.

To consider this in more detail, the Czech Republic provides a useful case. There is no meaningful transmission channel through hydropower generation or fisheries, with the main impact coming indirectly through higher global agricultural commodity prices. If this is the case, then eventually it would feed into domestic food inflation via higher global prices of grains, oilseeds, sugar, coffee, cocoa and animal feed. This leads to higher food prices across Europe and, because food accounts for roughly 15.3% of the Czech CPI basket, a strong impact on global food prices (we would worry if it rose by 10% or more) is to be expected. The passthrough into Czech food prices is about 30–50%, which means domestic food inflation could rise by 3–5% relative to the baseline. This implies an increase in headline CPI of around 0.5–0.8pp over a 1-2 year horizon. The CNB would likely view such a shock primarily as a supply-side factor and would normally look through it initially, but if core inflation remains elevated, wage growth becomes a risk. Given persisting energy-related risks, El Niño would represent an additional upside risk to the inflation outlook. Therefore, it could reinforce the case for keeping interest rates restrictive for longer rather than triggering a direct policy response.

On the fiscal side, the Czech Republic has a history of supporting households during inflationary episodes, mainly through targeted transfers and energy-related measures rather than food subsidies. Overall, El Niño is unlikely to become a major domestic macroeconomic shock for the Czech Republic, but it represents a meaningful upside risk to inflation through higher global food prices and could modestly complicate the disinflation process in 2027. The Czech Republic would not be a source of inflationary pressures for surrounding developed markets, but rather a recipient of a broader global food price shock.

## Gulf Cooperation Council

El Niño has limited direct bearing on the GCC. The Gulf economies are arid and hydrocarbon-based, with agriculture accounting for a marginal share of GDP, leaving little domestic exposure to the rainfall and temperature anomalies that weigh on economic activity in monsoon-dependent economies. The relevant transmission is indirect and, on balance, negligible: to the extent that El Niño firms non-fuel commodity prices, the effect reaches the Gulf as a net food importer but is largely offset at the consumer level by administered prices and subsidies on staples. What little remains is met by the budget rather than passed through CPI and is immaterial against the region's revenue base. On spillovers, the GCC does not transmit El Niño to developed markets; as a price-taker in global food, it absorbs the same commodity-price channel that DMs face directly.

## Africa

Egypt is nearing the end of harvesting what is expected to be a record wheat crop this year of an estimated 9.8–9.9m metric tonnes. Most of this is grown on land irrigated by the Nile, especially in the Nile Delta, which means that rainfall levels in Egypt, which are rarely high, are not such an issue for agricultural production. However, it raises other issues. Firstly, it means that rains in the Nile river basin are important, and in this respect at least El Niño periods are associated with heavy rains in Eastern Africa. Secondly, given that Egypt's arable land is limited, even with a record wheat harvest it will still have to import half of its wheat consumption as well as other food crops. Fortunately, rising global wheat and other food prices as a result of the Russia-Ukraine war have meant that the government has actively diversified its sources of food imports and improved storage facilities. Yet however proactive the government is, we still expect that rising global food prices will impact on inflation dynamics and slow the expected fall in the inflation rate in 2027.

Perhaps the most visible problem, however, is that El Niño periods can result in prolonged periods of very high temperatures – 40C plus – in Cairo and other major cities. This can increase evaporation levels from the Nile river and the Aswan High Dam, but critically can also lead to real strain on the electricity grid. Despite slow and ongoing efforts to boost renewable electricity production levels, rising electricity usage in the short term raises the need for gas imports. This could create a negative impact on fiscal and current account dynamics only a year after they have already been put under pressure from high global oil prices, and further strain on the country’s funding requirements.

In Nigeria, El Niño periods can cause a delayed start to the wet season in parts, with erratic rainfall patterns at times, especially in the north of the country $^{4}$ . The Sahel parts of West Africa are particularly vulnerable to drought into which some Nigerian northern states fall. However, given the erratic weather conditions experienced in recent years, notably flooding associated with La Niña periods and some limited issues with rainfall associated with the 2023 El Niño period, against the backdrop of ongoing security issues in key food producing areas in the Middle Belt, it is difficult to determine exactly how things will play out. Our basic assumption is that the domestic harvest will continue to be under some pressure as in recent years, which will keep domestic food price inflation elevated. We are also assuming that the government will raise the VAT rate in 2027 after the elections. The combination will make it hard for the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) to push inflation into single digits for any sustained period of time however tight it keeps monetary policy, given that food prices still account for 40% of the basket – around 15% is imported food – which makes up the consumer price index. However, falling inflation from recent high levels, helped by base effects, should still mean that it is able to ease the Monetary Policy Rate (MPR) steadily during 2027.

While Zambia is a country usually in the spotlight from a drought perspective, it has just been through a drought and seems to have recovered strongly and learnt some of the key lessons.

For South Africa, the impact is predominantly via food inflation. This is due to it being a substantial net exporter of yellow and white maize. The planting season starts in October which means the full extent of an El Niño impact will only be known much later in the year. Historically a significant El Niño has brought about a typical supply-side shock to food inflation, even if it is only through a rise in export parity prices. This year it could be aggravated by higher fertilizer prices and rising transport costs, depending on the persistence of the oil price as we wade through the final stage of the Middle East crisis. There is 8-10 month lag between farm prices and retail store shelves, where CPI prices are collected. Yet while the timing of this lag has remained fairly constant over the years, the passthrough coefficient has changed significantly. South Africa's consistently weak GDP growth forced food retailers to reduce opex in order to retain GP margins. This was made possible by high market concentration and large economies of scale. A volumes-over-price strategy has been a consistent amongst the food retailers for some years now.

However, even with a reduction in passthrough, the inflation target has been reduced very recently from 4.5% to 3.0% and so, the central bank response should be as-hawkish as in previous cycles where passthrough was larger. With the higher oil price already creating first and second round inflationary effects, the SARB has started to tighten monetary policy and if El Niño remains a concern, it will continue to remain hawkish in our view. After the 25bp hike in May, citing oil prices and potential El Niño scare, we continue to believe the SARB will deliver another two 2bp hikes, in July and September.

## Latin America

El Niño is not a tail risk for LatAm: it is the base case. Using a State-Dependent Local Projections analysis, we have estimated across eight Latin American economies over 1990–2026, we show that headline inflation responses in the El Niño state are between 2 and 17 times larger than the unconditional average. With food inflation already subdued and cutting cycles finished across the region, the 2026 context is more benign than 2023–24. Food inflation is already low, base effects are favorable, and rates are lower than before. Still, the event is developing faster than in prior cycles, compressing the time between the NOAA signal and the first measurable inflation impact $^{5}$ .

El Niño's inflationary impact in Latin America operates through three primary channels: food, energy and fishing and commodities. Each channel presents very different intensities across countries and understanding which channel dominates in each economy is the key to estimating the pass-through from climate to inflation, and hence monetary policy.

\- The food channel is the most direct and fastest, as El Niño brings excess rainfall and flooding to the Pacific coast while simultaneously bringing drought to Colombia, Central America, and parts of Brazil's northeast, with the net effect on food prices depending on which effect dominates and on the food share in the CPI basket, which ranges from approximately 20% in Chile to over 37% in Peru and Ecuador.

\- The energy channel operates primarily through hydroelectricity and is slower but more persistent, making it the primary driver of second-round inflation effects in the Andean region, where Colombia generates approximately $70\%$ of its electricity from hydroelectric sources.

\- The fishing and commodity channel is mostly Peru-specific but globally significant, connecting Peruvian weather to global food inflation with a lag of 3–6 months, as Peru is the world's largest exporter of fishmeal and El Niño

disrupts the cold-water upwelling off the Peruvian coast that sustains the anchovy population, collapsing the fishing industry; for Panama, the channel is unique, as El Niño reduces water levels in Gatún Lake, restricting Canal transit and raising logistics costs across the region.

We find a dramatic amplification of the inflationary response when El Niño arrives during an already-elevated inflation environment. Headline inflation responses in the El Niño state are 2 to 17 times larger than the unconditional average, with Colombia's result the most striking: in the El Niño state, a 1-standard deviation shock generates a cumulative headline inflation response of +3.26 pp at month 6 (Figure 16), more than 17 times the unconditional estimate of +0.19 pp, driven primarily by the energy channel through hydroelectric generation, consistent with the 2015–16 experience when Banrep initiated a new hiking cycle as food inflation surged.

Regarding Mexico, our estimate for the effect of El Niño on headline inflation is an accumulated effect (in a 12-month horizon) between 29-bps and 59-bps depending on the severity of the weather phenomenon (Figure 17). The effect would be reflected mainly in non-core fruits & vegetable inflation, which could have an accumulated effect between 4.8 pp and 9.6 pp. One of the most recent severe El Niño events in 2014–2016 resulted in fruits & vegetables inflation reaching a high of 27% YoY, close to 20 p.p. above the 10-year average. In 2023–2025 there was also an El Niño event, but the effect could have been amplified due to the fertilizer crisis arising from the Ukraine-Russia geopolitical conflict. In view of our inflation effect estimate and that the current Board composition is not inclined to react in the face of non-core temporary supply shocks, we think it is unlikely for Banxico to deviate from its forward guidance of keeping rates stable. Our end of year policy rate is at 6.50%.

Brazil and Peru show significant amplification. Brazil's fast response (+1.47 pp at month 2) is consistent with the northeast drought channel affecting coffee, sugar, and citrus prices, while Peru show an accumulate response +1.15 pp at month 6 (Figures 18 & 19). Ecuador, Chile, Panama, and Costa Rica show more limited or mixed headline responses for different reasons in each case, with Chile, Panama, and Costa Rica showing no statistically significant amplification in the El Niño state, though Chile's state-dependent core response tells a different story.

## Global Commodities: (Strong) El Niño is a real threat to global agricultural yields

Agriculture price risks are heavily skewed to the upside over the next 6-12 months, as they face major supply risks resulting from high fertilizer prices and from likely poor weather related to El Niño (with the only question being how bad the weather impact will be for net global food production) $^{6}$ . The US NOAA has updated its El Niño probabilities, sharply revising higher its forecast for a strong or record El Niño in the June update. This is based on expectations that sea surface temperatures in the Pacific Ocean will rise by more than 2° degrees Celsius in the next few months.

The correlation between a rise in the agricultural subindex (BCOMAG) and food inflation is relatively weak. However, with a 4–5 month lag (0.65 correlation, with 0.42 R-square), the correlation increases significantly and could potentially cause a rise in the headline CPI. Overall, there is very little evidence of the transmission channel, and it varies across countries. If a country is highly reliant on agricultural commodity imports, it typically has higher exposure to the risk of higher prices translating into domestic inflationary trends.

El Niño has different impacts on a variety of agricultural commodities, which introduces a bullish skew in our price outlook for those commodities. El Niño is a climate pattern characterized by the unusual warming of surface waters in the eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. It is the "warm phase" of the larger El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) phenomenon. Strong (or "super") El Niño events can significantly disrupt global weather patterns, leading to profound and varied impacts on the agricultural sector worldwide. Strong El Niño events occur roughly every 7–10 years, though climate change is increasing their frequency and intensity. The 1997–98 and 2015–16 events — both classified as "very strong" — caused a large drop in agricultural yields globally.

The warming of the Pacific Ocean during an El Niño event transfers a large amount of heat into the atmosphere. This disrupts the normal atmospheric circulation patterns, leading to a cascade of weather-related changes across the globe (Figures 20 & 21). These changes most commonly manifest as:

■ Droughts: In regions that typically receive reliable rainfall, such as Southeast Asia, Australia, and parts of Africa and India.

■ Increased Rainfall and Flooding: In the Americas, particularly along the west coast of South and Central America and in the southern United States.

■ Warmer Winters: In the northern United States and Canada.

These shifts in temperature and precipitation are the primary drivers of El Niño's impact on agriculture, which are explored below.

ICE soft commodities are highly exposed to a significant disruption in global weather patterns, creating major risks and volatility for production (Figure 22). The impact varies widely by commodity, depending on where it is grown. We believe cocoa is a key El Niño play among agricultural commodities, as its production is highly concentrated in the areas most impacted by this weather phenomenon.

A strong El Niño could impact sugar agricultural yields significantly as a potential drought in India (especially in Maharashtra and Karnataka), Thailand, Pakistan, Indonesia, Australia, the Philippines, and NNE Brazil would tighten global inventory. ## Turkey Turkey


The CBT's decision at the June MPC meeting to hold the policy rate rather than align it with the average funding rate signals a wait-and-see stance, preserving flexibility in the near term. This posture, however, does not bode well for alleviating the dollarization risk, which tends to resurface whenever the domestic or global backdrop deteriorates due to Turkey's still-elevated inflation and the current monetary policy strategy. In this regard, our recent analysis suggests that the real test is not whether hard-asset demand disappears, but whether the premium on holding lira settles durably lower, as the carry normalizes. So far, in our view, it has not. The de-dollarization since May 2023 has been impressive, but the carry sustaining it—high though it is in real terms—depends on a disinflation that is far from secured. Nor does the exchange rate offer much reassurance; the subdued FX volatility is primarily driven by the pre-determined depreciation path under the CBT's current framework whose limitations are becoming harder to ignore. Carry and currency stability thus rest on the same foundation: a disinflation that has yet to gain traction. Until it does, we believe the turnaround is likely to remain vulnerable to reversal.

## Argentina, Chile & Colombia


## Argentina

Argentina's economy presents a mixed outlook. GDP is growing, leveraged on non-labor-intensive segments such as mining and energy, while industrial activity remains weak, with a 2.8% YoY fall in April 2026 impacting 12 of 16 sectors. Conversely, inflation slowed to 2.1% MoM in May, though the YoY rate accelerated to 33.2%. The fiscal position remains strong, recording a financial surplus of 0.04% of GDP in May and a YTD surplus of 0.2% of GDP, despite decreased tax revenue and primary expenditure. The Central Bank has surpassed its USD 10 billion-dollar acquisition target, reaching USD 11 billion YTD; however, net reserves did not grow proportionally due to debt service payments and the government's decision not to yet access global capital markets. This, alongside sustained fiscal austerity, prompted S&P to upgrade Argentina's sovereign debt rating from CCC+ to B-and has improved the government's access to voluntary financing in capital markets. Nevertheless, political turmoil and judicial investigations into high-ranking officials continue to erode public confidence. On a positive note, the World Bank approved a USD 2 billion guarantee, with the IDB expected to contribute an additional USD 550 million, to help Argentina refinance dollar-denominated debt and regain international capital market access at lower costs. This multilateral support, combined with ongoing reserve accumulation, boosts optimism for managing over USD 25 billion in debt maturities due in 2027.

## Chile

The macro-outlook is shifting toward weaker growth under tightening fiscal constraints, with monetary policy increasingly reactive and credibility becoming the primary anchor for markets. The growth shock in 1Q26 has not yet been offset by incoming data, while inflation remains above target with persistent core components limiting the pace of convergence. This combination places the central bank in a prolonged hold, as neither disinflation nor activity provides a clear directional signal. At the same time, fiscal dynamics are emerging as the binding constraint, with a softer consolidation path and rising legislative friction increasing execution risk. The interaction between weak activity and fiscal slippage is

beginning to shape market pricing more directly, particularly in local rates and credit. What matters next is whether Senate approval of tax reform can stabilize fiscal expectations, as failure would reinforce a gradual erosion in credibility and push the adjustment burden further into 2027.

## Colombia

We expect tighter monetary policy under persistent inflation and widening fiscal constraints, with political transition reducing uncertainty but not relaxing the adjustment burden. Growth remains consumption-led while investment stays constrained, limiting expansion durability. Inflation persistence, reinforced by wages and emerging El Niño risks, keeps expectations elevated and anchors a renewed tightening cycle, with policy now less constrained post-election. Fiscal dynamics are becoming the binding constraint, as higher borrowing costs and weaker revenue performance push consolidation requirements onto the incoming administration. The transition to De la Espriella provides short-term clarity but does not ease the need for a credible fiscal strategy. What matters next is the speed of cabinet formation and early policy signaling in July, as well as whether the new administration can anchor consolidation expectations, which will determine if recent improvements in confidence can hold or give way to renewed pressure in FX and rates.

# Developed Markets Rates Strategy


## US Rates

Front-end yields moved higher last week on the back of a hawkish FOMC dot plot where nine members saw at least one rate hike by year-end 2026. Further, some FOMC members expected some easing in 2027, presumably as inflation falls over time. We think the front-end of the curve could be at risk of a continued selloff until the market sees evidence of core inflation normalizing, which may take some time (see US Rates Strategy - Widening the aperture). In our view, the long end of the Treasury curve should generally remain supported as the market is pricing in a possible policy mistake, and we still see 10y yields moving lower into year-end. Our thesis is: 1) core inflation will trend lower by Q4 without experiencing much spill-over from the Iran conflict, which may now be nearing resolution; 2) seasonal weakness in jobs data should move the unemployment rate higher throughout the summer; 3) USTs typically have a bullish tailwind in the summer months; 4) the market is wrong to price in \~50bp of rate hikes over the next year; and 5) while jobs data is strong, there is little evidence the low level of wage gains will spill over into services inflation. We continue to favor carry trades in the near term and in the medium term we think the belly of the 2s5s10s fly can outperform.

## Core Europe and EGB spreads

Our forecasts for € rates are essentially unchanged this month, other than a few tweaks. That’s despite the retreat in oil prices and the US-Iran MoU that extends the ceasefire for 60 days. The ECB delivered the 25bp hike in June, and a second hike may yet follow given pipeline pressures for € inflation. For sure, the ECB is highly unlikely to reverse the hike anytime soon given the broadening of inflation and with the duration of the supply shock extending with oil prices still above pre-conflict levels and geopolitical uncertainty likely to linger. Moreover, the bar is probably low for a second hike given the resilience of growth and with up to 2.5% likely seen as a neutral policy setting. The house view assumes a second hike in 3Q26. Either way, we suspect the market will find it hard to take out much more hike premium. With that in mind, we doubt there will be much bullish follow through in € duration. Even if one-and-done becomes base case, and hike premium softens, that probably only gets 10yr Bunds to 2.85% or so. For now, we find it more likely that 3% Bunds is a magnet level for H2. The MoU negates bearish risk by reducing the chance of more than two hikes. Indeed, the main impact of the deal on € rates might be to reduce tails, dampen volatility and encourage carry strategies. Turning to EMU spreads, we find near limits to tightening potential in the near-term, not least with the ECB likely to continue to lean hawkish. This is already reflected in the inability of EMU spreads to follow equities to pre-conflict levels. Seasonality also points to widening to the end of August. Lastly, OATs face a relatively high net supply profile over June–August. Collectively, these create headwinds and skew the risk towards widening, but domestic catalysts for such may be lacking until later in the year.


## UK

The second half is likely to be an interesting one for gilts. On the bullish side, the MoU and fall in oil prices likely keeps a lid on front-end yields by allowing the BoE to stay on hold. That certainly seems to be the preference of the majority for the MPC. Hawkish dissent did grow in June to 7-2 from 8-1 but the argument for preemptive tightening is weaker with the MoU and dovish domestic data. Rather, the MPC is likely content to hold, judging that market tightening is feeding through quickly and helping to dampen inflation. This effective tightening is likely persistent with the market unlikely to revert to rate cut pricing any time soon with oil prices still higher than pre-conflict. That limits the ability of gilts to rally by much in H2 in our view. Indeed, our economics team has pushed back the timing of the next rate cut slightly to 2Q27 from 1Q27 previously. On the bearish side, there is political and fiscal uncertainty with Andy Burnham winning the by-election. There are many permutations to how the political process might play out, which likely keeps gilts relatively volatile in the near-term. Fiscal clarity is only likely to emerge at the autumn Budget. While Burnham has pledged to stick to the fiscal rules, this does not preclude higher borrowing via the investment rule. Indeed, it is hard to imagine that a Burnham premiership, that is championing change, won’t look to borrow. Overall, we adjust our yield forecasts lower for the near quarters but continue to pencil in 5% for 10s at year-end with a steepening bias driven by the combination of no hikes, with the next move likely a distant cut, and fiscal risk premium building in longer tenors. However, we have moderated our year-end target for 2s10s steepening from 100bp to 75bp.

## Japan

The BoJ raised its policy rate to 1.00%, and we maintain our outlook for a terminal rate of 1.50%, with the next hike expected in December 2026, though the path beyond that is increasingly uncertain as Policy Board member Toichiro Asada dissented against the June hike — signaling growing dovishness — and further tightening in the second half of 2027 is expected to be difficult. We assign only a 25% probability to a hike occurring before December. The risk for an earlier hike mainly comes from the US as robust economic figures during summer may pressure on the BoJ via yen weakness. On the balance sheet front, the BoJ will continue reducing monthly JGB purchases toward around ¥2trn per month from April 2027. While annual losses from negative carry on JGB holdings could approach ¥4.5trn, the BoJ is considered well-prepared through its loss reserves. Additionally, the Securities Lending Facility minimum lending fee will rise from 25bp to 50bp effective July 16, 2026, increasing the cost of holding short JGB positions and proving positive for ASW longs, particularly in the 3y–6y sector.

On the fiscal front, risk has risen as public-private investment under Japan's new growth strategy through fiscal 2040 could total ¥370trn. The government spending potentially increasing by ¥10–¥30 trillion next fiscal year even excluding consumption tax cuts, in our view, representing a bearish risk for JGB duration that could materialize earlier than the previously anticipated October–December window.

The major geopolitical unknown was whether the US and Iran could find a path towards Strait of Hormuz trade flows restarting, and this question appears answered, with Iran and US confirming an MoU on June 15 and signing the MoU on June 17. On June 15 we shifted our forecasts for Brent oil down towards our prior bear case scenario, with new quarterly price forecasts of 3Q26/4Q26/2027 at \$75/\$70/\$65/bbl, respectively (down from \$110/90/80/bbl). We remain bullish on gold on a 6–12m view at \$5,000/oz on robust non-cyclical gold demand from lingering sovereign debt and debasement concerns, sustaining central bank reserve diversification trend, and increasing geopolitical fragmentation, though it is vulnerable to substantial volatility, and a resumption of the war or equity market correction is a major risk to the bullish base case, if only temporary.

We turn broadly bullish on industrial metals on underlying structural tailwinds given growth risks have largely dissipated following the Strait of Hormuz reopening deal. We see rallies higher in copper (to \$15k/t within 12 months), lithium (to \$35 k/t near-term), and tin (to \$60k/t) on structural demand tailwinds and supply constraints.

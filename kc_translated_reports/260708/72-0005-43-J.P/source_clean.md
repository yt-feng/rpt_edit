# Broadlines & Hardlines Retailing

Following up on our recently published Episode 14: Retail vs. AMZN in an Agentic World report we published with JPM Internet Analysts Doug Anmuth & Bryan Smilek (see the slides and webinar replay), this note takes a deeper look at the opportunities and risks ahead in the world of AI and agentic commerce. Team Internet believes that AI will ultimately serve as an incremental discovery channel and help expand the e-commerce TAM. From our perspective, we also see agentic as more of an opportunity at this point, albeit with puts/takes by category. Simplistically, LLMs represent a more potent version of “googling” and follow “threats” posed by other customer acquisition venues (e.g., TikTok, Pinterest, and Shopify). Despite high investor anxiety as these platforms emerged, all of these channels simply became another entry point to retailers’ respective categories that companies needed to embrace. As such, we echo the need for our companies to invest to win with the large LLMs, while harnessing their own agents “on platform” (i.e., their own websites/apps) to drive customer acquisition/retention, product discovery, basket building, and conversion. Investments in both areas can optimize proprietary data collection, an important sales and advertising revenue consideration.

Unclear monetization path shifts AI platforms' focus from agentic commerce to digital advertising. While OpenAI initially charged retailers an affiliate fee ( $\sim 4\%$ on avg. per JPM Analyst Brenda Duverce) on transactions completed through its Instant Checkout offering, AI platforms don't currently participate in the economics of agentic commerce transactions after this was deprecated and retailers now control checkout. In our view, there currently isn't much visibility as to how AI platforms will monetize agentic commerce transactions, with their focus seemingly narrowing on digital advertising (presumably LLMs clip some of the potential from retail media, but not material at this point).

Notably, unlike the original Amazon threat, retailers are generally being front-footed, including partnering with the likes of Open AI and Google to both shape the direction of agentic commerce and to stay on the front edge of the learning curve (the opposite of what happened in the 2010-15 timeframe when AMZN rose to e-commerce dominance). As noted below, WMT is leading the pack, as it should given its broad product offering and marketplace model, with TGT on solid footing, and W, BBY, HD, LOW, ULTA, and ASO all partnering with Google on UCP (Universal Commerce Protocol - an open-source set of standards that enables retailers to allow AI agents to browse their websites, build carts, and ultimately check out, Figure 2). The two biggest retailer moat-protectors are (1) they have retaken control of the transaction with checkout now facilitated through retailer app integrations on the LLMs (after OpenAI and the retailers shifted away from Instant Checkout where OpenAI controlled the transaction), and (2) retailers still handle fulfillment, keeping true disintermediation at bay. Notably, as an example of how retailers can benefit in an agentic world, BBY mgmt spoke to its depth of knowledge/data for its category to match a search to the best product as a competitive advantage that only increases in an LLM world (e.g., best college student laptop vs. best laptop for mom, gaming, or coding), albeit acknowledging that it (and most retailers, in our view) needs to better enable the data links between its website and the LLMs.

\- Fancy “googling”: AI platforms are driving <1% of web traffic across key e-commerce sites. Consumers are primarily using LLMs for product discovery with the long-term risk of delegated automated replenishment of commodities. That said, if/when we cross that bridge, LLMs don’t control the distribution process (a la AMZN//WMT) and many brands are reluctant to go DTC (Figure 1).

\- Unclear monetization path shifts AI platforms' focus from agentic commerce to digital advertising. While OpenAI initially charged retailers an affiliate fee ( $\sim 4\%$ on avg. per JPM Analyst Brenda Duverce) on transactions completed through its Instant Checkout offering, AI platforms don't currently participate in the economics of agentic commerce transactions after this was deprecated and retailers now control checkout. In our view, there currently isn't much visibility as to how the AI platforms will monetize agentic commerce transactions, with their focus seemingly narrowing on digital advertising. In our view, while AI platforms could take share of digital advertising at the margin, they lack the depth/breadth of customer data and attribution capabilities that gave rise to retail media proliferation in the first place.

\- Risk categories: grocery and brands with DTC fulfillment. Grocery/CPG face higher price discovery (potentially creating retailer margin pressure) while disintermediation risk exists for sought-after brands with an established DTC mechanism (e.g., Apple and Nike). Should these brands lose control of channel management or decide they want to clip the “retail margin” (vs. getting only wholesale profitability), they may disintermediate the retailer, as we saw in the early 2010’s with major electronics brands and Best Buy and on more-than-one occasion with Nike and its retail partners like DKS. On the other hand, retailers in highly emotive categories (e.g., beauty, pet) and where browsing/basket building is important (e.g., home decor) remain in the low-risk bucket.

\- Retailers need to partner with LLMs. Early engagement with the major LLMs to shape agentic commerce and create data connections, while implementing retailer app integration into the LLMs to support checkout, are important steps to maintaining category leadership, supporting product discovery, and harvesting data. Indeed, the LLMs offer customer acquisition opportunities and, at the very least, retailers need to guard against share loss. Ultimately, large retailers have scale and that enables the opportunity to win. To that end, and learning from how quickly the AMZN threat grew post-GFC, WMT, TGT, W, BBY, HD, LOW, ULTA, and ASO have all partnered with Google on its UCP to enable agentic search/discovery (Figure 2).

\- On-platform investment is arguably a bigger opportunity. Proprietary on-platform agents provide consumers with similar search benefits as well as advice (“Sparky, what’s a great Thanksgiving dinner?” and “Magic Apron, how do I replace a toilet?” with the retailer LLM potentially populating the consumer’s cart). WMT (Sparky), TGT, HD (Magic Apron), LOW (MyLow), ULTA (ULTA AI), WSM (Olive), ASO (Scout), and DKS (Coach) are all in-house agents. They also offer cost-efficient ways to solve customer service pain points (“Olive, where is my sofa delivery?”). WMT is generally ahead of the retail pack (as one would expect and is necessary given the breadth of their assortment and marketplace efforts) with TGT and then the rest of retailers we discuss chasing. Ultimately, to optimize success, we believe retailers will need to both engage through/with the major LLMs and also use their own agents on their owned platforms to drive customer acquisition/retention/satisfaction, product discovery, basket building, and proprietary data collection.

## The Agentic Debate: Disintermediation Risk Remains Low

While adoption remains early stages, the current state of agentic commerce reflects consumers primarily using LLMs for product discovery, inspiration, and basket building, with the potential progression to consumers transacting through AI platforms and delegating responsibility for automated replenishment of commoditized products, with discrete opportunities and risks for both these and more considered purchases. The bigger disintermediation risk comes when LLM agents become “smart enough” to handle the entire transaction through checkout, thereby disintermediating the retailer, which gives the LLM access to data and all the goodness that comes along with it (e.g., advertising revenues). That said, even if/when we cross that bridge, the most significant difference is that LLMs don’t control the distribution process, with the likes of WMT and AMZN still having scaled supply chain advantages vs. virtually all of the broader retail set, and many brands/vendors generally reluctant to carry the burden of distributing directly to the consumer.

At a high level, we see two areas of concern. First, LLMs elevate price discovery, which creates potential downward pressure on price-sensitive categories. This certainly pushes grocery/CPG to the top of the risk bucket, but it also creates opportunities for retailers where the consumer sees private label as a strong substitute (e.g., Kirkland Signature and Amazon Basics). Second, LLMs can potentially disintermediate retailers of sought-after brands in style categories where the vendor has an established direct to consumer mechanism. Apple in consumer electronics is a good example and we point to Nike's history of episodically disintermediating Dick's and other retailers given the desire to capture the full retail margin. Indeed, branded footwear and apparel seem particularly exposed on this front. On the other hand, retailers in highly emotive categories (e.g., beauty, pet) and where browsing/basket building is important (e.g., home furnishings/decor) remain in the low risk bucket.

## The Current State of Affairs: Agentic Threat Remains Limited

AI Platforms remain $<1\%$ of total web traffic across key e-commerce marketplaces and retailer sites (Figure 1), but marketplaces/retailers have experienced stronger engagement, conversion, and AOVs from AI-enabled customers. In our view, customer adoption of AI Platforms for the full shopping experience (bridging from discovery and basket building to checkout) is a function of existing collaborations/partnerships between retailers and AI platforms, with the shift from model-controlled checkout environments (e.g., OpenAI Instant Checkout) to retailer-controlled ones (e.g., app integrations via UCP) a key step in accelerating agentic commerce proliferation as consumers will now have better access to accurate and localized pricing, promotions, loyalty programs, inventory availability, etc. while also protecting retailer/marketplace advertising programs.

## Invest Early to Stay Ahead of the Curve

What is the internet infrastructure supporting agenetic commerce? Various e-commerce industry participants are collaborating to create an open standard of Internet infrastructure supporting AI-driven transactions. \~20 retailers & Internet companies are collaborating on Google's UCP council, including AMZN, BBY, HD, LOW, META, MSFT, ETSY, SHOP, TGT, ULTA, WMT, W, and others. Google's Universal Cart enables consumers to add items to their cart across multiple merchants and across services, and it leverages intelligent reasoning to provide personalized shopping assistance. The UCP enables seamless checkout from the Universal Cart. While OpenAI & Stripe have co-developed the Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP), Team Internet's view is that its scope is more narrowly focused on enabling secure checkout via Stripe vs. broader full-funnel agentic commerce capabilities, though it does offer agentic discovery. Anthropic's MCP enables developers to connect data sources and AI tools, though it is not primarily confined to e-commerce and instead is focused on broader web infrastructure. As noted above, enabling merchant scale and ease of AI integrations will be key to scaling agentic commerce.

Companies that invest ahead of the curve will see a first-mover advantage in terms of customer trust/mindshare, reinforcing the retailer's brand perch as a “shopping destination" and providing valuable data that is collected from the LLM queries.

\- Integrating with LLMs via UCP is a critical first step to both gaining share from agentic commerce adoption and maintaining a customer data advantage.

\- Retailers that also invest in a robust, proprietary data infrastructure stand to differentiate themselves, including both customer data to understand purchasing behavior (AMZN, COST, ULTA, and others with strong loyalty programs stand out in this regard) as well as accurate inventory/fulfillment systems so that consumers can trust localized/store-level product pricing and availability information. Many retailers have invested in the latter to contend with the e-commerce threat, given solid inventory visibility is required to support omnichannel retail (e.g., can't promise delivery speeds without understanding inventory positioning at a granular level), while the customer data/loyalty opportunity has been growing in recent years to support budding retail media ventures (and this becomes increasingly important in an agentic commerce world to improve personalized demand prediction and relevant basket-building recommendations, etc.).

As indicated in the chart above, on the Team Retail side, WMT is the furthest along in terms of engaging the agentic opportunity, with TGT next and the others fast-following. These companies recognize the importance of leaning in and shaping how agentic commerce develops in their respective categories. Importantly for WSM, nearly 90% of sales is driven by their own branded products, which mitigates much of the risk discussed above.

At the category level, we see the most risk in grocery given its low-consideration/commoditized products and recurring purchase nature, with the core value proposition of “agentic” commerce centered around saving consumers time. Indeed, while the early adoption timeline will remain choppy, we see grocery as the ultimate epicenter of agentic commerce disintermediation, with the aforementioned factors (low-consideration/commoditized products, recurring purchases) and high private label mix/lower brand affinity/ease of substitution allowing customers to eventually trust agentic models to purchase/replenish groceries on a recurring basis. Thus, true agentic commerce adoption could accelerate the pace of online grocery adoption. Moreover, whether in categories with high (Diet Pepsi vs. Diet Coke) or low brand affinity, should LLM agents control the transaction and only pass over fulfillment, retailers could lose high margin advertising revenue streams.

\- The shopping experience is just as important to the purchase decision as the product category, and company-specific initiatives will play a significant role in whether or not consumers ultimately engage with agentic commerce. For example, if customers have to be redirected to company websites to input payment or loyalty information (more than once), or if product pricing and availability/delivery speed differs between the external agentic interface and the retailer's website, consumers are less likely to engage beyond simple information gathering/product discovery. Similarly, retailers will have to balance how to leverage their control over a transaction with the ability to utilize AI for more personalized “basket building” product recommendations to drive high-margin advertising/marketplace revenues vs. disenfranchising consumers looking for automated replenishment of commodities that don’t want to be upsold.

\- Private Label remains a shield to price competition/share. In the early 2000s, retailers used private label as a less expensive, but “similar” quality product to its national-brand equivalent, offering consumers a way to save money and for retailers to generate margin benefits. Over the past 15 years, retailers have increasingly moved up the price continuum into the “better” (direct quality compare to mainline national brands) and even best buckets.

On the gen merch side, higher involvement purchases remain relatively low risk with branded single item products potentially seeing accelerated e-commerce adoption as well. We’ve always viewed the risk of e-commerce disintermediation in terms of how urgent a transaction is (e.g., break-fix items like car batteries are low), how ubiquitous product availability is, how perishable an item is (more=less risk), and whether the purchase is a high or low involvement (e.g., part of a broader project, decorative/fashion in nature in the case of home furnishings and parts of apparel/footwear, beauty are all high involvement purchases). We think these lenses still broadly apply and, despite the myth, people do enjoy shopping for high involvement purchases. Also, see the joy of the treasure hunt driving COST and off-price market share gains.

\- Product cycles matter. Specialty retail generally wins during periods of innovation and share threats rise during commoditization. The poster children of encroachment had significant product issues that opened the door for AMZN. For books/packaged media/office, consumption went digital. For electronics, it was the crescendo of a once-in-a-generation product cycle that rapidly turned negative as replacement periods lengthened and product proliferation turned to convergence on smart phones.

Brands behavior remains a critical lens, especially for single item purchases where the vendor has an established DTC platform: the Nike example. Looking back, when the likes of Samsung and Nike acted irrationally and lost control of product allocations, retailers were disintermediated (BBY post-GFC and DKS in 2017). Looking ahead, to the extent that Nike, for example, sees agentic as a means to gain market share (and capture retailer margins) and also has a feasible direct-to-consumer distribution model, that could disintermediate retailers. On the other hand, Samsung or Whirlpool are unlikely to integrate a direct-to-consumer supply chain (i.e., UPS isn't sending a TV or washing machine but can send a pair of sneakers).

## Advertising/Retail Media Risk Fades as Retailers Still Control the Transaction

During the initial iteration of agentic commerce (e.g., OpenAI Instant Checkout) when it appeared that AI platforms would control the transaction, investors were concerned this would cannibalize the budding high-margin retail media and marketplace revenue streams as brands shifted ad dollars to the AI platforms. However, given data fidelity, retailer pushback, and other issues that drove the shift away from Open AI's Instant Checkout model, the retailers/merchants have maintained control of the transaction, and can therefore continue to drive advertising revenue by leveraging proprietary customer data alongside insights generated by the AI platform to surface personalized, context-aware advertisements. This shift alleviates the advertising bear case, with the bull case opportunity over time that more robust customer data leads to higher ad conversion rates, and thus, advertising commissions for retailers/merchants, though it will take time for the retailers/merchants to dial-in the optimal mix & volume of ads, and for customers to start transacting through AI platforms vs. simple information gathering.

## Team Retail: Agentic Commerce an Opportunity with LLM and Owned Platform Engagement Critical

The points made above are very relevant, with early engagement with the major third-party LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini, etc.) to shape agentic commerce and create data connections, while implementing retailer app integration into the LLMs to support checkout are all important steps to maintain category leadership and support product discovery, basket building, and to harvest the data from product searches made on the LLM. Indeed, as indicated above, the major LLMs can be a good source of customer acquisition and, at the very least, need to be defended against as a potential source of customer loss. Ultimately, large retailers have scale and that offers the opportunity to win given the ability to invest. To that end, WMT, TGT, W, BBY, HD, LOW, ULTA, and ASO have all partnered with Google on its UCP (Universal Commerce Protocol) to enable agentic search/discovery on Google's Gemini LLM, while all of our retailers that have their own chatbot/LLM effort have app/catalog integration with OpenAI/ChatGPT, with most not yet offering agentic checkout on either Open AI or Google. Lastly, fulfillment remains a core skillset and an important asset in categories where the brands/vendors don't have a scaled DTC business, with threats around established DTC brands in clothing and footwear (e.g., NKE) and enhancing price discovery making for a more competitive grocery backdrop.

In addition, proprietary on-platform agents provide consumers with similar search benefits as well as advice (“Sparky, what’s a great Thanksgiving dinner?” and “Magic Apron, how do I replace a toilet?” with the LLM potentially populating the consumer’s cart). WMT (Sparky), TGT, HD (Magic Apron), LOW (MyLow), ULTA (ULTA AI), WSM (Olive), ASO (Scout), and DKS (Coach) are all in-house agents (DKS app only). They also provide cost-efficient ways to solve customer service pain points (“Olive, where is my sofa delivery?”), thus improving customer satisfaction.

Sparky leading the charge with TGT next. From Figure 2 above, and based on our experience, WMT's Sparky is the most advanced on-platform and in the third-party LLMs, with TGT next but its on-platform agent(s) at varying stages of beta. TGT has built an app experience within ChatGPT and Google Gemini to enable agentic search of its website, basket building, and ultimately check out. WMT launched Sparky in June 2025 and it has been trained on a long history of customer data, and it currently acts as an in-app assistant for product search/discovery and basket building (also available through third-party LLMs), with WMT citing half of app users engaging with Sparky and basket size $35\%$ higher. More recently, after pivoting away from OpenAI's Instant Checkout feature following low conversion/sales, WMT created a Sparky experience within ChatGPT that acts as an embedded shopping assistant to help build the basket and facilitate checkout.

Ultimately, to optimize success, we believe retailers will need to both engage through/with the LLMs and also use their own agents on their owned platforms to drive customer acquisition/retention/satisfaction, product discovery, basket building, and proprietary data collection.

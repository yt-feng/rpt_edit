# Global Data Watch: Asia
## EMAX resilience has a trifecta of underpinnings

In contrast to downside risks in China, there are upside risks to growth in the rest of Asia. Three factors have combined to underpin this. First, EMAX has proved relatively resilient and resourceful in the face of the energy shock. Daily tanker volumes of energy imports have improved substantially in recent weeks and are almost back to normal levels, as the region aggressively scouts for energy from other locations. Consequently, the feared non-linearities to growth seem to have been avoided for now. Second, tech continues to boom, visible both in the April IP prints as well as in strong May Korea tech exports this week, though growing price effects further buoy the nominal prints. Third, fiscal policy is active in the region to cushion the energy shock. Korea already saw a large supplementary budget after the oil shock and strong tech creates fiscal space that could potentially be used to provide more buffers. In India, the government has borne a significant fraction of the negative terms of trade shock, with retail prices increasingly more modestly. In May, Thailand announced a hefty fiscal package to the tune of 2.1% of GDP which should help lift growth in 2H26. Finally, in the Philippines, we expect the government to enact a supplementary budget worth 0.7% of GDP in 2H26, which should help offset the impact of monetary policy tightening. Together, these forces have contributed to resilience reflected in the May PMIs, with the EMAX output index rebounding after slipping in March and April, though it’s still not back at pre-conflict levels.

## RBI bucks the regional hawkish tilt

With growth holding up, several central banks have turned their attention to inflation and become progressively hawkish. The BSP and Bank of Indonesia have already hiked in recent weeks and we expect more to come. Last week, the Bank of Korea turned hawkish and we now expect the BoK to hike four times over the next year. The one central bank where we have been consistently more dovish than the market has been the RBI. The RBI held policy rates this week, as we had expected, but bucking market pricing that had built in a 60% chance of a 25 bps hike. Instead, the RBI delivered a booster shot of regulatory measures to attract capital inflows and shore up the Balance of Payments, in the wake of sustained pressures on the currency. In the event, it underscored the principle of separability under India's Inflation Targeting framework, wherein rates and liquidity are used to address inflation while other instruments (sterilized FX intervention and regulatory policy) are used to combat external sector pressures.

## China: 2Q risks skew downward, but with caveats

While PMI signals are mixed, we see some downside risks to 2Q GDP growth. Against the backdrop of weak April activity, latest NBS and RatingDog PMI readings offered a mixed bag: while the headline PMIs weakened, output and RatingDog new orders PMI remained in expansionary territory. Furthermore, May housing saw an improvement in secondary home sales, which may provide some support for consumer sentiment. Yet, hard data in May and June would need to bounce back discernibly to support our 2Q GDP forecast.

But even as there are downside risks to 2Q, caveats exist. First, this comes on the back of a very strong 1Q such that 1H growth would still average 5% or more.

# Australia: Data center boom in perspective

- The global wave of AI infrastructure spending has driven a surge in Australian data center investment
- Technology sector capex is rising sharply, while building approvals signal a steep construction upswing
- However, the scale remains small in macro terms and import leakage will moderate the GDP impact

Up to one in every five dollars approved for Australian non-residential building construction is now going towards building data centers $^{1}$ (Figure 1). The pick-up reflects a global surge in AI-related infrastructure investment, with the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO) forecasting Australian data center electricity demand (a proxy for output) to triple by 2030.

We have written separately about the implications of AI for the economy, through investment in software/data centers, productivity effects, and capital returns. Here, we provide an update on the data center investment lifecycle from construction to the operational phase, and provide estimates for its impact on Australia's National Accounts.

Figure 1: Australian data center approvals

[[KC_IMAGE_001]]


In the near term, the data center build-out phase is set to boost non-residential construction activity, but the scale remains small relative to the economy and is unlikely to be a material driver of GDP growth. Growth in business capital expenditure is also likely to be somewhat offset by capital imports, given Australia's limited role in producing data center hardware. Once operational, upstream demand should be most visible in telecommunications and business services, but again, import leakage remains meaningful.

## Spending big

Growth in tech infrastructure is already visible in a material pick-up in plant and equipment (P&E) investment by the technology sector (Figure 2). Technology sector P&E capex totalled A\$7.3bn through 2025, or about 0.2% of GDP. 1Q26 data point to another sharp increase in actual spending on a rolling four-quarter basis, and firms' forward-looking guidance for the remainder of the current fiscal year signals further upside. Although firms' expectations for FY27 capital expenditure sit below current levels, firms overshot their planned 2025 spending and have not revised down their FY26 intentions, suggesting further upgrades are plausible. Note that these figures do not include direct tech spending by non-tech businesses, e.g. software investment, currently contributing around +0.1%-pts to GDP.

Figure 2: Technology P&E capital expenditure

[[KC_IMAGE_002]]


Even taken at face value, this trajectory, while a notable step up from its historical average, remains small as a share of the overall economy. Compared to Australia's early 2010s mining capex boom, which peaked at nearly $7\%$ of GDP, the tech dynamic appears much smaller so far. Furthermore, while there is likely still upside for technology capital expenditure, a meaningful share of data center fit-out relies on imported capital equipment. Capital imports for tech equipment have also been surging and will mechanically offset some of the gross capex impulse to GDP.

## Laying the foundations

From a GDP perspective, the larger domestic value added impulse comes via construction activity, which can be tracked from approvals (Figure 1) through commencements and work done (Figure 3). On current pipeline metrics, the construction upswing is steep, but the scale looks modest relative to aggregate output. The value of work under construction has more than doubled through 2025 to A\$14.1bn, while the portion still to be completed is A\$8.9bn — a modest 0.3% of GDP.

This work will be distributed into measured GDP over multiple quarters as it is executed, implying a smaller annual growth contribution. Moreover, the level of approved but not yet commenced work does not currently point to a step-change higher from here. A more material growth impulse would require a renewed leg up in approvals.

## Up and running

Once operational, data centers contribute to growth through Gross Value Added (GVA) in the production measure of GDP. The relevant ABS category (Other Information/Media Services) is broad and bundles together publishing, motion picture services, TV/radio broadcasting, libraries and data center services. The group as a whole currently accounts for 1.4% of GDP. Taking AEMO's estimate of a tripling in energy use by 2030 as a proxy for output growth yields a contribution of +0.3%-pts to GDP per year as a plausible upper bound. In practice, the realised impulse is likely to be materially smaller. Data center operations represent only a portion of the broader category, and output is unlikely to scale one-for-one with capital or energy use.

Using the ABS input-output tables, related upstream inputs to data center operations can also be computed by industry $^{2}$ . The latest available data for 2023-24 indicate a service-heavy supply chain with a significant import component (Figure 4). Telecommunications is the dominant upstream input at roughly 25 per 100 of output, with about 19 sourced domestically and 6 imported. Professional and scientific services follow at 13, with a second tier including construction, electronics, software engineering, real estate, administrative services, and finance. Given that the ABS input-output data are released with a significant lag, the input mix is likely to evolve as data centers become a larger share of the segment.

Figure 4: Top 10 upstream data center operation inputs

[[KC_IMAGE_004]]


The sum of upstream domestic intermediate inputs yields the gross domestic output multiplier, a measure of industrial interconnectedness $^{3}$ . For data centers, this multiplier is estimated at 0.58x (Figure 5), only marginally above the all-industry average (0.56x), and below construction (0.80x) and electricity generation (0.78x).

Labour intensity (\$ employee cost per \$1 of industry gross output)

The labour intensity $^{4}$ of data center operations is also modest, sitting below professional services and software design, yet above more capital-intensive industries such as mining and manufacturing. This is consistent with data centers generating moderate, not outsized, demand spillovers to other industries and to the labour market. The operational stage should therefore support telecoms and business services, but import leakage is again likely to temper the net uplift to GDP.

## Japan

- Alongside the supplementary budget and off-budget investment, a consumption tax cut has emerged
- Ueda's hawkish remarks point to a June hike that is largely priced in; focus shifts to QT
- Data suggest 2Q consumption started strong, underpinned by firm wage gains
- Next week: 1Q GDP to be revised down; SME sentiment to recover

Developments surrounding fiscal policy again drew significant attention this week. The Diet approved a JPY3.1 trillion (0.5% of GDP) supplementary budget. Most of it consists of reserve funds that the Cabinet can disburse at its discretion without prior Diet approval. While the specific allocation will be determined by the Cabinet going forward, a large portion is likely to be directed towards energy subsidies. In addition, multiple media outlets reported this week that the government has begun coordinating to implement PM Takaichi's campaign pledge of a two-year, time-bound consumption tax cut on food—reducing the current 8% rate to 1% — from next April. If carried out, the government would forgo around JPY5 trillion (0.8% of GDP) in revenue over the two years starting next fiscal year. Together with last week's policy to formulate a growth-strategy plan off-budget, the increasing likelihood of the tax cut has clearly raised the odds that next fiscal year's JGB issuance will expand substantially. With inflation rising and the PM's approval ratings beginning to drift down, the administration may be under pressure to shore up support through fiscal expansion.

With underlying inflation remaining elevated and fiscal policy turning more expansionary, USDJPY this week returned to the 160 level—back to where it stood before the late-April currency intervention. Against this backdrop, Governor Ueda stated in a speech that there is a need to “properly discuss whether to raise rates,” sending a hawkish signal. However, with a June rate hike already largely priced in, attention has shifted to whether the BoJ will further reduce its JGB purchases from April 2027 onward. The BoJ’s survey of market participants released this week indicated that opinions are divided, but that calls to continue the tapering are in the slight majority. Meanwhile, the government—tilting toward fiscal expansion—may prefer a halt to the reductions. We expect the BoJ to strike a balance by slowing the pace of reductions from JPY200 billion per quarter to JPY100 billion per quarter from April 2027 onward. That said, this would not mean the BoJ’s QT is slowing; it would merely mean that the pace of acceleration becomes somewhat more gradual.

Turning to the data, this week's data suggest that accommodative monetary policy and fiscal support contributed to a solid start for household consumption in 2Q. April wages remained on a strong trend, and real consumption also posted robust growth, helped by subsidies for durable goods. In next week's second estimate of 1Q GDP, the strength in capex shown in the first estimate is expected to be revised down to negative growth, but overall GDP growth should still come in at an above-potential $1.2\% \mathrm{q} / \mathrm{q}$ saar (2.1% in the first estimate).

## 1Q GDP to be revised down on capex

The Ministry of Finance's 1Q26 corporate survey suggested capex fell $1.4\%$ oya excluding software, below our and market expectations (JPM: $5.5\%$ , Consensus: $5.4\%$ ). On a seasonally adjusted q/q basis, capex (ex. software) dropped $13.1\%$ q/q, largely unwinding the sharp $14.5\%$ q/q saar increase recorded in 4Q25 (Figure 1). As the survey forms a key input into the calculation of business investment in the GDP accounts, these results suggest that the resilience in capex shown in the first estimate of 1Q26 GDP report is likely to be reversed in the second estimate. In next week's second estimate, we expect capex growth to be revised from a $1.1\%$ q/q, saar rise to a $4.0\%$ decline, indicating a payback of the strong 4Q25 gain. As a result, we think headline 4Q real GDP will be revised to $1.2\%$ q/q saar, down from $2.1\%$ in the first estimate.

While nominal capex came in weaker-than-expected, sales and current profits continued to post solid growth, rising 5.3%q/q, saar, and 17.4%, respectively. Thus, we don’t think this report points to a trend slowdown in corporate activity. While non-manufacturers are not showing notable strength in either sales or profits, the strength in the manufacturing sector stands out. Manufacturing profits have risen at an annualized q/q pace of more than 50% for three consecutive quarters, underscoring robust corporate performance. The weakness in 1Q capex likely reflects, rather than a deterioration in fundamentals, firms’ initial response to the conflict that began in the Middle East during the quarter— postponing investment decisions.

Profit margins in both manufacturing and non-manufacturing are at historically high levels, suggesting that capex appetite should remain firm (Figure 2). That said, surging material costs and a tight labor market continue to weigh on the execution of planned investment. We expect the corporate profit environment to remain favorable, while also recognizing that supply constraints could become a binding constraint on economic activity.

## April base pay growth solid again

The monthly Employers' Survey again points to a strengthening underlying wage trend. Headline wages rose $3.5\%$ oya in April, well above our expectation of a $2.7\%$ increase. Base wage growth was particularly firm: scheduled earnings rose $4.2\%$ oya, the fastest pace since 1994. Some of this strength is tempered in the constant sample series (which removes sampling volatility), but even there, scheduled earnings increased $2.8\%$ oya, indicating an acceleration relative to last year.

This may indicate that wage momentum is spreading to smaller firms that do not typically participate in the shun-to wage negotiations. Part-time workers' hourly wages rose 4.9% oya, the fastest pace since March 2024. Sustained basepay gains should also contribute to stickiness in underlying core inflation.

## Real consumption surges in April

The BoJ's real Consumption Activity Index (CAI, travel balance adjusted) rose 1.6%m/m sa in April, the largest increase since September 2022. The upside surprise appears partly driven by durable goods, which rose 12.4%m/m sa, as auto-related subsidies and tax cuts that began in April likely boosted vehicle sales. Importantly, services consumption continued to post steady gains, rising 0.3%, and non-durable goods rebounded 1.2% despite a jump in energy prices—suggesting households remain resilient at the start of this quarter. This aligns with our view that solid wage growth and government measures to curb inflation are providing a buffer, helping households absorb energy-price shocks without a meaningful deterioration in consumer demand.

# Japan Focus: Energy subsidies broaden to curb inflation

Government subsidies are continuing to suppress CPI readings, particularly in public services and energy. In April, measures such as gasoline subsidies, tax cuts, and temporary electricity and gas subsidies reduced the core CPI (excluding fresh food) by roughly 1.0%pt. As a result, the gap between the reported CPI series and the BoJ's preferred "Consumer Prices excluding Institutional Factors" indicators has widened (Figure 1 and 2). Core CPI (excluding fresh food) rose 1.4%oya in April, but policy measures are estimated to have trimmed inflation by a further 1.4%pt.

A large share of the energy-related support comes from gasoline subsidies. With Brent crude still above \$100/bbl, retail gasoline prices before subsidies have risen to around 210 yen/liter since the conflict—close to the levels seen in 2023. By capping retail gasoline prices below 170 yen/liter, alongside a gasoline tax cut, gasoline-related measures lowered core CPI (ex. fresh food) by about 0.8%pts (Figure 3).

A second wave of energy price pressures is likely this summer as electricity prices begin to rise. Electricity tariffs typically reflect oil-price movements with a 3–6 month lag, implying that higher oil prices should feed through from mid-year. Against this backdrop, the government has announced plans for a supplementary budget to reintroduce electricity and gas subsidies over the summer. The measures should lower core CPI by 0.4–0.5%pt in Aug–Oct, while the impact on the %oya reading is expected at around 0.2–0.3%pt, reflecting base effects from last year's subsidies.

While these measures cushion households from the immediate impact of higher energy costs, they also increase the risk of a rebound in energy inflation once subsidies expire. In our forecast, energy prices begin to add to core inflation from late 2026, pushing core CPI above 3% oya by year-end. If that materializes, the government may extend electricity subsidies to limit further inflation. The planned three-month electricity and gas package this summer is estimated to cost 500 billion yen, but repeated extensions—together with sizeable ongoing outlays for the gasoline price cap—would heighten the risk of additional fiscal spending.

## Australia and New Zealand

• Australian 1Q GDP disappointed at +0.3%q/q
- RBA messaging likely to maintain hiking bias after a large minimum wage hike
- Business conditions expected to weaken further, with confidence stabilizing

Australia's 1Q GDP data was softer than expected at $+0.3\%$ q/q, below both consensus $(+0.4\% \mathrm{q} / \mathrm{q})$ and JPM expectations $(+0.6\% \mathrm{q} / \mathrm{q})$ . Tracking data over the past two weeks pointed to solid investment momentum in the first quarter. There was some disappointment in pockets of non-dwelling construction, which undershot rates implied by earlier indicators, but strength in other business outlays was preserved, particularly tech-related plant and equipment spending.

The economy grew 2.5% oya over the last year, though with weaker recent momentum in consumption. A similar pattern is evident in domestic demand, which rose a modest 0.4%q/q, but is up 3.5% oya (Figure 1). The large gap between final demand and GDP growth has been a persistent feature of recent years and lately, reflects import-intensive capital spending activity.

Household spending contributed +0.3%-pts to growth during the quarter, while government consumption slowed enough to make no net contribution (Figure 2). This in part reflects the technical transfer of final energy expenditure back to households, after recent years' electricity subsidies. Within gross fixed capital formation (GFCF), machinery and equipment investment surged 16.3%q/q, adding a solid +0.7%-pts. However, this was largely offset by a 0.5%-pt drag from higher imports. Non-dwelling construction made no net contribution, as a decline in engineering work ran counter to the gains suggested by the construction work done report. Dwelling construction edged up modestly (+0.7%q/q) and inventories were neutral, coming in softer than business indicators had implied. On the external side, exports fell 1.1%q/q, taking 0.3%-pts from growth, driven by broad-based weakness across key commodities as well as a softening in travel services.

## China: PMI eases though still in expansionary territory

May PMI reports point to a softer manufacturing pulse juxtaposed with improving non-manufacturing activity. Headlines were steady-to-lower: the NBS manufacturing PMI edged down to 50.0, while RatingDog's fell to 51.8, though remaining in expansion. Both surveys show production still growing but moderating (NBS: 51.2; RatingDog: 53.4), consistent with continued—though less vigorous—factory activity. On demand, NBS new orders slipped into contraction (49.9), while RatingDog new orders declined to 52.5, still among the stronger readings of recent years. The shared signal is that demand momentum is weakening, with exports becoming a clearer drag: NBS new export orders fell 1.7pts to 48.6 and RatingDog new export orders dropped 1.5pts to 49.6, both in contractionary territory.

Price components moderated but stayed elevated. NBS input prices remained high at 60.5 and output prices at 51.9; RatingDog input prices eased to 55.8 and output prices to 52.0. The deceleration suggests less inflationary pressure at the margin, but levels remain consistent with ongoing cost strain. Future output stayed in solid expansion, implying continued confidence despite export and cost concerns. The NBS non-manufacturing PMI improved, rising 0.7pt to 50.1, with services back in expansion and construction less weak.

May RatingDog and NBS PMIs depict a picture of slowing but still healthy manufacturing expansion. The impact of the Middle East conflict lingers but may begin to wane as negotiations for a peace deal remain on track. Price and cost pressures will persist, as will supply chain delays. While exporters may face demand challenges, the recent Trump-Xi summit might inject a sense of stability. Firms stayed optimistic about continued, albeit slower, growth, citing new product launches, technological advances, and improved capacity.

## Taiwan: PMI strengthened further

Output component rose 0.8pt to 55.6, the highest in more than four and a half years (Figure 2), and demand components were steady. The Middle East conflict has put pressure on supply chains, prompting higher quantity purchases and lengthening suppliers' delivery times to the worst level since end-2021. Input and export price PMIs stayed elevated, despite a slight moderation in the pace of increases.

## RBI holds rates, boosts capital inflows

The RBI kept policy rates on hold, as we had expected, defying market pricing that had priced in a 60% chance of a 25 bp hike. With the rupee under pressure, it simultaneously announced several measures to attract capital inflows—consistent with India’s inflation-targeting “separability” framework, where policy rates and liquidity are used to manage domestic inflation and growth dynamics, while FX intervention and regulatory tools are used to address external-sector issues. The focus on capital inflows also reflects the view we have espoused that current Rupee pressures are being driven more by the capital account than the current account, making capital inflow-supporting measures the more appropriate response to rate hikes.

Even as the RBI kept rates on hold, it predictably marked down its 2026-27 growth forecast and marked up its inflation forecast, given the adverse supply shocks confronting the economy. GDP growth was marked down from 6.9% oya to 6.6%, and CPI inflation was marked up from 4.6% to 5.1%, in line with our current forecast (JPM: 5.1%). Our call that the RBI would be on hold was predicated on:

- Headline inflation forecasts, averaging 5% in FY27, and therefore remaining well within the inflation-targeting band; and
- Core inflation (correctly measured) remaining benign and averaging just 2.2% at the moment.

Given that inflation is being hit by adverse supply shocks, our expectation was and is that the RBI will be inclined to look through the supply shocks unless one, or both, of the aforementioned criteria is violated: Headline CPI hovers close to the upper band of the inflation targeting band on a sustained basis and/or inflation gets more entrenched and generalized and spills into core. The RBI statement appeared to echo these principles, noting, “...excluding precious metals, core inflation remained much lower, at 2.1-2.2 per cent...underlying inflation pressures remain benign at this juncture.” However, it cautioned that “...generalization of inflation through second-round effects on expectations and wages is a distinct possibility, warranting a close vigil. The outlook also remains clouded by the sub-normal south-west monsoon forecast and

El Niño risks."

Benign core inflation in India for the last two years suggests limited pricing power for firms. That said, input costs are likely to rise discernibly. The extent to which firms will be able to pass this on remains the key known unknown. Similarly, El Niño risks have risen sharply, but given ample buffer stocks, the extent to which this translates into higher food inflation and inflation expectations remains an open question. The RBI forecasts core inflation at 4.7% for 2026-27. If gold and silver prices do not change, this would imply that underlying core (core ex-precious) will average 3.8%, although it is likely to be higher in the second half of the year. Therefore, we remain comfortable for now with our view that the RBI will remain on hold, barring the aforementioned risks materializing in the data.

Separately, the RBI unveiled several capital account initiatives: concessional FX swap windows to incentivize Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) to undertake External Commercial Borrowings and for banks to raise fresh three- to five-year FCNR(B) deposits; an expansion of FAR-eligible long-dated government securities and removal of certain FPI limits under the General Route, higher equity investment limits for NRIs/OCIs and parity access for other overseas individuals, and a restoration of the export proceeds realization timeline to nine months.

## US proposes 12.5% tariffs under Sec 301

The Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR) has launched several Section 301 investigations under the “unreasonable or discriminatory practices” authority. Two investigations include India:

- Forced-labour probe (which applies to 60 economies): Examined whether foreign governments failed to legally prohibit—or adequately enforce prohibitions against—the importation of goods made with forced labour, creating an unfair cost advantage.
- Excess-capacity probe (which applies to 16 economies): Examined whether state subsidies, economic interventions, and related policies contributed to “structural excess” in core manufacturing sectors, including textiles, automobiles, construction goods, petrochemicals, and steel etc.

This week, under the forced-labour probe, the USTR found that the 60 economies had not adequately policed imports made with forced labour and proposed additional duties of 10%–12.5%. The proposed duty for India is 12.5%. The proposed tariffs are not yet final. Stakeholders may submit requests to participate in public hearings by 22 June 2026, with the hearing window running through 6 July 2026. Public hearings are scheduled for 7 July 2026. The USTR will review comments and testimony before making a final determination on the proposed measures.

If the Section 301 tariffs are implemented around the time the Section 122 tariffs expire ( $24^{th}$ July), India could effectively see the 10% universal tariff replaced by a 12.5% tariff. This would raise the effective tariff rate from 10.4% to 12.1%, with additional upside risk if the parallel excess-capacity investigation results in further duties.

New Delhi is pursuing a dual-track response: contesting the Section 301 proceedings on their merits while simultaneously advancing the U.S.-India Bilateral Trade Agreement (BTA) framework announced in February.

## Asia Focus: Oil shock and China's substitution effects

China's oil imports have declined significantly after the Middle East conflict (Figure 1), with China's April industrial production along the oil and chemical chain reflecting the drag from oil disruptions. This has made people wonder if China is still resilient in the face of a still fluid Middle East situation.

Figure 1: China's oil imports

First, the decline in crude imports may be less disruptive than the headline number implies. Since 2025, China has built up its oil reserves by importing $\sim 10\%$ higher ( $\sim 1\mathrm{mbpd}$ ) than its normal consumption level, making the pre-conflict benchmark inflated by stockpiling needs.

That said, China has still reduced crude throughput, as seen in the drop in refiners' run rates for cost considerations. Refiners' margins have turned negative due to state price controls on oil products, leaving refiners little incentive to keep operations running at a high utilization level and making losses (Figure 2). While this has reduced refined product output, the downstream effects appear to have been limited so far. In chemicals, higher oil prices have made the coal-to-chemical route more cost-competitive. Coke oven operating rates have picked up (Figure 2), providing an alternative to oil-based chemical feedstocks, at least partially. Potential drawdowns of commercial inventories across different layers of the extended petrochemical supply chain also help cushion disruptions, reducing the risk of a larger, more immediate shock.

The substitution effect is most visible in transport, which accounts for roughly half of oil product uses. Data shows that while gasoline consumption declined amid higher costs of refueling, daily vehicle on road has not declined, and was even higher than pre-conflict levels. Explaining this contrast is the higher EV utilization in the on-road fleet. Electricity consumption at EV charging stations jumped 69% oya in April, vs. 55% in Jan-Feb (Figure 3), and ride-sharing orders (mostly run by EVs) spiked 27% in April. For longer-distance travel, although domestic flight activity underperformed the previous average in April and May, rail traffic (largely electrified) appears to have taken part of the demand diverted from air travel (Figure 4).

In effect, an economic optimization is underway: a rational reduction in operating rates alongside a shift in demand toward more cost-efficient modes. Given China's outsized role in the global oil market, a return to more “normal” import levels could tighten the regional supply–demand balance and generate spillover effects on pricing and flows—a dynamic worth monitoring closely.

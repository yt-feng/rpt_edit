## What could make us more positive/negative on the stock

In the near-to-mid term, we see dual challenges for the new leadership: extending the durability of the Dupixent franchise, while simultaneously demonstrating a credible path towards replacing its sales and profitability either through delivery on internal pipeline or BD/M&A.

We see the following as the key developments which could make us more positive on the stock, and which could potentially help rebuild investor confidence in Sanofi's R&D engine:

1) Progress on Dupixent lifecycle management initiatives: Lifecycle management remains a key area of investor focus, particularly as the new CEO begins to lay out long-term strategic priorities. Greater clarity on the scope for Dupixent's period of market exclusivity to extend beyond current market expectations could represent meaningful valuation upside by delaying biosimilar entry (consensus currently expects 2033-34 biosimilar entry). In addition, evidence that longer-acting Dupixent formulations offer meaningful differentiation beyond dosing convenience, such as improvements in efficacy, safety or patient outcomes, could further enhance franchise durability in an increasingly competitive market.

2) Rebuild and delivery of the pipeline: Following a period marked by pipeline disappointments, sentiment around the pipeline is very low. Key drivers to help raise sentiment are: (a) near-term catalysts: detailed Phase 2 lunsekimig data in asthma, to be presented at the annual European Respiratory Society meeting in September 2026, a decision on the future development path of itepekimab in COPD, and Phase 3 frexalimab results in Relapsing Multiple Sclerosis (RMS) and Non-Relapsing Secondary Progressive MS (nrSPMS) (expected in 2H27, primary completion in May 2027); (b) mid-term value drivers: Phase 3 duvakitug data in Ulcerative Colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD), expected in 2H28 and 2H29 respectively, represent a potentially differentiated opportunity in a fast-evolving and increasingly competitive IBD market; approval and launch of edoralprin alpha, a differentiated recombinant AAT therapy with potential regulatory clarity in 2H26 and longer-term upside from increased testing and earlier diagnosis of AATD-related COPD; and (c) early stage optionality: Sanofi's early stage pipeline is not reflected in our forecasts, but could provide upside over time. The PD-1×IL-15 asset offers high-risk, high-reward exposure to next-generation immuno-oncology, while the gene therapy platform could become a more meaningful growth driver if early clinical data in ophthalmology and rare diseases prove encouraging.

3) Value-accretive M&A: Disciplined, value-accretive M&A could help reinvigorate investor sentiment by strengthening confidence in Sanofi's ability to diversify growth ahead of Dupixent's eventual loss of exclusivity. Management has indicated capacity to deploy up to €15bn in 2026, and our analysis of prior deals suggests commercial-stage acquisitions offer the fastest path to earnings accretion, while clinical-stage bolt-ons provide the strongest long-term return potential. Successful capital deployment would represent an important step towards building the next growth platform beyond Dupixent.

The following could make us more negative on the stock:

1) Valuation re-rating without fundamental support: We could become more negative if Sanofi's shares were to materially outperform and trade at a premium without corresponding improvements in earnings expectations, pipeline de-risking or meaningful commercial catalysts. In our view, an expansion in valuation unsupported by fundamentals would leave limited scope for further upside while increasing downside risk if expectations are not met.

2) Further pipeline disappointments: Additional late-stage pipeline setbacks or weaker-than-expected clinical data would increase our caution, particularly given the importance of internal innovation to Sanofi's medium-term growth outlook. Further clinical disappointments would reduce future revenue potential and increase reliance on the existing commercial portfolio to sustain earnings momentum, while making Sanofi increasingly reliant on external innovation and M&A/licensing to support long-term growth.

## Dupixent: how much value remains to be unlocked through lifecycle management?

Dupixent remains the central pillar of the Sanofi investment story and continues to drive the majority of the company's earnings momentum. In 1Q26, Dupixent sales beat consensus by c.7%, driving 95% of the Group's top-line beat. While we expect Dupixent to remain the key growth driver, we anticipate its momentum to slow, as also noted by Sanofi in its 1Q26 earnings commentary, as the company enters a period of tougher comparators (note that in 2025, Dupixent received approvals in additional disease areas, including Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria (CSU - April-25 in the US) and Bullous Pemphigoid (BP - June-25 in the US), and advanced the commercial rollout in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) across geographies).

Against this backdrop, lifecycle management remains a key area of investor focus, particularly as the new CEO begins to lay out long-term strategic priorities. Sanofi outlined a ‘Defend, Extend, Innovate’ strategy for Dupixent lifecycle management, with the company emphasizing a strong patent portfolio extending to 2045, and noting its intent to vigorously defend all patents. In our Sandoz initiation (see here), we note that average Biosimilars in the US launched c.1.5-2 years after the compound patent expiry (Sanofi recently highlighted that its analysis suggested US biosimilar launch 2-6 years post compound patent expiry).

\- Defend - Pushing out the patent expiry beyond the market's base case for biosimilar entry in 2033-34 could drive upside to our DCF. Dupixent compound patents are seen by investors as expiring in 2031/32 - in the US, Dupixent compound patents are anticipated to expire in March 2031, with the European patent expiry is generally expected in September 2032, although methods of use patents extend into the early 2040s. Our discussions with investors suggest that the expectation in the market is for biosimilars to enter through 2033-34 as a base case. Currently, there are at least six biosimilars of dupilumab in development (Sandoz, Advanz/Alvotech, Formycon, Bio-Thera, Amneal Biosciences, Samsung Bioepis), and while biosimilar entry in the US could be pushed back by a few years, we would anticipate biosimilar entry in Europe shortly after patent expiration. Our analysis of the US/EU biosimilar markets (see our Sandoz initiation) highlights that biosimilars are generally launched in Europe in the year of patent expiry/LoE (or within 6-12 months), but delays in the US can be 1.5-2 years. Greater clarity around IP extension has been seen by some investors as a potential meaningful near-term catalyst - however, Sanofi noted there have been no patent challenges, and hence no settlements, and therefore there is no set time-frame to communicate to give more clarity to the market. We now model biosimilar entry/price pressure for Dupixent starting from 2033 and reduce our terminal growth rate from $0\%$ to $-2\%$ given R&D disappointments, implying a $-4\%$ impact on our DCF estimates.

\- Extend - Longer-acting formulations could extend the Dupixent franchise, offering longer treatment intervals combined with the trusted safety profile - we see this as only partially protecting the Dupixent franchise. At the 1Q26 results, Sanofi noted progress on a longer-acting Q4W Dupixent formulation, describing the approach as well-precedented, and enabled by higher concentrations or in combination with hyaluronidase. The company highlighted ongoing development of a higher-dose

Q4W regimen in asthma, noting that details on the development plan with partner Regeneron will be shared going forward. Currently, development is focused on the Q4W dosing interval, where there are already options available, and as such we see this as only partially protective of the franchise, particularly in ex-US markets.

Innovate - Of note, Regeneron has also highlighted a number of assets not included in the Sanofi alliance (Long-acting IL13 & IL4 antibodies with optimised binding properties and long acting IL-4xIL-13 bispecific). While a longer-acting formulation of Dupixent could provide an additional lifecycle management lever, we view the commercial impact as less clear cut. In addition, the competitive landscape continues to evolve, with emerging agents such as ABBV's zumilokibart seeking to differentiate through less-frequent dosing schedules while delivering comparable efficacy. As a result, dosing convenience alone may not be sufficient to materially extend the franchise, unless accompanied by clear advantages in efficacy, safety or patient outcomes. The use of co-contracting could be a driver, however, with PBM reform it is unclear how this will remain a benefit in the future.

Overall, we see a dual challenge for management: extending the durability of the Dupixent franchise while simultaneously demonstrating a credible path towards replacing its sales and profitability, either through delivery of the internal pipeline or BD/M&A. Below we outline the key pipeline opportunities we see, and our view on a value-accretive M&A strategy. Progress on either front under new leadership could be a key driver of upside for the stock and help reinvigorate investor sentiment, we believe.

## Near-term catalysts

## Frexalimab:

Phase 3 readouts for Frexalimab in both relapsing multiple sclerosis (RMS) and non-relapsing secondary progressive MS (nrSPMS) are expected in 2027 and could represent an important inflection point for Sanofi's neurology franchise, following the disappointments for tolebrutinib. Frexalimab targets the CD40L pathway, a differentiated mechanism positioned upstream of both adaptive and innate immune activation. Mechanistically, CD40L inhibition modulates T-cell, B-cell, macrophage, microglial, and dendritic cell activity without causing broad lymphocyte depletion, potentially offering a different balance between efficacy and immune preservation relative to anti-CD20 therapies. We note that continuous B-cell suppression with CD20's was a concern of the KOL at our innovation clinic back in March 2026 (see here).

Data so far in MS: In the Phase 2 RMS study, frexalimab demonstrated robust MRI efficacy, with the high-dose arm showing an \~89% reduction in new gadolinium-enhancing T1 lesions at Week 12 versus placebo, while the low-dose arm showed \~79% reduction. Importantly, these effects appeared durable with longer follow-up, with sustained suppression of inflammatory MRI activity observed out to \~96 weeks in extension data. Findings also showed both doses of frexalimab provided significant reduction in new or enlarging T2 lesions, a secondary endpoint of the study. The program also generated encouraging biomarker data, with neurofilament light chain (NfL) reductions of \~41% in the high-dose arm by Week 48, suggesting potential impact on underlying neuroaxonal injury beyond acute inflammatory control. Safety was also viewed favourably, particularly given the absence of broad lymphocyte depletion, serious infection signals, or immunoglobulin suppression typically associated with chronic B-cell depletion strategies.

Commercial outlook and positioning in MS: Sanofi is additionally advancing frexalimab in nrSPMS, which may ultimately represent a more differentiated opportunity for the asset, given the role of chronic innate immune activation and microglial biology in progressive disease. However, the broader multiple sclerosis (MS) market remains highly challenging (see our MS deep dive here). Anti-CD20 therapies are deeply entrenched, and we expect the class to maintain \~65% market share given their consistently strong efficacy, long-term data sets, and physician familiarity. As such, the efficacy bar for new entrants is high, and so market expectations for frexalimab currently appear relatively modest in consensus estimates. The competitive landscape is also becoming increasingly crowded, with BTK inhibitors advancing across RMS and progressive MS alongside a growing number of early-stage CAR-T approaches. In our view, frexalimab's opportunity may ultimately depend less on displacing CD20 therapies broadly and more on establishing a differentiated profile in patients where long-term immune preservation, progression biology, or chronic treatment tolerability become increasingly important considerations.

Other indications: Beyond multiple sclerosis, Sanofi has expanded frexalimab development into kidney transplant rejection (Phase 2/3 FREXERA study, primary completion in Aug 2029) and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) (Phase 2a RESULT study, primary completion in Dec 2026). In transplantation, the CD40/CD40L axis plays a central role in T-cell activation, B-cell maturation, antibody production, and antigen-presenting cell signalling, making it one of the most biologically validated co-stimulatory pathways in graft rejection. Current standard immunosuppressive regimens, primarily calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus, are effective but associated with significant long-term toxicities including nephrotoxicity, infection risk, and cardiovascular complications. CD40L inhibition therefore offers the potential for more targeted immune modulation without broad immune depletion. Importantly, according to studies newer Fc-engineered antibodies such as frexalimab are designed to avoid the thromboembolic issues that historically limited earlier CD40L programs. In FSGS, CD40/CD40L signalling has been implicated in podocyte injury, inflammation, and fibrosis in FSGS, providing a rationale for targeting the pathway in progressive kidney disease. While still early, success in these renal indications could support the idea that CD40L modulation has applicability across multiple immune-mediated settings beyond neurology alone and could materially strengthen confidence in both the mechanism and Sanofi's broader innovation capabilities.

Our estimates: In our model, we include peak unadjusted sales of €3bn at a 40% PoS. In the event of positive Phase 3 results, de-risking our current 40% PoS would lead to 8% upside to our DCF-based valuation, with failure implying 5% downside, all else equal.

## Itepekimab

Itepekimab, Sanofi's anti-IL-33 antibody developed with Regeneron, has had a mixed clinical trajectory that has weighed on investor sentiment toward the IL-33 class (beyond AstraZeneca's tozorakimab). In Phase 3 COPD (AERIFY program), itepekimab delivered a split outcome, meeting its primary endpoint in AERIFY-1 with a $c.27\%$ reduction in moderate-to-severe exacerbations, but failing in AERIFY-2, where lower-than-expected exacerbation rates (partly COVID-related) likely reduced statistical power. While safety remained broadly comparable to placebo and management emphasised the need for further data interrogation, the inconsistency raised questions on both trial design and the robustness of IL-33 inhibition in COPD.

By contrast, AstraZeneca's tozorakimab has reported positive Phase 3 results across three COPD trials, demonstrating consistent efficacy not only in the primary population (former smokers) but also in broader patient groups across smoking status, eosinophil levels and disease severity. Mechanistically, tozorakimab is designed to inhibit both canonical ST2-mediated signalling and alternative IL-33 pathways, potentially providing broader suppression of IL-33 biology than itepekimab (which primarily targets the IL-33/ST2 axis) and Roche's astegolimab (which targets the ST2 receptor and did not meet the primary endpoint of a statistically significant reduction in the AER at 52 weeks in the Phase 3 ARNASA study). While this validates IL-33 as a therapeutic target, we believe it also raises the competitive bar for itepekimab. Even if itepekimab progresses to market, tozorakimab's differentiated profile and likely broader label are likely to limit its commercial opportunity.

Sanofi has signalled continued commitment to the asset beyond COPD, with ongoing development in chronic rhinosinusitis (with and without nasal polyps), indicating a broader respiratory franchise approach. In our model, we include peak unadjusted sales of €2.5bn across indications at a 20% PoS. De-risking our current 20% PoS leads to 6% upside to our DCF-based valuation, with failure implying 1% downside, all else equal.

## Mid-term catalysts

## Duvakitug and the TL1A opportunity:

Duvakitug is emerging as an increasingly important asset within Sanofi's pipeline, with growing conviction around both its clinical profile and commercial potential. Duvakitug has demonstrated best-in class efficacy signals in Phase 2b IBD trials, including encouraging data in both induction and maintenance settings, reinforcing confidence in durability of response. The asset currently sits third in the class in terms of development timelines, behind Roche and the class leader Merck & Co.). Beyond inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), where partner Teva Pharmaceutical (covered by Matt Dellatore) sees a c.US\$3-5bn peak sales opportunity, the program is being actively expanded into additional indications. Notably, Teva on its 1Q26 earnings call, outlined three development buckets: T2, non-T2, and fibrotic diseases, and emphasised a disciplined, criteria-driven approach to indication prioritisation based on market size, mechanistic rationale, probability of regulatory success, and speed to market, with further updates expected through 2026. Overall, duvakitug appears to be transitioning from a relatively underappreciated asset to a potentially meaningful growth driver, with upside linked not only to IBD but also to broader lifecycle expansion opportunities. Below, we explore its potential and relevance within IBD, as well as the opportunity set beyond IBD.

Mechanism of action and relevance in IBD: TL1A is a cytokine belonging to the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily. In healthy individuals, TL1A levels temporarily increase to help immune cells fight infections. However, in patients with IBD, TL1A is chronically overexpressed in the inflamed intestinal mucosa. Its mechanism of action is characterized by a dual role in driving both inflammation and fibrosis. Key drivers of clinical differentiation for this mechanism are:

a) Dual-Action MoA (Inflammation + Fibrosis) and potential first-in-class anti fibrotic potential: Unlike legacy biologics that solely target inflammatory cascades, TL1A blockade disrupts the TL1A-DR3 axis. This not only downregulates key pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN- $\gamma$ , TNF- $\alpha$ , IL-17) but also directly halts fibroblast activation and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. One of the most significant unmet needs in IBD (particularly in Crohn's disease) is the development of fibrostenosis (intestinal strictures). Currently, there are no approved medical therapies that directly target fibrosis in IBD, leaving surgical bowel resection as the only definitive treatment. Because TL1A directly stimulates fibroblasts, anti-TL1A antibodies have the unique potential to halt or even reverse collagen deposition and scarring, fundamentally altering the natural trajectory of the disease.

b) Potential to raise the efficacy ceiling in refractory population. Currently, existing biologics often fail to achieve long-term, deep remission. According to studies, approximately one-third of patients do not respond to initial treatment, and up to 40% lose response over time. TL1A inhibitors have demonstrated robust clinical remission and endoscopic healing rates in Phase 2 trials, offering a potentially effective option for patients with multi-biologic-refractory disease.

c) Favourable Safety Profile: Early clinical data suggest that TL1A inhibitors may not be as broadly immunosuppressive as traditional anti-TNF therapies. By acting more as an amplifier of specific inflammatory and fibrotic signals, rather than a broad immune suppressant, TL1A blockade may offer a safer long-term profile with a reduced risk of infections.

d) Enabling Precision Medicine: Genetic polymorphisms in the TNFSF15 gene, which encodes the TL1A protein, are strongly correlated with IBD risk and severity. This genetic link provides a rationale for using molecular biomarkers to identify specific patient subpopulations most likely to respond to TL1A blockade and thereby optimize trial probabilities of success and drive targeted commercial uptake.

Data so far: Data from the Phase2b RELIEVE UCCD study demonstrated compelling efficacy at week 14 across both UC and CD. In UC, 36.2% (low-dose) and 47.8% (high-dose) of patients with UC treated with duvakitug achieved clinical remission compared to 20.45% on placebo - placebo-adjusted rates were 15.7% (low dose) and 27.4% (high dose) at week 14. Further higher clinical remission rates were observed for both doses of duvakitug versus placebo in both advanced therapy (AT)-experienced (22% and 29% placebo adjusted response in low- and high-dose respectively) and AT-naïve subgroups of patients (12% and 26% placebo-adjusted response in low- and high-dose respectively). In patients with CD, 26.1% (low-dose) and 47.8% (high-dose) treated with duvakitug achieved endoscopic response compared to 13.0% on placebo - placebo-adjusted rates were 13.0% (low dose) and 34.8% (high dose) at week 14. Higher endoscopic response rates were observed for both doses of duvakitug versus placebo in both AT-experienced (7% and 44% placebo adjusted response in low- and high-dose respectively) and -naïve subgroups of patients (22% and 29% placebo adjusted response in low- and high-dose respectively). Maintenance data indicate a highly differentiated profile for duvakitug, with rates of clinical remission and endoscopic response of 58% and 55% in UC and CD, respectively, at the 900mg Q4W dose alongside a clean safety profile.

## Efdoralprin alpha

Efdoralprin alfa is an emerging asset within the rare disease pipeline, targeting alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) related emphysema. Mechanistically, it is a recombinant AAT-Fc fusion designed to restore functional AAT levels while enabling extended dosing intervals, addressing key limitations of plasma-derived augmentation therapies. The Phase 2 ElevAATe study delivered a positive dataset, demonstrating superiority in maintaining AAT levels within the normal range versus standard of care and supporting less frequent dosing (Q3W/Q4W), underpinning a differentiated profile on both efficacy and convenience. In the US, Sanofi expects to determine in 2H26 whether the existing dataset could support a filing, suggesting potential for an accelerated path. In Europe, however, a Phase 3 outcomes study is likely required, anchoring a base case of 2028 readout and 2028/29 launch for broader approval.

Treatment landscape in AATD-related emphysema: The current treatment landscape for AATD-related emphysema is dominated by four approved plasma-derived augmentation therapies- Prolastin-C (Grifols), Glassia (Takeda), Zemaira (CSL) and Aralast NP (Takeda) which have established alpha-1 antitrypsin replacement as the standard of care. These products are purified from human plasma and aim to restore circulating AAT levels sufficiently to inhibit neutrophil elastase-mediated lung damage. However, plasma-derived therapies are associated with several inherent limitations, including dependence on plasma collection and fractionation infrastructure, variability in supply, and the need for lifelong weekly intravenous infusions to maintain protective AAT concentrations. Efdoralprin alfa is designed as a recombinant AAT-Fc fusion protein that could potentially address several of these constraints. By leveraging Fc-mediated half-life extension, the asset has demonstrated the ability to maintain functional AAT levels within the normal range with Q3W/Q4W dosing in Phase 2 studies, representing a meaningful reduction in treatment burden versus current augmentation therapies. In addition, recombinant manufacturing removes reliance on plasma supply, potentially improving scalability and supply security. As such, if efdoralprin alfa is ultimately able to secure US approval based on the existing dataset, or if a future Phase 3 study establishes a sufficiently compelling clinical profile to support broader regulatory approval, the asset has the potential to meaningfully disrupt a plasma-derived AAT augmentation market.

Another key angle highlighted by management is the opportunity in premature COPD associated with AATD, which remains significantly under-diagnosed and under-tested today. Current clinical practice often identifies patients only once disease is advanced, limiting the impact of augmentation therapies. Sanofi has pointed to the lack of systematic screening and early testing for AATD in COPD populations as a major gap, suggesting that broader adoption of testing could materially expand the diagnosed patient pool. In this context, efdoralprin alfa could play a role not only as a treatment innovation but also as a catalyst for earlier intervention paradigms, where restoring AAT levels before significant lung damage occurs may have greater disease-modifying potential. If testing rates improve and earlier-stage patients are captured, this could represent a meaningful expansion of the addressable market beyond the traditional severe AATD population. Looking ahead, the trajectory of efdoralprin alfa will likely be defined not just by clinical outcomes, but by how effectively the field shifts toward earlier diagnosis and treatment. Greater uptake of AATD testing in COPD, particularly in patients with early or atypical disease, could meaningfully increase patient identification, positioning the asset to benefit from both improved clinical management

and a structurally expanding market.

## SAR445877 (PD-1 IL-15)

Mechanism: PD-1/IL-15 fusion proteins are designed to address a key limitation of checkpoint inhibitors by combining immune reactivation with immune amplification in a single construct. PD-1 blockade removes inhibitory signalling on exhausted T cells, while IL-15 drives proliferation and activation of CD8+ T cells and NK cells. By co-localising these effects, the approach aims to both reinvigorate and expand anti-tumour immunity directly within the tumour microenvironment, particularly in checkpoint-refractory or “cold” tumours. Critically, linking IL-15 to a PD-1 antibody enables more targeted cytokine delivery, which could improve the efficacy-toxicity balance relative to earlier systemic IL-15 approaches. At our Innovation Summit in March 2026, management described the mechanism as biologically plausible and opening up new therapeutic avenues, although still at an early stage with initial data appearing promising in 3L msCRC.

Data so far: Sanofi's PD-1/IL-15 asset (SAR445877) remains in early clinical development, with initial Phase 1/2 data (ASCO 2025) suggesting a more encouraging profile than prior attempts in the class (Pfizer's discontinued PD-1/IL-15 fusion, PF-07209960). While the dataset is small and early, it provides an initial signal that Sanofi may be achieving a more favourable balance between immune activation and tolerability, historically a key challenge for IL-15-based therapies.

## Gene therapy

Sanofi's gene therapy portfolio remains early stage but provides longer-dated optionality across ophthalmology and neuromuscular disease, with the potential to complement its core franchises if clinical proof-of-concept is established. The company is advancing multiple AAV-based programs, including: (i) SAR446597 (Bb C1s AAV) in Phase 1 for geographic atrophy (GA); (ii) SAR402663 (sFLT01 AAV) in Phase 2 for wet AMD; and (iii) SAR446268 (DMPK AAV) in Phase 1 for myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1). All three assets are built around the premise of one-time, durable expression of therapeutic proteins, aiming to shift treatment paradigms away from chronic dosing.

In ophthalmology, SAR446597 (Bb C1s AAV) is being evaluated in Phase 1 for geographic atrophy (GA) secondary to dry age-related macular degeneration. The program targets complement pathway dysregulation, a validated mechanism in GA, with the aim of providing sustained intraocular inhibition via a single administration, potentially improving on the burden and variability associated with frequent injections. SAR402663 (sFLT01 AAV) is in Phase 2 for wet AMD, where it seeks to deliver continuous anti-VEGF activity through gene expression, potentially reducing or eliminating the need for repeated anti-VEGF injections. Given the large and established anti-VEGF market, a durable gene therapy approach could represent a meaningful shift in treatment paradigm, particularly in patients requiring frequent dosing.

Among late-stage retinal gene therapy assets in wet AMD, the most advanced programs-AbbVie/REGENXBIO's ABBV-RGX-314, 4D Molecular Therapeutics's 4D-150, and Adverum Biotechnologies's Ixo-vec (ADVM-022) have demonstrated encouraging proof-of-concept around durable anti-VEGF expression and substantial reduction in injection burden. ABBV-RGX-314 has shown sustained intraocular anti-VEGF expression with preservation of visual acuity and meaningful reductions in rescue injections, supporting progression into multiple Phase 3 studies. Ixo-vec demonstrated c.80% reductions in annualized anti-VEGF injection burden in the OPTIC study while maintaining stable BCVA and retinal anatomy, although inflammation management remains an important focus for the program. 4D-150 has generated encouraging Phase 1/2 data with durable efficacy signals and a potentially differentiated profile through dual VEGF-A and VEGF-C inhibition. Overall, these programs suggest retinal gene therapy may increasingly approach the efficacy of current anti-VEGF standards while offering a potentially significant reduction in treatment burden through one-time administration. However, despite these encouraging datasets, we believe the key debate is likely to shift from whether gene therapy can reduce injections to whether it can achieve a sufficiently favourable long-term risk-benefit profile relative to increasingly durable injectable anti-VEGF therapies. In our view, major concerns remain around intraocular inflammation, controllability of transgene expression, long-term retinal safety, and physician willingness to adopt irreversible treatment approaches in a chronic disease setting where current therapies are already highly effective and increasingly less burdensome.

## What might the capital allocation policy (particularly BD M&A) look like under the new CEO?

We have analysed Sanofi's historical M&A transactions (>US\$1bn) and estimated the ROIC for each acquisition over our forecast horizon, comparing these returns against the company's estimated 8% WACC to assess shareholder value creation. Our analysis suggests that outcomes vary materially by transaction type, with commercial deals generally offering the fastest path to value accretion, clinical-stage bolt-ons generating the strongest long-term return potential (albeit with higher pipeline risk), and platform acquisitions requiring greater upfront investment and longer timelines, while offering meaningful long-term ROIC expansion where multiple assets are successfully commercialised.

Exhibit 12: Summary of deals and value creation


Key takeaways from our analysis:

■ Commercial Deals (Transformative, Bolt-On & Other Commercial): Commercial transactions represent the broadest category within Sanofi's M&A history, with outcomes varying materially by deal size, asset maturity, and acquisition valuation. Genzyme (US\$20.1bn, 2011), the sole transformative commercial deal in our analysis, exceeded the 8% WACC by Year 3 on our estimates, and reached peak ROIC of \~19%, before moderating toward \~11% as key products matured. Among commercial bolt-ons, returns appear closely linked to valuation discipline and commercial execution. Kadmon (US\$1.9bn, 2021) and Dynavax (US\$2.2bn, 2025) exceeded WACC within three years on our estimates, generating peak ROIC of \~36% and \~19%, respectively. By contrast, Provention Bio (US\$2.9bn, 2023) does not exceed WACC in our model horizon, with peak ROIC of \~6%, highlighting the sensitivity of returns to peak sales assumptions relative to acquisition cost. Larger transactions such as Bioverativ (US\$11.6bn, 2018) and Blueprint (US\$9.1bn, 2025) fall between these outcomes, exceeding WACC by Year 6 and Year 4 respectively, with peak ROIC of \~23% and \~15%. Overall, commercial deals appear to provide the clearest path to near-term value accretion, although ultimate returns remain dependent on acquisition valuation and asset durability.

Clinical Bolt-On Deals: Clinical-stage bolt-ons, in our view, offer some of the strongest long-term return profiles within Sanofi's M&A portfolio, albeit with longer timelines and higher pipeline risk. Principia Biopharma (US\$3.7bn, 2020) is projected to deliver peak ROIC of \~70% by 2037 on our estimates, supported by rilzabrutinib and tolebrutinib peak sales potential. Kymab (US\$1.45bn, 2021) follows with projected peak ROIC of \~64%, while Inhibrx (US\$2.2bn, 2024) reaches \~36% on our estimates. However, time-to-value creation is longer, with ROIC expected to exceed WACC in Year 4 for Inhibrx, Year 7 for Principia, and Year 9 for Kymab. The category also carries meaningful binary risk, as illustrated by Synthorx (US\$2.5bn, 2019), which resulted in a full impairment following discontinuation of its lead programme. Overall, clinical bolt-ons in the US\$1–4bn range appear capable of generating attractive long-term returns where Sanofi can leverage existing therapeutic and commercial infrastructure.

Platform Deals: Platform acquisitions typically involve longer development timelines and upfront investment, with returns weighted toward outer years as multiple assets are developed from the underlying technology. Ablynx (US\$4.8bn, 2018) is the clearest example in our analysis, with the nanobody platform expected to exceed WACC by Year 12 and peak ROIC reaching \~48% by 2037 on our estimates, supported by multiple pipeline assets including Cablivi, brivekimig and lunsekimig. Translate Bio (US\$3.2bn, 2021) follows a similar pattern, with ROIC expected to exceed WACC by Year 8 and peak ROIC reaching \~21%, reflecting the longer development timelines associated with mRNA platforms. Vicebio (US\$1.6bn, 2025) remains too early stage for meaningful returns analysis. Overall, platform transactions appear less suited to near-term value accretion given the upfront investment required and longer time to commercialisation, but can support significant long-term ROIC expansion where the platform generates multiple commercially successful assets.

What could Sanofi Do? Our view on M&A strategy ahead of Dupixent LoE
In terms of capital allocation and M&A, Sanofi's approach has been focused on investing in organic growth, pursuing selective bolt-on acquisitions, maintaining a progressive dividend and executing share buybacks. On the 1Q26 earnings call, the company noted that it would generally target bolt-on acquisitions in the €2-5bn range, while emphasising that it would not discount larger opportunities if they are attractive and offer shareholder value. At our Innovation Summit in March 2026, Sanofi indicated that it expects increased activity in immunology, continued but more selective investment in rare diseases, right-sizing in vaccines, and sees ophthalmology as a potential area of breakout growth. Further, on platforms management noted a focused approach, prioritizing areas of strength such as monoclonal and bispecific antibodies, where more assets are expected to enter the clinic and highlighted emerging capabilities in blood brain barrier shuttle and AAV platforms. We see Sanofi having balance sheet capacity of c.US\$39bn for M&A, if we were to assume that the company was willing to move to 3x ND/EBITDA (3x being a typical limit historically for Pharma companies for financial leverage), vs. c.0.6x currently (GSe 2026E) and an estimated c.0.2x by end-2027E (GSe). Of note, the company has indicated that it could invest up to €15bn in 2026 in BD M&A, while retaining an AA credit rating.

Given the approaching loss of exclusivity for Dupixent, our ROIC analysis suggests Sanofi may be better positioned pursuing a balanced M&A strategy combining near-term commercial bolt-ons with selective clinical-stage acquisitions, rather than relying on a single large-scale transformative transaction. Commercial bolt-ons appear to offer the fastest path to value accretion, typically exceeding WACC within \~3 years on our estimates, while also providing a nearer-term revenue contribution. However, our analysis indicates that returns remain highly sensitive to acquisition valuation. Clinical-stage bolt-ons in the sub-US\$5bn range appear to offer the strongest long-term return potential, with projected peak ROIC ranging from \~36%-70% on our estimates, particularly where Sanofi can leverage existing capabilities in immunology, neurology, and rare disease. By contrast, while large transformative acquisitions can provide immediate scale, our analysis suggests they may carry more moderate long-term returns alongside greater integration and portfolio maturity risk, as illustrated by the declining ROIC profile observed in Genzyme over time.

Platform acquisitions can also generate substantial long-term ROIC expansion, although the return profile is typically back-end weighted and requires longer development timelines and significant upfront investment. Given the timing of Dupixent's anticipated LoE, we believe Sanofi is likely to prioritise transactions with clearer medium-term revenue contribution. Overall, our analysis supports a more diversified acquisition approach, combining selective clinical-stage bolt-ons with commercially accretive assets, while maintaining disciplined acquisition valuation.
